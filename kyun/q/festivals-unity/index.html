Why does India celebrate so many festivals?

On or around 14 January, people across India celebrate the same harvest moment under different names, including Pongal, Lohri, Bihu and Makar Sankranti.

The short answer

India celebrates so many festivals because it holds so many different peoples, religions, languages and regions in one country. Each brings its own calendar of special days. Hindus, Muslims, Christians, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis, Jews and many tribal communities all have festivals of their own.

On top of religious days, there are harvest festivals tied to farming seasons, regional new years, and national days like Republic Day. Different states also use different calendars, some based on the Sun, some on the Moon and some on both. Put it all together, and there is hardly a month without a celebration somewhere in India.

Surprising but true

India has only three national holidays fixed by the central government (Republic Day, Independence Day and Gandhi Jayanti), yet calendars list dozens of festival holidays that differ from state to state.

Following the farm and the seasons

Many Indian festivals began as celebrations of farming. When a crop was harvested, there was food, rest and something to be thankful for.

In mid-January, as days start to lengthen, farmers in many states mark a winter harvest. In Tamil Nadu it is Pongal, when families cook rice with milk and jaggery until it boils over, a sign of plenty. In Punjab it is Lohri, with bonfires and songs. In Assam it is Magh Bihu, with feasts. In Gujarat it is Uttarayan, when the sky fills with kites, and in many states it is Makar Sankranti. In Kerala, the harvest festival is Onam, in late summer.

Many religions, one calendar year

India is home to most of the world's Hindus, one of the largest Muslim populations of any country, and many Christians, Sikhs, Buddhists and Jains. Each faith has its own festivals.

Diwali, the festival of lights, is celebrated by Hindus, Sikhs and Jains, each for their own reasons. Eid al-Fitr marks the end of Ramadan, the month of fasting for Muslims. Christmas is a public holiday across India. Guru Nanak Jayanti honours the founder of Sikhism. Buddha Purnima marks the Buddha's birth and enlightenment. Mahavir Jayanti honours the 24th Jain tirthankara. Parsis celebrate Navroz, their new year.

How do Indian calendars work?India has an official national calendar, yet most families plan festivals using a different one, and which one depends on where they live.

Why the dates keep moving

Festivals jump around because India uses several calendars at once. The government's official dates follow the solar Gregorian calendar, like most of the world. But many Hindu festivals follow lunisolar calendars, which count months by the Moon but add an extra month every few years to keep in step with the seasons.

The Islamic calendar is purely lunar. Its year is about 11 days shorter than the solar year, so Eid moves earlier each year. Over about 33 years, it passes through every season. That's why Eid can fall in the heat of May one decade and the cool of winter another.

Sharing across communities

In many Indian cities and villages, festivals are shared. Neighbours bring sweets to each other at Diwali, Eid and Christmas. Schools often celebrate several festivals in one year. Durga Puja pandals in Kolkata draw visitors of every faith, and crowds of all backgrounds gather to see Christmas lights on Park Street.

Some temples, dargahs and gurdwaras welcome visitors of all religions. The langar, a free community meal at gurdwaras, feeds anyone who comes, whatever their background.

Festival times can also bring tensions, and disputes over processions or noise sometimes make the news. But for many Indians, celebrating each other's festivals is part of everyday life.

National days

Three days belong to the whole country: Republic Day on 26 January, Independence Day on 15 August and Gandhi Jayanti on 2 October. They are celebrated by everyone, with flag hoisting, parades and school events.

On Republic Day, a grand parade moves along Kartavya Path in New Delhi. Floats from different states show off their dances, crafts and festivals. In a single morning, you can see how many traditions fit inside one country.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In India, each festival is celebrated only by people of that religion.

Truth: Many festivals are shared across communities. Neighbours of different faiths often exchange sweets at Diwali, Eid and Christmas, and some festivals, like Holi or the harvest festivals, are enjoyed as much for the season as for religion.
