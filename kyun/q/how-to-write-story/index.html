How do you write a good story?

Roald Dahl wrote almost all his books in a small hut at the bottom of his garden, sitting in an old armchair with a board across his knees.

The short answer

A good story usually has a character the reader cares about, something that character wants badly, and a problem that gets in the way. The story follows what they do about it, and ends with something changed, either the situation or the character.

Beyond that, good stories tend to start quickly, show things happening instead of explaining them, and leave out the boring bits. Most of all, good stories are rewritten. Writers rarely get it right the first time, and the best ones keep fixing their stories until every sentence earns its place.

Surprising but true

Many famous books went through several full drafts. Writers often say the real work is not writing the story but rewriting it.

Start with a want

Think of almost any story you love. Harry Potter wants to belong. Matilda wants to be treated fairly. In the Panchatantra, a monkey wants to escape from a crocodile who plans to eat his heart. Wanting something gives a character a direction, and gives the reader a question: will they get it?

Then comes the obstacle. It can be a villain, a storm, a rule, a fear or another person who wants the opposite. The bigger and more personal the obstacle, the more readers worry, and worrying keeps them reading.

Begin in the middle

Many beginners start a story with the character waking up, brushing their teeth and eating breakfast. Readers get bored before anything happens. Experienced writers often start close to the trouble: a letter arrives, a dog runs away, a stranger knocks.

You can always explain the background later, a little at a time. A story is like a conversation with the reader. Nobody wants to hear a long list of facts before the interesting part.

Show, don't just tell

"Asha was scared" tells the reader something. "Asha's hands were shaking so much she dropped the key twice" shows it. Showing lets readers work things out for themselves, which makes them feel part of the story.

Small, concrete details help too. Instead of "a nice house", try "a pink house with a mango tree that dropped fruit onto the neighbour's car". Ruskin Bond is a master of this. In his stories, you can almost smell the rain on the pine trees of Mussoorie.

Every detail has a job

The Russian writer Anton Chekhov gave advice that writers still repeat. In his letters, he said that if a gun hangs on the wall in the first act of a play, it should be fired by the last. In other words, don't include things that don't matter, and pay off the things you do include.

This works in reverse too. If your hero will need to climb a tree in the last chapter, it helps to show earlier that she is a good climber. Otherwise the ending feels like a cheat.

The secret is rewriting

Roald Dahl wrote in a small hut in his garden in England, in pencil on yellow paper, and rewrote his pages again and again. Many writers say their first draft is just a way of finding out what the story is about.

When you finish a draft, put it away for a day. Then read it aloud. You will hear clumsy sentences and spot places where nothing happens. Cut them. Ask a friend which part they liked best and where they got bored. Then try again.

Read a lot

Nearly every writer says the same thing: read widely. Read comics, poems, adventure books, myths and newspapers. Notice how other writers start stories, build suspense and end chapters. Keep a notebook for ideas, overheard words and strange things you see. One odd detail, like a cow standing on a bus stop at midnight, can be the seed of a whole story.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Good writers get it right the first time.

Truth: Almost all writers rewrite, cut and change their stories many times. First drafts are meant to be messy. The magic usually happens in the second, third or tenth draft.

Try this

Write a story in exactly six sentences. Sentence one introduces a character. Sentence two says what they want. Sentences three and four put something in their way. Sentence five shows how they try one last time. Sentence six tells us what changed. Read it aloud to someone and ask what they remember most.
