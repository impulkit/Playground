Why do short strings play high notes?

Press a sitar string halfway down. The note jumps up, exactly one whole octave.

The short answer

A plucked string wobbles back and forth very fast. Each wobble pushes on the air, sending out waves of sound. The number of wobbles each second is called the frequency, and your ear hears it as pitch. More wobbles mean a higher note.

A shorter string wobbles faster. When a sitar player presses a string against a fret, only the part between the fret and the bridge can vibrate. Press halfway along and that part is exactly half as long, so it vibrates twice as fast. You hear the same note, one octave higher: Sa to the next Sa.

Length is one of three things that set a note. Tightness and thickness matter too.

Surprising but true

Sa and Pa sound good together because one vibrates exactly 3 times for every 2 of the other.

Three ways to change a note

Tighten a string and it springs back faster, so its pitch rises. That's what musicians do when they turn the pegs to tune up. A thicker, heavier string moves more slowly, so it plays lower. That's why the bass strings on a guitar are fat and the high strings thin.

The same rules work in your body. Your vocal cords are stretched folds of tissue in your throat. Tighten them and your voice goes up. Children's vocal cords are shorter and lighter than adults', which is one reason children's voices are higher. A mosquito's whine is high for a related reason: its tiny wings beat several hundred times a second.

Notes that fit together

About 2,500 years ago, Greek thinkers linked to Pythagoras noticed that strings with simple length ratios sound good together. Halve a string and you get an octave, a ratio of 2 to 1. Take two-thirds of it and you get the note we call Pa when the full string plays Sa, a ratio of 3 to 2.

These ratios explain why Sa and Pa sound so restful together. For every two vibrations of Sa, Pa makes exactly three, so their waves keep lining up. Indian musicians studied these relationships closely too. The ancient Natyashastra describes 22 shrutis, tiny steps of pitch within an octave.

Inside a sitar

A sitar has a long hollow neck and a round body, traditionally made from a dried gourd. Most have six or seven playing strings, plucked with a wire pick worn on the finger. Underneath lie eleven to thirteen thinner sympathetic strings. Nobody plucks them. They start to hum on their own when a matching note is played above, adding a shimmering echo.

The frets are curved metal bars tied to the neck, so the player can move them to suit a raga. Players also pull a string sideways across a fret. That stretches it, raises its pitch and creates the sliding, singing notes, called meend, that make sitar music sound so much like a voice.

The buzz that Raman measured

Play a sitar and you hear a bright, buzzing tone, quite unlike a guitar. The secret is the bridge, a wide, gently curved piece of bone or horn. The vibrating string brushes against it with every wobble, which adds many extra high tones. Shaping that curve just right is a skilled job called jawari.

In 1921, the physicist C. V. Raman, who later won the Nobel Prize, published a study of how Indian stringed instruments such as the veena and tanpura produce their rich sound. Decades later, Ravi Shankar carried the sitar's sound to the West, playing at festivals in the USA in the 1960s. Behind every raga he played were the same simple rules about how strings move.

Myth or fact?

Myth: High notes travel faster through the air than low notes.

Truth: All notes travel through air at the same speed, about 343 metres a second. If they didn't, music heard from far away would arrive with its high and low notes jumbled up.

Try this

Hold a ruler flat on a table with most of it sticking out over the edge. Press the end on the table firmly and twang the free end. Now slide the ruler so less of it sticks out, and twang again. The shorter the part that wobbles, the faster it moves and the higher the buzz.
