How deep is the ocean, and what lives down there?

Dive in. Keep going. It gets dark, cold and very strange.

The short answer

The ocean covers about 71% of Earth, and its average depth is about 3.7 km. That is about 50 Qutub Minars, or about nine Empire State Buildings, stacked on top of each other. Its deepest point, in the Challenger Deep, is about 11 km down.

Scientists divide the ocean into layers, like floors in a very tall building. Sunlight only reaches the top 200 metres or so. Below 1,000 m it is completely dark. And for every 10 metres you go down, the water pressure rises by the weight of the whole atmosphere pressing on you at the beach.

Each layer has its own kind of life, and many deep-sea animals make their own light.

Surprising but true

We have better maps of the Moon's surface than of the deep ocean floor.

The sunlight zone

The top 200 m is the busiest part of the sea. There is enough light here for tiny drifting plants called phytoplankton to make food by photosynthesis. They are so many that they make around half of the oxygen in the air you breathe.

Almost everything in the ocean depends on them. Small animals eat the phytoplankton, fish eat the small animals, and bigger hunters eat the fish. Coral reefs, dolphins, sea turtles and most of the fish we eat live in this zone. It is also the only layer most people ever see. Even a strong swimmer holding their breath rarely gets below about 10 m, a tiny scratch on the surface of the sea.

The twilight and midnight zones

From 200 m to 1,000 m is the twilight zone. A little blue light leaks down, but not enough for plants. Animals here often have huge eyes, silvery sides or glowing bellies.

Every night, a vast crowd of shrimps, jellies and small fish swims up from these depths to feed near the surface, then sinks back down before dawn. It is probably the biggest journey made by animals anywhere on Earth, and it happens every single day.

Below 1,000 m lies the midnight zone. No sunlight reaches it at all. The water is near freezing. Here the only lights come from animals like the anglerfish, and sperm whales dive down to hunt giant squid in the darkness.

The abyss and the trenches

Below 4,000 m comes the abyss, a huge, flat plain of soft mud. It covers more of Earth than all the land put together. Below 6,000 m, in a few deep cracks called trenches, lies the deepest zone of all.

Deep-sea animals have soft, watery bodies without air spaces, so the huge pressure doesn't crush them. Many eat "marine snow": bits of dead plankton and droppings drifting down from above. When a whale dies and its body sinks to the floor, it can feed crowds of creatures for decades.

Life without the Sun

In 1977, scientists in the US submersible Alvin explored the seabed near the Galápagos Islands. They found hot springs gushing from cracks in the ocean floor, called hydrothermal vents. Around them lived giant tube worms, clams and crabs.

These animals don't depend on sunlight at all. Bacteria at the vents make food from chemicals in the hot water, and the worms and clams live off the bacteria.

Most of the deep sea has still never been seen. Only about a quarter of the seabed has been mapped in detail. Robot submarines and India's planned crewed submersible, Matsya-6000, keep searching, and new animals turn up on almost every trip.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The sea is blue because it reflects the sky.

Truth: Water itself is very slightly blue. It soaks up red light and lets blue light bounce around, so deep, clear water looks blue even under a grey sky.

Try this

Put a red, a yellow and a blue crayon side by side. Look at them through a sheet of blue cellophane or a blue plastic folder. The red one looks almost black. Deep in the sea, where only blue light is left, red animals hide the same way.
