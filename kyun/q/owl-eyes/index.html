Why do owls turn their heads so far?

An owl can look over its own back, because its eyes are stuck in place like the headlights of a car.

The short answer

Owls turn their heads so far because they can't move their eyes. An owl's eyes are huge for its body, and to fit in the skull they grew long and tube-shaped, like a pair of binoculars. A ring of bone inside each eye holds it firmly in place.

So when an owl wants to look to the side or behind, it turns its whole head. Its neck is built for this. It has twice as many neck bones as yours, and blood vessels with clever safety features, letting it turn its head up to about 270 degrees without harm.

Surprising but true

An owl's eyes are not balls. They are long tubes held in place by rings of bone, so they cannot roll around at all.

Eyes like binoculars

Most animals' eyes are round balls that roll in their sockets. An owl's eyes are long and narrow, with a big lens at the front. That shape lets a lot of light fall onto the retina, which helps owls see in dim light. In some owls the eyes take up more room in the skull than the brain does.

Both eyes face forward, like ours. This gives owls very good depth perception, the ability to judge exactly how far away a mouse is before pouncing. The cost is a narrower view. To make up for it, owls bob, tilt and swivel their heads constantly.

A neck full of tricks

Your neck has 7 small bones called vertebrae. An owl has 14. More joints mean more ways to bend and twist.

There is a second problem. If you twisted your neck sharply, you could squeeze the blood vessels that feed your brain. In 2013, a team at Johns Hopkins University in the United States studied owls that had died of natural causes. They injected dye into the blood vessels and took scans. They found that an owl's main neck arteries run through wide bony tunnels with room to spare. Near the head, the vessels widen into small pools that store extra blood while the neck is twisted.

Ears that see

Owls don't rely on eyes alone. Many have a flat, round face called a facial disc. It works like a satellite dish, gathering sound towards the ears.

In barn owls, one ear opening sits higher than the other. A squeak from below reaches one ear a fraction earlier and louder than the other. In the 1960s, the American scientist Roger Payne showed that barn owls could catch mice in a completely dark room, using sound alone. Add feathers that let them fly almost silently, and you have one of the best night hunters there is.

How do your ears hear?The three smallest bones in your body sit inside each ear, passing sound along like a relay team.

Owls and people

Owls live on every continent except Antarctica. In India, the spotted owlet often roosts in city trees, and the large Indian eagle-owl hunts on rocky hills. In the United States, the great horned owl lives everywhere from deserts to big-city parks.

People have told stories about owls for thousands of years. In ancient Athens, the little owl was linked to Athena, goddess of wisdom. In Hindu tradition, the owl is the vehicle of Lakshmi, goddess of wealth. Sadly, false beliefs about owl magic still lead to some being caught and sold illegally around festivals in India, which conservation groups are working to stop. Owls are protected by law in both India and the United States, and the best place to meet one is in the wild, as one of many animals of the night.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Owls can turn their heads all the way round in a full circle.

Truth: They can't. The limit is about 270 degrees in each direction. That is still far more than a person can manage, which is about 90 degrees.

Try this

Keep your head still and look as far left as you can using only your eyes. Now pretend your eyes are glued in place. Ask a friend to stand behind you and hold up some fingers, and see how far you have to turn your head and body to count them. That is an owl's problem every time it wants to look around.
