What were the first video games?

One of the first video games was a tennis match on a lab machine built to measure electricity, with a glowing dot for a ball.

The short answer

The first video games were made by scientists, not game companies. In 1958, a physicist named William Higinbotham built Tennis for Two for a visitors' day at Brookhaven National Laboratory in New York. Two players turned knobs to hit a glowing dot over a net on a small round screen. Visitors queued to play it, but after a second open day in 1959 it was taken apart.

In 1962, students at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) made Spacewar!, in which two spaceships fought around a star. Ten years later, games left the lab. The Magnavox Odyssey plugged into home televisions, and Pong filled arcades. From there it took only a few years for games to become a business worth billions.

Surprising but true

Tennis for Two was shown at only two visitors' days, in 1958 and 1959, before it was taken apart so its parts could be reused.

Games on giant machines

Early computers filled whole rooms and cost more than a house. Nobody bought one to play games. The game ran on a PDP-1, a machine about the size of a couple of big refrigerators. Students wrote it in their spare time, as long lists of instructions that the machine stored in binary code. They shared the code freely, so copies spread to other universities.

The game had real physics. The star in the middle pulled ships toward it with gravity, and players had to steer around it. Many people who later built the computer industry first touched a computer by playing it late at night.

The brown box that came home

In the 1960s, an engineer named Ralph Baer had an idea: millions of homes already had a television, so why not play games on it? His team built a test machine nicknamed the Brown Box. It became the Magnavox Odyssey, sold in 1972 as the first home video game console.

The Odyssey could only draw a few white squares and a line. To add colour, it came with plastic sheets you stuck on the TV screen. The Brown Box is now kept at the Smithsonian in Washington, DC.

Pong and the arcade boom

Also in 1972, a new company called Atari put Pong in a bar in California. It was a simple table tennis game, but people loved it. Within a few years, arcades with rows of coin-operated machines opened across the USA and Japan.

In 1978, the Japanese company Taito released Space Invaders, with rows of aliens marching down the screen. Pac-Man followed in 1980. Its designer, Toru Iwatani, wanted a game that would appeal to everyone, including people who didn't like shooting games.

A crash and a comeback

By 1983, shops in North America were flooded with too many poor games, and sales collapsed. Many people thought video games were a passing craze.

Then the Japanese company Nintendo brought its Famicom console to America in 1985, renamed the Nintendo Entertainment System, along with Super Mario Bros. Sales recovered. In 1989 came the Game Boy, with the puzzle game Tetris, first written in 1984 by Alexey Pajitnov in Moscow.

The Indian story

Few Indian homes had game consoles in the 1980s. In the 1990s, many Indian children played cheap copies of the Nintendo Famicom, sold in local markets as "TV games" with cartridges holding dozens of games. Others played handheld brick games at school breaks.

Today India has one of the largest numbers of gamers anywhere, mostly playing on phones. Every one of those games still uses the same basic trick as Tennis for Two: a screen made of pixels, a computer following rules, and a player trying to beat them.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Video games were invented by toy companies to sell to children.

Truth: The earliest games were made by scientists and university students on huge, expensive machines, mostly for fun and to show what computers could do. Toy and arcade companies came later, once the machines got cheaper.

Try this

Play "paper Pong" with a friend. Draw a court on a sheet of paper, put a coin in the middle, and take turns flicking it toward each other's goal line with one finger. Then think about which rules a computer would need to know to run the same game.
