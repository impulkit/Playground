Why can't you tickle yourself?

Your brain knows exactly where your own fingers are going, so it turns the feeling down before it arrives.

The short answer

Every time you move, your brain makes a quick prediction of what that movement will feel like. A part at the back of your brain called the cerebellum compares the prediction with what your skin actually reports.

When you try to tickle yourself, the prediction is almost perfect. Your brain knows exactly when and where the touch will come, so it turns the feeling down. When someone else tickles you, the touch is a surprise. Your brain can't predict it, so the feeling arrives at full strength. That surprise is a big part of what makes tickling tickly. It's the same reason you don't notice your own tongue in your mouth, but you'd notice someone else's finger at once.

Surprising but true

Rats laugh when tickled. Their squeaks are too high for human ears, but special microphones pick them up.

Two kinds of tickle

Scientists describe two kinds of tickling. The first is a light, creeping feeling, like an ant walking on your arm or a feather on your cheek. It makes you want to scratch or brush it off. This kind probably helps animals notice insects and parasites on their skin.

The second kind is the deep, poking tickle on the ribs, armpits or soles of the feet that makes people laugh and squirm. It mostly needs another person. These spots are also places where the body is soft and easily hurt, which has led some scientists to think tickling may help children learn to protect them in play.

The tickling robot

In the late 1990s, at University College London, a team including Sarah-Jayne Blakemore built a simple robot to test this. A person moved a lever with one hand, and the robot brushed a soft piece of foam over their other palm.

When the robot moved at exactly the same time as the lever, the touch hardly tickled. When the scientists added a delay of just a fifth of a second, or changed its direction, it felt more ticklish. The more the brain's prediction was off, the more it felt like a stranger's touch.

Laughing rats

People aren't the only ones who laugh when tickled. Young chimpanzees and gorillas make panting laughs during tickle games. In the 1990s, an American scientist named Jaak Panksepp found that rats make high-pitched chirps when tickled on their bellies. The sound is too high for us to hear, but a bat detector can pick it up.

Tickled rats chased the scientist's hand to be tickled again, which suggests they liked it. Their chirps are ultrasound, sound waves that vibrate too fast for human ears, like the calls of bats.

Tickles and feelings

Tickling is strange because it can be fun and unpleasant at once. Many people laugh hard while being tickled even when they don't like it, because the laugh is partly automatic, a bit like a reflex. So a laugh during tickling doesn't always mean "keep going".

Tickling is usually a game between people who know each other well, such as parents and babies or brothers and sisters. Babies start laughing at tickles at around four months. Mood matters too. When you're anxious or cross, being tickled is much less fun, which shows how much the brain shapes even this simple feeling. Most people can make their own skin feel a light itch, but almost nobody can make themselves laugh with their own fingers.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If someone laughs while being tickled, they must be enjoying it.

Truth: Laughing during tickling is partly automatic. Many people laugh hard while really disliking it, so the laugh isn't a sign they want more. It's always fine to say stop.

Try this

Run a feather or a soft paintbrush slowly along your own palm, then ask a friend to do exactly the same. Rate each one from 0 to 10 for tickliness. Then put your hand lightly on top of your friend's hand while they do it, and rate it again.
