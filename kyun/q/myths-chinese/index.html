What are some famous Chinese legends?

An archer shot nine suns out of the sky. A monkey made of stone fought an army of heaven. And a monk walked all the way to India for books.

The short answer

Chinese legends are stories told for thousands of years about how the world began, why the Moon glows and how heroes outwitted gods. They include the giant Pangu, whose body became mountains and rivers, and Nüwa, a goddess who shaped the first people from yellow clay.

The best-loved tales star an archer who saved Earth from scorching heat, a goddess who lives on the Moon, and a cheeky Monkey King. These stories are still told at festivals, in films, comics and cartoons, from Beijing to Singapore.

Surprising but true

China's Moon missions are named Chang'e, after the goddess of the Moon, and its Moon rovers are named Yutu, the Jade Rabbit who lives with her.

A giant hatches the world

One story says that in the beginning, everything was mixed up inside a giant cosmic egg. Inside slept a giant called Pangu. When he woke, he cracked the egg. The light, clear part rose to become the sky, and the heavy, dark part sank to become the Earth.

Pangu stood between them, pushing them apart, growing taller each day for 18,000 years. When he finally died, his breath became the wind, his voice thunder, his eyes the Sun and Moon, and his blood the rivers.

Ten suns and a lonely goddess

Long ago, the story goes, there were ten suns, the sons of a god. They took turns crossing the sky, one each day. But one day, all ten rose at once. Crops burned and rivers dried up.

A great archer, Hou Yi, shot down nine of them with his arrows, leaving one sun to light the world. As a reward, he received a potion of everlasting life. His wife, Chang'e, drank it, and floated up to the Moon, where she lives with a white Jade Rabbit.

At the Mid-Autumn Festival, families eat mooncakes and gaze at the full moon, looking for Chang'e and her rabbit. China has named its Moon missions after her. In 2019, Chang'e 4 became the first spacecraft to land on the Moon's far side.

What is on the far side of the Moon?The far side of the Moon isn't dark at all. When we see a new moon, the side facing away from us is in full sunshine.

The Monkey King

The most famous hero of all is Sun Wukong, the Monkey King. Born from a stone, he learns magic, can leap thousands of kilometres in one somersault and has a staff that shrinks to the size of a needle.

He causes chaos in heaven, defeats armies of celestial soldiers and is finally trapped under a mountain for 500 years. To make up for it, he must guard a monk on a dangerous journey to the west.

His adventures fill Journey to the West, a long novel from the 1500s and one of China's great classic books. Some scholars think the Monkey King may have been partly inspired by stories of Hanuman that reached China through trade and Buddhism. Others think he grew from Chinese folk tales. The question is still open.

A real journey to India

The novel's monk is based on a real man, Xuanzang. In about 629 CE, he left China and travelled over deserts and mountains to India, hunting for Buddhist books.

He studied for years at Nalanda, the great university in what is now Bihar. When he returned to China around 645, he carried hundreds of texts and spent the rest of his life translating them. His travel record is still one of our best descriptions of 7th-century India.

Dragons and a monster called Nian

Chinese dragons are long, snake-like and usually kind. They live in seas, rivers and clouds, and bring rain to farms. Emperors used the dragon as their symbol.

Another tale explains Chinese New Year. A beast called Nian came each year to eat crops and frighten villagers. But it was scared of loud noises and the colour red. So people hung red papers and lit firecrackers, and the festival's noisy, red celebrations began.

Stars across a river

One of the gentlest legends tells of a cowherd and a weaver girl who fell in love but were separated by the Milky Way, a river in the sky. Once a year, magpies form a bridge so they can meet. The pair are the stars Altair and Vega.

That night is celebrated as Qixi, sometimes called Chinese Valentine's Day. Like many of the world's oldest stories, it looks up at the sky and finds a reason for what it sees, much as dragon legends found reasons for rain and storms.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Chinese dragons are fierce fire-breathing monsters, like the ones in European stories.

Truth: In Chinese legends, dragons are usually wise and kind. They live in rivers, seas and clouds, and control rain. Emperors used the dragon as their symbol of power and good fortune.
