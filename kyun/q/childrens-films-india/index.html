What are some great Indian films for children?

India's first 3D film was a Malayalam children's movie from 1984 about a mischievous little genie, and audiences watched it wearing paper glasses.

The short answer

India has made wonderful films for children in Hindi, Bengali, Tamil, Malayalam, Marathi and many other languages. Some are fantasies full of magic. Others are gentle, funny stories about ordinary children at school, at home or in the street.

A few favourites that many families love are Satyajit Ray's Goopy Gyne Bagha Byne (1969), Mr. India (1987), Makdee (2002), The Blue Umbrella (2005), Taare Zameen Par (2007), Stanley Ka Dabba (2011), Chillar Party (2011) and the Tamil film Kaakkaa Muttai (2014). Each shows children as clever, brave and worth listening to.

Surprising but true

In 1955, the year Satyajit Ray released Pather Panchali, the Government of India set up a society just to make films for children.

Magic and music

Satyajit Ray is best known for serious films, but he also made one of India's most loved children's movies. Goopy Gyne Bagha Byne follows two hopeless musicians, a singer and a drummer, who are chased out of their villages for making terrible noise. A king of ghosts gives them magic powers, including music that freezes anyone who hears it. They use it to stop a war.

Ray was also a writer for children, and his grandfather Upendrakishore Ray Chowdhury had written the original Goopy and Bagha story. The film's songs are still sung by children in Bengal.

Witches, genies and invisible heroes

In Mr. India (1987), a kind man who looks after orphans finds a watch that makes him invisible. Children loved its villain, Mogambo, and its mix of comedy and adventure. In the Malayalam film My Dear Kuttichathan (1984), three children free a little genie from a magician. It was India's first 3D film, and audiences wore special glasses to watch it.

In Makdee, directed by Vishal Bhardwaj, a girl in a village outwits a "witch" who is said to turn children into animals. The same director later made The Blue Umbrella, based on a story by Ruskin Bond, about a girl in the Himalayas and the beautiful umbrella everyone in the village wants.

School, lunchboxes and friendship

Some of the best Indian children's films are about everyday life. In Taare Zameen Par, eight-year-old Ishaan struggles in school until an art teacher realises that he has dyslexia, a difference in the way the brain handles reading, and helps him shine. Many parents said it changed how they thought about children who find school hard.

Stanley Ka Dabba is about a boy who never brings lunch to school and a greedy teacher who keeps stealing other children's food. Chillar Party follows a gang of children in a Mumbai housing colony who fight to save a stray dog. I Am Kalam (2011) tells of a poor boy in Rajasthan who is inspired by A.P.J. Abdul Kalam and dreams of going to school.

From the south

In Kaakkaa Muttai, two brothers from a Chennai slum see a new pizza shop and become determined to taste a pizza, though it costs more than their family can spare. The film is funny and sharp, and it won the National Film Award for Best Children's Film. Like many films from South India, it reached viewers across the country through subtitles.

A society for children's cinema

In 1955, the Government of India set up the Children's Film Society, India, to make and show films for young audiences. It produced hundreds of films and ran the International Children's Film Festival, where the top prize is the Golden Elephant. The festival was held in Hyderabad for many years, and children sat on its juries to help pick the winners. In the early 2020s, the society was merged into the National Film Development Corporation, which continues some of its work. Today, streaming services also commission Indian films and series for children, giving new storytellers a chance.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Indian children's films are mostly cartoons or dubbed Hollywood movies.

Truth: India has a long tradition of live-action films made for and about children, in many languages, from Satyajit Ray's fantasies in Bengali to Tamil and Marathi films that have won national awards.

Try this

Hold a family film night with a children's film in a language you don't usually watch, using subtitles. Afterwards, each person names one thing that felt familiar and one thing that felt new. You might be surprised how much a story can cross languages.
