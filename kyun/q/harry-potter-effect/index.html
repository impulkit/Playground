Why do some books get children reading?

In July 2007, children around the world queued outside bookshops at midnight to buy a 600-page book, and many finished it within days.

The short answer

Books get children reading when they are hard to put down. Exciting stories, characters children care about and worlds they want to return to all help. So does choice: children read more when they can pick books they like, not just ones set for school.

Series help a lot. Once you love a character, you want the next book, and each one makes the following one easier. Being read to, seeing adults read and having books close at hand, at home or in a library, all make reading a habit.

Harry Potter showed all of this on a giant scale. Its seven books, published between 1997 and 2007, sold more than 600 million copies in over 80 languages. Many children who had never read a long book read all seven, some more than once.

Surprising but true

The first Harry Potter book was turned down by about a dozen publishers before Bloomsbury, a small London firm, agreed to print it in 1997.

The boy who lived, and almost wasn't printed

J. K. Rowling, a single mother living in Edinburgh, sent her first book to many publishers. About a dozen said no. Bloomsbury in London finally took it. The story often told is that the chairman gave the first chapter to his eight-year-old daughter, who demanded the rest.

Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone came out in 1997 with a small first print run. In the USA, it was renamed Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone. Word spread mostly from child to child, in playgrounds and classrooms, before newspapers caught on.

Midnight queues for a 600-page book

By the later books, publication days became events. Children dressed as wizards and queued outside bookshops at midnight. Harry Potter and the Deathly Hallows, the final book, sold millions of copies in its first 24 hours in 2007.

The books grew longer as the series went on. The fifth, Order of the Phoenix, runs to more than 750 pages in its British edition. Yet many children who had found 100 pages hard now raced through it. Because readers had grown up alongside Harry, each book felt like meeting an old friend.

In India, the books were translated into Hindi and several other Indian languages, and English editions sold widely in cities. Rowling filled the books with invented words, from "Quidditch" to "Muggle", and "muggle" was later added to the Oxford English Dictionary. Readers who learned those words were also picking up long, tricky ones like "ominous" and "incantation" along the way.

Did it really make more readers?

Many parents and teachers believe it did, and surveys for publishers found many young fans said the books made them enjoy reading. But big studies of reading habits, such as one by America's National Endowment for the Arts in 2007, found that teenagers overall were reading for fun less than before.

Both can be true. A book can turn millions of children into readers while, at the same time, other things like screens compete for everyone's free hours. Researchers still argue about how big the "Harry Potter effect" was.

Other books that did the same

Harry Potter was not the first series to do this. Enid Blyton's Famous Five and Secret Seven hooked generations of children in Britain and India. Nancy Drew mysteries did it in the USA. In India, Amar Chitra Katha and Tinkle comics got many children reading before they ever opened a novel.

More recently, series like Diary of a Wimpy Kid and Percy Jackson have pulled in readers, especially those who said they didn't like books. What they share is simple. Children want to know what happens next.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Reading comics doesn't count as real reading.

Truth: Comics and graphic novels use plenty of rich words and ask readers to follow a story through pictures and text together. Many librarians recommend them as a way into reading, and many keen readers started with comics.

Try this

Start a "book swap shelf" at home or in class. Everyone brings one book they enjoyed and writes a one-line reason on a slip of paper tucked inside. Take one you haven't read, and after a week, add your own slip to it for the next reader.
