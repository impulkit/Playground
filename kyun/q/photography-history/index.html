How was photography invented?

The oldest surviving photograph from a camera needed hours, perhaps days, of sunlight to appear. It shows a blurry view of rooftops from a window in France.

The short answer

Photography was invented when people found chemicals that change when light hits them, and a way to make that change permanent. A camera had existed for centuries as a dark box or room with a small hole or lens, which threw an upside-down image of the outside onto a wall. The trick was to capture that image instead of tracing it.

In France in the 1820s, Nicéphore Niépce coated a metal plate with a kind of tar that hardens in light, and made the oldest photo that still survives. In 1839, Louis Daguerre announced a much faster method, and photography spread across the world within a few years.

Surprising but true

The first known photograph of a person was an accident. A man stood still long enough to have his shoes polished on a Paris street, so he was the only figure in the scene who didn't vanish.

The dark room

The idea behind the camera is very old. Thinkers in ancient China noticed that light through a small hole makes an upside-down picture. Around 1000 CE, the scientist Ibn al-Haytham, working in Cairo, explained why. Artists later used darkened boxes with lenses, called a camera obscura, Latin for "dark room", to help them draw.

What they could not do was keep the picture. When you closed the box, it was gone.

A view from a window

Around 1826 or 1827, Niépce pointed a camera out of an upstairs window at his country house in France. Inside was a metal plate coated with bitumen, a tar that hardens where light hits it. After a very long exposure, at least many hours, he washed away the soft, unlit parts. What remained was a blurry image of rooftops and a courtyard.

That plate still exists. It is kept at the Harry Ransom Center at the University of Texas at Austin, in the United States.

Daguerre and Talbot

Niépce worked with Louis Daguerre, who carried on after Niépce died. Daguerre used silver-coated copper plates treated with iodine, then brought out the hidden image with mercury fumes. Exposures took minutes instead of hours. In 1839, the French government bought the method and announced it to the public. Daguerreotypes were sharp and beautiful, but each one was unique and could not be copied.

In England, William Henry Fox Talbot invented a different process. He made a negative on paper, with light and dark reversed, and printed as many positive copies from it as he liked. Almost all film photography for the next 150 years worked on Talbot's idea of negative and print.

Photos in India

Photography arrived in India within a few years of its invention. Studios opened in Bombay, Calcutta and Madras, and photographers travelled to record temples, mountains and cities.

One of the most famous was Lala Deen Dayal, born in 1844. He photographed palaces, railways and royal events, and became court photographer to the Nizam of Hyderabad. Thousands of his glass plates survive and give a detailed picture of India in the late 1800s.

A button for everyone

Early photography needed heavy equipment and chemistry. In 1888, George Eastman in the United States sold the first Kodak camera, already loaded with film for 100 pictures. When it was full, you posted the whole camera back to the company, which developed the photos and reloaded it. Its slogan was "You press the button, we do the rest." In 1900, Kodak's Brownie camera cost just one dollar.

In 1975, a Kodak engineer named Steven Sasson built the first digital camera. It weighed about 3.6 kg, took 23 seconds to record one black-and-white picture onto a cassette tape, and the picture had just 10,000 pixels. Today a phone camera takes millions of pixels in a fraction of a second.

Myth or fact?

Myth: People in old photos look serious because they had to hold still for many minutes.

Truth: Early photos did need long exposures, but by the 1850s and 1860s a portrait took only a few seconds. Serious faces were mainly the fashion. Portraits were formal and expensive, and a big grin was thought silly for something meant to last forever.

Try this

Find nature's pinhole cameras. On a sunny day, look at the shadow under a leafy tree. The little round spots of light are tiny pictures of the Sun, made by gaps between leaves acting like pinholes. Never look straight at the Sun. During a partial eclipse, those spots turn into little crescents.
