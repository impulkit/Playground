Why is Holi played with colours?

In the town of Barsana, women playfully beat men with long sticks during Holi, while the men hide under shields and try to splash them with colour.

The short answer

Holi is a Hindu spring festival celebrated on the full moon of the month of Phalguna, usually in March. People throw coloured powder called gulal and spray coloured water at friends, family and strangers. For one day, almost everyone ends up pink, green and yellow.

The colours have several meanings. Many people link them to the god Krishna, who in popular stories playfully coloured Radha and the cowherd girls of Braj. Holi also celebrates the end of winter, when fields turn green and trees burst into flower. And on Holi, rich and poor, young and old, all get coloured the same.

Surprising but true

The bright orange colour of traditional Holi came from the flowers of the palash tree, which bloom in a blaze of orange just as winter ends.

The bonfire before the colours

The night before Holi, people light bonfires called Holika Dahan. The story behind it comes from Hindu tradition. A demon king named Hiranyakashipu wanted everyone to worship him. His son Prahlad stayed devoted to the god Vishnu.

The king's sister Holika had a cloak, or in some tellings a boon, that kept her safe from fire. She sat in a fire with Prahlad on her lap to kill him. Instead, the story says, Holika burned and Prahlad walked out unharmed. The bonfire marks the victory of good over evil, and families often roast new grain in it.

Krishna, Radha and Braj

In the Braj region around Mathura and Vrindavan, where Krishna is believed to have grown up, Holi lasts more than a week. A popular story says the young Krishna, who had dark blue skin, worried that fair Radha would not like him. His mother told him to colour Radha's face any shade he wished.

In Barsana, linked with Radha, women hold Lathmar Holi, playfully hitting men with long sticks called lathis. In Vrindavan's temples, priests throw flower petals instead of powder. Pilgrims come from across India to join in.

Where the colours came from

Traditional Holi colours came from plants. The orange flowers of the palash tree, also called tesu, were soaked in water to make a bright orange dye. Turmeric gave yellow, beetroot and hibiscus gave pink and red, and henna leaves gave green.

Today most powders are made in factories. Some contain harsh chemicals that can hurt skin and eyes. Many families have gone back to herbal colours, and some cities sell colours made from flowers collected from temples. Oiling your hair and skin before playing makes colour easier to wash off.

Holi across India and beyond

Holi looks different in different places. In West Bengal, it is called Dol Jatra or Basanta Utsav, and people sing and wear yellow. In Punjab, Sikhs celebrate Hola Mohalla the day after Holi, with displays of martial arts and horse riding. Sweets such as gujiya, filled with sweet khoya and nuts, and the cold milk drink thandai are part of the day almost everywhere.

Holi is also a public holiday in Nepal. Indian communities in the USA, the UK, Mauritius, Guyana and Fiji celebrate it too. In American cities, "Festival of Colors" events draw people of many backgrounds, and colour runs, races where runners are showered with powder, borrowed the idea.

A day when rules relax

For one day, Holi turns ordinary life upside down. Children can splash their teachers, and neighbours who have quarrelled are expected to make up. The saying "Bura na mano, Holi hai", meaning "Don't be offended, it's Holi", sums up the mood. It's also a reminder to play kindly. Not everyone wants to be soaked, and a quick "Can I?" before colouring someone keeps the fun fair. Like Diwali, it ends with sweets shared between houses.

Myth or fact?

Myth: All Holi colours are natural and safe.

Truth: Many cheap colours are made with industrial dyes or chemicals that can irritate skin and eyes. Doctors and pollution boards suggest using herbal or homemade colours and keeping colour away from eyes and mouths.

Try this

Make your own gentle Holi colour. Mix a spoonful of turmeric with a few spoonfuls of cornflour or besan for yellow. For pink, ask an adult to help you boil a grated beetroot, cool the water and mix it with cornflour, then dry it in the sun. Test a little on your hand first.
