How does radio travel through the air?

Radio waves don't need air at all. They are a kind of invisible light that can cross empty space.

The short answer

A radio wave is a ripple of electric and magnetic energy. It belongs to the same family as visible light, but its waves are much longer, so our eyes can't see it. Like light, it travels at about 3 lakh kilometres a second and needs no air at all.

To send a radio signal, a transmitter makes electrons rush up and down a metal aerial millions of times a second. That shaking sends ripples spreading outwards. Far away, the ripples reach another aerial and make its electrons shake in the same rhythm. The receiver picks out that tiny shake and turns it back into sound, pictures or data.

Surprising but true

In 1895, Jagadish Chandra Bose used very short radio waves in Calcutta to ring a bell and set off a small explosion across a room, without any wires.

Light you can't see

Scientists call the whole family of these waves the electromagnetic spectrum. At one end are gamma rays and X-rays, with waves smaller than an atom. In the middle is the narrow band of light we can see. At the far end are radio waves, which can be as short as a grain of rice or longer than a football field.

Each station sends its waves at its own frequency, the number of ripples per second. When you turn a radio's dial, you are choosing which frequency to listen to and ignoring the rest. An FM station at 100 megahertz is sending 100 million ripples every second.

Carrying a voice on a wave

A plain radio wave carries no message. To add one, the transmitter changes the wave in a pattern that matches the sound. In AM radio, the height of the waves rises and falls. In FM radio, the waves bunch closer together and spread apart. The receiver reads those changes and rebuilds the sound.

Phones, Wi-Fi, TV, GPS and garage-door remotes all use radio waves too, each at its own set of frequencies. Governments divide the airwaves into bands so that one signal doesn't drown out another. Long waves bend around hills and can follow the curve of the Earth. Very short waves travel in straighter lines, which is why mobile towers need to be fairly close together and why an FM station fades as you drive away from the city.

Bose, Marconi and the first signals

In 1887 and 1888, the German scientist Heinrich Hertz showed in his laboratory that these invisible waves were real. A few years later, in 1895, Jagadish Chandra Bose gave a public demonstration in Calcutta. He sent very short radio waves, now called microwaves, across a hall and through walls to set off a bell. He chose not to patent most of his work.

Around the same time, the Italian inventor Guglielmo Marconi turned radio into a business. In 1901 his team reported receiving a signal sent across the Atlantic Ocean, from Cornwall in England to Newfoundland. Ships soon carried radios, and in 1912 the sinking Titanic used one to call for help. In India, regular broadcasting began in the 1920s, and the national broadcaster took the name All India Radio in 1936.

Listening to the sky

Stars, galaxies and clouds of gas send out radio waves too. Astronomers catch them with giant dish-shaped aerials. Near Pune, the Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope uses 30 dishes, each 45 metres across, spread over about 25 kilometres of farmland.

Radio waves also travel the other way. NASA's Deep Space Network sends commands to the Voyager probes, launched in 1977 and now more than 20 billion kilometres from Earth. Their reply, far weaker than a phone signal by the time it reaches us, takes about a day to arrive. Giant dishes in California, Spain and Australia take turns listening, so one of them always faces the right part of the sky.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Radio waves travel through the air, so they can't work in space.

Truth: Radio waves need nothing to travel through. They cross empty space more easily than air, which is how NASA still talks to the Voyager spacecraft beyond the planets.

Try this

Tune a small radio, or a phone with an FM radio app and earphones, to a clear station. Put it inside a closed steel dabba or a metal biscuit tin and listen. The sound usually fades or turns to hiss, because the metal box blocks the radio waves from reaching the aerial. Try a plastic box too and compare.
