Who discovered that washing hands saves lives?

In 1847 a young doctor in Vienna asked his colleagues to wash their hands, and deaths on his hospital ward fell almost overnight.

The short answer

The person usually credited is Ignaz Semmelweis, a Hungarian doctor working in Vienna, Austria. In 1847 he made doctors and students on his maternity ward wash their hands in a chlorine solution before examining mothers. Deaths from a deadly infection called childbed fever dropped from about 1 in 10 mothers to around 1 or 2 in 100.

At the time, nobody knew that germs cause disease. Semmelweis guessed something invisible was travelling on doctors' hands. Many senior doctors refused to believe him. It took decades, and the work of scientists like Louis Pasteur and Joseph Lister, before handwashing became normal in hospitals.

Surprising but true

Semmelweis worked out that handwashing saved lives about 15 years before scientists proved that germs cause disease.

A puzzle of two wards

Vienna General Hospital had two maternity clinics. One was run by doctors and medical students, the other by midwives. Mothers knew which was which. Some begged to go to the midwives' ward, and some even gave birth in the street rather than enter the doctors' ward.

They were right to be scared. In the doctors' clinic, about 1 in 10 mothers died of childbed fever in some years. In the midwives' clinic it was far fewer. Semmelweis was baffled. Same city, same hospital, same kinds of patients.

The clue

In 1847 a friend of his, a doctor named Jakob Kolletschka, cut his finger with a student's knife during a post-mortem, an examination of a dead body. He fell ill and died, with signs much like childbed fever.

That gave Semmelweis his idea. The doctors went from cutting up dead bodies straight to examining mothers, without washing. He thought they were carrying "cadaverous particles" on their hands. The midwives never did post-mortems.

The chlorine bowl

In May 1847 he placed a bowl of chlorinated lime solution at the ward entrance. Everyone had to scrub until the smell of the dead room was gone. Within months, deaths in his clinic fell sharply, to around 1 or 2 in 100.

He was saving hundreds of lives a year, but he couldn't explain why it worked. Many doctors felt insulted at the idea that they themselves were spreading death. Semmelweis also wrote angry letters attacking his critics, which won him few friends. He lost his job in Vienna and moved back to Budapest. He died in 1865, aged 47, his ideas still widely ignored.

Others who saw it

He wasn't alone. In Boston, the American doctor and writer Oliver Wendell Holmes had argued in 1843 that childbed fever was spread by doctors. He too was attacked by senior professors.

Proof arrived in the 1860s. Louis Pasteur showed that tiny living things, seen through microscopes, cause rotting and disease. In Britain, Joseph Lister used carbolic acid to clean wounds and tools, and surgery deaths fell. The age of germ-aware medicine had begun.

Why soap still matters

Today we know hands carry bacteria and viruses from everything we touch. Soap molecules have one end that grabs grease and one end that loves water. They pull germs off skin and wrap them up so water can rinse them away, as soap and germs explains in more detail.

Health experts say washing with soap can cut stomach infections by roughly a third. During the COVID-19 pandemic of 2020, children from Chennai to Chicago learned to scrub for 20 seconds, often by humming a short song twice.

The WHO and UNICEF now promote Global Handwashing Day every 15 October. Hospitals put hand gel by every bed. Semmelweis's chlorine bowl turned out to be one of the cheapest life-savers in history, and your own skin carries more bacteria than you might like to know.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You need very hot water to wash germs off your hands.

Truth: Soap and scrubbing do the work, not the heat. Water hot enough to kill germs would burn your skin. Cool or warm water with soap, scrubbed for 20 seconds, works well.

Try this

Rub a little cooking oil and a pinch of cinnamon on your hands to stand in for germs. Rinse with water only for five seconds and look. Then wash with soap for 20 seconds, scrubbing between fingers and under nails, and compare.
