How does the Olympic torch travel?

The Olympic flame is lit by sunlight in Greece, then carried by runners, ships, planes and once even a radio signal bounced off a satellite.

The short answer

A few months before every Olympic Games, the flame is lit among the ruins of ancient Olympia in Greece. A woman playing a high priestess holds a torch inside a curved mirror that focuses sunlight until it bursts into flame.

From there, the flame travels to the host city in a relay. Thousands of torchbearers carry it a short stretch each, passing the fire from torch to torch. When roads run out, it goes by boat, plane, train, horse or canoe. At the opening ceremony, the last torchbearer lights a big cauldron, which burns until the Games end.

Surprising but true

In 1976 the flame crossed the Atlantic as an electronic signal. A sensor in Athens turned it into pulses, a satellite beamed them to Canada, and a laser relit the flame in Ottawa.

Not as ancient as it looks

The Greeks did keep a sacred fire burning at Olympia during their Games, and relay races with torches were a real ancient sport. But the modern flame is much newer. It first appeared at the 1928 Amsterdam Olympics, burning in a tower.

The relay itself was dreamed up by Carl Diem, an organiser of the 1936 Berlin Games. More than 3,000 runners carried the flame about 3,000 km from Greece to Germany. Hitler's government used it, and the whole Olympics, as propaganda to show off Nazi Germany. The tradition survived anyway, and today it is used to celebrate peace.

Keeping it alive

The torch has to keep burning in wind and rain, so modern torches are clever bits of engineering. Most burn a gas such as propane, stored in a small tank in the handle. Designers test them in wind tunnels and under fake rainstorms.

Flames still go out sometimes. In 1976, a rainstorm put out the cauldron in Montreal during the Games, and a worker relit it with a cigarette lighter. Officials quickly put that out and relit it properly from a backup flame. Backups travel everywhere with the relay, burning inside miners' safety lamps, so a lost flame can always be replaced with the "real" one.

The weirdest journeys

That 1976 flame crossed the ocean in the strangest way of all. In Athens, a sensor turned the flame into electronic pulses. A satellite sent them to Ottawa, Canada, where they triggered a laser that lit a new torch.

Other trips are just as odd. For Sydney 2000, a diver carried a special flare-like torch underwater along the Great Barrier Reef. For Beijing 2008, climbers took a flame to the top of Everest. Before the 2014 Sochi Games, astronauts took a torch to the International Space Station and on a spacewalk, but unlit. An open flame on a space station would be far too dangerous.

A flame on a sailing ship

For Paris 2024, the flame left Greece aboard the Belem, a three-masted sailing ship built in 1896. It sailed into Marseille, the French city founded by Greek settlers about 2,600 years ago, a neat link between the two countries. Then it toured France and its overseas territories, including islands in the Caribbean and the Pacific.

The next Summer Games, in Los Angeles in 2028, will bring the relay back to the USA. Los Angeles hosted in 1932 and 1984 too.

India and the torch

The Olympic torch relay for Beijing 2008 passed through New Delhi on a shortened, heavily guarded route because of protests. India has started its own torch traditions too. In 2022, a torch relay was held for the first time for the Chess Olympiad, beginning in Delhi and visiting 75 cities before the event in Chennai.

Every relay is a reminder of how far a small flame can travel when enough people pass it on. And the story of how humans first learned to control fire is older than any Olympics.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Olympic torch relay is an ancient Greek tradition, unbroken since the first Olympics.

Truth: Ancient Greeks did keep a sacred flame at Olympia and held torch races, but the long relay to the host city was invented for the 1936 Berlin Games. It is less than a hundred years old.

Try this

Look at the inside of a shiny steel bowl or a big spoon in sunlight and notice the bright spot of reflected light it makes on a wall. A curved mirror gathers light into one place. Don't try to start a fire with it, but notice how small and bright that spot can get.
