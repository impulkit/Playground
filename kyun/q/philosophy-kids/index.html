What is philosophy, and can kids do it?

If you replace every part of a boat one by one, is it still the same boat? People have argued about that for 2,000 years.

The short answer

Philosophy is thinking hard about big questions that can't be settled just by measuring or looking things up. What makes something fair? How do you know you aren't dreaming right now? Is it ever right to break a promise? Do animals have minds?

Scientists mostly ask what happens. Philosophers often ask what something means, and how we can know it. They answer with careful reasons, and they argue with each other to see which reasons hold up. Children are often very good at this, because they haven't yet learned to stop asking "but why?"

Surprising but true

Isaac Newton called his famous book on gravity "The Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy". In his day, physics was still a branch of philosophy.

The boat that keeps changing

Here is a puzzle the Greek writer Plutarch described almost 2,000 years ago. The hero Theseus had a wooden ship. Over the years, as planks rotted, workers replaced them one at a time. In the end, not one original plank was left. Is it still the ship of Theseus?

Now suppose someone collected all the old planks and built a second ship from them. Which one is the real ship? There is no measuring tape that can settle this. You have to think about what "the same" means, and that is philosophy. It matters too, because your own body replaces many of its cells, yet you feel like the same person.

Four big families of questions

Philosophers sort their questions into groups. Ethics asks how we should live and treat others, and books on ethics fill whole libraries. Logic studies what makes an argument strong or weak, and it later helped build logic for computers. A third group asks about knowledge: how do you know anything for certain? A fourth asks what is real: are numbers real things, or just ideas in our heads?

These questions sound abstract, but they turn up in daily life. When a cricket umpire decides whether a catch was clean, or a judge decides whether a law is fair, they are using ideas that philosophers argued about first.

Thinkers from many places

In Greece, Socrates walked the streets of Athens asking people questions until they realised they knew less than they thought. In China, Confucius taught about kindness, family and good government. In India, the Buddha asked why people suffer, and thinkers in schools such as Nyaya wrote detailed rules for reasoning and debate.

India had six classic schools of philosophy, called darshanas, as well as Buddhist and Jain thinkers and the Charvakas, who doubted anything they couldn't see. They argued with each other for centuries, and each side sharpened its ideas against the others.

Philosophy in the classroom

In the 1970s, an American professor named Matthew Lipman noticed that his university students struggled to reason clearly. He decided it would help to start younger. He wrote stories for children that raised puzzles and let classes discuss them. His idea, called Philosophy for Children, has spread to schools in many countries.

Today the old questions are getting new life. Engineers building self-driving cars have to decide what the car should do in an emergency. People designing artificial intelligence argue about whether a machine could ever understand anything. Those are philosophy questions, and nobody has settled them yet.

How to do it yourself

You don't need a degree to start. Philosophy begins whenever someone asks a question and refuses to accept the first easy answer. Is it fair that the oldest child gets the biggest room? Should a robot pet have feelings? If everyone in your class lied once, would lying become all right?

Good philosophers listen as much as they talk. They try to say exactly what they mean, look for examples that might prove them wrong, and change their minds when a better reason comes along. It can feel strange at first to argue about something with no answer at the back of the book. That strangeness is a sign you've found a real philosophical question.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Philosophy is just people sharing opinions, so any answer is as good as another.

Truth: Philosophers have to give reasons, and others test those reasons. Some arguments fall apart when you look closely, while others hold up. Logic, ethics and even science grew out of this careful checking.
