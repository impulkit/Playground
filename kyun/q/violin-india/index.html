How did the violin become part of Carnatic music?

Two hundred years ago the violin was a foreign instrument heard in British army bands in Madras. Today almost every Carnatic concert has one on stage.

The short answer

The violin came to South India with Europeans in the 1700s. In Madras, now Chennai, and in royal courts like Thanjavur, Indian musicians heard it in military bands and at European gatherings. In the early 1800s, a few gifted musicians began playing Carnatic music on it.

They found that the violin could do something many Indian instruments found hard: hold a long note and slide smoothly between notes, just like a singer's voice. So they changed how it was held and tuned, and made it their own. Today the violin is the standard instrument accompanying singers in Carnatic concerts.

Surprising but true

A Carnatic violinist sits cross-legged on the floor and rests the scroll of the violin on their foot, holding it almost like a small cello.

The first players

Two names are linked with the violin's arrival. One is Baluswami Dikshitar, younger brother of the great composer Muthuswami Dikshitar. He is said to have learned the violin from a European musician in Madras and then adapted it for Carnatic music.

The other is Vadivelu, one of four brothers from Thanjavur known as the Thanjavur Quartet, who served at the court of Maharaja Swathi Thirunal of Travancore. He was a brilliant violinist, and the Maharaja is said to have given him an ivory violin. Different sources give different credit, and both men probably played a part.

Muthuswami Dikshitar himself enjoyed the Western band music he heard in Madras. He wrote a set of short Sanskrit songs, called nottuswaram, set to Western band tunes.

Holding it differently

A Western violinist stands or sits on a chair with the violin under the chin. A Carnatic violinist sits cross-legged on the floor, with the body of the violin against the chest and the scroll, the curled top, resting on the right foot or ankle.

This position frees the left hand. Carnatic music is full of gamakas, ornaments where a note bends, slides or shakes. The player's fingers glide along the strings, sometimes several notes in one sweep. The strings are usually tuned to the main note, Sa, and to Pa, the fifth, in two octaves, instead of the Western tuning.

Shadowing the singer

In a concert, the violinist sits to the singer's side, often with a mridangam drummer on the other. The violinist follows the singer almost note for note, repeating phrases a moment later and filling in when the singer pauses for breath. During improvised parts, the singer and violinist take turns, answering each other like a conversation.

It takes great skill, because the violinist often doesn't know exactly what the singer will do next. Good accompanists learn hundreds of compositions and ragas so that they can follow anywhere.

Masters of the violin

In the 1900s, violinists became stars in their own right. Mysore T. Chowdiah used a violin with seven strings for a deeper, fuller sound. Lalgudi G. Jayaraman composed many pieces and was known for making the violin sound almost like singing. T. N. Krishnan and M. S. Gopalakrishnan were famous soloists, and A. Kanyakumari has accompanied leading singers for decades.

L. Subramaniam mixed Carnatic music with jazz and Western classical music, playing with orchestras around the world. The violin is now used in Hindustani music too: N. Rajam developed a style that imitates the human voice.

Two worlds, one instrument

Today, December in Chennai is concert season, when hundreds of performances fill the city's music halls. On almost every stage, the violin sits beside the singer and the drums. An instrument that once played European marches now helps carry ragas composed centuries ago. The veena and the human voice are far older in Carnatic music, but the violin has earned its seat beside them. Many young students in Chennai, Bengaluru and among Indian families in the USA now pick it as their first instrument.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The violin in Carnatic music is played exactly the way Western violinists play it.

Truth: It is the same instrument, but Carnatic players sit on the floor, rest it on the foot, tune it differently and use sliding notes that Western classical music rarely uses.

Try this

Stretch a rubber band around an open box or a plastic container. Pluck it, then press the band down with one finger at different points and pluck again. Now slide your finger along while it is still ringing. That sliding sound is a little like a gamaka on the Carnatic violin.
