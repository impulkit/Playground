Where is the windiest place on Earth?

At Commonwealth Bay in Antarctica, the wind averages about 80 km/h all year, as fast as a car on a highway, and almost never stops.

The short answer

The windiest place on Earth, on average, is Commonwealth Bay on the coast of Antarctica. The wind there averages about 80 km/h over a whole year. That is like standing in the open window of a car on a highway, all day, every day. Gusts often top 240 km/h, faster than many cyclones.

But "windiest" can mean two things. If you want the strongest single gust ever measured on the ground, the record goes to Barrow Island off Western Australia. In 1996, during Cyclone Olivia, an instrument there recorded a gust of 408 km/h.

Surprising but true

The explorer Douglas Mawson found the wind at Commonwealth Bay so fierce that he called his book about it The Home of the Blizzard.

An avalanche of air

Commonwealth Bay's wind isn't caused by storms. It comes from the shape of Antarctica itself. The middle of the continent is a vast dome of ice, more than 3 km thick in places. The air above it gets extremely cold, and cold air is heavy.

That heavy air slides downhill towards the coast, like water running off a roof. At Commonwealth Bay the slope gets steeper, and the air is funnelled into a narrow stream. It speeds up and pours out to sea. Winds like these are called katabatic, from a Greek word meaning "going down".

The home of the blizzard

In 1911, the Australian explorer Douglas Mawson set up his expedition base at Cape Denison, on Commonwealth Bay. He didn't know he had chosen the windiest spot on the planet. His team had to crawl outside on hands and knees, and they used special ice spikes on their boots to stop being blown away.

Mawson later called his book The Home of the Blizzard. His wooden huts still stand there, more than a century later, packed with snow and looked after by an Australian heritage group. His story is part of the wider tale of polar explorers.

A mountain that held the record

For over 60 years, the record for the fastest gust belonged to Mount Washington in New Hampshire, USA. On 12 April 1934, scientists at the weather station on its summit measured a gust of 372 km/h (231 miles per hour). The summit gets hit by storms from several directions and sits in the path of strong winds high above the ground.

In 2010, the World Meteorological Organization, keeper of the official weather records, checked the Barrow Island reading from 1996 and accepted it as the new world record. Mount Washington still claims the record for the strongest gust measured by people at a staffed station.

Even faster: tornadoes

The wind inside a strong tornado can be faster than any of these, but it is hard to measure. Instruments on the ground usually get destroyed. In 1999, a radar truck measured winds of around 480 km/h in a tornado near Oklahoma City. Because radar measures wind above the ground, that figure is kept on a separate list.

Wind as a resource

Strong, steady wind is also valuable. In Tamil Nadu, the Muppandal area near Kanyakumari has thousands of turbines catching wind that blows through a gap in the Western Ghats. Gujarat, Karnataka and other states also have big wind farms.

Engineers look for places where the wind is strong but not wild, because gusts like those at Commonwealth Bay would tear a machine apart. In fact, the best spot for a wind turbine is somewhere with a steady breeze, far gentler than the wind that pinned Mawson's men to the ice.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The windiest place on Earth must be somewhere hit by lots of hurricanes or cyclones.

Truth: Hurricanes bring the strongest gusts, but only for a short time. The place with the highest average wind is Commonwealth Bay in Antarctica, where cold air drains off the ice almost every day of the year.

Try this

Make a simple wind meter. Tape a strip of tissue paper to the end of a pencil and hold it up outside or near a fan. Watch how far it lifts. Try it on different days, or at different fan speeds, and draw the angle of the strip each time.
