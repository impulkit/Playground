What are the oldest trees on Earth?

A twisted pine in the mountains of California was already a sapling when the pyramids of Giza were being built, and it is still alive.

The short answer

The oldest known individual trees are Great Basin bristlecone pines, which grow high in the dry mountains of the western USA. One of them, nicknamed Methuselah, has more than 4,800 rings. It began growing around the time the first pyramids rose in Egypt and the cities of the Indus Valley were taking shape.

Some trees are older in a different way. A few clone themselves, sending up new trunks from the same roots for thousands of years. The trunk you see may be young, but the living root system underneath may be far older than any single tree.

Surprising but true

In 1964, a researcher cut down a bristlecone pine in Nevada to count its rings, and found it was about 4,900 years old, one of the oldest trees ever known.

Counting rings

Each year, a tree in a seasonal climate grows a new layer of wood under its bark. In spring the wood is pale and fast-growing, and in late summer it is darker and denser. Together they make one ring. Count the rings from the outside to the centre and you have the tree's age.

Scientists use a hollow drill called an increment borer to pull out a thin core without harming the tree. Ring patterns also record weather. A run of narrow rings means years of drought or cold. By matching patterns from living and dead trees, researchers have built ring calendars thousands of years long, a science called dendrochronology. These records help scientists reconstruct past climates.

The bristlecone pines

Bristlecone pines live at about 3,000 metres in California's White Mountains, where the soil is poor, the wind is fierce and rain is rare. They grow very slowly, sometimes adding less than a millimetre of width a year. Their wood is dense and full of resin, which protects it from insects and rot.

Many old bristlecones look half dead. Strips of bark die back, leaving bare, twisted wood polished by the wind, while a narrow ribbon of living bark keeps a few branches green. The US Forest Service keeps Methuselah's exact location secret to protect it from souvenir hunters.

In 1964, before these trees were widely understood, a researcher studying ice-age glaciers in Nevada cut down a bristlecone with permission from the Forest Service. Counting its rings revealed about 4,900 years. The tree, later called Prometheus, became a lesson in why old trees need protecting.

Clones and roots

On a mountain in Sweden stands a scraggly spruce called Old Tjikko. Its visible trunk is only a few hundred years old. But scientists dated bits of wood from its root system to around 9,500 years ago, just after the last ice age. As trunks died, new ones grew from the same roots.

In Utah, a grove of aspen trees called Pando is really one organism linked underground, with tens of thousands of trunks. Some scientists think its roots are many thousands of years old, though estimates vary widely.

The sacred fig of Anuradhapura

The oldest tree planted by people with a recorded date grows in Anuradhapura, Sri Lanka. The Sri Maha Bodhi is a sacred fig said to have grown from a branch of the tree in Bodh Gaya under which the Buddha gained enlightenment. According to Sri Lankan chronicles, it was planted in 288 BCE. Gold railings and wooden props now support its old branches, and pilgrims visit daily.

India's old giants

India has very old trees too. The Great Banyan in the botanical garden near Kolkata is more than 250 years old. Its main trunk was removed after disease in the 1920s. Yet it lives on through thousands of aerial roots that have become trunks, covering an area about the size of a city block. Banyans can spread for centuries this way, which makes their true age hard to pin down.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The biggest trees are always the oldest.

Truth: Size depends on the species and on growing conditions. Giant sequoias are huge and old, but the oldest single trees known are bristlecone pines, which are short, gnarled and slow-growing, often no taller than a two-storey house.

Try this

Find a tree stump, a log or a cut branch and count its rings with an adult. Wide rings mean good growing years, narrow ones mean dry or cold years. See if you can find a run of narrow rings and guess what happened those years.
