How does an air conditioner cool a room?

An air conditioner doesn't make cold air. It picks heat up from your room and throws it outside, along with buckets of water.

The short answer

An air conditioner, or AC, is a heat mover. It works much like a refrigerator. Inside the indoor unit, a fan blows warm room air across cold metal coils. A special liquid called a refrigerant flows through the coils and evaporates there, turning into a gas. Evaporating soaks up heat, so the coils get cold and so does the air blowing past them.

The warm gas then travels through a pipe to the outdoor unit. There, a pump called a compressor squeezes it, making it very hot. A second fan blows outdoor air across a second set of coils, carrying the heat away. The refrigerant cools, turns back into a liquid and flows inside to do it all again. That's why standing next to an outdoor unit on a summer afternoon feels like standing beside an oven.

Surprising but true

The first modern air conditioner, built in 1902 in New York, was made to stop paper swelling in a printing factory, not to keep people cool.

Why your AC drips

Air always holds some water as an invisible gas. When warm, damp air touches the cold indoor coils, some of that water turns back into droplets, like the drops that form on a cold glass of lassi. The water collects in a tray and drips out through a small pipe.

On a humid day in Chennai or Kolkata, an AC can pull litres of water from a room. Drying the air is a big part of how it makes you comfortable. Your body cools itself by sweating, and sweat evaporates far more easily in dry air.

A printer's problem

In 1902, a young American engineer named Willis Carrier was asked to help a printing company in Brooklyn, New York. In hot, humid summers, the paper swelled and shrank, so colours printed one after another didn't line up. Carrier built a machine that blew air over cold coils to control the moisture.

It worked, and workers noticed the factory was pleasantly cool. Soon cinemas, shops and offices wanted the same thing. American cinemas advertised cool air in summer, and going to the pictures became a way to escape the heat. Home ACs spread in the USA after the 1950s and in India much later.

Why are heatwaves getting worse?A summer heatwave that might have come once in a lifetime a century ago now arrives far more often, and scientists can measure why.

Tons that weigh nothing

In India, people often describe ACs in tons, such as a 1.5-ton split AC. This has nothing to do with weight. It comes from the days when buildings were cooled with ice. One ton of cooling is the amount of heat needed to melt a ton of ice over 24 hours, roughly 3.5 kilowatts.

India's Bureau of Energy Efficiency gives ACs star ratings, so buyers can see which use less electricity. Since 2020, new ACs sold in India must start at 24 °C unless the user changes it. Each degree warmer saves power. Setting the AC very low doesn't cool the room faster, either. It only makes the machine run for longer, which is one of the most common ways people waste electricity.

The heat goes somewhere

Every AC pumps heat out of a room and into the street, and its motor adds more. In crowded cities, thousands of outdoor units make the air outside a little hotter. Power stations that burn coal or gas to run ACs add to climate change, which brings more hot days and more need for cooling.

The International Energy Agency has warned that cooling is one of the fastest-growing uses of electricity in the world. Engineers are working on better refrigerants, smarter buildings with shade and thick walls, and ACs that use far less power.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Setting the air conditioner to its lowest temperature cools the room faster.

Truth: Most ACs cool at their full rate until they reach the set temperature. A lower setting makes them run longer, not faster, and wastes power. India's energy agency suggests about 24 °C.

Try this

Find where the water from an AC goes. Ask an adult to show you the small pipe that drips outside, or a split AC's outdoor unit. On a hot, humid day, put a bowl under the drip for ten minutes and see how much water collects. All of it came out of the air in the room.
