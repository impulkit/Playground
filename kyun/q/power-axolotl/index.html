How can axolotls regrow body parts?

Bite off an axolotl's leg and it grows a new one, bones, muscles, nerves and all, with no scar left behind.

The short answer

When an axolotl loses a leg, the stump does not scar over the way your skin does. Instead, cells near the wound go back to an early, flexible state, a bit like they were in an embryo. They pile up into a little bump called a blastema. Over a few weeks, that bump grows into a brand-new leg, with bones, muscles, nerves and skin in all the right places.

Axolotls can do this for their tail, parts of their spinal cord, and even bits of their heart, eyes and brain. Scientists study them hoping to learn how human bodies might one day heal better.

Surprising but true

The axolotl's set of genes is about ten times longer than yours, one of the longest of any animal ever read.

A salamander that never grows up

The axolotl (say "AX-uh-lot-ul") is a salamander from the lakes around Mexico City. Most salamanders start life in water with gills, then change into land animals, like tadpoles turning into frogs. The axolotl skips that change. It keeps its frilly pink gills, which stick out of its head like a feathery crown, for its whole life.

Its face seems to be smiling. That, plus its pink, gold or dark colours, has made it a pet-shop favourite. The axolotl even appears on Mexico's 50-peso banknote.

How the regrowth works

Your body is built from cells, and most of yours have fixed jobs: skin cells stay skin, bone cells stay bone. An axolotl's wound cells can partly forget their jobs. Skin cells, muscle cells and others near the cut become young again and start dividing quickly.

Nerves are essential. If the nerves to a limb are cut, the stump will not regrow. Somehow the nerve endings send signals that tell the blastema to keep building. The cells also seem to remember where they are, so a cut at the elbow grows a forearm and hand, not a whole new shoulder.

The new limb is not a rough copy. It works as well as the old one.

A giant instruction book

In 2018, scientists finished reading the axolotl's genome, its full set of genetic instructions. It turned out to be about 32 billion letters long, roughly ten times the length of the human genome. Printed in ordinary books, it would fill a library.

Researchers are now hunting through it for the genes that switch regrowth on. Some of the same genes exist in people, but in us they seem to stay quiet after we are born.

Can humans regrow anything

A little. Your liver can regrow to near full size even if a large part is removed, which is why living people can donate part of theirs. Very young children can sometimes regrow the tip of a finger. Your skin and the lining of your gut replace themselves constantly. But a lost arm or leg does not come back.

Scientists hope that understanding axolotls might one day help wounds heal without scars, or help repair injured spinal cords. That is still a long way off.

Millions in tanks, few in the wild

There is a sad twist. Axolotls are common in labs and aquariums around the world, yet in the wild they are Critically Endangered. They live naturally only in the canals of Xochimilco, the last remains of a great lake system the Aztecs once farmed. Surveys found about 6,000 axolotls per square kilometre in 1998, and only about 35 by 2014.

Pollution, a growing city, and introduced fish such as carp and tilapia, which eat axolotl eggs and young, are to blame. Local farmers and scientists are now cleaning some canals to make refuges. And the axolotl is not the only amphibian with a superpower: one North American frog can freeze solid all winter and hop away in spring.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The axolotl is a kind of fish.

Truth: It is a salamander, an amphibian. It looks fishy because it keeps its gills and tail fin from babyhood, but it has four legs with fingers and toes.

Try this

Pinch off a stem of mint or money plant with a few leaves on it and stand it in a glass of water on a bright windowsill. Change the water every few days. Within a week or two you should see new white roots, a plant version of regrowing a lost part.
