What is Warli art?

With just a circle, a triangle and a line, Warli painters can show a whole village dancing, farming and celebrating a wedding.

The short answer

Warli art is a style of painting made by the Warli people. They are an Adivasi (tribal) community who live mostly in the hills and forests north of Mumbai, in Maharashtra. For generations they have painted white figures on the reddish-brown mud walls of their homes.

The paintings are built from three basic shapes. A circle can be the sun, the moon or a head. A triangle can be a mountain, a tree or half a body. A square marks out a sacred space. From these, painters create busy scenes of farming, hunting, festivals and dancing, full of tiny people who seem to be on the move.

Surprising but true

For a long time Warli paintings were made only on the mud walls of homes, so each one slowly faded and had to be painted again.

Rice paste on mud walls

The traditional "paint" is made from things a farming family already has. Walls are smoothed with a mix of mud and cow dung, which dries to an earthy brown. The white comes from rice ground into a paste with water and a little gum to help it stick.

The brush was often a bamboo twig, chewed at one end until it frayed. That gave the painter thin, slightly uneven lines, which is part of why Warli figures look so lively. Because mud walls get repaired and replastered, the paintings were never meant to last forever. They were renewed for each special occasion.

A painting for a wedding

Warli painting began as a ritual art, and its most important use is at a wedding. Married women of the community would paint a square called a chauk on the wall of the house. Inside it they drew Palaghata, a goddess linked with fertility and a good harvest.

Around the chauk spread scenes of everyday village life: people carrying water, a bullock cart, birds in trees, a man climbing a palm. The painting brought blessings to the couple, and it also recorded how the community lived, much like a family photo album on a wall.

The tarpa dance in a circle

One scene turns up again and again. Dozens of dancers hold hands in a great spiral, curling inwards around a musician who plays the tarpa, a long wind instrument made from a dried gourd. The Warli dance this way at harvest time.

Painters show the spiral with tiny figures that get smaller towards the centre. It works a bit like a whirlpool: your eye gets pulled round and round. Circles matter in Warli thinking because life, the seasons and the dance all turn in loops, with no real beginning or end. You can see a similar love of balanced patterns in rangoli drawn on doorsteps across India.

From village walls to the world

For centuries hardly anyone outside the Warli villages saw these paintings. That changed in the 1970s, when an artist named Jivya Soma Mashe began painting Warli scenes on paper and cloth instead of walls. His work was shown in galleries in India and abroad, and he received the Padma Shri in 2011.

Today Warli designs appear on saris, mugs, railway station walls and school notebooks. This has brought income to some Warli artists, but it has also raised a worry. When factories print the patterns cheaply, the people whose tradition it is may earn nothing. Like Madhubani painting from Bihar, Warli art now has a legal tag that links the name to the community.

Older than it looks

Warli scenes remind many people of the hunters and dancers painted on rock shelters at Bhimbetka, which are thousands of years old. Some writers suggest the style may descend from that kind of ancient rock painting. It is a tempting idea, but it is hard to prove, because mud walls crumble and very old Warli paintings simply have not survived. What is certain is that the style has been handed down from parent to child for a very long time, without ever needing a pencil or a printed guide.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Warli painting is just simple stick figures that anyone could copy.

Truth: The shapes are simple on purpose, but each one carries meaning, and arranging hundreds of tiny figures so the whole scene moves takes years of practice. Warli painters also follow rules about what goes where, especially in wedding paintings.

Try this

Get a sheet of brown paper or card and some white paint or chalk. Using only circles, triangles and lines, paint a scene from your own day, such as a school bus, a cricket match or your family at dinner. See how few marks you need for people to recognise what is happening.
