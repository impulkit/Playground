How does your body fight germs?

Right now, an army of cells is patrolling your blood, eating invaders whole and remembering the faces of germs you met years ago.

The short answer

Your body fights germs in layers. The first layer keeps them out: your skin, the sticky mucus in your nose, the tears in your eyes and the acid in your stomach. Most germs never get past these.

When some do, white blood cells rush in. Some of them gobble up anything that looks foreign. Others build exact weapons for each new germ, called antibodies, which stick to the germ and mark it for destruction. Once the fight is won, a few of these cells stay behind for years, remembering. That's why you rarely catch the same illness twice. Together, all these parts are called the immune system.

Surprising but true

White blood cells were discovered fighting germs inside a see-through baby starfish, after a scientist pushed a rose thorn into it.

The walls of the castle

Think of your body as a fort. Your skin is the outer wall, made of tough, dead cells packed tightly together. Openings like the nose, mouth and eyes are guarded too. Mucus traps dust and germs, and tiny hairs sweep it away. Tears and saliva contain a chemical that breaks open some bacteria.

Even the friendly microbes on your skin and in your gut help. They take up space and food, leaving less room for harmful germs. A cut or a scrape is a hole in the wall, which is why the body rushes to seal it with a scab.

The soldiers who eat

Some white blood cells, called phagocytes, wrap themselves around a germ and swallow it whole, then digest it. They were discovered in 1882 by Ilya Mechnikov, a Russian scientist working in Messina, Italy. He pushed a rose thorn into a see-through starfish larva, and the next morning he saw cells crowded around the thorn, attacking it. He shared a Nobel Prize for the idea in 1908.

When these cells fight, the area often gets red, warm and swollen. That is inflammation. Extra blood brings more soldiers to the spot.

How did microscopes reveal a hidden world?In the 1670s, a Dutch cloth seller peered through a lens smaller than a pea and saw tiny creatures swimming in a drop of pond water. Nobody had ever seen them before.

Weapons made to measure

The second team is slower but sharper. Cells called B cells make antibodies that fit one particular germ the way a key fits a lock. T cells hunt down your own cells that have been taken over by a virus and destroy them. It takes days to build these weapons the first time, and that is often when you feel most ill.

Antibodies are shaped like the letter Y. The two arms grab the germ, and the stem works like a flag that tells the eating cells where to bite. Your body can make billions of different antibody shapes, so there is usually one that fits even a germ it has never met before.

These cells gather in small lumps called lymph nodes, in your neck, armpits and elsewhere. When you have a sore throat, you might feel swollen bumps under your jaw. Those are lymph nodes busy making an army.

A long memory

After the fight, most of the new soldiers die off, but some memory cells live on, sometimes for a lifetime. If the same germ returns, they recognise it at once and strike before it can spread.

Some germs keep changing their outer coats, so memory cells don't recognise them. Flu viruses do this, which is why flu vaccines are updated every year. Colds come from more than 200 different viruses, so beating one doesn't protect you from the next. The immune system can also make mistakes and attack harmless things, as in allergies, or even the body itself.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You can boost your immune system with special foods, drinks or pills.

Truth: The immune system isn't a muscle you can pump up, and an overactive one causes problems like allergies. Sleep, ordinary healthy food, exercise and vaccines help it work the way it should.

Try this

Play germs and guards. One person is a germ who tries to cross the room, and the others are white blood cells who must tag them. In round two, give the guards a picture of what the germ is wearing, so they spot it faster. That's how immune memory speeds things up.
