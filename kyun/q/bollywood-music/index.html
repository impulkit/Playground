How are Bollywood songs made?

In most Hindi films, the hero singing on screen isn't singing at all. Someone else recorded the song in a studio weeks earlier.

The short answer

A Bollywood song is made by a team. The film's director explains the moment in the story: a wedding, a break-up, a chase in the rain. The music director composes a tune. A lyricist writes the words. Arrangers add instruments, and playback singers record the song in a studio.

Only then is the song filmed. On set, the recording plays through loudspeakers while actors dance and move their lips to match. This is called lip-syncing. That is why the voice you hear and the face you see usually belong to different people, and why great playback singers became as famous as the stars.

Songs from the very start

India's first talking film, Alam Ara, came out in 1931 and already had seven songs. The actors sang live as they were filmed, with musicians hiding off camera. One film made the next year, Indrasabha, is said to have had around seventy songs.

Singing live was hard. Actors had to be good singers as well as good actors, and every mistake meant filming again. In the mid-1930s, studios began recording songs separately and playing them back during filming. Playback singing changed everything. Actors no longer needed to sing, and the best voices in India could be heard on screen.

From tune to recording

Often the tune comes first. A music director might hum a melody or play it on a harmonium. The lyricist then writes words that fit. Sometimes it works the other way round, with a poem set to music.

In the classic era, dozens of musicians recorded together in one big room: violins, flutes, sitar, mandolin, tabla and dholak. The singer stood at a microphone, and everyone had to get it right in one go. Today, each part is usually recorded separately on computers and mixed later. A singer may record a line dozens of times, and engineers pick the best pieces.

The great voices

From the 1940s to the 1990s, a small group of singers recorded thousands of songs. Lata Mangeshkar and her sister Asha Bhosle sang for heroines. Mohammed Rafi, Kishore Kumar and Mukesh sang for heroes.

Fans could recognise these voices in a single line. Music directors such as S.D. Burman, his son R.D. Burman, and the duo Shankar–Jaikishan became household names. Poets like Sahir Ludhianvi and Gulzar wrote lyrics that people still quote.

A radio station across the sea

In the 1950s, India's national broadcaster, All India Radio, cut back on film songs, which some officials saw as too light. Listeners turned elsewhere.

Radio Ceylon, from Colombo in Sri Lanka, began playing Hindi film songs, and its countdown show Binaca Geetmala, hosted by Ameen Sayani from 1952, became hugely popular across India. Families gathered around radios to hear which song was number one. All India Radio later started its own film music service, Vividh Bharati, in 1957.

New sounds

From the 1990s, composers like A.R. Rahman brought digital recording, electronic sounds and global styles into film music. In 2009, his song Jai Ho, with words by Gulzar, won an Academy Award.

Today, songs are often released online before a film comes out, to build excitement. Many become hits at weddings and parties, and in Indian communities from Toronto to Texas. A good song can outlive the film it was made for by decades.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Bollywood stars sing their own songs.

Truth: Almost all film songs are sung by playback singers while actors lip-sync. A few stars have sung some of their own songs, but they are the exception.

Try this

Pick a song you know well and hum its tune. Now write new words for it about your school day, making sure each line has the same number of syllables as the original so it fits the tune. Sing your version to a friend. Many film lyricists work this way, fitting words to a ready-made tune.
