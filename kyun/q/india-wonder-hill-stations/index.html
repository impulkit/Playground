Why did the British build hill stations?

Every summer, the whole government of British India packed its files onto mules and porters and moved up a mountain to Shimla.

The short answer

In the hot months, the plains of north India can reach 45°C. For British officials and soldiers raised in cool, rainy Britain, that heat felt unbearable. Many also fell ill and died from diseases like malaria and cholera. So, from the 1820s onwards, they built small towns high in the hills, where the air was cooler.

These "hill stations" became summer homes, hospitals, schools and holiday spots. The biggest of them, Shimla in Himachal, became the summer capital of British India from 1864. For several months a year, the people ruling one-sixth of humanity did it from a mountain ridge.

Surprising but true

Kodaikanal, one of South India's best-known hill stations, was started in 1845 by American missionaries, not by British officials.

Climbing away from the heat

The trick is height. Air gets thinner and colder as you climb, by roughly 6.5°C for every 1,000 m. Shimla sits about 2,200 m up, so it is often around 14°C cooler than Delhi. Ooty in the Nilgiris and Darjeeling in the eastern Himalaya are at similar heights.

Before the railways, moving the government was a giant chore. Files, furniture, pianos and officials travelled up by bullock cart, pony, palanquin and the backs of porters. Later, tiny mountain trains zigzagged up the slopes and made the trip far easier.

Shimla's grandest building, the Viceregal Lodge, was finished in 1888. It had electric lights from the start, a rare luxury anywhere in 1888. After independence it became a research institute, and today visitors can walk the same wooden corridors where viceroys once decided the fate of millions.

Running from "bad air"

Doctors in the 1800s believed many diseases came from foul air rising from swamps and hot plains. The word malaria itself comes from Italian for "bad air". Hill air felt cleaner, so the hills seemed healthier.

They were partly right, for the wrong reason. Malaria is spread by mosquitoes, and the mosquitoes that carry it struggle in cold, high places. So soldiers did recover better in the hills. They just didn't know why until Ronald Ross proved the mosquito link in India in 1897.

A little England in the clouds

Homesickness shaped these towns as much as heat. The British built cottages with English names, stone churches and a long street called the Mall for evening walks. Boarding schools opened so children didn't have to be shipped to Britain, such as the Lawrence School at Sanawar in 1847.

In Darjeeling, a doctor named Archibald Campbell planted tea seeds in 1841. The hills turned out to be perfect for it, and Darjeeling tea became one of the most prized in the world.

Not a new idea

Escaping the summer was not a British invention. The Mughal emperor Jahangir loved spending summers in the cool valleys of Kashmir, two centuries earlier. Indian rulers had their own hill palaces too.

The idea also travelled. Kodaikanal in Tamil Nadu was started in 1845 by American missionaries looking to escape the plains. In the early 1900s, Americans ruling the Philippines built the hill town of Baguio as their own summer capital, inspired by the British hill stations of India.

Hill stations today

After 1947, hill stations became holiday towns for Indian families. Shimla, Mussoorie, Ooty, Munnar and Mount Abu now fill with tourists every summer. Matheran, near Mumbai, still bans cars, so visitors walk, ride horses or take a hand-pulled rickshaw.

Popularity has a price. Towns designed for a few thousand people now host many times that, and Shimla ran desperately short of water in 2018. Some writers still find peace there, though. Ruskin Bond has lived in Landour above Mussoorie for decades, and the quiet hills fill his stories. Your body notices those heights too, in ways explained by the science of altitude.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The British discovered places like Shimla and Ooty, which were empty before they came.

Truth: People already lived in and around these hills. Toda herders grazed buffaloes on the Nilgiri plateau around Ooty, and Lepcha and other communities lived around Darjeeling. The British built new towns, but on land that already had owners and names.
