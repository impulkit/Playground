How do owls fly without a sound?

A pigeon claps and whooshes. An owl glides past your ear in total silence.

The short answer

Most of the noise of a flying bird comes from air rushing and tumbling off the edges of its wings. Owls have feathers that calm that air down. The front edge of each main wing feather has a row of tiny stiff teeth, like a comb. The back edge is soft and frayed. The surface is covered in velvety fuzz.

Together, these break up big noisy swirls of air into many tiny quiet ones, and soak up the sound of feathers rubbing. Owls also have big wings for their weight, so they can fly slowly and glide often. To a mouse rustling in the grass, an owl arrives with almost no warning. Engineers have copied owl feathers to design quieter fans and trains.

Surprising but true

Japan's bullet train has a part shaped like owl feathers, to make it quieter.

Ears that see in the dark

Silent flight helps the owl hear as well as stay hidden. If its own wings were loud, it could not hear its prey. A barn owl's flat, heart-shaped face works like a satellite dish, gathering sound and funnelling it to the ears.

In barn owls, one ear opening sits higher on the head than the other. A sound from below reaches the lower ear a fraction louder. By comparing both ears, the owl can pinpoint a mouse up, down, left or right. In experiments in the USA, barn owls caught mice in total darkness using hearing alone. You use a simpler version of this trick to tell where a sound comes from, as explained in how ears hear.

A train with owl feathers

In the 1990s, Japan's bullet trains had a noise problem. The pantograph, the arm on the roof that touches the overhead power line, howled as it cut through the air at high speed. An engineer named Eiji Nakatsu, who was also a keen birdwatcher, studied owl feathers. His team gave the pantograph small saw-tooth edges, a bit like the owl's comb. The train became noticeably quieter.

Swallowed whole

Owls usually swallow small prey whole. Their stomachs digest the soft parts, but fur, feathers and bones are packed into a neat bundle called a pellet. A few hours later the owl coughs it up. Scientists collect owl pellets from under roosts to find out exactly what owls eat. Many classrooms in America and Europe pull them apart with tweezers and find tiny skulls and jaws inside.

Barn owls live on every continent except Antarctica, including all over India. Farmers sometimes welcome them, because a single family of barn owls can eat many rats and mice in one breeding season.

Owls in stories

Owls have been given very different meanings around the world. In ancient Greece, the little owl was linked with Athena, goddess of wisdom, and many Western picture books still show owls as wise. In India, the owl is the vahana, or vehicle, of the goddess Lakshmi, who is worshipped at Diwali. Yet "ullu" is also a playful Hindi word for a silly person.

Not all owls are silent. Fish owls, found in India and other parts of Asia, have lost most of the silent features, because fish cannot hear wings above the water. Silence, it turns out, is only worth having if your dinner can hear you coming. The soft feathers themselves have a long history, which you can trace in where feathers came from. And bats, which hunt using sound in a very different way, are explained in echolocation.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Owls can turn their heads all the way round.

Truth: Owls can turn their heads about 270 degrees, three-quarters of a full circle. That is far more than we can, because their eyes are fixed in place and they must turn the whole head to look around.

Try this

Wave a stiff piece of cardboard quickly past your ear and listen to the whoosh. Then glue or tape a fringe of cotton wool or soft cloth along one edge and try again. Is it quieter? That soft edge is the owl's trick.
