How do waves shape coastlines?

In January 1990, a stone arch on Australia's coast suddenly fell into the sea, leaving two startled tourists stranded on a brand-new island.

The short answer

Waves are the sea's sculptors. Every breaking wave slams water, air and loose stones against the shore. Over years and centuries, this wears away rock, carving cliffs, caves, arches and pillars standing alone in the water.

How fast it happens depends on the rock. Soft clay or chalk can lose a metre or more in one stormy year. Hard granite might barely change in a human lifetime. That's why many coastlines zigzag, with tough rock jutting out as headlands and softer rock scooped into bays.

The sea builds as well as destroys. The sand and pebbles it grinds from cliffs get carried along the shore and dropped somewhere else, building beaches, spits and sandbars.

Surprising but true

Much of a cliff's erosion doesn't happen slowly and evenly. It often comes in sudden collapses, sometimes after years with almost no visible change.

How a wave breaks rock

Waves get their energy from wind blowing over the open ocean, sometimes thousands of kilometres away. When they reach shallow water near the coast, they slow down, grow taller and crash.

When a wave hits a cliff, it squeezes air into every crack. As the wave pulls back, the air bursts out again. Over thousands of waves, this widens the cracks and loosens blocks of rock. Geographers call it hydraulic action.

Waves also throw sand, gravel and boulders at the cliff, scraping it like sandpaper. That's abrasion. Some rocks, such as chalk and limestone, slowly dissolve in seawater as well.

Notches, caves and stacks

Most damage happens at the bottom of a cliff, where waves hit hardest. They cut a groove called a notch. The rock above hangs over it until it can no longer hold its own weight and crashes down. The cliff steps back, leaving a flat shelf of rock at its old foot.

Where waves attack a headland from both sides, a sequence follows. Cracks widen into caves. Caves on opposite sides meet and become an arch. The arch roof falls, leaving a stack. The stack is undercut and topples into a stump. The white chalk stacks of Old Harry Rocks, on England's south coast, show several of these stages side by side.

A bridge falls down

Along the coast of Victoria in southern Australia stand the Twelve Apostles, a row of limestone stacks rising out of the Southern Ocean. Fewer than a dozen remain, and one of them crumbled in 2005.

Nearby stood London Bridge, a double arch joined to the mainland. In January 1990, the arch closest to the shore suddenly collapsed. Two visitors who had walked out to the far end found themselves cut off on a new island. Nobody was hurt, and a helicopter lifted them off.

Something similar can happen to houses. In Pacifica, California, storms in the winter of 2015–16 ate away a cliff so fast that apartment buildings at its edge had to be emptied and later pulled down.

India's changing shore

India has more than 11,000 km of coastline, counting its islands, by a 2024 measurement, and much of it is sandy rather than rocky. At Varkala in Kerala, though, red and orange cliffs rise straight above the beach. Cliffs like these are unusual on Kerala's low, sandy coast, and they have been declared a national geological monument.

A government study of India's coast from 1990 to 2018 found that about a third of it was wearing away. Some places, such as parts of the Odisha and West Bengal coasts, have lost villages to the sea. Sea walls and rock barriers can slow the damage, though they sometimes push the problem further along the shore.

As sea levels rise and strong storms hit more often, waves will reach higher up cliffs and beaches. Coastal towns around the world are deciding whether to build walls, plant mangroves or move back from the edge.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Cliffs wear away slowly and steadily, a tiny bit every day.

Truth: Waves do work at cliffs all the time, but most of the change comes in sudden falls, often during storms. A cliff can look the same for years and then lose several metres in a single night.

Try this

Build a little cliff from damp sand in a tray, with a pebble wall at one end. Push water at it in gentle waves with a ruler or your hand. Watch where the sand is undercut, what happens to the overhang, and how the pebble part holds out longer.
