Who wore the first shoes?

The oldest shoes ever found were woven from tree bark about 10,000 years ago and left in a cave in Oregon.

The short answer

Nobody knows exactly who wore the first shoes, because early shoes were made of bark, grass and animal skin, which rot away. But we have clues.

The oldest shoes ever found are woven sandals from Fort Rock Cave in Oregon, in the United States, made about 9,000 to 10,000 years ago. Scientists think people were wearing some kind of footwear much earlier, perhaps 40,000 years ago, based on changes in their toe bones. The first shoes were probably simple wraps of hide tied around the foot, to protect it from cold, sharp stones and thorns.

Surprising but true

Scientists can tell people were wearing shoes about 40,000 years ago without finding a single shoe, just by looking at how their toe bones changed.

Clues in the toes

Walking barefoot makes the small bones in your toes strong and thick. Wearing shoes takes some of that load away, so over time, the toes of people who wear shoes become slightly thinner.

The American anthropologist Erik Trinkaus compared toe bones from early humans. He found that toes began getting thinner around 40,000 years ago, during the last ice age. His idea is that people had started wrapping their feet. Not every scientist agrees on the exact date, but it is a clever way to find a shoe without finding a shoe.

Sandals in a cave

In 1938 the archaeologist Luther Cressman dug in Fort Rock Cave in Oregon. Under a layer of volcanic ash, he found dozens of sandals woven from shredded sagebrush bark. They had flat soles and flaps that folded over the toes.

Years later, radiocarbon dating showed the sandals were about 9,000 to 10,000 years old. They are still the oldest shoes known. The dry cave and the ash had protected them for thousands of years, the kind of lucky find archaeology depends on.

The Iceman's boots

In 1991 hikers in the Alps found the body of a man who had died about 5,300 years ago. Scientists call him Ötzi. His shoes had soles of bearskin, tops of deerskin and a net of string stuffed with dry grass to keep his feet warm.

In 2010 an even older leather shoe turned up in a cave in Armenia. It was about 5,500 years old, made from one piece of cowhide laced around the foot, and it was stuffed with grass too.

Shoes in old India

In India, wooden sandals called paduka or khadau have been worn for a very long time. They have a knob that sits between the big toe and the next one. In the Ramayana, Bharata places Rama's padukas on the throne while Rama is in exile. Leather shoes such as the jutti of Punjab and the Kolhapuri chappal of Maharashtra are still made by hand today.

Left foot, right foot

For long stretches of history, many shoes were made on straight wooden forms, so either shoe fitted either foot. Shaped left and right shoes became normal in Europe and America only during the 1800s, when new machines made them cheaper to produce.

Rubber on the road

In 1839 the American inventor Charles Goodyear found a way to toughen rubber with heat and sulphur. Rubber soles soon followed. By the early 1900s, canvas shoes with rubber soles were sold for sports, the ancestors of today's sneakers. Shoes have changed a great deal since the Fort Rock sandals, but the job is the same: keeping feet safe from the ground.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Shoes have always come in left and right pairs.

Truth: For long stretches of history, many shoes were made on straight forms and could be worn on either foot. Shaped left and right shoes became normal in Europe and America only in the 1800s.

Try this

Stand on a sheet of paper and ask a friend to trace around both of your bare feet. Compare the two outlines. Then look at the soles of an old pair of shoes and find where they have worn down most. Your walking leaves a pattern, just as it did for people thousands of years ago.
