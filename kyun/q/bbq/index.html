Why is barbecue so popular in America?

A Texas pitmaster may stay awake all night tending a fire, just to cook one piece of beef slowly enough.

The short answer

In the United States, barbecue means cooking meat very slowly, with low heat and the smoke from burning wood. A cook, called a pitmaster, might keep a pork shoulder or a beef brisket at around 110 to 135 °C for ten hours or more. The meat comes out dark outside, pink just under the surface, and soft enough to pull apart with a fork.

Barbecue is popular because it turns cheap, tough cuts into something delicious, and because it is a social food. It has long been the centrepiece of church picnics, political rallies, family reunions and Fourth of July parties. Each region of the South is fiercely proud of its own style.

Surprising but true

Real barbecue is cooked at a lower temperature than many home ovens use for baking a cake.

Low and slow

The meat used in barbecue comes from hard-working muscles, like the chest or shoulder of a cow or pig. These are full of collagen, a tough protein that holds muscle fibres together. Cook them fast and they turn rubbery.

Cook them slowly, though, and the collagen gradually melts into soft gelatin. Fat melts too and keeps the meat moist. The smoke adds flavour and a pinkish "smoke ring" just under the crust, made when gases from the fire react with the meat. Wood choice matters: hickory, oak, pecan and, in Texas, mesquite each give a different taste. The science of cooking here is mostly about patience.

Where the word comes from

When Spanish explorers reached the Caribbean in the late 1400s, they saw the Taíno people cooking and drying food on frames of green sticks raised over a fire. The Spanish wrote the word as barbacoa. Most historians think this is the root of "barbecue", though nobody can be completely sure.

Over the next centuries, cooking whole animals over pits of coals became common in the American colonies. George Washington wrote in his diary about going "to a barbicue".

What is fire?Fire isn't a thing at all. It's something happening, a fast chemical reaction that gives off heat and light.

Who did the cooking

In the American South, much of the actual pit work for big barbecues was done by enslaved African Americans, and later by Black cooks and pitmasters. They dug the pits, tended the fires through the night and developed many of the techniques still used. Historians now say that the story of barbecue cannot be told without them.

In the 20th century, decades after the end of slavery, millions of Black Americans moved north in search of work and fairer treatment. They carried barbecue to cities such as Chicago and Kansas City, where it took new forms.

Four famous styles

Barbecue fans argue endlessly about whose is best. In the Carolinas, the favourite is pork, often a whole hog, with a thin, sharp vinegar sauce. In Memphis, Tennessee, people love pork ribs rubbed with dry spices. In Kansas City, almost any meat goes on, covered in thick, sweet tomato sauce.

In central Texas, the king is beef brisket, seasoned with little more than salt and black pepper. Famous Texas barbecue joints often sell out by early afternoon, and people queue for hours.

Fire cooking in India

India has its own long tradition of cooking with fire and smoke, though usually faster and hotter. A tandoor, a clay oven, reaches far higher temperatures than an American barbecue pit, cooking naan and tikka in minutes. Seekh kebabs sizzle over glowing coals in Lucknow and Old Delhi, and bhutta, roasted corn, is sold on street corners each monsoon. The goal is different, but the love of smoke and flame is shared.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Barbecue and grilling are the same thing.

Truth: Grilling cooks food quickly over high, direct heat, like a burger on a hot grate. Barbecue cooks meat slowly with low heat and wood smoke, often for many hours. Many Americans still call any outdoor cookout a "barbecue", which adds to the confusion.

Try this

Run a sauce taste test at home with an adult. Mix one spoon of tomato ketchup with half a spoon of vinegar, and another spoon of ketchup with half a spoon of honey. Dip cucumber or bread in each and vote on which you prefer. You have just tasted the difference between a sharp Carolina-style sauce and a sweet Kansas City one.
