Why do events have mascots?

In 1982, a smiling baby elephant called Appu became the face of the Asian Games in Delhi. Indians still remember his name.

The short answer

A mascot gives an event a friendly face. A sports tournament, a festival or a company is a big, hard-to-picture thing. A cheerful cartoon animal is easy to remember, draw and love, especially for children.

Mascots also do practical jobs. They appear on posters, tickets, badges and toys, and selling those toys helps pay for the event. They often show off something from the host place, like a local animal or symbol. Inside the stadium, a person in a big costume dances, waves and keeps the crowd happy during the boring bits. That's a lot of work for one smiling character.

Surprising but true

The word "mascot" became popular because of a French comic opera from 1880, La Mascotte, about a girl who brings good luck to everyone around her.

From lucky charm to cartoon

The word comes from the French mascotte, meaning a lucky charm. It spread after a French comic opera called La Mascotte became a hit in 1880. The story's heroine brings luck to whoever keeps her close.

At first, a mascot could be any lucky object or animal. Soldiers kept goats and dogs as regimental mascots. Sports teams in the United States started keeping live animals, like bulldogs and goats, at their games. Over time, many teams switched to costumed characters, which don't get scared by a roaring crowd.

Olympic mascots

The Olympics got its first official mascot in 1972. Waldi, a striped dachshund, represented the Munich Games in Germany. Since then, every Games has had one or more.

Some were animals, like Misha the bear cub at Moscow 1980. Others were stranger. The Paris 2024 mascots, the Phryges, were two red, floppy caps based on a hat worn in the French Revolution. Mascot designers try to say something about the host country in one simple shape. They also want something children will happily take home as a soft toy.

How do adverts try to persuade you?The first official TV advert in the USA, in 1941, showed a watch ticking over a map of the country for about ten seconds. It cost the company $9.

Appu and Shera

India's most loved mascot is probably Appu, the baby elephant of the 1982 Asian Games in Delhi. He was a cheerful cartoon elephant, and he was everywhere: on posters, badges and Doordarshan. Two years later, Delhi opened an amusement park called Appu Ghar, named after him.

For the 2010 Commonwealth Games, also in Delhi, the mascot was Shera, a cartoon tiger. Tigers are India's national animal, and the design was meant to say that India was strong and ready.

Mascots that sell

Companies use mascots too, and some are older than any sports mascot. The Michelin Man, made of tyres, dates from 1898. In India, the little Amul girl in her polka-dot frock has appeared on Amul butter adverts since 1966. Her billboards make jokes about the week's news, and people look out for them.

These characters work because people trust faces. Studies of advertising show that children recognise brand characters from a very young age. That's one reason some countries have rules about using cartoon mascots to sell sugary food to children.

Life inside the suit

Being a costumed mascot is harder than it looks. The suits are heavy and hot, and in an Indian summer or a Texas afternoon the person inside can overheat quickly. Many performers can see only through a small mesh panel, so a helper often walks beside them. Most mascots also have a rule never to speak, because a real voice would break the spell for young fans.

Changing with the times

Mascots sometimes have to change. In the United States, many school and sports teams once used names and mascots based on Native American people. Many Native groups said these were hurtful stereotypes. Over the years, many teams changed them, including two big professional teams in 2020 to 2022.

Even small details matter. Designers now test mascots with children before launch, to check that they look friendly and not scary. Somewhere, a design team is already arguing about what the mascot for the 2028 Los Angeles Olympics should look like.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A team's mascot brings it good luck.

Truth: The word did start with lucky charms, but there's no evidence that mascots change results. What they can do is lift the mood of a crowd and give fans, especially children, something to cheer for.

Try this

Design a mascot for your school or your town. Choose an animal, plant or object that belongs to your area and give it a name that's easy to shout. Draw it from the front and the side, then ask your friends what they think it stands for, without telling them first.
