What is the Acropolis?

The Parthenon in Athens survived for over 2,000 years, then was blown apart in a single afternoon in 1687 when gunpowder stored inside exploded.

The short answer

The Acropolis is a flat-topped hill of rock that rises in the middle of Athens, the capital of Greece. "Acropolis" means "high city" in Greek. Many ancient Greek towns had one, a safe high place for temples and a last line of defence. The one in Athens is by far the most famous.

On top stand the ruins of temples built about 2,500 years ago. The biggest is the Parthenon, a temple to Athena, the goddess of wisdom and the protector of the city. Beside it stand smaller temples, including the Erechtheion, whose porch roof is held up by six stone statues of young women instead of columns. Millions of people climb up to see it all every year, usually early in the morning, before the summer sun turns the bare rock into an oven.

Surprising but true

The Parthenon has almost no perfectly straight lines. Its floor bulges slightly in the middle and its columns lean a little inwards, which makes it look straighter from a distance.

Built when Athens was at its richest

Around 450 BCE, Athens was a powerful city. It had led other Greek cities against the Persian Empire and collected money from its allies. A leader named Pericles used some of that money to rebuild the Acropolis, which Persian soldiers had burned in 480 BCE.

The Parthenon went up between 447 and 432 BCE. The sculptor Phidias led the decoration. Inside stood a giant statue of Athena, about 12 metres tall, covered in gold and ivory. It was lost long ago, and nobody knows exactly what happened to it. Those were also the years when Athens ran an early form of democracy, with male citizens voting on laws.

Tricks with straight lines

The Parthenon looks perfectly neat, but it is full of tiny curves. The floor rises a few centimetres in the middle, like a very gentle hump. The columns swell slightly halfway up and lean a little inwards. If you could stretch their lines upwards, they would meet well over a kilometre in the sky.

Why bother? Many experts think the builders were correcting an optical illusion. A long, perfectly flat floor can look as if it sags in the middle, and perfectly straight columns can seem to bend. Others think the curves also helped rainwater drain and made the building stronger.

Who were the Greek gods?The ancient Greeks believed their gods lived on a real mountain, argued like a big family and sometimes turned people into spiders.

A temple, a church, a mosque and a gunpowder store

The Parthenon has had many lives. In the centuries after ancient Greece, it became a Christian church. When the Ottoman Empire ruled Athens, it became a mosque.

In 1687, an army from Venice attacked the Ottomans in Athens. The Ottoman soldiers were storing gunpowder inside the Parthenon. On 26 September, a Venetian cannonball hit it. The explosion blew out the roof and brought down many columns. What you see today is mostly the result of that one day.

The marbles in London

In the early 1800s, agents of a British ambassador, Lord Elgin, removed about half of the surviving Parthenon sculptures and shipped them to Britain. He said he had permission from the Ottoman rulers. Today they are in the British Museum in London.

Greece has asked for them back for decades, and many people agree. The British Museum says it holds them legally and shows them to visitors from all over the world. In 2009, Athens opened the new Acropolis Museum at the foot of the hill. Its top floor has empty spaces left for the missing sculptures, next to plaster copies.

Repairing a ruin

Since 1975, Greek teams have been carefully repairing the Acropolis. Earlier repairs used iron clamps, which rusted, swelled and cracked the marble. Engineers are replacing them with titanium, which doesn't rust. They also use fresh marble from Mount Pentelikon, the same mountain the ancient builders quarried, so new pieces match the old.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ancient Greek temples and statues were always plain white marble.

Truth: Many were painted in bright colours. Scientists have found traces of blue, red and gold paint on Greek sculptures. Most of it has worn away over the centuries, leaving the white stone we see today.

Try this

Stand two pencils upright on a table, then tilt their tops very slightly towards each other. From across the room, they will look perfectly straight and steady. The Parthenon's builders used this kind of trick with stone columns 10 metres tall.
