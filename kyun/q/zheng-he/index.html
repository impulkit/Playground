How big were Zheng He's treasure ships?

Eighty-seven years before Columbus, a Chinese admiral sailed for India with more than 300 ships and nearly 28,000 men.

The short answer

Zheng He was an admiral of China's Ming dynasty who led seven great voyages across the Indian Ocean between 1405 and 1433. His fleets visited Southeast Asia, India, Sri Lanka, Arabia and East Africa.

The biggest ships, called treasure ships, were enormous. Ming records describe them as about 44 zhang long, roughly 120 to 135 metres, longer than a football field. Many historians doubt this, because wooden ships of that size would be very hard to build and steer. Even cautious estimates of 60 to 75 metres make them several times longer than Columbus's Santa María, which was probably about 18 metres. The fleets also carried warships, supply ships, water tankers and horse transports.

Surprising but true

Zheng He's fleet visited Calicut, in Kerala, on all seven of its great voyages.

A boy from Yunnan

Zheng He was born in 1371 in Yunnan, in southwest China, into a Muslim family. His birth name was Ma He. When Ming armies conquered Yunnan, he was captured as a boy and sent to serve in the household of a prince, Zhu Di.

He grew into a tall, capable officer and helped Zhu Di seize the throne in 1402. The new emperor, known as Yongle, gave him the name Zheng and put him in charge of a plan to show China's power across the seas.

A floating city

The first fleet left in 1405. Ming records say it had about 317 ships and 27,800 people: sailors, soldiers, doctors, translators, astrologers and officials. It was like a floating town.

The ships were built at a great shipyard near Nanjing. They had many masts, watertight compartments inside the hull, and huge rudders. Sailors steered with magnetic compasses and star charts, and timed their voyages with the monsoon winds, which blow towards India in summer and back in winter.

Calicut, again and again

Every one of the seven voyages stopped at Calicut, today's Kozhikode in Kerala. It was one of the busiest ports in the Indian Ocean, a market for pepper, cotton cloth and gems.

In Sri Lanka, the fleet left a stone tablet at Galle in 1409, carved in three languages: Chinese, Tamil and Persian. It records gifts to the Buddha, to a Hindu god and to Allah. It was found in 1911 and is now in the National Museum in Colombo.

Gifts, giraffes and power

The voyages were mainly about prestige and trade. Zheng He carried silk, porcelain and gold as gifts, and invited rulers to send envoys to the emperor. Dozens of kingdoms did.

Later voyages reached Hormuz in Persia, Aden in Arabia and Malindi and Mogadishu on the African coast. In 1414, a giraffe arrived at the Ming court, sent from Bengal. The court saw it as a qilin, a creature of legend that appears under a wise ruler. The fleets also fought pirates and sometimes local armies, so they were not always peaceful.

Why the voyages stopped

The voyages cost a fortune. After Yongle died in 1424, many officials argued that the money was better spent at home, on defending the northern border against the Mongols.

Zheng He led one last voyage in 1431 and probably died on the way back, around 1433. China then turned away from long ocean voyages. Some records of the voyages are said to have been destroyed by officials who opposed them. Just a few decades later, Portuguese ships rounded Africa and reached Calicut themselves, in 1498.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Zheng He's fleet discovered America in 1421.

Truth: A popular book made this claim in 2002, but historians found no real evidence for it. Zheng He's fleets sailed the Indian Ocean, as far as East Africa, not the Pacific coast of the Americas.

Try this

Measure out 18 metres in a playground or corridor with a tape measure or by counting steps, and mark it. That is roughly the length of Columbus's Santa María. Then try to pace out 70 metres, a careful estimate for Zheng He's biggest ships, and 125 metres, the size given in Ming records.
