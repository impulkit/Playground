Who was Tipu Sultan?

Rockets fired by Tipu Sultan's army so impressed the British that they built their own. Those rockets later lit up a battle in America.

The short answer

Tipu Sultan ruled the kingdom of Mysore, in today's Karnataka, from 1782 to 1799. He took over from his father, Hyder Ali, a soldier who had seized power in Mysore. Tipu was a fierce opponent of the British East India Company, and Mysore fought them in four wars, the first two led by his father and the last two by Tipu.

He is remembered for his iron-cased rockets, his interest in new technology and trade, and his attempts to ally with France against Britain. He died fighting at the walls of his capital, Srirangapatna, in 1799. Today he is one of the most argued-about figures in Indian history.

Surprising but true

The "rockets' red glare" in the US national anthem describes British Congreve rockets, which were developed from rockets captured from Tipu's Mysore.

Father and son

Hyder Ali rose from a cavalry officer to become the real power in Mysore in the 1760s. His son Tipu, born in 1750 at Devanahalli near today's Bengaluru, was trained in languages, religion and warfare from a young age.

Tipu fought beside his father against the British. In 1780, at the Battle of Pollilur near Kanchipuram, their forces crushed a British army. A Mysore rocket is thought to have blown up British ammunition carts during the battle. When Hyder Ali died in 1782, Tipu became ruler.

Iron rockets

Mysore's soldiers used rockets made of iron tubes packed with gunpowder, tied to long bamboo poles or sword blades. They could fly up to around two kilometres. Thousands of rocket men were part of Tipu's army.

After Tipu's defeat, the British shipped captured rockets to England. An inventor named William Congreve studied them and developed the Congreve rocket. Britain fired these rockets at Fort McHenry, in Baltimore, in 1814. Watching the attack, the American lawyer Francis Scott Key wrote of "the rockets' red glare" in the poem that became the US national anthem. A weapon from Mysore had echoed all the way to Maryland.

How does a rocket work?A rocket doesn't push against the air. It throws hot gas out of the back so hard that the gas pushes it forward.

A king who tried new things

Tipu was curious about technology and trade. He introduced a new calendar, new coins and new weights and measures. He set up trading posts abroad and encouraged silk farming in Mysore, which is still famous for its silk today.

He sent ambassadors to France and to the Ottoman Empire looking for allies against the British. In 1799 Napoleon, then fighting in Egypt, wrote him a letter promising help. It never came in time.

His most famous possession is Tipu's Tiger, a life-size wooden tiger attacking a European soldier. Inside is a small organ, so that turning a handle makes the tiger growl and the man wail. It is now in the Victoria and Albert Museum in London.

The fall of Srirangapatna

The Third Anglo-Mysore War ended badly for Tipu in 1792. He lost about half his kingdom and had to send two young sons to the British as hostages until he paid a huge sum.

In 1799 the British, with their allies, attacked again. They besieged Srirangapatna, an island fortress in the Kaveri river. On 4 May 1799 they broke through the walls. Tipu was killed fighting near a gateway. His palace was looted, and many of his treasures were carried off to Britain.

A ruler people still argue about

People still argue about Tipu. Many Indians see him as a brave patriot who died fighting colonial rule, an early hero of resistance. He gave grants to Hindu temples, and his letters to the Sringeri math, a Hindu monastery, show respect.

Others point to evidence that his armies forced conversions and treated some communities very harshly, including Hindus in Kodagu and Malabar and Catholic Christians in Mangaluru. Historians argue over how much of this was religious and how much was the brutal warfare of the time. Both sides agree he was one of the toughest opponents the British met in India.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Tipu Sultan invented the rocket.

Truth: Rockets had been used in China and elsewhere for centuries. What made Mysore rockets special was their iron casing, which gave them more power and range than European rockets of the time.

Try this

Make a balloon rocket. Thread a long string through a straw and tie it tight between two chairs. Blow up a balloon, hold it closed, tape it to the straw, then let go. The air rushing out backwards pushes the balloon forwards, the same idea behind every rocket.
