What are Kerala's snake boat races?

In Kerala, wooden boats longer than three school buses race across a lake with more than 100 people aboard, rowing to the beat of a song.

The short answer

Kerala's snake boat races are contests between huge, narrow wooden boats called chundan vallam. Each boat is more than 30 metres long, about as long as three school buses, and carries over 100 people. Most are rowers. A few steer with long oars at the back, and a few sing or beat time.

The races take place on Kerala's lakes and rivers, mostly in August and September around the harvest festival of Onam. The most famous, the Nehru Trophy Boat Race on Punnamada Lake near Alappuzha, draws huge crowds every year.

Surprising but true

The most famous race began by accident in 1952, when villagers held a surprise race for Prime Minister Nehru, who got so excited he jumped into the winning boat.

Built like a dragon

A snake boat is long and thin, with a high, curved stern that rises out of the water at the back. Traditionally, it is built from a hardwood called anjili, or wild jack, by master carpenters who follow old rules for its size and shape. Building one can take months.

Each boat belongs to a village, and people treat it with great respect. Traditionally the wood is rubbed with a mixture that includes fish oil, to keep it slippery and waterproof. Some villages say only certain people may step aboard, and only barefoot.

A snake boat sits very low in the water, its sides only a little above the surface. Rowers almost touch the lake as they paddle. The long, narrow shape cuts drag, the push of water against the hull, so a well-timed crew shoots forward surprisingly fast. On a calm lake the boats look like they are skimming rather than floating.

How an accident became a festival

In 1952, Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru visited Alappuzha. Local people arranged a race of snake boats in his honour. A boat called Nadubhagam Chundan won. Nehru was so thrilled that, despite security worries, he jumped aboard the winning boat.

Back in Delhi, he sent a silver trophy shaped like a snake boat, and it has been the prize ever since. Since 1955 the race has been held on Punnamada Lake. Today it takes place on the second Saturday of August, over a course of about 1,370 metres, and dozens of boats of different kinds take part.

Rowing to a song

On race day, rowers sit in pairs down the length of the boat, each with a short paddle. Singers chant vanchipattu, traditional boat songs. As the boats near the finish, the songs grow faster and louder, and the oars churn the water white.

It looks like a riot of splashes, but the timing is exact. If even a few rowers fall out of rhythm, the boat slows. Teams train for weeks before the season. In 2019 Kerala launched a Champions Boat League, a series of races across the state with the best teams competing, a little like a cricket league on water.

The temple boats of Aranmula

Not every race is about winning. On the Pampa River, the Aranmula boat race is linked to the Parthasarathy temple. Its boats, called palliyodam, are seen as sacred. They glide in pairs during the Onam season, carrying offerings, and crowds line the banks to watch more than to cheer a winner.

In Aranmula, villagers also prepare a huge feast called the vallasadya for the boat crews. It can include more than 60 dishes served on banana leaves, and hosts keep refilling plates whenever a rower asks.

Snake boats were once part of real naval battles between kingdoms in old Kerala, used to move warriors quickly through the backwaters. Today, races fill Kerala's waterways with music in the Onam season. And how a wooden boat with over 100 people inside stays afloat at all is a neat piece of physics explained in why ships float.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Snake boat races were invented to entertain tourists.

Truth: Snake boats go back centuries, to the war boats of old Kerala kingdoms. The modern races grew out of village and temple traditions long before tourists arrived.
