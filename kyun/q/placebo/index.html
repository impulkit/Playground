How can a sugar pill make you feel better?

A pill with no medicine in it can ease real pain, and scientists can now watch the brain doing it.

The short answer

A placebo is a pretend treatment, such as a sugar pill or a salt-water injection, with no medicine in it. Yet when people believe they're getting real medicine, many of them feel better. Their pain eases, their sickness settles or they feel less tired. This is the placebo effect.

It happens because your brain is always predicting what will happen next. If it expects relief, it can turn down pain signals and release its own calming chemicals. The word comes from Latin and means "I shall please".

The effect is real, but it has limits. A placebo can change how you feel. It cannot kill germs or repair a torn muscle.

Surprising but true

In 1978, scientists found that a drug which blocks the body's own painkillers also blocked the relief people got from a fake painkiller.

The body's own medicine cabinet

In 1978, three scientists in San Francisco studied people who had just had a wisdom tooth pulled. Some were given a fake painkiller and said they felt better. Then the researchers gave them naloxone, a drug that blocks the body's natural painkillers, called endorphins. The relief faded.

That was a big clue. The fake pill had nothing in it, but believing in it had made the brain release real chemicals. Later brain scans showed areas that handle pain becoming quieter when people expected relief.

Expectation also works the other way. If people are warned that a pill may cause headaches, some get headaches even when the pill is fake. This is called the nocebo effect, from a Latin word meaning "I shall harm".

A test in 1784

Over two hundred years ago, a doctor called Franz Mesmer became famous in Paris. He claimed an invisible force he called "animal magnetism" could cure illness. People fainted and shook in his sessions.

The French king set up a commission to check, and one member was the American scientist Benjamin Franklin. The team blindfolded people. Some were told they were being "magnetised" when they weren't, and they still reacted. Others were secretly magnetised and felt nothing. The commission decided the effects came from imagination. It was one of the first blind tests in history, and blind tests are still the backbone of medical science.

Can numbers lie?During the Second World War, planes came back full of bullet holes. A mathematician said to protect the parts that had no holes at all.

Why trials use fake pills

When scientists test a new medicine, they usually split volunteers into two groups. One group gets the real drug and the other gets an identical-looking placebo. Ideally nobody, not even the doctors, knows who got which until the end. This is called a double-blind trial.

If the medicine group does clearly better, the drug works. If both groups improve equally, the improvement probably came from expectation, from time passing or from people getting better on their own. That last part matters. Colds clear up, sore muscles heal and bad days pass. Some of what looks like a placebo effect is simply the body doing its normal job, which is why experts argue about how big the effect really is.

Strange rules of make-believe

Researchers have noticed some odd patterns. Fake injections often seem to work better than fake pills. Branded-looking packets can do better than plain ones. In one study at Harvard in 2010, patients with a stomach condition were openly told their pills were placebos, and many still felt better.

None of this means you should swap medicine for sugar. Placebos don't shrink tumours or cure infections, and anyone who feels unwell should talk to a parent or a doctor. What placebos do show is how tightly the mind and body are wired together.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The placebo effect means the person was never really ill, or that it's all in their head.

Truth: Placebos can cause real changes in the brain, such as the release of the body's own painkillers. They mostly change how symptoms feel, though, and they can't cure an infection or heal a broken bone.

Try this

Try a fair test the way medical researchers do. Give two friends the same plain biscuit, but tell one it's a "special extra-crunchy" kind. Ask each to score the crunch out of ten without seeing each other's answers. Then talk about why a fair trial keeps people from knowing which version they got.
