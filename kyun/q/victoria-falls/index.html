What are Victoria Falls?

On the border of Zambia and Zimbabwe, a river nearly 1.7 km wide drops into a narrow crack in the ground, throwing up spray you can see from far away.

The short answer

Victoria Falls is a huge waterfall on the Zambezi River in southern Africa, on the border between Zambia and Zimbabwe. The river, almost 1.7 km wide at that point, tumbles over a cliff into a deep, narrow gorge. In some places it falls about 108 m, roughly twice the height of the main part of Niagara Falls.

Because the river is so wide and the drop so deep, Victoria Falls is often described as the largest curtain of falling water on Earth. In the rainy months, the spray rises hundreds of metres into the air and can be seen from many kilometres away. That is why the local name is Mosi-oa-Tunya, the smoke that thunders.

Surprising but true

On some nights around the full moon, the spray at Victoria Falls makes a pale rainbow by moonlight, called a moonbow.

A crack in the rock

The falls drop into a crack. About 180 million years ago, lava flooded this part of Africa and cooled into hard basalt. As it cooled, it split along long cracks, which later filled with softer material.

When the Zambezi began flowing across the basalt, it found these weak lines and scoured them out. The water falls into one crack, then escapes through a narrow gap at one end. Over a very long time, the river has eaten backwards into new cracks, abandoning old ones. The zigzag gorges below the falls are former waterfall sites, a record of how the waterfall has moved upstream. Geologists count several older gorges, and at one end of today's falls the river is already cutting into the next weak line.

Above the edge, the Zambezi is wide and dotted with islands. One of them, Livingstone Island, sits right on the lip of the falls.

Rain, flood and drought

The amount of water changes hugely through the year. After the rainy season, around March and April, more than 500 million litres can pour over the edge every minute. Then the spray is so thick that visitors get soaked, and sometimes you can hardly see the falls at all.

By October and November, near the end of the dry season, the flow is much lower. Parts of the Zambian side can shrink to bare rock. In very dry years, such as 2019, photos of the thin falls made news around the world.

Rainbows and a rainforest

All that spray creates its own weather. On sunny days, rainbows hang in the gorge, sometimes double ones. Around the full moon, the spray can even make a moonbow, a faint rainbow by moonlight.

The constant drizzle feeds a small strip of rainforest on the Zimbabwe side, with ferns, palms and fig trees, in a region that is otherwise dry savanna. Monkeys, baboons and birds live in it. Just upstream, hippos and crocodiles live in the river, and elephants sometimes wander along the banks.

Explorers, a bridge and brave swimmers

The Tonga, Lozi and other peoples living nearby knew the falls for centuries before outsiders arrived. In November 1855, the Scottish missionary David Livingstone became the first European known to see them. He named them after Britain's Queen Victoria.

In 1905, a steel arch bridge was finished across the gorge just below the falls, carrying a railway over the Zambezi. It still links Zambia and Zimbabwe, and people now bungee jump from it.

On the Zambian side, at the very lip of the falls, is a rock pool called the Devil's Pool. In the low-water months, guides take visitors to swim in it, right at the edge of the drop. A natural rock wall under the water stops them from going over. Few swimming pools anywhere have a view like it, straight down a wall of falling water into the gorge.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Victoria Falls is the tallest waterfall in the world.

Truth: Angel Falls in Venezuela drops about 979 m, roughly nine times as far. Victoria Falls is famous because it is both very wide and high, which makes it one of the largest single sheets of falling water anywhere.

Try this

On a sunny day, stand with your back to the sun and spray a fine mist from a garden hose or a spray bottle in front of you (with permission, outdoors). Move until a rainbow appears in the mist. Victoria Falls makes rainbows the same way, using its own spray.
