How big was a dinosaur egg?

Even the biggest dinosaurs laid eggs smaller than a football.

The short answer

Bigger eggs need thicker shells, and a baby inside must breathe through the shell. So eggs can't grow too big.

Giant sauropod mothers laid many eggs in pits, covered them with soil and plants, and let the warm ground do the work. The eggs of India's titanosaurs were roughly round and about 15–17 cm across, a little bigger than an ostrich egg but smaller than a football.

The babies hatched small and grew very fast. A hatchling you could carry in your arms might one day grow longer than a cricket pitch.

Surprising but true

A baby from an egg smaller than a football could grow longer than a cricket pitch.

Why eggs stay small

An egg is a living package. The baby inside needs oxygen and must get rid of carbon dioxide, so the shell is full of tiny holes called pores. A huge egg would need a very thick shell to avoid cracking. But a thick shell lets less air through, and a baby might struggle to break out of it.

So even the biggest animals that ever walked on land laid eggs you could hold in two hands. An ostrich egg, the biggest laid today, weighs about as much as two dozen hen's eggs. The largest titanosaur eggs held even more.

A nursery in the Narmada valley

India has some of the richest dinosaur nesting grounds in the world. Around Dhar in Madhya Pradesh, scientists recorded 92 nests holding 256 titanosaur eggs, described in 2023. The nests were close together, like a nesting colony of seabirds.

The eggs were laid in shallow pits and buried, much as sea turtles bury theirs on beaches today. Shell structure suggests the nests were covered, warmed by the soil or rotting plants. One egg from Dhar, reported in 2022, had a second eggshell inside it, an "egg within an egg". This happens in birds too, and it was the first time it had been found in a dinosaur.

A snake in the nest

At Dholi Dungri in Gujarat, scientists made one of the most dramatic finds in the history of Indian fossils. A snake, about 3.5 m long, was preserved coiled around a crushed titanosaur egg, right next to a hatchling. They named it Sanajeh indicus, which means "ancient gape from India".

The snake could not open its jaws very wide, so it probably could not swallow eggs whole. It may have waited for babies to hatch. A sudden mudflow seems to have buried the whole scene about 67 million years ago. Snakes still raid nests today, and one Indian snake, the king cobra, even builds a nest of its own.

Eggs of all shapes

Not all dinosaur eggs were round. The bird-like dinosaurs laid long, narrow eggs, set in neat circles with the pointed ends facing the middle. Giant relatives of Oviraptor laid the longest dinosaur eggs known, about 45 cm long.

In 2020, scientists found that some dinosaurs, such as Protoceratops and an early long-neck called Mussaurus, laid soft, leathery eggs, a bit like a turtle's. The very first dinosaurs may have laid soft eggs too, and hard shells may have evolved more than once. That could explain why eggs of some dinosaurs are almost never found: soft shells rarely fossilise.

From egg to giant

The real wonder is how much a baby sauropod grew. From an egg smaller than a football, it might grow to more than 20 m long and many tonnes. Read about how they grew up in dinosaur babies, and why they grew such long necks.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The biggest dinosaurs laid the biggest eggs.

Truth: The giant long-necked sauropods laid eggs only about 15–20 cm across. The longest dinosaur eggs known came from large bird-like dinosaurs related to Oviraptor, which were much smaller animals.

Try this

Gently squeeze a raw egg end to end in your palm over a sink (with an adult's permission). It is surprisingly hard to break, because the curved shell spreads the force. That shape lets a thin shell carry the weight of a sitting bird, or a layer of soil over a dinosaur nest.
