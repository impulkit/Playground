Who invented punctuation?

For centuries, Greek and Roman books had no spaces, no full stops and no commas, just one long river of capital letters.

The short answer

Nobody invented punctuation all at once. It was built up over about 2,000 years by many people: librarians in Alexandria, monks in Ireland, scholars at the court of Charlemagne and printers in Venice. Each added marks to solve a problem, and the useful ones stuck.

The earliest Greek and Roman texts were written with no spaces and no punctuation at all, a style called scriptio continua. Reading was mostly done aloud, so readers worked out pauses themselves. As more people started reading silently, and in languages that were not their own, they needed help, and dots, spaces and squiggles crept onto the page.

Surprising but true

Spaces between words were made popular by Irish and British monks around 1,300 years ago, because Latin was a foreign language to them and run-together words were hard to read.

The librarian's dots

Around 200 BCE, Aristophanes of Byzantium, who ran the great library in Alexandria, suggested a system of dots for reading aloud. A dot high up on the line meant a long pause. A dot in the middle meant a short pause, and a low dot a medium one. You might call it the great-grandparent of the full stop, colon and comma.

Even our names for marks come from Greek. Komma meant "a piece cut off", kolon meant "a limb" and periodos meant "going round". The library itself was one of the great libraries of the ancient world.

What happened to the Library of Alexandria?The ancient world's most famous library did not vanish in a single dramatic fire. It faded away over hundreds of years.

Monks discover the space bar

For a long time, Romans thought dots were for beginners. Then, from about the 7th century, monks in Ireland and Britain began copying Latin religious books. Latin was a foreign language to them, so run-together words were a real puzzle. They started leaving spaces between words.

The idea was so useful that it spread across Europe. Scribes at the court of Charlemagne, around 800 CE, also developed a clear new handwriting with small letters, which became the ancestor of the lowercase alphabet you are reading now.

Marks that showed your voice

Medieval monks sang and chanted their prayers, so many marks were about the voice. A mark called the punctus interrogativus, a dot with a wavy line above, showed that the voice should rise at a question. Over centuries it became the question mark.

The exclamation mark has a similar story. It began as a mark of admiration or wonder, sometimes called the "note of admiration". The popular story that it came from the Latin "io", meaning joy, is probably a legend.

Printers fix the rules

When the printing press arrived in the 1450s, punctuation began to settle down. In Venice in the 1490s, the printer Aldus Manutius published books using a semicolon, among the first in print. His family also helped make the comma look the way it does today.

Printers wanted every copy to look the same, so they made rules, and schools followed them. Spanish, in 1754, made a different choice: upside-down question marks and exclamation marks at the start of sentences, so readers know early which tone to use. ¿Qué tal?

Punctuation in India

Indian scripts have their own marks. In Devanagari, used for Hindi, Marathi and Sanskrit, the full stop is a straight vertical line, the purna viram or danda. Ancient Sanskrit verses used a single line to end half a verse and a double line to end a full one.

Urdu, written right to left, uses a question mark facing the other way. Tamil and Bengali texts have adopted many European marks, such as commas and question marks, since the 1800s.

New marks keep coming

In 1962, an American advertising man, Martin Speckter, invented the interrobang, a question mark and exclamation mark merged into one, for sentences like "You did what?!". It never caught on, though some typewriters included it.

Today, people punctuate messages with smileys and hearts. Some linguists think emojis work a bit like the old voice marks of medieval monks, telling the reader how a sentence should sound.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The question mark came from the Latin word "quaestio", with a small q written over an o.

Truth: It is a popular story, but there's no real evidence for it. Historians think the question mark grew out of a medieval mark called the punctus interrogativus, a dot with a squiggle above it that showed a rising voice.
