Where did Halloween come from?

The first jack-o'-lanterns were not pumpkins. In Ireland and Scotland, people carved scary faces into turnips and lit them from inside.

The short answer

Halloween is celebrated on 31 October, mainly in the USA, Canada, the UK and Ireland, and more and more in other countries, including India. Children dress up in costumes, carve pumpkins and go from house to house saying "trick or treat" to collect sweets.

Its roots go back a long way. Many historians link it to Samhain, an ancient festival of the Celtic peoples of Ireland, Scotland and nearby lands. Later, it mixed with the Christian feast of All Saints. Irish and Scottish immigrants carried the customs to America, where Halloween grew into the festival known today.

Surprising but true

Researchers who searched decades of news reports found no confirmed case of a stranger poisoning children's Halloween sweets at random, despite years of warnings.

Samhain and the dark half of the year

For the Celts, Samhain, held around 1 November, marked the end of the harvest and the start of winter, the dark half of the year. As the seasons turned, nights grew long and cold.

According to old stories, Samhain was a time when the boundary between the living and the dead grew thin. People lit bonfires, and some may have worn disguises so wandering spirits would not recognise them. Because few written records survive from those times, historians are careful about how much they claim.

All Hallows' Eve

In the 700s and 800s, the Christian church in western Europe settled on 1 November as All Saints' Day, also called All Hallows. "Hallow" is an old word for a saint or holy person. The evening before became All Hallows' Eve, shortened over time to Halloween.

All Souls' Day, on 2 November, was set aside for praying for the dead. In parts of Britain, poor people and children went "souling", going door to door offering prayers in return for small cakes. Some historians see this as an ancestor of trick-or-treating.

Turnips become pumpkins

In Ireland and Scotland, people carved faces into turnips and other root vegetables and put a coal or candle inside. A folk tale told of "Stingy Jack", a man who tricked the devil and was doomed to wander with only a glowing coal in a turnip.

In the 1800s, large numbers of Irish people came to the USA, especially after the famine of the 1840s. They found pumpkins, which grow well in North America and are much easier to carve. The pumpkin jack-o'-lantern was born.

Trick or treat

The phrase "trick or treat" became common in North America in the 1930s. After the Second World War, as sweets became cheaper and suburbs spread, trick-or-treating became a huge event for American children. Today Americans spend billions of dollars on Halloween sweets, costumes and decorations each year.

Halloween also sits in a season of festivals about the dead around the world. In Mexico, the Day of the Dead, on 1 and 2 November, honours family members who have died with marigolds and photographs. It's a separate festival, with its own roots, but it falls at the same time of year.

Why being scared can be fun

Halloween is full of ghosts, skeletons, witches and bats, though real bats are harmless insect-eaters. Many people enjoy a little fright when they know they are safe. Scientists say a short scare makes the heart beat faster, then floods us with relief, which can feel exciting.

In Indian cities, Halloween parties have become popular in schools and malls. India also has its own times for remembering ancestors, such as Pitru Paksha, a fortnight in which many Hindu families offer food and prayers to those who have died.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Strangers often poison Halloween sweets.

Truth: Sociologist Joel Best studied reports going back to 1958 and found no proven case of a stranger randomly poisoning trick-or-treaters. It's still sensible for an adult to check sweets, mainly for allergies and choking hazards.

Try this

Make a safe jack-o'-lantern without a knife. Draw a spooky or funny face on an orange or a small pumpkin with a marker. Put a battery tea-light beside it in a dark room and see how shadows change the face when you move the light.
