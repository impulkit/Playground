How does encryption keep secrets?

Every time you see a little padlock next to a web address, your messages are being scrambled into nonsense that would take ordinary computers far longer than the age of the universe to crack.

The short answer

Encryption turns a readable message into scrambled nonsense, so only someone with the right key can turn it back. The scrambling follows a set of maths rules. The key is a secret number that tells the rules exactly how to scramble and unscramble.

Your phone uses encryption all the time: when you send a chat message, pay with a phone, or visit a website with a padlock in the address bar. Anyone who intercepts the data along the way sees only gibberish. Without the key, breaking modern encryption by guessing would take today's computers far longer than the universe has existed.

Surprising but true

Some of today's encryption relies on a simple fact about numbers. Multiplying two huge prime numbers is easy, but working backwards to find them can take longer than a lifetime.

Shifting letters

People have hidden messages for thousands of years. Julius Caesar is said to have used a simple cipher: move every letter three places along the alphabet, so A becomes D and B becomes E. "CAT" becomes "FDW".

The weakness is that there are only 25 possible shifts. An enemy can try them all in minutes. Even trickier ciphers can be cracked by counting letters, since E is the most common letter in English. The Arab scholar al-Kindi described this trick in the 800s, and it has cracked many of history's secret codes since.

The Enigma machine

In the Second World War, the German military used Enigma, a typewriter-like machine with spinning wheels that changed the code with every key pressed. The settings changed every day, giving an astronomical number of combinations.

Polish mathematicians, led by Marian Rejewski, first broke Enigma in 1932. They shared their work with Britain just before the war. At Bletchley Park, Alan Turing and thousands of others built machines to speed up the search. Their work helped the Allies read many German messages.

What are prime numbers?Every time you pay with a phone or log in to a website, your secrets are protected by numbers that cannot be split into smaller whole numbers.

Keys you can give away

Old ciphers had one big problem. Both people needed the same secret key, and handing it over safely was hard. In 1976, Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman published a way around it, and soon after, a method called RSA put the idea to work.

It works like an open padlock. You hand out copies of your padlock to anyone. They can snap it shut on a box with a message inside. But only you have the key that opens it. In computers, the "padlock" is a public key and the "key" is a private one, linked by maths involving huge prime numbers.

How big is big?

Modern methods such as AES use keys with 128 or 256 binary digits. A 128-bit key has about 340 followed by 36 zeros possible values. A computer testing a billion billion keys every second would need over ten trillion years to try them all. That is hundreds of times the age of the universe.

So attackers rarely try. Instead they steal passwords, trick people, or look for mistakes in software.

Locked for whom?

Many messaging apps now use end-to-end encryption, which means even the company running the app cannot read your messages. Supporters say this protects ordinary people, journalists and businesses. Some police and governments worry it also hides criminals, and want a way to read messages with a court's permission. Security experts warn that any secret back door could be found by others too. The debate continues around the world.

Looking ahead, powerful quantum computers might one day break some of today's public-key methods. In 2024 the US standards agency NIST published new methods designed to resist them.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Encrypted means impossible to read, for ever.

Truth: Strong encryption is extremely hard to break, but weak keys, stolen passwords or mistakes in software can let people in. Codes also age, as computers get faster, so experts keep replacing old methods with stronger ones.

Try this

Make a cipher wheel. Cut two paper circles, one a little smaller than the other, and write the alphabet around the edge of each. Pin them together through the centre with a split pin, turn the inner wheel to choose a shift, and send a secret message to a friend who knows the shift.
