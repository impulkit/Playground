What are some old Indian maths shortcuts?

To square 65 in your head, multiply 6 by 7 to get 42, then stick 25 on the end. The answer is 4,225.

The short answer

India has a long tradition of clever arithmetic. Some shortcuts that children learn today are quick tricks for multiplying and squaring numbers, often taught as "Vedic maths". Others come from ancient Indian mathematicians who found brilliant ways to handle square roots, zero and huge numbers.

A famous trick is squaring any number that ends in 5. Take the digits before the 5, multiply by the next number up, then put 25 on the end. So 35 × 35 is 3 × 4 = 12, then 25, giving 1,225. Tricks like this feel like magic, but each one has a solid reason behind it.

Surprising but true

Around 2,500 years ago, the Baudhayana Sulba Sutra gave a value for the square root of 2 that is correct to five decimal places.

Why the 5 trick works

Take a number ending in 5, like 65. It's 60 + 5. When you square it, you get 60 × 60, plus 2 × 60 × 5, plus 5 × 5. That works out to 3,600 + 600 + 25. The first two parts together are always 60 × 70, which is 4,200. So the answer is 6 × 7 hundreds, plus 25.

This is algebra hiding inside a party trick. Once you see why it works, you can invent your own.

Near a hundred

Another trick helps with numbers close to 100. Say you want 97 × 96. Note how far each is below 100: 3 and 4. Take one of those away from the other number: 97 − 4 = 93. That gives the first two digits. Then multiply the gaps: 3 × 4 = 12. Answer: 9,312.

This method is called nikhilam in the book Vedic Mathematics, written by Bharati Krishna Tirtha and published in 1965. The book became popular in schools. But historians point out that its sutras aren't in the Vedas. It is a modern book with an old-sounding name. Still, the Vedic maths tricks are a quick, fun way to practise sums in your head.

Real ancient genius

Much older Indian maths is just as clever. The Sulba Sutras, written between about 800 and 500 BCE, were manuals for building fire altars of exact shapes. To get the shapes right, priests needed precise geometry.

Baudhayana's Sulba Sutra gives a way to find the square root of 2: 1 + 1/3 + 1/(3 × 4) − 1/(3 × 4 × 34). Work it out and you get 1.4142156. The true value starts 1.4142135. That's correct to five decimal places, over 2,000 years before calculators.

The rules of zero

In 628 CE, the mathematician Brahmagupta wrote down rules for working with zero and negative numbers. He explained that a number minus itself is zero, and that a debt minus zero is a debt. These rules let people use zero as a real number, not just an empty space.

Indian numerals, including zero, later spread through the Arab world to Europe. The digits 0 to 9 you write today descend from them.

Maths in verse

In 1150 CE, Bhaskara II wrote Lilavati, a maths book full of puzzles in poetry. One asks about bees: one-fifth of a swarm goes to a kadamba flower, one-third to another flower, three times their difference to a third flower, and one bee is left flying about. How many bees are there? The answer is 15.

Legend says Bhaskara named the book after his daughter, Lilavati. Whether that's true or not, it's still read by students. And India kept producing mental maths stars, like Shakuntala Devi, who in 1980 multiplied two 13-digit numbers in her head in 28 seconds. The record-breaking life of Shakuntala Devi began with card tricks she learned as a small child from her father, a circus performer.

Myth or fact?

Myth: All the tricks in "Vedic Mathematics" come straight from the ancient Vedas.

Truth: The book was written in the 20th century by Bharati Krishna Tirtha. Historians have not found its 16 sutras in the Vedas. The tricks work and are fun, but ancient Indian maths is far bigger and older than this one book.

Try this

Practise the "multiply by 11" trick. For 43 × 11, add the two digits (4 + 3 = 7) and put the answer in the middle: 473. Try 52, 36 and 71. Then try 75 × 11, where the digits add up to 12. Work out what to do with the extra 1.
