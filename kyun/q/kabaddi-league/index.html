How did the Pro Kabaddi League start?

In 2014, a village game played on mud and chanted under the breath was moved onto bright blue mats under stadium lights, and crores of people tuned in.

The short answer

The Pro Kabaddi League began on 26 July 2014 in Mumbai. A company called Mashal Sports, led by the sports broadcaster Charu Sharma and backed by the businessman Anand Mahindra, wanted to do for kabaddi what the IPL had done for cricket. They created city teams, bought TV time, hired commentators and moved the game indoors onto colourful mats.

It worked. Millions of families who had played kabaddi as children, or had never watched it at all, started following teams with names like Bengal Warriors, Telugu Titans and Patna Pirates. Kabaddi became one of the most watched sports on Indian television.

Surprising but true

Some of the league's biggest stars come from Iran, a country that knocked India out in the semi-finals and went on to win men's kabaddi gold at the 2018 Asian Games.

A game everyone already knew

Kabaddi has been played in Indian villages for a very long time. It needs no ball, bat or net, only a patch of flat ground and two teams. A raider crosses into the other half, tries to tag defenders and gets back, all while chanting "kabaddi, kabaddi" so everyone can hear they haven't taken a breath. The defenders try to stop the raider from returning.

India dominated kabaddi at the Asian Games for decades, winning every men's gold from 1990 until 2018, when Iran won. India took the title back in 2023. But kabaddi had rarely been on TV, and top players often struggled to earn a living from the sport. Many worked for the railways, the police, the army or banks, which hired sportspeople and gave them time to train.

Made for television

The organisers changed the look of the game. Muddy courts became blue and orange mats. Players got numbered shirts and bright team kits. Matches were shortened to 40 minutes, with two halves of 20, so they fit neatly into an evening TV slot.

They also tweaked the rules to keep games tense. If a team fails to score on two raids in a row, the next is a "do-or-die" raid: score or lose a point. When a defence is down to three or fewer players and still stops the raider, it is a super tackle worth two points. Knock out the whole opposing team and you earn an "all out" bonus.

Stars on the mat

The league turned players into stars. Pardeep Narwal, nicknamed the "Dubki King" for ducking under linked arms, became the league's top raider. Defenders like Fazel Atrachali from Iran became famous for crushing tackles. Players from Iran, South Korea, Bangladesh and Kenya have all played in the league.

Raiders need fast feet and quick decisions. Defenders need strong grip, balance and powerful muscles in the legs and back. Teams now have fitness trainers, video analysts and physiotherapists, just as football and cricket clubs do.

Champions so far

The first season, in 2014, had eight teams. Jaipur Pink Panthers won the trophy. The league grew to twelve teams in 2017, and Patna Pirates won three titles in a row in 2016 and 2017. In 2025, Season 12 ended with Dabang Delhi beating Puneri Paltan 31 to 28 in a close final, for their second title.

A wider ripple

The success of Pro Kabaddi encouraged other traditional sports. Ultimate Kho Kho, a professional league for kho-kho, started in 2022. Kabaddi itself has spread too. It is played at the Asian Games, and countries from Iran to Kenya now send players to India to learn and compete.

For many village players, the league opened a door that didn't exist before. A good raider from a small town in Haryana or Tamil Nadu can now earn a professional salary, play in big indoor arenas and see his face on posters across the country.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Kabaddi is just pushing and wrestling, with no real tactics.

Truth: Pro teams plan raids, defensive positions and timing as carefully as cricket captains plan field placings. Coaches study each opponent's favourite moves, and the bonus line, super tackle and do-or-die raid rules all change how a team plays.

Try this

Play a gentle version of kabaddi with friends on grass or a soft floor. Mark a centre line. One player crosses the line and tries to tag someone while saying "kabaddi, kabaddi" in one breath, then gets back home. Defenders hold hands in a chain to block, with no grabbing or pushing. Ask an adult to referee.
