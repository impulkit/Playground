Who was Benjamin Franklin?

Benjamin Franklin left school at 10, yet he proved lightning is electricity, invented bifocal glasses and helped write the rules of a new country.

The short answer

Benjamin Franklin was an American printer, writer, scientist, inventor and statesman who lived from 1706 to 1790. He is one of the Founding Fathers of the United States, the group of leaders who broke away from Britain and set up the new country.

He showed that lightning is a form of electricity, and invented the lightning rod to protect buildings. He also invented bifocal glasses and a better wood stove, started a public library and a fire company, and helped write and sign the Declaration of Independence and the US Constitution. His face is on the $100 note.

Surprising but true

Franklin refused to patent his inventions, including the lightning rod and the Franklin stove. He wrote that people should share inventions freely, as he had benefited from the inventions of others.

The printer's apprentice

Franklin was born in Boston, the 15th of 17 children of a soap and candle maker. His family could afford only two years of school. At 12, he became an apprentice to his older brother, a printer. He read every book he could borrow.

At 17, he ran away to Philadelphia with almost no money. He worked hard, set up his own print shop and became rich. His yearly almanac, Poor Richard's Almanack, was full of weather predictions, jokes and sayings like "Early to bed and early to rise, makes a man healthy, wealthy, and wise". It sold thousands of copies a year.

Catching lightning

In the 1740s, scientists in Europe and America were excited about static electricity, making sparks with glass tubes and jars. Franklin noticed that sparks looked and crackled like tiny lightning. He wondered whether lightning was the same thing.

In June 1752, according to his own account, he flew a silk kite in a thunderstorm in Philadelphia, with a metal key tied to the string. When he moved his knuckle near the key, a spark jumped. He took it as proof. Some historians think the details were tidied up later, but scientists in France had already proved the same idea a month earlier, using one of his designs.

He then invented the lightning rod, a pointed metal rod on a roof connected by a wire to the ground. It gives lightning a safe path, protecting the house. Lightning rods are still used on buildings, including temples and towers in India.

A head full of ideas

Franklin's mind never stopped. As he aged and needed two pairs of glasses, one for reading and one for distance, he cut the lenses in half and put them into one frame. These were bifocals. He designed the Franklin stove, which heated rooms better and used less wood. He also mapped the Gulf Stream, a warm ocean current in the Atlantic.

He started one of the first lending libraries in America, a volunteer fire company and a hospital in Philadelphia. He also helped found what became the University of Pennsylvania.

Building a new country

Franklin began as a loyal British subject but came to believe the colonies should be free. In 1776, he helped Thomas Jefferson edit the Declaration of Independence and signed it. During the American Revolution, he went to France and persuaded the French king to send money, soldiers and ships. Without that help, the Americans might have lost.

In 1787, at 81, he was the oldest delegate at the convention that wrote the US Constitution.

A changing view of slavery

For much of his life, Franklin owned enslaved people and printed advertisements for slave sales in his newspaper. Over time, his views changed. In his last years he became president of the Pennsylvania Abolition Society, and in 1790 he asked Congress to end slavery. Congress refused. He died two months later, aged 84. About 20,000 people attended his funeral, the largest Philadelphia had seen.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Franklin's kite was struck by lightning.

Truth: If it had been, he would almost certainly have died. Franklin said the kite picked up electric charge from the storm cloud, enough to make a small spark, but not a full lightning bolt.

Try this

Rub a balloon on a woollen jumper or your hair, then hold it near small bits of torn paper. Watch them jump up. This is static electricity, the same kind of charge Franklin was studying when he decided lightning was a giant spark.
