How does a bone turn into stone?

It takes the right accident, lots of mud, and millions of years.

The short answer

Most dead animals rot or get eaten. A fossil forms only when something gets buried fast, in mud, sand or volcanic ash.

Water seeping through the sediment carries minerals. They fill the tiny holes in the bone and harden into stone.

Much later, erosion uncovers it, and someone has to be lucky enough to spot it. The whole process usually takes millions of years, though some fossils form much faster in the right conditions.

Surprising but true

Only a tiny fraction of all animals that ever lived became fossils. Every fossil is a lucky survivor.

Why most things never become fossils

When an animal dies, scavengers, bacteria, sun and rain usually destroy it within weeks. Hard parts like bones, teeth and shells last longest, so they're what we usually find. Soft animals, like worms and jellyfish, almost never fossilise.

Where an animal lived matters too. Seas, lakes and river floods lay down mud again and again, burying things quickly. Mountains and forests do the opposite: they wear away. That is why sea creatures are the most common fossils, and why we know so little about animals that lived on hills.

Turning to stone

If a body is quickly covered by sediment, it's protected. Over time, groundwater carries dissolved minerals into the tiny spaces in bone and leaves them behind, like filling a sponge with stone. This is called permineralisation.

Sometimes the original material dissolves completely and is replaced, grain by grain, by a different mineral. In other cases the body dissolves away and leaves a hollow in the rock, called a mould. If the hollow later fills with minerals, it makes a natural cast. Layers of mud pressed down become rock themselves, as explained in types of rock.

Wood turned to stone

Trees can fossilise too. At Petrified Forest National Park in Arizona, whole logs over 200 million years old have turned to colourful quartz. India has its own stone forests. The Akal Wood Fossil Park near Jaisalmer has fossil tree trunks from the Jurassic, when the area was near a coast. The Mandla Plant Fossils National Park in Madhya Pradesh holds fossil plants from around the end of the age of dinosaurs.

Other kinds of fossils

Footprints pressed into mud, burrows, eggs and even poo can become fossils. These are called trace fossils, because they show what animals did rather than what they looked like. Insects trapped in tree resin become amber fossils.

Some fossils hardly change at all. Woolly mammoths have been found frozen in the ground in Siberia, with skin and hair. At La Brea in Los Angeles, animals sank into sticky tar that soaked into their bones and preserved them. Very fine mud or ash can even keep traces of feathers and colour.

Finding them

Fossils are found where old rock layers are exposed: cliffs, riverbanks, quarries and deserts. Deserts are especially good, because there are few plants to hide the rock. To know their age, scientists read the rock layers around them.

Finding a fossil is only the start. Diggers wrap fragile bones in plaster jackets, like a cast on a broken arm, before carrying them to a lab. There, people clean away the rock with tiny drills and brushes, sometimes for months. A big skeleton can take years to prepare. Then scientists can finally study it, and museums may make lightweight casts to put on display while the real bones are kept safe in storage.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A dinosaur fossil in a museum is the animal's real bone.

Truth: Most fossils are rock. Minerals have filled or replaced the original bone, keeping its shape. Many skeletons on display are also casts, copies made from moulds of the real fossils.

Try this

Fill a small sponge with very salty water and leave it on a plate in a sunny window for a week. As the water dries, salt crystals form inside the holes. Fossilisation is a little like this, with minerals from groundwater filling the tiny spaces in bone.
