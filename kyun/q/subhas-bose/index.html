Who was Subhas Chandra Bose?

He escaped house arrest in disguise, crossed half the world by submarine, and raised an army to free India.

The short answer

Subhas Chandra Bose was one of the leaders of India's fight for freedom from British rule. Millions of Indians still call him Netaji, "respected leader". He was brave, impatient and sure that India would only be free if it fought hard for it.

Bose believed the British would not leave just because Indians protested peacefully. During the Second World War he went abroad, looked for help from Britain's enemies, and led an army of Indian soldiers called the Indian National Army. His army did not win, but its story shook the British and inspired Indians everywhere.

Surprising but true

In 1943 Bose travelled from Germany to Japanese-held Asia by submarine, swapping from a German submarine to a Japanese one in the middle of the Indian Ocean.

A top student who said no

Bose was born in Cuttack in 1897, the ninth of fourteen children of a well-known lawyer. He was a brilliant student, and as a teenager he was deeply moved by the teachings of Swami Vivekananda. At college in Calcutta he was expelled for a time after a clash with a British teacher. In 1920, in England, he passed the very hard Indian Civil Service exam, coming fourth. That job meant a safe life working for the British government.

Within a year he resigned. He wrote that he could not serve a government he wanted to remove. He came home and joined the freedom struggle, working with the leader C.R. Das in Calcutta. He was jailed again and again, eleven times in all.

The split with Gandhi

Bose was elected president of the Indian National Congress in 1938 and again in 1939. He respected Gandhi but disagreed with him. Gandhi believed in non-violence and patience. Bose wanted a quicker, tougher push, and was ready to use force.

The quarrel grew, and Bose resigned in 1939. He started his own party, the Forward Bloc. The British, worried about him, put him under house arrest in Calcutta.

A great escape

In January 1941, Bose slipped past the police guarding his house, disguised as an insurance agent. He crossed into Afghanistan, then travelled through the Soviet Union to Germany.

In 1943 he made an even stranger trip. A German submarine carried him south around Africa. Near Madagascar, he climbed into a rubber boat in rough seas and crossed to a Japanese submarine waiting nearby.

Azad Hind

In Singapore, Bose took charge of the Indian National Army, made up largely of Indian soldiers captured by Japan. In October 1943 he set up a Provisional Government of Free India. It even had a women's regiment, named after Rani Lakshmibai and led by Captain Lakshmi Swaminathan.

His army fought alongside Japan in Burma and northeast India in 1944, but it was forced back. His famous call was "Give me blood, and I will give you freedom."

Bose's choice to work with Nazi Germany and Imperial Japan is still debated. Supporters say he was using his enemy's enemy. Critics point out that both governments did terrible things in the war.

After the war

Japan surrendered in August 1945, and Bose is believed to have died soon after when his plane crashed in Taipei. Then the British put three INA officers on trial at the Red Fort in Delhi. The trials caused huge protests across India, and even some Indian sailors in the British navy rose up. Many historians think this helped convince Britain it could no longer rely on Indian soldiers.

India became free in 1947. Bose's birthday, 23 January, is now marked as Parakram Diwas, "Day of Courage". Ross Island in the Andaman Islands, where he stayed in December 1943 when he raised the Indian flag at Port Blair, now carries his name.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Everyone agrees on what happened to Bose in 1945.

Truth: Most evidence points to a plane crash in Taipei on 18 August 1945. One official inquiry, in 2005, disagreed, and the mystery still fills books and films.
