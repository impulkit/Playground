What are the stories of young Krishna?

In one old story, a mother peers into her toddler's mouth to check for mud, and sees the whole universe spinning inside.

The short answer

For hundreds of millions of Hindus, Krishna is a god, believed to be a form of Vishnu who came to Earth. The stories of his childhood are some of the best loved in India. They are told mainly in the Bhagavata Purana, a long Sanskrit text, and also in the Harivamsa and the Vishnu Purana.

In these stories, young Krishna is a cheeky cowherd boy. He steals butter, plays the flute, teases the village girls and outwits demons sent to kill him. Grandparents tell the tales at bedtime, temples act them out, and painters, poets and dancers have retold them for well over a thousand years.

Surprising but true

In many paintings Krishna is blue, but old Sanskrit texts often call him "shyama", which means dark, like a rain cloud.

A baby smuggled through a storm

The story begins with a frightening prophecy. King Kamsa of Mathura hears that the eighth child of his sister Devaki will kill him. So he locks Devaki and her husband Vasudeva in prison.

When Krishna, the eighth child, is born at midnight, the guards fall asleep and the doors swing open. Vasudeva carries the newborn in a basket across the flooded Yamuna river. In many tellings, a great serpent spreads its hoods over the baby like an umbrella. Krishna is left with the cowherd Nanda and his wife Yashoda in Gokul, who raise him as their son. His birthday is celebrated every year as Janmashtami, with midnight prayers and cradles decorated with flowers.

The butter thief

In Gokul, Krishna is a handful. He and his friends sneak into homes to steal butter and curd hung from the ceiling in pots. When the pots are too high, the boys climb on each other's shoulders. In Maharashtra today, teams build human towers to break a pot of curd during the dahi handi festival, copying this tale.

One day, Yashoda hears he has eaten mud. She asks him to open his mouth. Inside, she sees the sun, the moon, the stars and the whole universe. Then she forgets what she saw, and he is just her little boy again. Many people see this as the heart of the stories: a mighty god who chooses to be a child.

What are Cheriyal scrolls?Long before cinema reached Telangana's villages, storytellers unrolled painted scrolls as long as four autorickshaws and sang the pictures to life at night.

Snakes, storms and a lifted hill

Young Krishna also faces danger. The demoness Putana tries to poison him and fails. The many-headed serpent Kaliya poisons the river, so Krishna jumps in and dances on its heads until it leaves.

The most famous tale is about Govardhan hill. When the villagers stop offering prayers to Indra, the rain god, he sends a seven-day storm. Krishna lifts the whole hill on one finger, and the village shelters beneath it like a giant umbrella. Pilgrims still walk the roughly 21-kilometre path around Govardhan hill near Mathura.

A flute heard across the arts

Krishna's flute, his peacock feather and his cows appear everywhere in Indian art. Painters in the hills of Kangra showed him in soft greens and blues, and Kangra paintings are still famous for it. The 16th-century poet Surdas wrote hundreds of songs about Krishna as a child, often from Yashoda's point of view.

In Braj, the region around Mathura and Vrindavan, Holi lasts for days because of stories of Krishna playing with colours. Dances such as Manipuri retell his circle dance with the gopis, the village girls.

The boy grows up

Krishna does not stay in Vrindavan. He returns to Mathura, defeats Kamsa and later becomes a king. As an adult, he is a key figure in the epic Mahabharata, where he guides the warrior Arjuna. And the strangest part? The same butter-stealing boy is the teacher who speaks the Bhagavad Gita, one of the most read religious texts in the world.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Krishna is shown blue because the stories say his skin was blue.

Truth: Many old texts describe him as dark, using words like "shyama" or comparing him to a rain cloud. Painters chose blue as a way to show that deep, cloudy darkness, and the colour stuck.
