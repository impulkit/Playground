How is technology changing farming?

In Indian villages, women are training as drone pilots to spray crops, and on big American farms, tractors steer themselves to within a few centimetres.

The short answer

For thousands of years, farmers judged their fields by eye and by hand. Now many also use machines that measure. Soil sensors report how wet the ground is. Satellites and drones photograph crops and spot sick patches before people can. Tractors guided by GPS drive in perfectly straight lines. Phone apps tell farmers tomorrow's weather and today's prices.

The big idea is called precision farming. Instead of giving a whole field the same water, fertiliser and pesticide, you give each patch only what it needs. That saves money and water, and keeps extra chemicals out of rivers.

Surprising but true

A drone can spray a hectare of crops (a square 100 metres on each side) in roughly ten to fifteen minutes, a job that takes a person with a backpack sprayer several hours.

Eyes in the sky

A farmer standing at the edge of a field sees the nearest rows. A drone flying overhead sees all of it. Cameras on drones and satellites can detect light our eyes miss, which shows whether leaves are healthy and green inside or starting to struggle. A yellowing patch might mean pests, thirsty roots or poor soil.

In India, drones are also used to spray crops. Under a government plan announced in 2023, called Namo Drone Didi, women in village self-help groups are trained as drone pilots and hired by farmers nearby. A drone can cover a hectare, a square 100 metres on each side, in about ten to fifteen minutes, and the person controlling it stays out of the spray. Drones are also being tried for spreading seed and fertiliser.

How do satellites help us every day?Weather forecasts, maps on your phone, card payments and cricket on TV all depend on machines circling far above your head.

Fields that talk

Small sensors pushed into the soil can measure moisture and temperature and send the readings to a phone. This is part of the internet of things, where ordinary objects are connected to the internet. A farmer can switch on a water pump from a phone, or get a warning before frost.

Drip irrigation goes with this. Thin pipes carry water drop by drop to the base of each plant instead of flooding the whole field. It can save a large share of the water, which matters in places where the monsoon fails or wells are running low.

Tractors that steer themselves

On large farms in the United States, Canada and Australia, many tractors use GPS to steer. The driver turns at the end of the field, and the tractor follows straight rows within a few centimetres. Some combine harvesters map how much grain each part of the field produced, so next year's seed and fertiliser can be adjusted patch by patch. Companies are now testing tractors that work with nobody in the cab at all.

India's farms are mostly much smaller. The average holding is about one hectare, and many families farm less than that. A giant machine makes no sense on a plot you can walk across in two minutes. So Indian technology often looks different: apps, rented machines and shared drones rather than giant self-driving harvesters.

Old problems, new tools

In the 1960s, the Green Revolution gave Indian farmers new seeds, fertilisers and irrigation, and grain harvests jumped. It also left some soils tired and some water tables falling. Smart farming tries to keep yields high while using less.

There are worries too. Machines and data can be expensive, and some farmers ask who owns the information collected from their fields. In places with weak mobile signal, an app is not much use. Experts say the technology helps most when it is cheap, simple and explained by people farmers already trust.

Since 2015, India's Soil Health Card scheme has tested farm soil and told farmers which nutrients their land is short of. The tools are new, but the questions are the ones farmers have always asked: when will it rain, what does this soil need, and what will my crop sell for?

Myth or fact?

Myth: Farming technology is only for huge, rich farms.

Truth: Some machines are expensive, but many tools reach small farmers too. In India, farmers can rent machines from shared hiring centres, get weather alerts and crop prices on basic phones, and hire drone pilots by the hour.

Try this

Plant a few seeds in two cups of soil. Water one cup only when you poke your finger in and the soil feels dry. Water the other the same amount every day whether it needs it or not. Watch which seedlings do better over two weeks, and how much water each cup used.
