Who was Simón Bolívar?

A rich orphan from Caracas swore an oath on a hill in Rome. Twenty years later, six countries were free of Spanish rule, and one was named after him.

The short answer

Simón Bolívar was a soldier and politician from Venezuela. In the early 1800s much of South America belonged to the Spanish Empire, ruled from far-off Madrid. Bolívar led armies that pushed Spain out of a huge stretch of the continent.

Six of today's countries owe their independence partly to him: Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia and Panama. Bolivia is even named after him. Across Latin America he is called El Libertador, the Liberator, and almost every town has a plaza or a statue in his honour.

Surprising but true

Bolívar led his army over the frozen passes of the Andes in 1819, a crossing so hard that many soldiers and nearly all the horses died on the way.

A rich boy who lost everything

Bolívar was born in Caracas in 1783 into one of the wealthiest families in the colony. By the age of nine he had lost both parents. A free-thinking tutor, Simón Rodríguez, filled his head with new ideas about liberty. His young wife died of fever less than a year after their wedding.

On a trip to Europe in 1805 he climbed the Monte Sacro hill in Rome with his old tutor. There, by his own later account, he swore not to rest until Spanish rule in America was broken. Back home, the chance came quickly. Napoleon had invaded Spain in 1808, and Spain's grip on its colonies was slipping.

Defeat, exile and a second chance

The early fighting went badly. Bolívar's first republics collapsed, and twice he had to flee. In 1815, in exile in Jamaica, he wrote a famous letter setting out his hopes for a free South America. He argued that the colonies had been kept like children, barred from running their own affairs, and that Spain could never win them back by force. He also warned that freedom would be harder to keep than to win.

Help came from an unexpected place. The president of Haiti, Alexandre Pétion, gave him ships, guns and money. Haiti asked one thing in return: free the enslaved people in the lands you win. Bolívar promised, though he kept the promise only in part.

Over the Andes

In 1819 he tried something his enemies thought impossible. He marched his army across the plains during the rainy season, then up into the Andes, the longest mountain range above the sea on Earth. Soldiers from the hot lowlands climbed passes nearly 4,000 metres high in thin clothes. Many died of cold.

The survivors came down on the other side and took the Spanish by surprise. At the Battle of Boyacá on 7 August 1819 they won the victory that freed what is now Colombia. More wins followed, and by 1824 Spanish power in South America was almost gone.

A hero with a hard side

Bolívar dreamed of one strong country, Gran Colombia, and even of a union of all the new nations. But regions quarrelled, and he grew more forceful. In 1828 he made himself dictator, and some of his old friends turned against him. That same year, plotters burst into his palace in Bogotá at night to kill him. His companion Manuela Sáenz, a fierce revolutionary from Ecuador, held them off while he escaped through a window. He later called her "the Liberator of the Liberator".

Gran Colombia broke apart, and Bolívar, ill and worn out, set off for exile. He died in Santa Marta, on the Colombian coast, in 1830, aged just 47. Two currencies carry his name, Venezuela's bolívar and Bolivia's boliviano. In Lima, the capital of Peru, a bronze Bolívar on horseback still faces the national Congress.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Bolívar freed South America on his own.

Truth: He was the most famous leader, but many others fought too. José de San Martín freed Argentina and Chile and much of Peru, and Antonio José de Sucre won the last great battle, at Ayacucho in 1824.
