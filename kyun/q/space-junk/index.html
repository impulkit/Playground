What is space junk?

Millions of bits of old rockets and broken satellites circle Earth faster than bullets. Even a fleck of paint can chip a window.

The short answer

Space junk, or space debris, is anything made by people that is circling Earth but no longer does a useful job. It includes dead satellites, empty rocket stages, tools dropped by astronauts, and millions of bits from explosions and collisions, right down to flecks of paint.

The European Space Agency tracks about 40,000 objects, and estimates there are more than 50,000 pieces larger than 10 cm, about the size of an orange. There are about a million between 1 cm and 10 cm, and well over 100 million smaller than that. Because they travel so fast, even small pieces can damage working satellites and the International Space Station.

Surprising but true

When the US space station Skylab fell back to Earth in 1979, pieces landed near the town of Esperance in Australia, which jokingly fined NASA 400 dollars for littering.

Faster than bullets

To stay in orbit a few hundred kilometres up, an object has to move at about 28,000 km/h. Two objects on crossing paths can hit each other at even higher speeds.

At that speed, a piece the size of a marble can wreck a satellite. Even a speck of paint can pit a window. In 2016, the British astronaut Tim Peake photographed a chip about 7 mm across in one of the space station's windows. It was probably made by a fleck of paint or metal only a few thousandths of a millimetre wide.

The space station has shields to stop small bits. For bigger ones, ground controllers track the debris and, when something will pass too close, fire the station's engines to nudge it out of the way. This happens a few times a year.

How the mess grew

The first satellite, Sputnik, went up in 1957. Since then, thousands of rockets have left upper stages, covers and bolts in orbit. Old batteries and fuel tanks have sometimes exploded, scattering pieces.

In February 2009, an American communications satellite called Iridium 33 crashed into Kosmos-2251, a dead Russian satellite, about 790 km above Siberia. It was the first accidental crash between two whole satellites, and it created thousands of new pieces. Tests in which countries destroyed their own satellites with missiles have added more.

Today, companies are launching thousands of small satellites to provide internet from space, so orbits are getting busier than ever.

A chain reaction

In 1978, a NASA scientist named Donald Kessler warned of a possible chain reaction. If there is enough debris in one orbit, crashes make more debris, which causes more crashes, and so on. In the worst case, some orbits could become too dangerous to use for many years. This is called the Kessler syndrome.

Scientists disagree about how close we are to that point. Most agree that the number of pieces is growing, and that it would be wise to clean up before it gets worse. Like plastic in the ocean, it is much easier to stop adding junk than to clear it up afterwards.

What happens to it

Low down, a few hundred kilometres up, there are still faint traces of air. They slowly drag on debris until it falls and burns up, usually within a few years or decades. Higher up, junk can stay in orbit for centuries.

When big objects do come down, most of them burn up. In 1979, the 77-tonne US space station Skylab came down over the Indian Ocean and Western Australia. The local council of Esperance sent NASA a 400-dollar fine for littering. It went unpaid for 30 years, until an American radio host raised the money.

Cleaning up

Space agencies now ask satellite operators to bring their spacecraft down within a set number of years after their missions end, or move them to out-of-the-way "graveyard" orbits. Some new satellites carry extra fuel just for this.

Engineers are also testing ways to grab old junk, with robotic arms, nets and magnets. In 2024, a Japanese spacecraft flew close to an old rocket stage and photographed it, a first step towards catching it. Keeping orbits clean matters for everyone who uses weather forecasts, satellite TV or GPS.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Falling space junk is a big danger to people on the ground.

Truth: Most debris burns up as it falls through the air, and what survives usually lands in the ocean. Only one person is known to have been touched by space debris, Lottie Williams in Oklahoma in 1997, and she was not hurt.

Try this

Set up a few rows of dominoes or playing cards standing on their edges. Knock one over and watch the others fall. Space scientists worry about something similar, where one crash makes debris that causes more crashes. Then think up a way to keep the rows apart so one fall can't topple the rest.
