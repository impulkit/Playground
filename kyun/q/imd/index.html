What does the India Meteorological Department do?

In 2013, a warning from India's weather office helped move nearly 10 lakh people out of the path of Cyclone Phailin before it struck Odisha.

The short answer

The India Meteorological Department, or IMD, is India's national weather service. It watches the skies, the seas and the air over the whole country, every hour of every day. From that, it makes weather forecasts and warns people about danger.

Its job covers a lot more than whether to carry an umbrella. IMD forecasts the monsoon, which millions of farmers depend on. It tracks cyclones, heatwaves, cold waves, fog and thunderstorms. It gives weather information to airports, ships, farmers and disaster teams. It also keeps long records of India's climate, going back well over a century. The United States has a similar service, the National Weather Service.

Surprising but true

The India Meteorological Department is older than the telephone. It was set up in 1875, a year before Alexander Graham Bell's patent.

Started in 1875

In the 1860s, a series of disasters struck India. A huge cyclone hit Calcutta in 1864, and drought caused a terrible famine in Odisha in 1866. The British government decided it needed proper weather records.

IMD was founded in 1875, with Henry Blanford as its first chief. Early weather watchers read thermometers and rain gauges by hand and sent the results by telegraph. In 2025 IMD celebrated 150 years. It is now part of the Ministry of Earth Sciences, with its headquarters at Mausam Bhawan in New Delhi.

How IMD watches the weather

IMD gathers measurements from thousands of places. Automatic weather stations record temperature, rain, wind and humidity. Twice a day, from dozens of stations, staff release weather balloons carrying small instruments that radio back readings as they rise.

Doppler radars scan for rain and storms within a few hundred kilometres. Indian weather satellites, such as INSAT-3DR, photograph clouds over the whole country and the oceans around it. All this data goes into supercomputers, which run models of the atmosphere to work out what will happen next.

How do computers forecast the weather?In 1922, a scientist dreamed of a giant hall where 64,000 people with pencils would calculate tomorrow's weather. Today one supercomputer does far more, in minutes.

Watching for the monsoon

Every April, IMD gives its first forecast for the coming monsoon, saying whether rain is likely to be below normal, normal or above normal. It updates the forecast in late May or June.

IMD also announces when the monsoon reaches Kerala, usually around 1 June. Farmers, power companies and governments plan around these forecasts. A weak monsoon can mean lower crops and higher food prices, so the whole country pays attention.

Naming and tracking cyclones

IMD runs a regional centre for the World Meteorological Organization that watches cyclones across the north Indian Ocean, including the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea. It names them from a list suggested by 13 countries in the region. Names such as Phailin, Fani and Biparjoy came from this list.

The biggest success story is in Odisha. In 1999 a super cyclone there killed about 10,000 people. When Cyclone Phailin approached in October 2013, IMD predicted its path and strength days in advance. Officials moved nearly 10 lakh people into shelters. Phailin was just as strong, but the number of deaths was kept to a few dozen.

Colours that warn

IMD uses colour codes so warnings are easy to understand. Green means no warning. Yellow means watch the weather. Orange means be prepared. Red means take action. Schools may close, fishing boats stay on shore and disaster teams get ready.

Heat has become a growing concern. IMD now issues heatwave warnings that help cities open cooling shelters and change working hours for outdoor workers.

Forecasts on your phone

IMD's forecasts now reach people through apps like Mausam and Meghdoot, the second made for farmers. Farmers can get advice about when to sow, spray or harvest. Fishermen receive warnings of high waves.

Some weather is still hard to forecast, especially sudden storms like hailstorms that grow in less than an hour. For those, IMD issues "nowcasts" covering the next three hours. It's a race against the clouds.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Weather forecasts are mostly wrong anyway.

Truth: Forecasts have improved enormously. A three-day forecast today is about as accurate as a one-day forecast was in the 1980s. Small, sudden storms are still hard to predict, which is why they get the most attention when forecasts miss.

Try this

Keep a weather diary for a week. Each morning, write down IMD's forecast for your city from a newspaper, the radio or the Mausam app. Each evening, write what really happened. At the end of the week, give the forecasts a score out of seven and see how often they were right.
