How did doctors learn about the body?

For over a thousand years, doctors in Europe learned anatomy from books based on the insides of monkeys and pigs.

The short answer

Doctors learned about the body slowly, over thousands of years, by watching patients, treating injuries, opening up bodies and, later, using new tools. Each step built on earlier ones, and many old ideas turned out to be wrong.

Ancient Egyptian, Indian, Chinese and Greek doctors wrote down what they saw and what seemed to help. Later, doctors in Baghdad, Cairo and Persia collected and improved that knowledge. From the 1500s, European doctors began carefully cutting open human bodies after death and drawing what they found. Then came microscopes, stethoscopes and X-rays, which let doctors see things no eye could. Each tool opened up a little more of the body.

Surprising but true

The first stethoscope was a rolled-up sheet of paper. A French doctor made it in 1816 because he felt awkward pressing his ear to a patient's chest.

The oldest medical books

One of the oldest surviving medical texts is the Edwin Smith Papyrus from ancient Egypt, copied around 1600 BCE from even older writings. It describes 48 cases of injuries, from head wounds to broken bones, and how to examine and treat them. It even notes that an injury to the brain can affect the body far away.

In India, the Sushruta Samhita describes many surgical tools and operations, including repairing a damaged nose with skin from the cheek. Historians disagree about when it was written, but it grew over centuries and is more than 2,000 years old in parts. Sushruta is often called a father of surgery. The Charaka Samhita, a text of Ayurveda, covers diet, illness and medicines.

Greek ideas and a long mistake

In ancient Greece, Hippocrates and his followers taught that illness has natural causes, not punishments from gods. Doctors still take an oath named after him. Later, a Greek doctor in Rome named Galen wrote hundreds of works. Human dissection wasn't allowed, so he cut open monkeys and pigs. Some of what he described was right for animals but wrong for humans.

His books were trusted for over 1,300 years. In the 11th century, the Persian scholar Ibn Sina wrote the Canon of Medicine, a giant textbook used in Europe and Asia for centuries.

Looking for themselves

In 1543, a young doctor in Italy named Andreas Vesalius published a huge book of anatomy with detailed drawings, based on human bodies he had dissected himself. He showed that Galen had made many errors. In 1628, the English doctor William Harvey showed that the heart pumps blood round and round the body in a loop.

In the 1670s, a Dutch cloth merchant, Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, used his own microscopes to see tiny living things in water and in his own mouth. In 1816, René Laennec invented the stethoscope from rolled paper. In 1895, Wilhelm Röntgen discovered X-rays, and within weeks doctors were photographing bones inside living people.

Germs and modern medicine

In the 1860s to 1880s, Louis Pasteur in France and Robert Koch in Germany showed that many diseases are caused by germs. Surgeons began washing hands and cleaning tools, and far more patients survived operations. Vaccines and, later, antibiotics changed medicine again.

The doors of medical school opened slowly. Elizabeth Blackwell became the first woman to earn a medical degree in the USA in 1849. In 1886, Anandi Gopal Joshi, from Maharashtra, graduated from a medical college in Pennsylvania, one of the first Indian women trained in Western medicine. Today, scanners, genetic tests and tiny cameras let doctors see inside the body without a single cut, in hospitals around the world.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Doctors in the past knew almost nothing useful about the body.

Truth: Ancient doctors in Egypt, India, Greece, China and the Islamic world made careful observations, set broken bones, treated wounds and did surgery. Many of their ideas were wrong, but many were sharp and some are still used.

Try this

Make a paper stethoscope like Laennec's. Roll a sheet of stiff paper into a tube about 2 cm wide and tape it. With permission, place one end gently on a friend's chest, slightly left of centre, and your ear to the other end in a quiet room. Can you hear the heartbeat? Try it after they've jumped about.
