Which animals live the longest?

Somewhere in the Arctic Ocean there may be sharks still swimming that were born before the Taj Mahal was finished.

The short answer

The animal with the longest life we can actually count is a clam. An ocean quahog, pulled up from the seabed off Iceland in 2006, had 507 growth lines in its shell, one for each year. Among animals with a backbone, the record probably belongs to the Greenland shark. A 2016 study found the largest ones are at least 272 years old, and most likely about 400.

On land, the champion is a giant tortoise named Jonathan, who lives on the island of St Helena in the Atlantic. He hatched around 1832 and was about 194 in 2026, still munching grass.

Surprising but true

A clam dredged up off Iceland in 2006 turned out to be 507 years old. It had started growing before Akbar was born.

The slowest shark

Greenland sharks live in freezing water, mostly in the North Atlantic and Arctic. They grow about one centimetre a year and swim so slowly they look half asleep. The study suggested females don't have babies until they are around 150 years old.

Because they have no bony rings to count, scientists looked inside their eyes. The middle of the eye lens forms before birth and never changes. Measuring carbon in it gave the age. The biggest shark tested, about 5 metres long, came out at somewhere between 272 and 512 years, with about 400 as the best guess.

Whales, tortoises and a clam named Ming

Bowhead whales, which live in the Arctic, can pass 200. Hunters in Alaska have found old stone and ivory harpoon tips buried in living whales, made in styles that were last used over a hundred years earlier.

Tortoises are famous for long lives too. Kolkata's Alipore Zoo once had an Aldabra giant tortoise called Adwaita, who died in 2006. Some claimed he was 250, though nobody could prove it.

The 507-year-old clam was nicknamed Ming, after the Chinese dynasty that ruled when it was young. Sadly, scientists only learned its age after opening it.

What are the oldest trees on Earth?A twisted pine in the mountains of California was already a sapling when the pyramids of Giza were being built, and it is still alive.

Why cold and slow wins

Many record holders share a pattern: they live in cold places, move slowly and grow slowly. A slow body burns energy gently and may build up damage more slowly. Some, like tortoises and clams, are also well protected from predators, so they have time to grow old.

The naked mole rat breaks the rules. It's a small, wrinkly rodent from East Africa that can live more than 35 years, while ordinary rats manage about three. Scientists study it closely, hoping to learn more about ageing in people.

How long do pets and people live?

For comparison, most dogs live about 10 to 13 years and cats around 12 to 18. A mouse is old at two. Elephants can reach 60 or 70, and a few famous parrots have lived past 80, outliving their first owners.

People are long-lived for mammals of our size. The oldest person whose age was carefully checked was Jeanne Calment of France, who died in 1997 aged 122 years and 164 days. She was born in 1875 and said she had met the painter Vincent van Gogh as a girl.

Plants beat every animal. Some bristlecone pines in California are more than 4,800 years old. Certain aspen groves and seagrass meadows grow as giant connected clones, and they may be older still.

The ones that cheat

Some animals blur the idea of age altogether. Glass sponges on the deep seabed may live for thousands of years, though their ages are hard to check. Tiny freshwater animals called hydras keep replacing their cells and show almost no signs of getting old.

And the strangest part? A small jellyfish in the Mediterranean can turn itself back into a baby when it's hurt or starving, which is why some call it the immortal jellyfish.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Bigger animals always live longer than smaller ones.

Truth: Size helps, but it's far from the whole story. A naked mole rat the size of a sausage can live over 35 years, ten times longer than a rat. Cold, slow lives often matter more than size.

Try this

Make a "lifespan ladder". On a long strip of paper, mark one centimetre for every year. Put a mouse at 2 cm, a dog at about 12 cm, a human at about 80 cm, a bowhead whale at 2 m and a Greenland shark at about 4 m. Unroll it down a corridor and stand at each mark.
