Are mushrooms plants?

Mushrooms grow out of the ground like plants, but their family tree puts them closer to you than to a mango tree.

The short answer

No. Mushrooms belong to their own group of living things, the fungi. For a long time scientists filed them with plants, because both stay in one place and grow out of soil. Then they looked closer.

Plants are green because they use sunlight to build their food. Fungi have no green colour and cannot do this at all. Instead they grow into dead leaves, wood or soil, release juices that break the food down outside their bodies, and soak it up. DNA studies show that fungi are more closely related to animals than to plants. So the mushroom on your pizza is, in a family-tree sense, nearer to you than to the tomato under it.

Surprising but true

One honey fungus in Oregon, USA, spreads under about 9 square kilometres of forest, which may make it the largest living thing on Earth.

The mushroom is only the tip

The mushroom you see is a bit like an apple on a tree. It is the part that makes and releases spores, the fungus version of seeds. The real body is hidden: a mass of fine white threads called mycelium spreading through soil or wood.

Those threads can grow enormous. In the Malheur National Forest in Oregon, USA, one honey fungus covers about 9 square kilometres, an area bigger than 1,200 football fields. Scientists think it could be thousands of years old. From above, all you would notice is a scattering of mushrooms and some dying trees.

Nature's clean-up crew

Without fungi, forests would be buried under their own fallen leaves and logs. Fungi are among the few living things that can break down tough wood, returning its goodness to the soil. That is why a fallen tree in a damp forest crumbles within years.

Most land plants also team up with fungi underground. The fungus wraps around or grows into the roots and passes the plant water and minerals. In return, the plant feeds it sugar. Popular books describe a "wood wide web" where trees share food and warn each other through these threads. The partnership is real, but how much trees really talk through it is still argued over. In 2023, a team of forest scientists reviewed the studies and warned that many of the boldest claims go well beyond the evidence.

Farmers, glowers and bread makers

Some animals grow fungi on purpose. Leafcutter ants in the Americas and many termites in India raise fungus gardens inside their nests. After the first monsoon rains, large edible mushrooms called Termitomyces push up from Indian termite mounds, and villagers collect them as a seasonal treat.

A few mushrooms even glow in the dark. In 2018, scientists found a new glowing species growing on bamboo in Meghalaya, and they described it two years later. Its light comes from bioluminescence, the same chemistry that lights up fireflies.

Tiny single-celled fungi called yeasts help make bread rise, and they play a part in fermenting many foods. A blue-green mould gave us penicillin, one of the first antibiotics, after Alexander Fleming noticed in 1928 that it killed bacteria on a dish in his lab.

Eat with care

Mushrooms such as button, oyster and shiitake are farmed and safe to cook. Wild ones are a different story. Each year, people in India and the USA fall sick after picking mushrooms that looked harmless. Experts who know fungi well still check spores and other tiny details under a microscope before eating anything they find. Some of the most dangerous species, such as the death cap, look plain and harmless, and the illness can take hours to begin. For everyone else, the safe rule is simple: enjoy wild mushrooms with your eyes and a camera.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You can tell if a wild mushroom is poisonous by its colour, or by whether it turns a silver spoon black.

Truth: There is no simple test. Some deadly mushrooms are plain white or brown and look a lot like safe ones. Never eat a mushroom found outdoors unless an expert has identified it.

Try this

Make a spore print. Ask an adult for a fresh button mushroom from the market, gently pull off the stalk, and place the cap gill-side down on white paper. Cover it with a bowl and leave it overnight. In the morning, lift the cap and you will find a pattern drawn by millions of tiny spores.
