What is a tariff?

When a T-shirt made in Tiruppur is sold in New York, the USA may add a tax at its border before it reaches the shop. That tax, called a tariff, can decide whether the shirt sells or sits on a shelf.

The short answer

A tariff is a tax a country charges on goods brought in from other countries. In India, it is called customs duty, and it is collected by customs officers at ports, airports and border posts. It is paid at the border, by the company that brings the goods in.

Countries use tariffs to protect their own makers, to raise money, and as a bargaining chip in trade talks. But tariffs can also make things dearer for shoppers and hurt exporters in other countries.

Surprising but true

Many people think the country that makes the goods pays a tariff. In fact, the company bringing the goods in pays it at the border, and the cost often ends up in the price shoppers pay.

How it works

Most tariffs are a percentage of the value of the goods. Here is a simple example:

  • 1. A shop in the USA orders shirts from India worth 1,000 dollars.
  • 2. The USA has a tariff of 10% on these shirts.
  • 3. When the shirts arrive, the American shop pays 100 dollars to the US government.
  • 4. Now the shirts cost the shop 1,100 dollars, so it may raise its prices, buy fewer shirts, or ask the Indian maker for a discount.

If shirts from another country face a 20% tariff, Indian shirts look cheaper, and the shop may buy more from India. That is why countries fight over a few percentage points.

Think of a fair where outside sellers must buy an entry ticket, but local sellers walk in free. The ticket makes it harder for outsiders to compete. If one group of outsiders gets a cheaper ticket than the rest, they have an edge. A tariff works like that ticket, but for goods.

Tariffs do several things at once:

  • - Protect local makers from cheaper foreign goods.
  • - Raise money for the government.
  • - Push other countries in trade talks: "lower your tariffs and we will lower ours."

Some tariffs are a fixed amount instead of a percentage, such as a set charge for every kilogram. Countries also use special extra tariffs, called anti-dumping duties, when they think a foreign company is selling goods below a fair price to knock out local makers. In India, the Finance Minister often changes customs duties in the yearly Budget, which is why the prices of things like phones, gold or toys can move the day after the Budget speech.

Where it came from

Taxes on trade are ancient. Kingdoms charged traders at city gates, river crossings and ports. In India, rulers taxed goods moving along roads and through ports for centuries, and the British charged customs duties too.

In 1930, the USA raised tariffs on thousands of goods with a law called the Smoot-Hawley Act. Other countries hit back with their own tariffs, world trade shrank, and many historians say this made the Great Depression worse.

After the Second World War, countries agreed to cut tariffs step by step. In 1995, the World Trade Organization was set up to keep trade rules fair. Countries also signed free trade agreements with each other to lower tariffs even further.

What is a free trade agreement?A shirt made in Tiruppur can cost more in another country just because it crossed a border. A free trade deal is a promise to take that extra cost away.

India's story

Before 1991, India kept very high tariffs, some above 150%, to protect its young industries. Imported goods, from cameras to chocolates, were rare and costly. After the 1991 reforms, India cut tariffs a lot, and trade grew fast. India still keeps high tariffs on some things, like imported cars and some farm goods, to protect local makers and farmers.

In 2025, the USA put tariffs of up to 50% on many Indian goods, partly over India buying Russian oil. Exporters in places like Tiruppur, famous for knitwear, and Surat, famous for diamonds, worried about losing orders. In February 2026, an interim deal cut the tariff on many Indian goods to 18%, while India kept protecting sensitive farm products like wheat, rice and dairy.

US rules kept changing through 2026, after court cases and new laws. In November 2026, the USA assured India that its exporters would get lower tariffs than rivals such as Vietnam, Bangladesh and Indonesia, once the full deal was signed. The USA is India's biggest export market, so these few points matter for lakhs of jobs.

What people argue about

Supporters of tariffs say they protect jobs, help new industries grow and stop other countries from dumping cheap goods. India's leaders have used tariffs to protect farmers and to push "make in India".

Critics say tariffs raise prices for families, make factories pay more for parts, and invite other countries to hit back. They say the best way to create jobs is to make goods better and cheaper, not to block imports. Economists often agree that some protection can help, but too much hurts. People argue about where to draw the line.

Myths and mix-ups

"The exporting country pays the tariff." The importer pays it at the border.

"A tariff is the same as GST." GST is a tax on things sold inside India, whether made here or abroad. A tariff is charged only when goods cross the border.

"Tariffs always save jobs." They may save jobs in one industry but cost jobs in others that use imported parts or sell abroad.

What it means for you

Tariffs are one of the oldest tools in trade, and they still shape daily life. They change the prices of many things your family buys, from imported phones and toys to gold and edible oil. They also affect jobs, especially in towns that make goods for other countries.

When India gets lower tariffs abroad, its exporters can sell more, which helps factories and farms, and can shrink the trade deficit. Learning why countries trade and why people pay taxes helps you see why a few percentage points can make front-page news.

Myth or fact?

Myth: When the USA puts a tariff on Indian goods, India's government pays it.

Truth: The American company bringing in the goods pays the tariff to the US government. It may pass the cost to its customers, or ask Indian sellers to cut their prices. Either way, the tariff can make Indian goods harder to sell.
