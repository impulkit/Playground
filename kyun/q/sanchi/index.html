What is the Great Stupa at Sanchi?

The Great Stupa at Sanchi is covered in carvings of the Buddha's life, yet the Buddha himself never appears in human form, not even once.

The short answer

The Great Stupa is a huge dome of stone on a hilltop at Sanchi, about 46 kilometres from Bhopal in Madhya Pradesh. It is one of the oldest stone buildings in India. It was started in the 3rd century BCE by the emperor Ashoka, who became a Buddhist and spread the faith across his empire.

A stupa is a Buddhist monument built over relics, the remains of a holy person or objects linked to them. People do not go inside. Instead they walk around it in a circle, clockwise, as a form of prayer. Sanchi's stupa is ringed by a stone railing and four tall carved gateways, called toranas, that are among the finest early sculpture in India.

Surprising but true

One of the stone carvings on the south gateway was paid for by the ivory carvers of nearby Vidisha, and an inscription says so.

A dome that grew

Ashoka's stupa was smaller, made of brick. About a century later, it was enlarged to nearly twice its width and covered in stone. Builders added a railing, a raised walkway and a small stone box on top holding three stacked umbrellas, symbols of honour.

The finished dome is about 36 metres across and 16 metres high. It is shaped like a half-sphere, or hemisphere, like an upturned bowl. Some people see it as the dome of the sky over the earth, or as a mountain at the centre of the world.

Around the Great Stupa stand smaller stupas, temples and monasteries, built over more than a thousand years. One of them, a small flat-roofed shrine known as Temple 17, dates from the Gupta period, around the 5th century CE, and is among the oldest stone temples still standing in India.

Gateways full of stories

Around the 1st century BCE, four gateways were raised, facing north, south, east and west. Each has two square pillars and three curved beams across the top, every surface crowded with carvings. Elephants, lions, peacocks, dancers and city crowds fill the stone.

Many panels show scenes from the life of the Buddha and from the Jataka tales, stories of his past lives. In one, he is a monkey king who stretches his body across a river so his troop can escape. The craftsmanship is so fine that scholars think ivory carvers worked on it. An inscription on the south gateway records a gift from the ivory carvers of Vidisha.

A Buddha you never see

Look closely at the gateways and you will notice something missing. The Buddha himself is never carved as a person. Artists showed him through symbols instead: a Bodhi tree, a wheel, his footprints, an empty seat with an umbrella over it.

Historians think early Buddhists felt that a teacher who had gone beyond the cycle of birth and death should not be shown in human form. Statues of the Buddha appeared a few centuries later, in places like Mathura and Gandhara.

Forgotten and found

Buddhism slowly faded in this part of India, and Sanchi was left alone for centuries. In 1818 a British officer, General Henry Taylor, came across the ruins. Treasure-seekers and amateur diggers soon broke into some stupas looking for gold, causing real damage.

Between 1912 and 1919, the archaeologist John Marshall led a careful repair and rebuilt fallen gateways. Relics of two of the Buddha's chief disciples, found in a nearby stupa in 1851 and taken to Britain, came back to India and are now kept in a temple at Sanchi.

Today Sanchi is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and the Great Stupa appears on the back of India's ₹200 note. Buddhist pilgrims from Sri Lanka, Thailand, Japan and elsewhere still walk slowly around it, just as monks did more than 2,000 years ago.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Buddha lived or taught at Sanchi.

Truth: There is no record that the Buddha ever visited Sanchi. The stupa was built about two centuries after his death, probably because the hill was close to the rich trading city of Vidisha and had quiet space for monks.

Try this

Draw a story from your own life without drawing yourself. Use objects to show where you were, such as your shoes by the door, your bag on a chair or your plate at the table. Show it to someone and see if they can follow the story. That is how Sanchi's sculptors told the Buddha's life.
