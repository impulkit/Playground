What is life like in Ladakh?

Ladakh is a desert, but a freezing one. Its main town is about 3,500 m up, it gets less rain in a year than Mumbai can get in a day, and winter nights fall far below freezing.

The short answer

Ladakh lies in India's far north, high in the mountains between the Himalayas and the Karakoram. Its main town, Leh, is about 3,500 m above sea level. The air is thin, so visitors are told to rest for a day or two before walking much. The tall Himalayas block the monsoon clouds, so Ladakh gets very little rain or snow. It is a cold desert of brown and purple mountains, deep blue skies and green fields along the rivers.

Fewer than 3 lakh people live in this huge area. They farm in short summers, herd animals on high pastures and get through long, hard winters. Since 2019, Ladakh has been a union territory, run directly by the central government.

Surprising but true

Some Ladakhi villages build towering cones of ice in winter, called ice stupas, so they have water for their crops in spring.

Farming in a short summer

Almost all water in Ladakh comes from melting snow and glaciers. Villagers dig channels to lead streams onto their fields, where they grow barley, wheat, peas and vegetables. Apricot trees are everywhere. In summer, apricots are dried on rooftops to eat in winter.

Barley flour, roasted and called tsampa, is a basic food. People drink butter tea, made with salt and butter, which gives energy in the cold. Houses are built of mud bricks with thick walls and flat roofs, where firewood and animal fodder are stored for winter.

Ice stupas

Early spring is a hard time. Fields need water for planting, but the streams are still frozen or low. In 2013, the engineer Sonam Wangchuk built a tall cone of ice by spraying stream water into the freezing night air. Because a cone has little surface for its size, it melts slowly through spring, just when farmers need water. He called them ice stupas, after the shape of Buddhist shrines, and several villages now build them.

Monasteries and mosques

Many people in the Leh area are Buddhists. Monasteries such as Thiksey and Hemis sit on hilltops, with prayer flags fluttering and monks chanting inside. At festivals, monks dance in painted masks. In the Kargil area, most people are Muslims, and mosques and Buddhist monasteries are both part of Ladakh's landscape.

Animals of the high desert

The rare snow leopard lives on Ladakh's rocky slopes, hunting wild sheep and goats. Its thick fur and long tail help it survive. In the Nubra valley, two-humped Bactrian camels walk the sand dunes, left over from the days of caravan trade with Central Asia.

On the high Changthang plateau, nomads herd Changthangi goats. In the extreme cold, these goats grow a very fine, soft underlayer of wool, which is made into pashmina shawls.

Winter, roads and lakes

In winter, temperatures in Leh can drop below −20 °C, and Dras, near Kargil, is one of the coldest inhabited places in India. Snow closes the mountain passes for months. The Zanskar River freezes so hard that people walk along it, a journey called the Chadar, or "blanket", trek.

Ladakh also has some of the highest roads on Earth. In 2021, a road over Umling La pass, about 5,800 m up, was recognised by Guinness World Records as the highest motorable road in the world. Pangong Tso, a long, salty lake about 4,350 m high, changes colour from blue to green as the light shifts. Most of the lake lies across the border in Tibet.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Deserts are always hot and sandy.

Truth: A desert is a place that gets very little rain or snow. Ladakh is a cold desert, with freezing winters and bare rocky mountains. Antarctica is the biggest desert of all.
