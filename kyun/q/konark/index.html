Why is Konark Sun Temple shaped like a chariot?

The Konark temple was built as a giant stone chariot for the Sun god, with 24 carved wheels and seven horses to pull it across the sky.

The short answer

The Konark Sun Temple, on the coast of Odisha, was built around 1250 CE for Surya, the Hindu Sun god. In Hindu stories, Surya rides across the sky every day in a chariot pulled by seven horses. So the temple was designed as that chariot, made of stone.

Around its base are 24 huge carved wheels, 12 on each side. In front, stone horses strain forward as if pulling the whole temple towards the sunrise. The chariot shape was a way of turning a building into a story: the Sun's daily journey, frozen in stone.

Surprising but true

In 1903, British engineers filled the temple's surviving hall with sand and sealed it up to stop it collapsing, and the sand stayed inside for more than a hundred years.

A king and his temple

The temple was built by King Narasimhadeva I of the Eastern Ganga dynasty, who ruled much of what is now Odisha. Like many Hindu temples, it faces east, so the first rays of the rising sun would reach the entrance.

Thousands of workers are thought to have worked on it for years. They used several kinds of stone, including a soft, greenish rock called chlorite for detailed carvings, and iron beams to hold some of the heavy blocks together.

Wheels that tell the time

Each wheel is about 3 metres across, taller than an adult. Each one has eight thick spokes and eight thinner ones in between, all covered in carvings of flowers, animals and people.

Many guides explain that the wheels work as sundials. The shadow of a spoke's axle falls on the rim and shows the time of day, so you can measure time by the Sun. Whether the builders really planned them as clocks, or simply as symbols of the Sun's cycles, historians still discuss. Either way, the idea matches the temple's subject perfectly.

The missing tower

What you see at Konark today is not the whole temple. The main shrine once had a towering spire, perhaps around 70 metres tall, which would have been visible far out at sea. European sailors called it the Black Pagoda and used it as a landmark.

That tower has fallen, and only its base remains. What still stands is the jagamohana, the great audience hall in front of it. By the early 1900s, it too was cracking. In 1903 British engineers filled it with sand and sealed its doors to stop it collapsing. In recent years, the Archaeological Survey of India has begun carefully removing that sand to repair the hall.

Carvings everywhere

Nearly every surface at Konark is carved. You can see musicians, dancers, hunters, soldiers, elephants, kings, gods and scenes of everyday life, including people cooking, fighting and travelling. Some carvings show animals that were brought from far away, like a giraffe. Some historians think this shows that traders brought news, or even animals, from Africa.

Three large statues of Surya on the outer walls face different directions. Some people think they show the Sun at morning, noon and evening, linking again to time and the seasons.

On a banknote and a heritage list

Konark became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1984. A picture of the temple appears on the back of India's ten-rupee note, and the wheel is one of Odisha's best-known symbols.

Every December, the Konark Dance Festival takes place in an open-air stage near the temple, with Odissi and other classical dancers performing. Many of the poses carved on the walls look like movements from Odissi dance, so it can feel as if the stone dancers have stepped down to join them.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Konark's main tower fell because a giant magnet on top was removed.

Truth: This is a famous legend, said to explain why ships' compasses went wrong nearby. There is no evidence for a giant magnet. Historians think the tower fell because of weak foundations, sandy ground, storms or earthquakes, though nobody is sure which.

Try this

On a sunny day, push a pencil upright into a lump of clay on a sheet of paper outdoors, in a safe spot near home. Mark where the pencil's shadow falls every hour and write the time beside it. You've made a simple sundial, like the ones people say the Konark wheels can work as.
