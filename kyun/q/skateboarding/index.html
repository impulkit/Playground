How do skateboard tricks work?

In an ollie, a skateboarder leaps into the air and the board seems to stick to their feet, with no straps and no glue.

The short answer

Skateboard tricks work by using the board as a lever and the skater's feet to steer it through the air. In the basic trick, the ollie, the skater snaps the tail of the board down hard against the ground. The tail bounces up and the whole board pops into the air.

At the same moment, the skater jumps and slides the front foot forward along the rough grip tape on top. That drags the front of the board upwards until it is level. To the watcher, the board seems glued to the feet. Almost every other trick, from kickflips to grinds, starts with an ollie.

Surprising but true

When skateboarding first appeared at the Olympics in Tokyo, the women's street gold went to Momiji Nishiya of Japan, aged 13.

Surfing on the street

Skateboarding began in California in the 1950s, when surfers wanted something to do when the sea was flat. They fixed roller-skate wheels to planks of wood and called it sidewalk surfing. Its moves still borrow from riding waves.

The first boards had clay or metal wheels that were noisy, slippery and dangerous on rough ground. Skateboarding faded in the 1960s, partly because so many riders got hurt.

The wheel that changed everything

In the early 1970s, an engineer named Frank Nasworthy began selling skateboard wheels made of polyurethane, a tough, slightly soft plastic. Suddenly boards gripped the road and rolled smoothly over cracks. Skateboarding exploded in popularity.

At the same time, a long drought in California left many backyard swimming pools empty. Skaters in Santa Monica and Venice, near Los Angeles, began riding up and down the curved pool walls. Their bold style created vert skating, where riders fly above the edge of a ramp.

The ollie, step by step

The ollie was invented in 1978 by a Florida skater, Alan Gelfand, nicknamed "Ollie", on ramps. In 1982 Rodney Mullen worked out how to do it on flat ground. Street skating was born.

Here is the physics. The board works as a lever, with the back wheels as the pivot. Stamping on the tail pushes the board's front up and the tail down. When the tail strikes the ground, it rebounds and the board leaps. Then friction between shoe and grip tape lets the front foot drag the board up. Finally the skater pushes the front down to level it and bends the knees to land.

Flips, grinds and spins

Once a skater can ollie, other tricks open up. In a kickflip, the front foot flicks off the edge of the board so it spins under the skater before they land. In a grind, the skater ollies onto a rail or ledge and slides along it on the metal trucks that hold the wheels.

Spins work like a gymnast's somersault. Skaters crouch and pull their arms in to spin faster, then stretch out to slow down before landing. The best skaters can spin 900 or even 1,080 degrees, that is three full turns, above a ramp.

From car parks to the Olympics

For years skateboarding was a street culture, and skaters were often chased out of plazas and car parks. Many cities now build skateparks instead. India has growing skate scenes too, in cities such as Bengaluru and in village skateparks built by community groups.

Skateboarding joined the Olympics at the Tokyo Games, held in 2021, with street and park events. Several medallists were teenagers. Its basic tool still has just four wheels, two axles and a board, and much of what a skater learns is how to fall safely, which is why helmets and pads matter.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In an ollie, the skater just jumps and the board follows on its own.

Truth: If you simply jump, the board stays on the ground. The skater has to pop the tail and drag the board up with their front foot, using friction from the grip tape.

Try this

Place a ruler across a pencil on a table, like a seesaw. Tap one end down sharply with your finger and watch the other end jump up. That quick pop is the first half of an ollie. Keep it on the table and away from faces.
