Why is the Suez Canal so important?

In March 2021, one giant container ship got stuck sideways in the Suez Canal for six days, and hundreds of ships queued up at both ends.

The short answer

The Suez Canal is a 193 km channel of seawater across Egypt. It joins the Mediterranean Sea in the north to the Red Sea in the south. Ships sailing between Asia and Europe can use it instead of going all the way around Africa.

For a voyage between Mumbai and London, that saves thousands of kilometres and well over a week at sea. Before the trouble that began in late 2023, more than 20,000 ships a year used the canal, often said to carry about 12% of the world's trade. Oil, cars, clothes, phones and grain all pass through it. When the canal is blocked, shops and factories on the other side of the world can feel it within weeks.

Surprising but true

After a war in 1967, fifteen cargo ships were trapped in the canal for eight years. Their crews ran their own sports contests and even printed stamps.

A short cut through the desert

People had dreamed of joining these seas for thousands of years. Ancient Egyptian rulers dug canals linking the Nile to the Red Sea, but they silted up and were abandoned.

The modern canal was built by a French-led company under Ferdinand de Lesseps, starting in 1859. In the early years, huge numbers of Egyptian peasants were forced to dig, often by hand, in brutal heat. Many died, though nobody knows exactly how many. Later, steam-powered dredgers did much of the work. The canal opened on 17 November 1869 with fireworks and a grand parade of ships.

No locks needed

The Suez Canal is simpler than the Panama Canal. The two seas are at almost the same level, and the land between is low desert, so there is no need to lift ships. The canal also passes through some lakes along the way.

Ships travel in convoys, one behind another, guided by Egyptian pilots who know every bend. Parts of the canal allow only one-way traffic, so convoys take turns. In 2015 Egypt opened a new parallel channel along part of the route so more ships could pass in both directions.

What it meant for India

The canal changed travel between Europe and India. Before 1869, sailing ships from Britain took months to reach Bombay around Africa. Through Suez, steamships could do it in a few weeks. Bombay's port boomed, and Mumbai grew into India's great trading city. Cotton, tea and indigo flowed out, and British goods and officials flowed in.

Six days of blockage

In March 2021, strong winds pushed the Ever Given, a container ship about 400 m long, sideways across the canal. Its bow was wedged in one bank and its stern near the other. For six days nothing could pass. Diggers and dredgers scooped sand from around the bow, and tugboats, helped by a high tide, pulled it free on 29 March. By then hundreds of ships were waiting.

Crisis and conflict

In 1956, Egypt's president, Gamal Abdel Nasser, took control of the canal from the company that ran it. Britain, France and Israel then invaded. Under pressure from the USA, the Soviet Union and the United Nations, they withdrew. The UN sent one of its first peacekeeping forces to the area.

After a war in 1967, the canal was closed for eight years. Fifteen ships were trapped in one of its lakes the whole time. Their crews, from many countries, visited each other, held a sailing contest and printed their own stamps.

Since late 2023, attacks on ships in the Red Sea led many shipping companies to avoid the area and sail around Africa instead. Traffic through the canal fell sharply, and Egypt lost billions of dollars in fees. In late 2025 and early 2026, some of the big shipping lines began cautiously sending ships back through. Some soon switched back to the long route around Africa when the risks rose again. A narrow ribbon of water in the desert still decides how fast much of the world gets its goods.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Suez Canal uses locks to lift ships, like the Panama Canal.

Truth: The Suez Canal has no locks. The Mediterranean and the Red Sea are at almost the same level, and the land between them is low and flat, so the canal is a long, open channel of seawater.
