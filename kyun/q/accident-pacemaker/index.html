How was the pacemaker invented by mistake?

In 1956, an engineer in New York reached into a box for a tiny electrical part, picked the wrong one, and heard his circuit start to beat like a heart.

The short answer

Your heart has its own built-in electrical timer, a small patch of cells that fires a pulse about once a second. If that timer slows down or the signal gets blocked, the heart can beat too slowly. A person may feel faint, tired or dizzy.

A pacemaker takes over the job. It is a small metal case with a battery and a circuit, placed under the skin near the collarbone. Thin wires run to the heart and deliver a tiny pulse whenever the heart needs one.

One of its inventors found the key idea by accident. In 1956, an American engineer named Wilson Greatbatch put the wrong part into a circuit, and it began to pulse in the rhythm of a heartbeat.

Surprising but true

The first person to get a pacemaker sewn inside his body, Arne Larsson of Sweden, went on to receive 26 of them and lived to 86, outliving both the surgeon and the engineer who gave him the first one.

The wrong resistor

Greatbatch was working at the University of Buffalo on a gadget to record heart sounds. He needed a resistor, a small part that controls how much electricity flows. He reached into a box and pulled out one that was 100 times stronger than the one he wanted.

When he switched on, the circuit gave a short electrical squirt lasting less than two thousandths of a second. Then it rested for about one second and repeated. Greatbatch stared at it. He later said he knew instantly what it could do, because doctors had told him about patients whose hearts needed exactly that kind of steady nudge.

A race on two continents

Greatbatch was not the only one working on the idea. In the early 1950s, American doctors used pacemakers as big as a microwave oven, plugged into the wall. Patients had to stay close to a socket. In 1957, after a power cut put patients at risk, Earl Bakken in Minnesota built a battery pacemaker small enough to wear.

The first one placed fully inside a person's body came in 1958, in Stockholm. The engineer Rune Elmqvist and the surgeon Åke Senning built it for Arne Larsson, a 43-year-old whose heart kept stopping. His wife had pleaded with them to try. The first device failed within hours, and the backup also lasted only a short time. But the idea worked.

Greatbatch and the surgeon William Chardack implanted their own design in a patient in Buffalo in 1960. It was soon made and sold widely.

The battery problem

Early pacemakers ran on batteries that lasted only a year or two. Every time they died, patients needed another operation. Some batteries also leaked inside the body.

Greatbatch spent years on this problem too. In the 1970s he helped develop a lithium-iodide battery for pacemakers that could last ten years or more. Most implanted pacemakers in the following decades used this type.

How small can they get?

The first wearable pacemakers were about the size of a paperback book. Most today are close to the size of a matchbox and weigh around 20 to 30 g, about as much as an AA battery.

Some newer models skip wires altogether. These leadless pacemakers are about the size of a large vitamin pill and sit right inside the heart, fed in through a vein in the leg. They also sense when you are running or climbing stairs, and speed up the beat to match, much as your own heart would.

A spark that runs the body

Larsson lived with pacemakers for 43 years. He died in 2001, aged 86, after outliving both Elmqvist and Senning. Today more than a million pacemakers are fitted around the world every year.

The pacemaker works because the heart is, in a sense, electrical. So is the rest of you, and the same kind of tiny pulses that it copies are firing right now along the wires of your nervous system.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A pacemaker restarts a heart that has stopped, like the paddles in hospital shows.

Truth: That is a defibrillator. A pacemaker is a small device that sends gentle, regular pulses to keep a slow or uneven heart beating at the right speed. Some modern devices do both jobs.

Try this

Find your own pacemaker at work. Press two fingers gently on the inside of your wrist below your thumb and count the beats for 15 seconds, then multiply by four. Jog on the spot for a minute and count again. A healthy heart speeds up by itself, and modern pacemakers can sense movement and copy that.
