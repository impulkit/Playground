How do pulleys make lifting easy?

With enough pulleys and rope, you could lift a small car by hand. You would just have to pull a very long rope.

The short answer

A pulley is a wheel with a groove around its edge for a rope. By itself, a single pulley fixed to a beam doesn't make a load lighter. What it does is let you pull down to lift something up. That's easier, because you can use your own body weight to help. This is how a flag goes up a flagpole and how a bucket comes up a village well.

The real magic happens when a pulley moves with the load. Now the load hangs from two lengths of rope instead of one, and each holds half the weight. Add more pulleys and more rope, and the load is shared between even more strands. With four strands, you pull with only a quarter of the force. The catch is that you must pull four times as much rope.

Surprising but true

A pulley never saves you work. It lets you use a smaller pull over a longer distance, so the total effort comes out the same, or a little more because of friction.

Trading force for distance

Scientists call the amount of help a machine gives its mechanical advantage. A lever gets it by having a long arm and a short arm. A set of pulleys gets it by sharing the weight between many strands of rope.

Here's the trade in numbers. Say you want to lift a 100 kg crate onto a truck, 1 metre up. With a single fixed pulley you pull 1 metre of rope with the force of 100 kg, more than most adults can manage. With a set of pulleys that gives four strands, you pull with the force of about 25 kg, but you pull 4 metres of rope. The same work gets done, just spread over a longer distance. A little extra is always lost to friction in the wheels.

Archimedes and the ship

The Greek thinker Archimedes, who lived in Syracuse in Sicily over 2,200 years ago, studied levers and pulleys. The writer Plutarch tells a story about him written down about three centuries later. King Hiero challenged Archimedes to show what his machines could do. Archimedes rigged up a system of pulleys and, sitting calmly, drew a fully loaded ship onto the shore with one hand.

We can't be sure the story happened exactly that way. But the physics is sound. With enough pulleys, one person really can move something far heavier than themselves. It just takes a lot of rope and patience.

Pulleys all around you

Look up at a building site and you will see pulleys on top of tall cranes. Steel cables run over them, so the crane's motor can lift tonnes of concrete. Lifts in tall buildings hang from steel ropes running over a big pulley wheel, with a heavy counterweight on the other side to balance the car.

Sailing ships once carried hundreds of pulleys, called blocks, so sailors could raise heavy sails by hand. Gyms use pulleys in weight machines. Window blinds and old-style curtain cords use them too.

A pulley in every village

Many Indian villages drew water from deep open wells. A rope ran over a simple pulley, often called a ghirni in Hindi, fixed to a frame above the well. The pulley let people pull downward with their body weight instead of hauling each pot straight up, hand over hand.

The builders of India's great stone temples and Egypt's pyramids probably used ramps, levers and simple lifting frames to raise heavy blocks. Exactly how they did it is not always recorded. Whatever the method, they were using the same rule a pulley follows: a smaller push, spread over a longer distance, can move almost anything.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pulleys and other machines give you free energy, so you do less work.

Truth: Machines trade force for distance. A pulley lets you pull with less force, but you have to pull more rope. The work done, force times distance, stays the same or gets a little bigger because of friction.

Try this

Make a pulley with a broom handle. Ask two friends to hold a broom handle each, about a metre apart. Tie a rope to one handle, then loop it back and forth around both handles four or five times. Pull the loose end, and you can drag your two strong friends together, however hard they resist.
