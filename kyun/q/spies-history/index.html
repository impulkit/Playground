How did spies work in the past?

In the Second World War, an army unit fooled enemy spies with fake tanks made of rubber that a handful of soldiers could lift.

The short answer

Spies have always tried to find out what the other side is planning, without being caught. For most of history, they did it with eyes, ears and memory. They counted soldiers and ships, listened to gossip in markets, read stolen letters and sent messages in code or invisible ink.

As technology changed, so did spying. Pigeons carried notes across enemy lines. Radios sent secret messages across Europe. Codebreakers used early computing machines to read enemy signals. But the basic job stayed the same: learn secrets, pass them on, and stay hidden.

Surprising but true

George Washington's spies in New York used invisible ink, a code book and even a washing line, where certain clothes hung out sent signals.

Chanakya's network

More than 2,000 years ago in India, the Arthashastra, a book on running a kingdom linked to Chanakya, adviser to the Mauryan emperor Chandragupta, described spies in great detail. Its spies could pose as monks, students, merchants, farmers or wandering performers.

The book advised kings to have spies watch enemies, foreign courts and even their own ministers. It suggested checking reports from several spies, so no single liar could fool the king. Similar advice appears in China's Art of War, traditionally credited to Sun Tzu, which says knowing the enemy wins battles.

Washington's secret ring

During the American Revolution, George Washington needed to know what the British army was doing in New York. From 1778, a group called the Culper Ring sent him reports. Its members included a farmer, a tavern keeper and a shopkeeper.

They used a code book with numbers for important words. Washington was 711. They wrote with an invisible "sympathetic stain". One story says Anna Strong, a member, signalled where to meet by hanging a black petticoat and a number of handkerchiefs on her washing line. Their reports helped warn of British plans.

Spies for freedom

During the American Civil War, Harriet Tubman, who had escaped slavery, worked as a scout and spy for the Union Army. In 1863 she helped lead the Combahee River Raid in South Carolina, which freed more than 700 enslaved people. Her knowledge of hidden paths and her network of contacts made it possible.

How was the Enigma code cracked?The German Enigma machine could be set up in about 159 quintillion ways, and every night at midnight its settings changed.

Radios and codebreakers

In the Second World War, spying became huge. Noor Inayat Khan, a descendant of Tipu Sultan, was born in Moscow and grew up in Paris and London. In 1943 she became a secret radio operator for Britain in Nazi-occupied Paris. For months she moved from flat to flat, sending messages while the Gestapo hunted her. She was captured and killed in 1944, and received the George Cross for her bravery.

At Bletchley Park in England, thousands of codebreakers worked to read German messages scrambled by the Enigma machine. Pigeons carried messages too, and some won medals.

Fooling the spies

Spying also works in reverse. Armies try to trick the other side's spies. In 1944 and 1945, a secret US unit nicknamed the Ghost Army used inflatable rubber tanks, recorded truck sounds and fake radio messages. The aim was to make the Germans think a big army was somewhere it was not. A rubber tank could be pumped up in minutes and carried by a few soldiers, a cousin of the party balloon.

In medieval Japan, shinobi did secret scouting and spying for warlords. Later stories turned them into the black-clad ninjas of films, who are far more magical than the real thing ever was.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Spies in history were mostly glamorous agents in disguise, like in films.

Truth: Most spying was slow and ordinary work, like counting ships, copying letters, listening in markets and passing notes. Many of the most useful spies were shopkeepers, servants, farmers and radio operators whom nobody suspected.

Try this

Write a secret message with a cotton bud dipped in milk on white paper and let it dry. To read it, rub a soft pencil lightly over the paper, or press a dark crayon on its side across it. The dried milk shows up as a different shade. No heat is needed.
