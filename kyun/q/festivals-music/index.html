What happens at a music festival?

Every January, hundreds of musicians sit together on the banks of the Kaveri river and sing the same five songs at once, to honour a composer who died in 1847.

The short answer

A music festival is a gathering where many musicians perform over one or more days, often on several stages. Some are held in fields with tents, food stalls and crowds of tens of thousands. Others take place in concert halls, temple grounds or along river banks.

India and the USA both have famous festivals. In 1969, the Woodstock festival in New York State drew about 400,000 people for three days of rock and folk music. In Chennai, the December Music Season fills dozens of halls with Carnatic music for weeks, as it has since 1927.

Surprising but true

About 400,000 people turned up to the Woodstock festival in New York State in 1969, far more than expected. Roads jammed for kilometres and many people simply walked in.

Woodstock and the big field festival

Woodstock was held on a dairy farm in Bethel, New York, and the organisers expected far fewer people than came. About 400,000 turned up. Traffic stopped for kilometres, fences fell, and the organisers gave up selling tickets. It rained, the fields turned to mud, and food ran short.

Even so, the festival was remembered as peaceful, and it became a symbol of the 1960s. In England, the Glastonbury Festival began on a farm in 1970 and now draws around 200,000 people. Festivals like these mix rock, pop, folk and other styles on many stages at once.

Music on the river bank

Every January, musicians gather at Thiruvaiyaru in Tamil Nadu, on the banks of the Kaveri, for the Thyagaraja Aradhana. It honours Thyagaraja, one of the great composers of Carnatic music, who died there in 1847.

The highlight comes when hundreds of singers and instrumentalists sit together and perform his five Pancharatna kritis, or "five gems", in one huge group. Famous performers sit beside students. Thousands of listeners come, many sitting on the sand.

The Season in Chennai

In Chennai, the Madras Music Academy began holding a December conference in 1927. Today, December and early January are called "the Season". Hundreds of concerts take place in sabhas, or music halls, across the city, many of them free or cheap.

Fans hop from one hall to the next, and the canteens become almost as famous as the concerts. In Pune, the Sawai Gandharva festival, started in 1953 by the singer Bhimsen Joshi, does the same for Hindustani music. Some performances there have gone on all night.

How the sound reaches everyone

A big stage needs powerful sound equipment. Microphones pick up voices and instruments, a mixing desk balances them, and tall stacks of speakers send sound across the field. Sound fades as it spreads, and it travels at about 343 metres a second, as how sound travels explains.

So big festivals put extra speakers, called delay towers, far back in the crowd. They are timed to play a fraction of a second late, so the sound from the stage and the tower arrive together. Without them, people at the back would hear an echo.

Looking after a crowd

A festival is like a small town that pops up for a few days. Organisers plan water taps, toilets, first-aid tents, lost-children points and ways out in an emergency. Some fields are planned weeks ahead, down to where each food stall goes.

Loud music can damage hearing, especially close to the speakers. Many festivals now hand out earplugs, and children often wear ear defenders. Your ears can recover from a little noise, but very loud sound for hours can cause lasting harm. In Nagaland, the Hornbill Festival, held every December since 2000, mixes rock concerts with traditional Naga music and dance.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Music festivals are a modern Western invention.

Truth: People have gathered for days of music for centuries. In India, the Thyagaraja Aradhana has been held in Thiruvaiyaru since the 1800s, and temple festivals with music go back much further.

Try this

Stand at one end of a playground or long corridor while a friend claps two pieces of wood or two books together at the other end. Watch their hands closely. On a big enough field, you may notice the sound arrives a tiny bit after you see the clap.
