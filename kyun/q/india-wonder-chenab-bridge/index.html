How was the world's highest railway arch bridge built?

Trains in Jammu and Kashmir now cross a steel arch 359 metres above the Chenab river, higher than the top of the Eiffel Tower.

The short answer

The Chenab Rail Bridge crosses a deep Himalayan gorge in the Reasi district of Jammu and Kashmir. Its deck stands 359 metres above the river. That is about 30 metres higher than the tip of the Eiffel Tower, and as tall as a 120-storey building. It is the highest railway arch bridge in the world.

Engineers built it from about 28,000 tonnes of steel. The great arch spans 467 metres in a single leap, with no support in the middle. Building it took years of work on steep, crumbling slopes, with giant cable cranes, careful rock studies and a lot of patience.

Prime Minister Narendra Modi opened it on 6 June 2025. Vande Bharat trains now run across it between Katra and Srinagar.

Surprising but true

Before the arch was finished, its two halves hung out over the gorge for months, held up only by steel cables, until they met in the middle in 2021.

Why build it here?

For more than a century, the Kashmir Valley had no railway link to the rest of India. Roads over the mountains could close for days in winter because of snow and landslides. The bridge is part of a long rail line that winds through 36 main tunnels and hundreds of bridges to connect the valley.

The Chenab gorge was the hardest gap of all. The river runs at the bottom of a deep, narrow valley. Building tall piers down there would have been slow and risky, so the engineers chose an arch instead. An arch pushes its weight sideways and down into the rock on each side of the gorge, like a person bracing their arms against a doorway.

Hanging out over the gorge

Work began in 2004, but stopped in 2008 over worries about the steep slopes and strong winds. Engineers from India and abroad, including experts from IISc Bengaluru, studied the rock again. Work restarted in 2010 with stronger foundations and slopes held in place by anchors and concrete.

To build the arch, two cable cranes were strung between tall towers on both sides of the valley. They worked like a giant ropeway, carrying steel pieces out over empty air. The arch grew outward from each bank at the same time. Each half was tied back with steel cables so it wouldn't fall while it was still incomplete.

In April 2021 the two halves finally met in the middle, high above the river. Workers called the last piece the "golden joint".

Ready for wind, quakes and cold

The Himalaya is still rising, and this region has strong earthquakes. The bridge was designed to survive the most severe kind of shaking expected in the area. It can also stand up to winds of about 260 km/h, faster than the strongest cyclone gusts most coastal cities ever see.

Sensors on the bridge watch the wind all the time. If gusts get too strong, signals stop trains from crossing until it calms down. The arch is made of hollow steel boxes, and some were filled with concrete to make it heavier and steadier in the wind.

Winter temperatures here can fall well below freezing. Steel shrinks in cold and stretches in heat, so the bridge has joints that let it move a little without cracking. It is designed to last about 120 years.

Taller than Paris

The Eiffel Tower is about 330 metres tall to its tip. Stand it on the riverbed under the Chenab Bridge and a train would still pass about 30 metres above its top.

For people who live nearby, the bridge means faster, safer journeys in every season. For visitors, the view from a train window is dizzying. The river looks like a thin blue-green ribbon far below. The Vande Bharat trains on this route were also adapted for the cold, with heated windscreens and pipes that don't freeze.

And the strangest part? India's longest railway tunnel in the Himalaya lies on this same line, part of a whole family of record-breaking spans and tunnels you can explore in the world's longest bridges.

Myth or fact?

Myth: An arch bridge is weakest at the top of its curve, so it needs pillars in the middle.

Truth: A well-built arch turns weight into a squeeze that flows down both sides into the ground. The Chenab arch carries its trains across 467 metres without any pillar standing in the river.

Try this

Make an arch from a strip of thin card bent between two stacks of books. Press gently on top, then add a small weight. Now try a flat strip of card with no arch. Notice how the arch pushes into the books and holds far more.
