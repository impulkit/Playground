How do organ transplants work?

The first lasting organ transplant worked because the patient and the donor were identical twins, so the body didn't know the new kidney was a stranger.

The short answer

In a transplant, surgeons take a healthy organ from one person, the donor, and put it into someone whose own organ has failed. Kidneys, livers, hearts, lungs, the pancreas and the corneas of the eye can all be transplanted.

The surgery itself is hard but possible. The bigger challenge is the immune system, the body's defence force. It attacks anything it sees as foreign, including a donated organ. So doctors match donors and patients as closely as they can, and patients take medicines afterwards to stop their bodies rejecting the new organ.

Surprising but true

Part of a liver can be given by a living person, and both pieces, the one kept and the one given, grow back to almost full size within a few months.

The twins who made history

For years, doctors tried transplanting kidneys, but the patients' bodies always rejected them. Then, in 1954, a young man named Richard Herrick was dying of kidney failure in Boston. He had an identical twin, Ronald.

Because identical twins share the same genes, the surgeon Joseph Murray reasoned that Richard's body might accept Ronald's kidney. On 23 December 1954, he transplanted it. It worked, and Richard lived for eight more years. Ronald lived a long life with just one kidney. Murray won the Nobel Prize in 1990 for this work.

Fooling the immune system

Most people don't have an identical twin, so transplants needed a way to quiet the immune system. In the 1960s and 1970s, scientists developed medicines that did this. A drug called cyclosporine, which came into wide use in the early 1980s, made transplants far more successful.

Doctors also check how well donor and patient match. First comes blood group. Then they compare tissue markers, proteins on the surface of cells that act like a name badge. The closer the match, the less the immune system complains. Even so, almost every transplant patient takes anti-rejection medicine for life.

How has medicine changed over time?Before 1846, there was no reliable way to put a patient to sleep for surgery. The best surgeons were simply the fastest.

Living and deceased donors

Some organs can come from a living person. You have two kidneys but can live well with one, so parents, brothers, sisters and husbands or wives often donate a kidney. Part of the liver can be given too, because it regrows.

One of the oldest kinds of transplant is the cornea, the clear window at the front of the eye. The first successful one was done in 1905 by Eduard Zirm, in what is now the Czech Republic. Corneas don't have blood vessels, so the immune system rarely attacks them. They can be donated within a few hours after a person dies, even at home, and eye banks across India collect them to restore sight.

Hearts, lungs and most other organs come from people who have died, usually in hospital after brain death, when machines are still keeping their blood flowing. With the family's agreement, one such donor can save up to eight lives. In Indian cities, police sometimes clear a "green corridor" through traffic so an ambulance carrying a donated heart can cross town in minutes. Speed matters, because a heart lasts only about four to six hours outside a body.

Too few organs

India carried out more than 18,900 transplants in 2024, a record and among the highest numbers in the world. Yet far more people need one. Most donated kidneys and livers in India come from living relatives, and organ donation after death is still rare compared with countries like Spain or the USA. India's 1994 transplant law makes buying or selling organs a crime.

To help, doctors are exploring new sources. In 2024, surgeons in Boston gave a living man a kidney from a pig whose genes had been edited to make it more human-friendly. Other teams are growing tiny organ-like tissues in labs. In the meantime, the simplest help comes from people who choose to be organ donors and tell their families, so that their wishes can be followed.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Organs can be taken from anyone who dies, at any time.

Truth: Most organs, such as hearts and lungs, can only come from people who die in hospital, usually after brain death, while machines keep blood flowing. Their families must agree. Corneas and some tissues can be given in more situations.

Try this

Find out your blood group if your family knows it, and ask a few relatives theirs. Make a chart of who could give blood to whom, using the rule that group O can give to all, and AB can receive from all. Matching blood groups is also one of the first checks for an organ transplant.
