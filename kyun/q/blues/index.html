Where did the blues come from?

Around 1903, a bandleader waiting for a late train in Mississippi heard a man sliding a knife along his guitar strings, and never forgot the sound.

The short answer

The blues is a kind of music created by African Americans in the Deep South of the United States, especially in the Mississippi Delta, in the late 1800s and early 1900s. It grew out of the work songs, field hollers and spirituals sung by enslaved people and their descendants, which carried musical ideas from West Africa.

A blues song is often about hard times: poverty, loneliness, lost love, unfair treatment or travelling far from home. It has a recognisable sound, with "blue" notes that bend between the usual pitches, a simple repeating chord pattern and a call-and-response feel. The blues became the root of jazz, rhythm and blues, rock and roll and much of the popular music heard today.

Surprising but true

The British band the Rolling Stones named themselves after a song by the Mississippi blues singer Muddy Waters.

Songs from the fields

Enslaved Africans in America were forbidden many things, but they kept making music. Working in cotton fields, they sang together to keep time, share news and lift their spirits. One person would call out a line and the others would answer.

After slavery ended in 1865, life for Black Americans in the South remained very hard, with unfair laws, low pay and violence. Field workers sang long, wavering "hollers" across the fields. Churches sang spirituals. From these sounds, a new style of song emerged, sung by one person with a guitar, harmonica or piano.

The Tutwiler train station

The bandleader W.C. Handy later wrote about a night around 1903, at a station in Tutwiler, Mississippi. Waiting for a delayed train, he heard a man pressing a knife against his guitar strings and sliding it, so the strings moaned. The man was singing about going "where the Southern cross the Dog", a place where two railway lines met.

Handy described it as the strangest music he had ever heard. He began writing down and arranging blues tunes. His "Memphis Blues" (1912) and "St. Louis Blues" (1914) became hits, and he became known as the Father of the Blues.

The 12-bar pattern

Many blues songs follow a pattern called the 12-bar blues: twelve short sections of music built on three chords, repeated again and again. The words often follow an A-A-B shape. The singer sings a line, repeats it, then answers with a new line.

The "blue notes" are what give the music its ache. Singers and guitarists bend certain notes slightly flat, between the keys on a piano. Listeners trained in Indian classical music sometimes hear something familiar in these sliding, in-between sounds.

Records and the big cities

In 1920, Mamie Smith recorded "Crazy Blues". It sold hugely, and record companies rushed to record Black singers. Women like Ma Rainey, called the "Mother of the Blues", and Bessie Smith became stars.

Between about 1910 and 1970, millions of Black Southerners moved north in what is called the Great Migration. Musicians went too. In Chicago, Muddy Waters plugged in an electric guitar and made the blues louder and tougher, so it could be heard in noisy clubs.

The blues around the world

In the 1960s, young British musicians fell in love with American blues records. Bands such as the Rolling Stones, who named themselves after a Muddy Waters song, brought blues-based rock back to America. B.B. King toured the world for decades with his guitar, which he called Lucille. Today the blues is honoured with museums in Mississippi and Memphis, and its twelve bars sit underneath countless songs on the radio.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The blues is always sad music.

Truth: Blues songs often deal with hard times, but many are funny, boastful, flirty or made for dancing. Singing about trouble was also a way of getting through it, and many listeners say the blues makes them feel better, not worse.

Try this

Write your own blues verse about something small that went wrong, such as missing the school bus. Sing the first line, sing it again, then add a third line that answers it. Clap a slow, steady beat while you sing and let your voice slide between notes.
