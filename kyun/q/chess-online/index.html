How did online chess become so popular?

A game invented in India about 1,500 years ago now has one website with more than 250 million members, more than the population of Brazil.

The short answer

Chess is very old, but for most of history you needed a partner sitting across the board. The internet changed that. Now you can open an app and get a game in seconds against someone in Chennai, Chicago or Cairo, at any hour.

Several things then pushed chess online into a boom: lockdowns in 2020, a hit TV show, games short enough to play on a bus, and stars who streamed their games live. Chess.com, the biggest site, passed 100 million members in December 2022 and more than 250 million by 2026. India has become one of the busiest chess countries of all.

Surprising but true

In January 2023, so many new players rushed to Chess.com at once that its servers struggled to keep up.

From letters to apps

People played chess at a distance long before computers. In correspondence chess, players posted one move at a time by letter, and a single game could take more than a year. In the 1990s, websites such as the Internet Chess Club let people play live over slow dial-up connections.

Chess.com started in 2007. Lichess, a free site run as a charity with no adverts, followed in 2010. Smartphones then put a chessboard in millions of pockets. Fast formats suit phones well: in bullet chess each player has just one minute for the whole game, so every move is a scramble.

What is artificial intelligence?In 1997, a computer beat the world chess champion. Today AI can chat, draw and translate, yet it can still make mistakes a child wouldn't.

The year everyone was at home

In 2020, schools and offices closed during the COVID-19 pandemic. People looked for games they could play from home, and chess was ready. Then, in October, Netflix released The Queen's Gambit, a drama about a fictional chess prodigy in 1960s America. Chess sets sold out in some shops, and millions joined chess sites.

That same year, the first Online Chess Olympiad was held. After a final disrupted by internet problems, the gold medal was shared by India and Russia. In early 2023, a fresh rush of players crowded onto Chess.com so quickly that its servers struggled under the load.

Chess becomes a show

Chess turned into something people watch as well as play. Grandmasters began streaming their games with live chat, talking through each move and joking with viewers. Big online events invited comedians and gamers to compete, which pulled in fans who had never touched a chessboard. Some of this looks a lot like esports, with commentators, prize money and fan clubs.

Computers changed the game too. Free chess engines, programs far stronger than any human, now check every game in seconds and show the best moves. That helps beginners learn fast. It also creates a problem: some people secretly use engines to cheat, so sites run detection systems to catch suspicious play.

What your rating means

Online chess gives every player a number called a rating. It goes up when you win and down when you lose, and beating a stronger player earns more points than beating a weaker one. The idea comes from Arpad Elo, a Hungarian-American physics teacher whose system was adopted by the world chess federation in 1970.

Because sites match you with people of a similar rating, most games feel close, win or lose. That keeps players coming back for one more game. The very best grandmasters have classical ratings above 2,700, while a keen beginner might start around a few hundred on some sites.

India's chess wave

Chess was born in India, in an early form called chaturanga, so its new popularity here feels like a homecoming. Viswanathan Anand became India's first grandmaster in 1988 and later world champion, inspiring a generation.

Many of that generation grew up playing thousands of online games. In December 2024, D. Gukesh of Chennai won the world championship at 18, the youngest undisputed champion in history. At the Chess Olympiad in Budapest that year, India won gold in both the open and women's sections. In 2025, Divya Deshmukh won the Women's World Cup. All of them trained with screens as well as boards.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You have to be a maths genius to be good at chess.

Truth: Studies have found only a modest link between chess skill and test scores like IQ. Strong players get strong mostly through lots of play and study, which helps them recognise patterns on the board quickly.

Try this

Play Chess960 with a friend or family member. Set up the pawns as usual, but shuffle the back-row pieces into a random order, with the same order for both sides and the king between the two rooks. Nobody can rely on memorised openings, so you both have to think from the very first move.
