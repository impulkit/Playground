How is papad made?

In 1959, seven women on a Mumbai rooftop borrowed 80 rupees and started making papads. Their business grew into one of India's best-known brands.

The short answer

Papad is a thin, crisp disc made from lentil flour. Most often it's made from urad dal (black gram) flour, mixed with salt, spices like black pepper or cumin, and a little water to make a stiff dough. The dough is beaten, divided into small balls, and rolled out paper-thin. Then the discs are dried in the sun until hard.

Dry papad keeps for months. To eat it, you roast it over a flame, heat it in a microwave, or fry it in hot oil. It puffs, blisters and turns crisp in seconds. Papad is served with meals across India, from Rajasthani thalis to South Indian banana-leaf meals, where it's called appalam or pappadam.

Surprising but true

Lijjat Papad, started by seven women in 1959, is run as a cooperative in which the women members are owners, not employees.

From dough to disc

Papad dough must be stiff and smooth. Makers pound or knead it for a long time, sometimes on a stone. A pinch of papad khar, an alkaline salt, is often added. It helps the papad expand and crisp up when cooked.

The dough is rolled into thin circles, sometimes just a millimetre thick. Expert makers can roll dozens an hour, each one perfectly round. The discs are then laid out on cloth or mats in the sun.

Why the sun matters

Drying removes most of the water, so microbes can't grow. That's why papad lasts so long. On a hot, dry day, papads may dry in a few hours. In damp weather, it can take much longer.

Many families make a year's supply of papad in summer, when the sun is strong. Women in villages and towns often work together on rooftops and courtyards, rolling and drying hundreds of papads at once.

The papad and the cooperative

On 15 March 1959, seven women from Girgaum in Mumbai borrowed 80 rupees and began making papads on a rooftop. They wanted to earn money from home, using a skill they already had.

Their business became Shri Mahila Griha Udyog Lijjat Papad. It's a cooperative, which means the members own the business together and share its profits. Women join as members, often making papads at home and bringing them to a local branch.

Today Lijjat has tens of thousands of women members and branches across India. It has received national awards and is often held up as an example of women building a business on their own terms.

How it puffs

Even dry papad has a little water left inside. When heated, that water quickly turns to steam, pushing the dough up into bubbles and blisters. As the steam escapes, the papad becomes light and crunchy.

A microwave heats the water inside the papad directly, so it puffs without any oil. Frying makes it puff more and taste richer, but it soaks up oil. Roasting over a flame gives a smoky, slightly charred taste.

Many kinds of papad

Not all papad is made from urad dal. Moong dal papads are lighter. Rice papads are white and delicate. Sago papads puff up like clouds. Potato papads are a favourite in parts of North India.

Bikaner in Rajasthan is famous for spicy papads with black pepper. In the south, small, thick appalams are fried until puffed, then crushed over rice and rasam. In Gujarat, papad is sometimes cooked into a curry called papad nu shaak.

Papad around the world

Indian restaurants in the UK and the USA often serve "poppadoms" with chutneys before a meal. The name comes from the South Indian pappadam. For many people abroad, it's their first taste of Indian food. It's a thin disc of lentil, dried in the sun, that started in Indian homes and rooftops.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Papad is always deep-fried, so it is always oily.

Truth: Papad can be fried, but many families roast it directly over a gas flame or in a microwave, with no oil at all. Roasted papad is light and crisp.

Try this

With an adult, roast one papad in the microwave for about 30 to 45 seconds and watch through the door. Notice where the bubbles start. Then compare it with a piece roasted carefully over a flame by the adult. Which one is crisper, and which puffs more evenly?
