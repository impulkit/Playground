What happens at a world climate summit?

Once a year, almost every country on Earth sends people to one city to argue about the weather of the future. Nothing can be agreed unless nearly everyone says yes.

The short answer

A world climate summit is a giant meeting where almost every country tries to agree on how to slow down global warming and cope with its effects. It is called a COP, short for Conference of the Parties, and it happens once a year in a different city.

Leaders, ministers, scientists, businesses and campaigners all come. For about two weeks, they argue over targets, money and fairness. Nothing is final until every country accepts the result.

Surprising but true

Decisions at a COP are made by consensus. There is no vote, so in theory a single country can block a deal that more than 190 others want.

How it works

The "parties" are the countries that signed a treaty on climate in 1992, under the United Nations. Today there are 198 of them, counting the European Union. Each year, one country hosts, and its minister acts as president, steering the talks.

The first days are for speeches and big announcements. Then the real work begins in small rooms. Officials go through draft texts line by line. A single word can take hours, because "must" means something very different from "should".

Think of planning a class trip where every student has a veto. Someone wants the beach, someone wants the hills, someone cannot pay much. You keep rewriting the plan until nobody blocks it. That is how a COP works, and why it often runs late into the final night.

A COP site is split in two. The official zone, run by the UN, is where governments negotiate. Next door, a public zone fills with stalls, talks and exhibitions from companies, cities, scientists and young campaigners. Tens of thousands of people can attend a single summit.

Most countries also join groups so their voice is louder. India often works with other developing countries, including a group with Brazil, South Africa and China.

Where it came from

By the 1980s, scientists were warning that greenhouse gases from burning coal, oil and gas were trapping heat around the Earth, upsetting the natural carbon cycle. In 1992, at the Earth Summit in Rio de Janeiro, countries signed the UN climate treaty. The first COP met in Berlin in 1995.

In 1997, the Kyoto Protocol set targets for rich countries only. In 2015 came the Paris Agreement, the most important deal so far. Every country agreed to try to keep warming well below 2°C, and to aim for 1.5°C.

Under Paris, each country writes its own climate plan and makes it stronger every five years. At later COPs, countries agreed to set up a fund for "loss and damage" in 2022 and to "transition away" from fossil fuels in 2023.

India's story

India hosted COP8 in New Delhi in 2002. At COP26 in Glasgow in 2021, India promised to reach net zero, where it adds no extra greenhouse gases to the air overall, by 2070. It also promised that half its power capacity would come from clean sources by 2030.

India reached that clean-power goal in 2025, five years early, thanks mainly to solar power, wind and hydropower.

India usually makes one argument very strongly. Rich countries burned fossil fuels for about 150 years to grow wealthy, so they caused most of the warming. An average Indian today releases about 2 tonnes of carbon dioxide a year, well under half the world average. India says rich nations should cut faster and pay to help poorer ones.

Indians also feel the effects up close: harsher heatwaves, heavier floods, melting Himalayan glaciers and rising seas along a long coastline.

The numbers that matter

  • - 1.5°C: the warming limit countries aim for. Earth is already about 1.3°C warmer than before factories. 2024 was the first full year above 1.5°C.
  • - USD 100 billion: what rich countries promised in 2009 to give poorer ones each year for climate action. They first reached it in 2022, two years late.
  • - USD 300 billion a year by 2035: the new goal agreed in 2024. Many developing countries called it far too small.
  • - USD 305 billion a year: what a 2026 UN report says poorer countries will need by 2035 just to adapt, for things like sea walls and heat-proof crops.
  • - USD 20 million: the first grant approved by the loss and damage fund, set aside for Nepal in 2026 after deadly floods. Nepal says rebuilding will cost billions.

What people argue about

The biggest fight is about money and fairness. Poorer countries say rich ones caused the problem and should pay more, mostly as grants rather than loans. Rich countries say fast-growing economies, including China and India, now produce a lot of emissions and should give more too.

Another argument is about speed. Island nations, which could vanish under rising seas, want fast and firm cuts. Countries that depend on coal or oil for jobs and electricity want more time.

People also argue about whether COPs work at all. Critics point out that emissions are still rising after 30 summits. Supporters say that, without them, warming would be far worse, and that clean energy has become cheap partly because these deals pushed countries to invest.

Myths and mix-ups

"COPs make laws that every country must obey." No. Most of the Paris Agreement relies on each country's own promises, checked in public, without fines.

"Only presidents and prime ministers go." Leaders usually come for a day or two. Most of the hard work is done by ministers and expert officials who stay for the full two weeks.

"Climate summits are only about pollution." They are also about adapting to heat and floods, protecting forests, helping farmers and sharing technology that is cleaner and more sustainable.

What it means for you

What is agreed at a COP slowly shapes everyday life. It affects how India makes electricity, which can change your power bill. It shapes whether new buses run on diesel or batteries, and which crops farmers grow as monsoons change.

Climate money can pay for flood warnings in Assam, cyclone shelters in Odisha and cooler roofs in hot cities. Each of these can protect families like yours.

And the children growing up now will live with the results longest. That is one reason young people across the world, including in India, speak up at these summits. Every COP decision is really about what the 2050s and 2070s will feel like.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Countries at a climate summit sign rules that force them to cut pollution, with fines if they fail.

Truth: Under the Paris Agreement, each country sets its own target and reports on its progress. There are no fines. Pressure comes from other countries, from public opinion and from regular check-ups called the global stocktake.

Try this

Hold a mini COP at home or in class. Give each person a role, like a rich country, a small island or a fast-growing country. Try to agree on one rule, such as "everyone cuts plastic use by half". Remember, nobody can be outvoted. See how long it takes.
