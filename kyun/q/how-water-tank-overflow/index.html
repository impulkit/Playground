How does a toilet cistern know when to stop?

Every toilet cistern hides a floating "brain" that rises with the water and shuts the tap at exactly the right moment, with no electricity at all.

The short answer

Inside a toilet cistern is a float, a hollow ball or plastic cup that bobs on top of the water. It is joined to the valve that lets water in from the pipe. When you flush, the cistern empties, the float drops and the valve opens. Water rushes in. As the water rises, the float rises with it. When the water reaches the right level, the float pushes the valve closed, and the hissing stops.

There is no electricity, no sensor and no chip. The cistern uses buoyancy, the upward push of water on things that float, and a simple lever. Just in case the valve fails, an overflow pipe carries extra water safely away.

Surprising but true

The same floating-valve idea is used in many rooftop water tanks in India, in cattle troughs on farms and in the carburettors of older cars and scooters.

The ball on a stick

In older cisterns, you can see a plastic or copper ball on the end of a long metal arm. This is called a ball cock. The arm is a lever. The ball sits on the long end, and the valve on the short end.

Because of the lever, a small upward push from the float becomes a strong push on the valve, enough to hold back water at mains pressure. Newer cisterns use a tall plastic tube with a float that slides up and down it, but the idea is the same.

You can adjust the water level by bending the arm or turning a screw. A lower level saves water with every flush.

What happens when you flush

When you press the handle or button, it lifts a seal, called a flapper or a flush valve, from a hole in the bottom of the cistern. Water pours down into the bowl, pushing waste through the S-shaped bend and into the drain.

When the cistern is nearly empty, the seal falls back into place. Now the float, sitting low, has opened the fill valve. Water refills both the cistern and, through a small tube, the bowl. The bowl must keep some water to block smells coming up from the sewers.

The bend that blocks the smell

In 1775, a London watchmaker named Alexander Cumming patented the S-bend, a curved pipe under the toilet that always holds a little water. That water plugs the pipe, so sewer gases can't come up. Every toilet, sink and drain today still uses a version of his idea.

Even earlier, in the 1590s, an English courtier named John Harington built a flushing toilet for Queen Elizabeth I. It didn't catch on. People needed piped water and sewers first, and those came hundreds of years later.

Half a flush or a full one

Many modern toilets have two buttons. The smaller one releases about 3 litres, the bigger one about 6. This dual flush system was developed in Australia around 1980, during a time of water shortages. Older toilets used as much as 9 to 12 litres every flush.

In India, the Bureau of Indian Standards sets rules for cistern sizes, and many new buildings use dual flush to save water. In dry cities like Chennai, every litre matters.

The float everywhere

Look inside a rooftop water tank in an Indian house, and you may find the same floating valve, stopping the pump's water from overflowing. Farm water troughs use it to refill for cattle. The carburettors of older cars and scooters used a tiny float too, to keep just the right amount of petrol ready for the engine.

Floating works because of a rule the Greek thinker Archimedes figured out more than 2,000 years ago, which also explains how huge steel ships stay afloat. And the toilet itself has an even longer story, going back thousands of years to the ancient toilets of Mohenjo-daro and Rome.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Thomas Crapper invented the flushing toilet.

Truth: Thomas Crapper was a real plumber in London in the late 1800s, and he sold toilets and patented some improvements. But flushing toilets existed long before him. Alexander Cumming patented the S-bend in 1775, and John Harington built a flushing toilet in the 1590s.

Try this

Build a float valve model. Push a drinking straw through a small hole near the bottom of a plastic cup and seal it with clay so water can flow in. Tie a small piece of sponge or a bottle cap to a pencil laid across the cup's rim like a see-saw, with the other end over the straw. Lower the cup into a bowl of water and watch the float rise and press on the straw.
