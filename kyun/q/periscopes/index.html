How does a periscope see around corners?

Two mirrors in a tube are enough to see over a wall, and for a hundred years they were a submarine's only eyes above the waves.

The short answer

A periscope is a tube with a mirror at each end. The top mirror is tilted at 45 degrees, facing out over the thing you want to see past, such as a wall or a crowd. Light from the scene hits it and bounces straight down the tube. At the bottom, a second mirror, also at 45 degrees, bounces the light out sideways into your eye.

The light has gone down and around a corner, so you can see over things while staying hidden or lower down. Its name comes from Greek words that mean "to look around".

Big periscopes on submarines add lenses and prisms to make a sharper, magnified view.

Surprising but true

Writing seen through a simple two-mirror periscope isn't backwards, because the second mirror flips back what the first one reversed.

Light that bounces

Light travels in straight lines until it hits something. A smooth, shiny surface like a mirror sends it off again at the same angle it arrived. A mirror tilted at 45 degrees turns a beam of light through a right angle, like a car turning a corner.

Two such mirrors, one above the other, turn the light twice. First it goes down the tube, then out to your eye. Each mirror also reverses the picture once, so after two bounces it's the right way round again. That's why you can read a sign through a periscope.

You don't need to understand it to use it. Your eyes just see the light arriving and your brain shows you the world on the other side of the wall.

Periscopes in the trenches

During the First World War, soldiers lived in long ditches called trenches. Raising your head above the edge was very dangerous. Soldiers used simple box periscopes to watch the land in front without showing themselves. Some were made from wood and cheap mirrors. Others were long, folding brass tubes.

Some inventors fitted periscopes to rifles, so a soldier could aim without lifting his head. These trench periscopes are now in museums such as the Imperial War Museums in Britain.

Eyes for a submarine

For most of the 1900s, a submarine under the surface was almost blind. To look around, the captain brought it up to just below the waves and raised a long periscope through the top. It might stick out of the water only a little, hard for an enemy ship to spot.

Submarine periscopes are long, carefully built tubes. Instead of plain mirrors, they use glass prisms that bend light without blurring, and sets of lenses that magnify distant ships. The captain could turn the periscope in a full circle by walking round with it, gripping its handles.

Cameras take over

Many modern submarines no longer have a tube you look through. They use a mast fitted with cameras and sensors. It pokes above the water and sends pictures by cable to screens inside, where the whole crew can see them. Because the mast doesn't need to pass straight down into the hull, designers can put the control room wherever they like.

Periscopes haven't disappeared, though. Tanks have small ones so the crew can see out while staying protected. Some people use cardboard ones to see over crowds at parades and golf tournaments. Scientists studying nuclear reactors use long periscopes to look inside safely. And building a periscope from a carton and two mirrors is still one of the best ways to learn how light bounces.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A mirror flips things from left to right.

Truth: A mirror doesn't swap left and right. It reverses front and back. Your raised right hand stays on the right side in the mirror, but your reflection faces the other way, so it looks like a left hand to you.

Try this

Make a periscope from a long juice or milk carton and two small mirrors (an adult can cut the holes). Make one window near the top on one side and one near the bottom on the other. Tape a mirror at 45 degrees behind each window, facing each other. Use it to peek over a sofa or around a doorway.
