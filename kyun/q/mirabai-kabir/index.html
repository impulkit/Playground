Who were Mirabai and Kabir?

A weaver from Varanasi and a princess from Rajasthan wrote songs that Indians still sing, five hundred years later.

The short answer

Kabir and Mirabai were two of the best-loved poets of the Bhakti movement, a wave of devotion that spread across India between about the 7th and 17th centuries. Bhakti poets said that anyone could reach God through love, whatever their caste, religion or education.

Kabir was a weaver who lived in Varanasi in the 1400s. Mirabai was a princess from Rajasthan who lived in the early 1500s. They never met, and they were very different people. Both wrote in the everyday speech of ordinary people, not in Sanskrit, and both are still sung today.

Surprising but true

More than 200 of Kabir's verses are part of the Guru Granth Sahib, the holy book of the Sikhs.

Kabir, the weaver who asked hard questions

Kabir grew up in a family of Muslim weavers in Varanasi, one of the holiest cities for Hindus. He worked at a loom, and weaving shows up again and again in his poems.

He refused to take sides. He mocked empty rituals of both Hindus and Muslims, from pilgrimages to fasting, and said God was inside every person. That made him enemies among priests of both faiths. It also made him popular with weavers, farmers and labourers.

The two-line poem

Kabir's best-known form is the doha, a couplet of two rhyming lines. Many Indian children learn this one at school: "I went searching for a bad person and found none. When I searched my own heart, I found no one worse than me."

His language mixed several north Indian dialects, the ancestor of modern Hindi. A legend says that when he died at Maghar, Hindus wanted to cremate him and Muslims wanted to bury him. When they lifted the sheet, they found only flowers, and each group took half. It is a story about Kabir's message more than a record of what happened.

Mirabai, the princess who chose Krishna

Mirabai was born into a Rajput royal family in Merta, in present-day Rajasthan. As a child, the story goes, she was given a small image of Krishna and decided he was her true husband.

She was married into the royal family of Mewar, whose capital was Chittor. After her husband died, she refused to behave as a royal widow was expected to. She sang and danced in temples with wandering holy people. Legends say her in-laws tried to poison her, and the poison turned to nectar. In later life she is said to have lived in Vrindavan and then Dwarka, places linked with Krishna.

Songs that kept travelling

Mirabai's songs, called padas or bhajans, are about longing for Krishna, often as a beloved who is far away. They are full of everyday images: dark clouds, a dusty road, anklets ringing as she dances.

Hundreds of songs carry her name, though scholars think many were written later by others in her style. In 1945 the singer M. S. Subbulakshmi played Meera in a Tamil film and sang her songs, which carried them to new audiences across India.

Why they still matter

Both poets spoke against the walls between people, Kabir with sharp humour and Mira with open-hearted song. Their verses turn up in classical concerts, folk music, Bollywood films and school textbooks. Kabir's followers formed the Kabir Panth, which still exists. When a song has been passed from voice to voice for five centuries, it has proved that singing can be as lasting as stone.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Kabir wrote his poems down in a book.

Truth: He almost certainly composed them aloud. Collections such as the Bijak and the verses in the Guru Granth Sahib were put together by others, so scholars still argue over which lines are really his.

Try this

Learn one Kabir doha by heart, such as the one about looking for bad people. Then ask a grandparent or older relative if they know any Kabir or Mira songs. You may find a poem has travelled through your own family.
