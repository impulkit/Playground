What does average mean?

In the 1950s, the US Air Force measured about 4,000 pilots to design an "average" cockpit. Not one pilot was average.

The short answer

An average is one number that tries to sum up a whole group of numbers. The trouble is that there are several kinds, and they can give very different answers.

The mean is what most people call "the average". Add all the numbers and divide by how many there are. The median is the middle number when you line them all up in order. The mode is the number that turns up most often.

For many groups, like the heights of children in a class, all three come out close together. But when a few numbers are very big or very small, they can split far apart. Choosing which average to use can change the story completely.

Surprising but true

When the US Air Force checked about 4,000 pilots against the average for ten body measurements, not a single pilot was close to average on all ten.

Why the mean can fool you

Suppose ten people in a room each earn ₹30,000 a month. The mean is ₹30,000. Now a billionaire walks in. The mean suddenly shoots up to many crores, but nobody else in the room got any richer.

The median hardly changes. The middle person in the line is still earning ₹30,000. That is why reports about money often use the median income. It tells you more about a typical person.

The mode is useful in different ways. A shoe shop cares about which size sells most, not the mean shoe size. Nobody wants a shoe in size 7.3.

Averages on the cricket field

In cricket, a batter's average is the total runs scored divided by the number of times they were out. If you score 50, 0 and 100 and get out every time, your average is 150 divided by 3, which is 50.

The most famous average in sport belongs to the Australian batter Don Bradman. In 52 Test matches, he averaged 99.94. Most great batters finish their careers somewhere between about 45 and 60. Sachin Tendulkar, who played 200 Test matches, averaged a little under 54. Bradman needed just four runs in his final innings to average exactly 100. He was out for zero.

The cockpit that fitted nobody

In the late 1940s, the US Air Force had a problem. Too many pilots were losing control of their planes, and the machines themselves weren't faulty. Cockpits had been built to fit the average pilot of the 1920s.

A researcher named Gilbert Daniels measured about 4,000 pilots on ten body sizes, such as arm length, chest and leg length. Then he checked how many were close to average on all ten. The answer was none.

The Air Force changed course. Cockpits got adjustable seats, pedals and straps that could fit a wide range of bodies. Car seats and office chairs that slide and tilt come from the same idea. Nobody is average at everything, so good design has to bend.

Averages in everyday life

Weather forecasts talk about "average rainfall" for a month. Doctors compare a child's height and weight with averages to check they are growing well, while knowing healthy children come in many sizes. A school report might show your class's average mark next to yours.

An average is a useful shortcut. It just leaves out a lot. Two classes can have the same mean score, yet in one everyone scored about the same, and in the other half scored very high and half very low. To see the whole story, you also need to know how spread out the numbers are, and a good chart often shows that best.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The average is always the middle of a group.

Truth: That's true only for the median. The mean can sit far from the middle if a few values are very big or very small. Most people in a group can be below the mean, which sounds odd but happens often with money.

Try this

Measure the hand span of everyone in your family, from thumb tip to little finger tip. Work out the mean by adding them and dividing by how many people there are. Then line them up in order to find the median. Are the two numbers the same, or does one big or small hand pull the mean away?
