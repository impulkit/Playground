How does the archerfish shoot down insects?

A fish in the mangroves spits a jet of water so accurately that it can knock a beetle off a leaf more than a metre above the river.

The short answer

The archerfish swims just under the water's surface, watching leaves and branches above. When it spots an insect, it presses its tongue against a groove in the roof of its mouth to make a narrow tube. Then it snaps its gill covers shut, firing a jet of water out of its mouth.

The jet can reach up to about 2 m, and the fish rarely misses within a metre. The insect gets knocked into the water, and the archerfish swims in to grab it. Often the fish knows where the insect will land before it hits the surface.

Surprising but true

In a 2016 experiment, archerfish learned to recognise individual human faces on a screen and spit at the right one.

Aiming through a window

Hitting a bug from underwater is harder than it sounds. Light bends when it moves from air into water, a process called refraction. From below, the insect isn't quite where it seems to be.

Archerfish correct for this. They seem to learn the right correction for different angles, though they often line up nearly straight below a target, where the bend is smallest. Scientists who tested them in labs found they are very accurate at many angles.

They also shoot harder at bigger insects, which cling more tightly to leaves. A small fly needs a little jet. A heavy beetle needs a strong one.

The trick of the jet

A water jet usually breaks into drops quickly. The archerfish has a neat trick. It squeezes the water out slowly at first and faster at the end. The back of the jet catches up with the front, forming a blob of water right at the moment it reaches the target.

So the fish isn't throwing a thin stream, but more like a water bullet that bunches up mid-air. Engineers have studied this as a way to send liquid precisely over a distance. Its sea cousin, the pistol shrimp, uses water as a weapon in a very different way.

Learning from others

Young archerfish are bad shots. They get better with practice. In a famous 2006 study in Germany, researchers found that archerfish could learn to hit fast-moving targets just by watching other archerfish do it. Few fish are known to learn this way.

In 2016, scientists at Oxford trained archerfish to spit at photos of one human face on a screen above their tank. Shown dozens of new faces, the fish still picked out the right one most of the time. Their small brains managed a job that takes a lot of computing for machines doing face recognition.

Life in the mangroves

Archerfish live in warm brackish water, a mix of fresh and salty, in mangrove swamps and river mouths from India across South-East Asia to northern Australia. In India, they live in the creeks of the Sundarbans, the mangrove forest shared with Bangladesh where tigers swim between islands.

They don't only shoot. They also jump out of the water to snatch insects from low branches, and eat food that falls in. In some places, people keep them as aquarium pets, and they have been known to spit at lights and at their owners' faces.

Mangroves give them a perfect hunting ground. Branches hang low over the water, packed with ants, spiders, grasshoppers and beetles. It is one of the many reasons scientists say insects hold up whole food chains, even underwater ones.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The archerfish just sprays water randomly until it hits something.

Truth: Archerfish aim carefully. They adjust for the bending of light, the height of the target and even how fast the jet will travel. Young fish also improve by watching skilled ones.

Try this

Put a coin in the bottom of an empty cup. Move your head back until the rim just hides the coin. Now ask someone to slowly pour water in, without moving the coin. The coin seems to rise into view, because light bends at the water's surface. Archerfish deal with that bend every time they aim.
