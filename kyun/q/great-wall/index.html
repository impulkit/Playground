Can you really see the Great Wall of China from space?

It's one of the longest things people ever built, but from space it's almost invisible.

The short answer

Not with just your eyes, and definitely not from the Moon. The Great Wall is very long but only about 5 to 8 m wide, and it's made of local earth and stone the same colour as the land around it. China's first astronaut, Yang Liwei, said after his flight in 2003 that he couldn't see it from orbit.

From the International Space Station, about 400 km up, astronauts can sometimes photograph it with a zoom lens, when the light and snow are just right. Big cities, highways and airports are much easier to spot, because they are wider and contrast with the land.

The idea that the wall can be seen from the Moon appeared in books before anyone had been to space at all.

Surprising but true

Some parts of the Great Wall were held together with a mortar made from sticky rice mixed with lime.

Many walls, not one

The "Great Wall" is really many walls built and rebuilt over about 2,000 years, to protect Chinese states from raiders from the north. Together they stretch about 21,000 km, over half the way around Earth.

Around 221 BCE, the first emperor of a united China, Qin Shi Huang, joined up older walls. Later dynasties added their own. The famous stone and brick sections that tourists climb near Beijing were mostly built by the Ming dynasty, between 1368 and 1644. The Ming wall alone runs for almost 9,000 km.

How it worked

The wall was more than a barrier. It had thousands of watchtowers, built so guards could see from one to the next. When raiders approached, soldiers lit fires at night or sent up smoke by day, passing warnings along the wall faster than a rider on horseback could.

Gates let traders and travellers through, and soldiers collected taxes there. Garrison towns grew up nearby. In that way, the wall worked a little like a guarded border, the kind you can read about in why countries have borders.

Sticky rice

Ming-dynasty builders mixed cooked sticky rice into their lime mortar. In 2010, scientists found that a substance in the rice, called amylopectin, made the mortar stronger, more waterproof and better at resisting cracks. Some of it still holds today, after 500 years of rain and frost.

Builders used whatever was local. In the western deserts, where there was little stone, they packed layers of earth, sand and reeds into walls.

Where the wall meets the sea

At its eastern end, near Shanhaiguan, the Ming wall runs right into the Bohai Sea, at a spot called Old Dragon's Head. In the far west, it ends at the fortress of Jiayuguan, on the edge of the Gobi Desert, a stop on the old Silk Road.

Between them, the wall climbs steep mountain ridges that seem impossible to build on. Builders carried bricks up on their backs, or tied them to goats and donkeys.

The work was hard and dangerous. Soldiers, farmers and prisoners were forced to build it, and many died. Chinese folk tales remember this, such as the story of Meng Jiangnu, whose tears for her husband, lost while building the wall, were said to make part of it collapse.

Crumbling away

Millions of tourists visit each year, especially at Badaling and Mutianyu near Beijing. Away from these sections, much of the wall is crumbling. A survey reported in 2015 that about a third of the Ming wall had disappeared, worn by weather or taken by villagers for building stone. It became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987, and repair work goes on.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Great Wall of China is the only human-made thing you can see from the Moon.

Truth: From the Moon, about 3,84,000 km away, you can't make out any human-made structure with the naked eye. Even from the space station, about 400 km up, the wall is very hard to spot, while big cities and airports are easier.

Try this

Tape a single hair to a window so it stands out against the sky. Walk slowly backwards and stop when you can no longer see it. Measure how far you got. The Great Wall seen from orbit is like that hair seen from kilometres away.
