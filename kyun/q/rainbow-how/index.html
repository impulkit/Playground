How does a rainbow form?

No two people ever see exactly the same rainbow. Each of us sees our own.

The short answer

A rainbow forms when sunlight shines into raindrops. Each drop works like a tiny prism. As light enters the drop, it bends. It bounces off the back of the drop and bends again on the way out. Each colour bends by a slightly different amount, so white sunlight spreads out into a band of colours.

You only see a rainbow when the Sun is behind you and rain is falling in front. The light comes back towards you at an angle of about 42°, which is why rainbows always form a curved arc.

Surprising but true

A rainbow is really a full circle. From the ground, the Earth gets in the way, but from an aeroplane you can sometimes see the whole ring.

White light is many colours

Sunlight looks white, but it is a mix of all the colours of the rainbow. In the 1660s, Isaac Newton shone sunlight through a glass prism in a darkened room in England. The light spread into a band of colours on the wall. When he passed those colours through a second prism, they joined back into white light.

That showed the colours were already inside white light, and the prism was only separating them. Newton wrote about it in his book Opticks. You can read more about mixing light and why it behaves differently from paint.

Inside a raindrop

When light passes from air into water, it slows down and changes direction. This bending is called refraction. It is the same effect that makes a straw look bent in a glass of water, and that lenses use to focus light.

Violet light bends a little more than red light. So when sunlight enters a raindrop, the colours begin to separate. The light reflects off the inside back of the drop, then bends again as it leaves. Red light comes out at about 42° from the direction of the incoming sunlight, violet at about 40°, and the other colours in between.

Why it's a curve

Each raindrop sends out all the colours, but only one colour from each drop reaches your eye. The drops sending you red are all at 42° from the point directly opposite the Sun, so together they form a circle. The drops sending you violet form a slightly smaller circle inside it.

From the ground, the land cuts off the bottom of that circle, so you see an arc. From a plane, or a high cliff with mist below, you can sometimes see a full circular rainbow. Because the angle depends on where your own eyes are, the person next to you sees light from a slightly different set of drops. Everyone sees their own rainbow.

Double rainbows

Sometimes a fainter second rainbow appears outside the first. Its light has bounced twice inside each raindrop, which flips the order of the colours: red is on the inside, violet on the outside. It sits at about 51°.

Look between the two bows and the sky is darker than elsewhere. This band is called Alexander's dark band, after the Greek thinker Alexander of Aphrodisias, who described it about 1,800 years ago.

When to look

Rainbows need low sunshine. If the Sun is higher than about 42° in the sky, the rainbow would be below the horizon. So in India, the best time is often a monsoon afternoon when the clouds break and the Sun shines in late in the day. In the USA, summer thunderstorms in the late afternoon often leave rainbows behind.

Waterfalls make rainbows too. On sunny days, the spray at Niagara Falls or at Jog Falls in Karnataka often holds a rainbow. And on clear moonlit nights with rain, a pale moonbow can appear. It looks almost white to our eyes, because our eyes don't see colour well in dim light.

Naming the colours

In India, many children learn VIBGYOR: violet, indigo, blue, green, yellow, orange, red. In the USA, they learn ROY G. BIV, the same colours in reverse. Both come from Newton's list. Other cultures have counted the colours differently, and some languages name five or six. The colours themselves don't change, only the words we use for them.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A rainbow has exactly seven colours.

Truth: A rainbow is a smooth band of colours that blend into each other, with no sharp edges between them. Isaac Newton chose to name seven, partly to match the seven notes of a musical scale.

Try this

Make a rainbow on a sunny morning or afternoon. Stand with your back to the Sun and spray a fine mist from a garden hose or a plant sprayer in front of you. Move the spray up and down until a rainbow appears. Then try a glass of water on a sunny windowsill and look for a small rainbow on the floor.
