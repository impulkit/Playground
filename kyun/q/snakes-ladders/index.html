Did Snakes and Ladders begin in India?

Snakes and Ladders began in India as a game about good and bad deeds, with the top square standing for freedom from the cycle of rebirth.

The short answer

Yes. Snakes and Ladders comes from India, where it was known by names such as gyan chaupar ("game of knowledge") and moksha patam. Players rolled dice or cowrie shells and moved up a board of squares. Ladders carried them up from squares marked with virtues. Snakes dragged them down from squares marked with vices.

The final square stood for moksha, liberation from the cycle of birth and rebirth. In the late 1800s the game reached Britain, lost its religious meanings and became a family game played around the world.

Surprising but true

Indian versions often had more snakes than ladders, a reminder that falling into bad habits is easier than climbing up through good ones.

A board about life

Indian boards were often hand-painted on cloth or paper, with gods, animals and labels in Sanskrit, Hindi, Marathi or other languages. Squares had names such as faith, generosity or knowledge at the foot of ladders. Anger, theft, pride and drunkenness sat at the snakes' heads.

The idea fitted beliefs shared by Hindus, Jains and Buddhists about karma, the idea that actions have consequences across lifetimes. Jain boards, sometimes called gyanbazi, have their own special squares and symbols.

How old is it?

Nobody knows exactly. Some popular accounts say the 13th-century Marathi saint Dnyaneshwar invented the game, but historians have not found evidence from his time. Most surviving boards were painted in the 1700s and 1800s. Some are now kept in museums such as the Victoria and Albert Museum in London.

The game is related to other Indian race games played on boards, like pachisi. Those are much older, as you can read in board games.

To Britain and America

British families in India took the game home. By the 1890s it was being sold in Britain as Snakes and Ladders. Board makers cut down the number of squares, usually to 100, and replaced Indian virtues with Victorian ones like obedience and hard work. Later versions dropped the labels entirely.

In 1943 the American company Milton Bradley released Chutes and Ladders, swapping snakes for playground slides. It showed pictures of children doing good deeds at the bottom of ladders and naughty ones at the top of chutes. That is a small echo of the game's Indian roots.

A game of pure chance

Snakes and Ladders needs no skill at all. Every move depends only on the dice, so a five-year-old has the same chance of winning as a grandmaster. That makes it a favourite with mathematicians who study probability.

Because each move depends only on the square you are on and the next roll, you can calculate the average length of a game. Mathematicians who studied the classic American board found a single player needs about 39 rolls, on average, to finish. Some games end in fewer than ten. A few, after unlucky snake after snake, seem to go on for ever.

Why we still play

Snakes and Ladders is often a child's first board game. It teaches counting, taking turns and handling the disappointment of sliding down a snake. In India, versions are still sold for festivals such as Diwali, and some temples and museums display old gyan chaupar boards.

It is a game where winning says nothing about how clever you are, which is part of its charm. Like playing cards, it travelled the world by changing to suit each place it reached.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Snakes and Ladders was invented as a children's game.

Truth: In India it began as a game for all ages that taught ideas about karma, good deeds and liberation. It became mainly a children's game after it reached Britain and lost its religious meanings.

Try this

Design your own Snakes and Ladders board on a sheet of paper with 30 squares. Label each ladder with a good habit, like "shared my lunch", and each snake with a bad one, like "skipped brushing teeth". Play it with a friend using a dice and see who reaches the top.
