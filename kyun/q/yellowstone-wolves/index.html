How did wolves change the rivers of Yellowstone?

In 1995, a few dozen wolves arrived in Yellowstone. Some scientists say the riverbanks started to change. Others say the story is too neat.

The short answer

Wolves were wiped out of Yellowstone National Park, in the western USA, by the 1920s. Without them, herds of elk grew large and stayed in the river valleys for long periods, nibbling young willow, aspen and cottonwood trees down to stubs.

In 1995 and 1996, park staff released 31 wolves caught in Canada. The wolves began hunting elk, and elk numbers fell. Some researchers found that willows and aspens then began to grow back along certain streams. Their roots helped hold riverbanks in place, and beavers returned to build dams. That chain of events, from a predator at the top down to the shape of a stream, is called a trophic cascade. How strong it really was in Yellowstone is still argued about.

Surprising but true

When wolves kill an elk, ravens, eagles, coyotes, bears and even beetles turn up to share the leftovers, so a wolf pack feeds far more than itself.

Why the wolves disappeared

When Yellowstone became the world's first national park in 1872, wolves were seen as pests that killed cattle and deer. Rangers and hunters shot, trapped and poisoned them. By about 1926, the last known pack in the park had been killed.

For almost seventy years, Yellowstone had no wolves. Scientists slowly realised that the park, famous for its geysers such as Old Faithful, was missing one of its main hunters. After years of public argument, including strong objections from ranchers, the government agreed to bring wolves back.

The famous chain

The first wolves were carried into the park in metal crates in January 1995. They were kept in large fenced pens to settle in, then set free. Within a few years they formed packs and spread across the park.

Ecologists William Ripple and Robert Beschta studied willow and aspen along streams. They reported that some trees, nibbled to the ground for decades, began shooting upward. Beaver colonies on the park's northern range grew from one in 1996 to about nine by 2009. Ponds behind beaver dams gave homes to fish, frogs and birds. In 2014 a short online video called "How Wolves Change Rivers" told this story and was watched by millions.

Why some scientists disagree

Other researchers looked at the same park and saw a more mixed picture. Elk numbers were also cut by human hunters outside the park, by a growing number of bears and cougars, and by harsh winters and dry summers. In many spots willows stayed short.

Some streams had changed so much while the wolves were gone that a few more trees could not fix them. Without beavers to raise the water level, banks had cut deep, and willow roots could no longer reach the water. Later studies that measured many more trees across the park found smaller effects than the early reports. Most scientists now agree that wolves matter, but they are one part of a complicated system, not a magic switch.

A wider lesson

The idea that big predators help keep whole landscapes healthy has spread around the world. In India, protecting tigers in reserves like Kanha and Corbett also protects the forests, rivers and smaller animals that share their home. That is one reason the tiger is called an "umbrella" species.

Today, Yellowstone usually holds around a hundred wolves in about ten packs. Visitors with telescopes gather in the Lamar Valley at dawn to watch them. Wolves that wander outside the park, though, can still be legally hunted in some neighbouring states, and the arguments that began in 1995 have never fully ended.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Wolves single-handedly fixed Yellowstone's rivers, exactly as popular videos show.

Truth: Wolves seem to have helped in some places. But many streams changed little, and scientists credit other causes too. Nature is usually messier than a three-minute video.
