Why do mangoes cost more in some months?

The first Alphonso mangoes of the year can cost several times more than the same mangoes a few weeks later, and nothing about the fruit has changed.

The short answer

Mango prices change because of two forces economists call supply and demand. Supply is how many mangoes are for sale. Demand is how many people want them and can pay for them.

Early in the season, in March, only a few mangoes have ripened, but people are desperate for the first taste of summer. Few mangoes plus lots of eager buyers means high prices. By May the trees are loaded and markets overflow with fruit. Now sellers have more mangoes than buyers want, so they cut prices to sell them before they rot. The mango is the same. The balance between sellers and buyers is not.

Surprising but true

When prices rise, farmers often plant more of that crop the next season, which can flood the market and send the price crashing.

The seesaw of prices

You can think of a price as a seesaw. On one end sit the sellers, on the other the buyers. If something is scarce, buyers compete for it and the price rises. If there is too much, sellers compete for customers and the price falls.

Prices also send messages. A high price tells farmers that people want more, so it is worth growing. A low price tells them to try something else. That is how millions of strangers, who never meet, end up roughly matching what is grown with what is eaten. Studying this is one of the first things you learn in economics.

When the weather sets the price

Farming depends on the weather, so food prices jump with it. In late 2019, heavy rains damaged onion crops in western India, and in some cities onion prices climbed past ₹100 a kilo. Families noticed it in every curry.

The USA had a similar shock in early 2025, when bird flu killed millions of egg-laying hens. With far fewer eggs for sale, prices hit a record. Shops limited how many cartons each customer could buy. A farm-to-plate chain is long, so a problem at the start shows up at the end.

Some prices are not left entirely to the market. The Indian government buys wheat and rice from farmers at a minimum support price, so a bumper harvest doesn't ruin them. It also sells grain cheaply to poorer families through ration shops. These rules soften the seesaw, though economists argue about how well they work and what they cost.

Not just fruit

Supply and demand work almost everywhere. Train and plane tickets cost more at Diwali or Thanksgiving, when everyone wants to travel home at once. Ride-hailing apps raise prices when it rains and few cabs are free. Shops use the same idea in reverse. Winter jackets go on sale at the end of winter, because a shop would rather sell them cheaply than store them for a year. A ticket to a World Cup final costs far more than a ticket to a practice match in the same stadium.

Sometimes demand jumps suddenly. During the early weeks of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, masks and hand sanitiser sold out in shops across the world and prices shot up until factories caught up.

Tricks to beat the season

People have found ways to even out the swings. Cold storage lets farmers keep potatoes and apples for months and sell them later. Mangoes are harder, because they spoil quickly, so families turn the glut into pickles, aam papad and pulp. Shops also bring fruit from other regions or countries where the season falls at a different time.

Different mango varieties ripen at different times too. Alphonso and Kesar come early, Langra and Dasheri a little later, and some kinds hold on into July. If you want cheap mangoes, wait for the middle of the season. If you want the first one of the year, be ready to pay for it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Shopkeepers can charge any price they like.

Truth: They can put any price on the label, but if it is too high, buyers go elsewhere or go without. Over time, prices are pushed towards what people will actually pay.

Try this

Hold a pretend market with friends. Give everyone 10 paper coins and put only three biscuits up for sale. Let people bid. Then repeat with ten biscuits for sale. Write down the winning prices each time and see how the number of biscuits changed them.
