Why is it wrong to lie?

The most famous story about George Washington never lying was very probably made up, by a writer who wanted to sell more books.

The short answer

A lie is saying something you know is false so that someone else will believe it. Most people think lying is wrong for two big reasons. It treats the other person unfairly, because it takes away their chance to decide things based on the truth. And it damages trust, which is what lets families, friends and whole societies work.

If you can't trust what people say, every conversation turns into detective work. That is tiring and lonely. Think of a cricket umpire who sometimes gives batters out on purpose. Soon nobody would accept any decision, and the game would fall apart. So nearly every culture has rules against lying, from the Ten Commandments to the Buddhist idea of right speech. India's national motto, Satyameva Jayate, means "truth alone triumphs".

Surprising but true

Learning to lie is a normal step in growing up. Psychologists see it as a sign that a young child has started to understand what other people know and think.

Why small children lie

Researchers have watched thousands of children play a "peeking game". A child sits facing a wall while a toy is placed behind them. The adult leaves, saying "don't peek". A hidden camera shows that most children peek. When asked, many of them then say they didn't.

Psychologist Kang Lee at the University of Toronto found that children start trying out lies at about 2 or 3 years old. By 4, most do it sometimes. This isn't a sign of a bad child. To lie, you have to understand that other people have minds of their own and can't see inside yours. It's one of the skills a growing brain is learning. Lee also found that children who were asked to promise to tell the truth before answering were more likely to be honest. Hearing a story that praised honesty helped too, while stories that warned of punishment did not.

How does a child's brain grow?A newborn's brain is about a quarter of its adult size. By the time a child starts school, it has reached about 90%.

What a lie costs

The person who is lied to loses something, even if they never find out. They make choices using false information. A friend who is told "the party's cancelled" stays home and misses out.

The liar pays too. One lie often needs another to cover it, and keeping track gets hard. When a lie is discovered, trust drops quickly and comes back slowly. Many people find that being caught in one lie makes others doubt them for a long time. Online, false stories can spread to millions in hours, which is why fake news worries people so much.

The white lie problem

Here is where thinkers disagree. Is it wrong to tell your grandmother you love the itchy sweater she knitted? Some say a gentle untruth that protects feelings is kind. Others say there is almost always an honest and kind thing to say instead, such as "Thank you for making this for me."

The German philosopher Immanuel Kant argued, in 1797, that you should never lie, even to a murderer who asks where your friend is hiding. Many people find that too strict. They say protecting a life matters more. The debate is part of philosophy, and it hasn't been settled.

Truth as a life's work

As a boy, Mohandas Gandhi watched a play about King Harishchandra, a legendary ruler who gave up his kingdom and family rather than break his word. Gandhi wrote that it moved him deeply. Later he called his autobiography The Story of My Experiments with Truth, and admitted in it to lies he had told as a child.

His idea was that honesty is something you practise, like cricket or the violin, and get better at slowly. Being truthful doesn't mean saying every thought aloud. It means not trying to make someone believe what you know is false. That is harder than it sounds, and most adults are still working on it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Young George Washington chopped down a cherry tree and told his father, "I cannot tell a lie."

Truth: There is no evidence this happened. The story first appeared in an 1806 edition of a biography by Mason Locke Weems, written years after Washington died. Historians think Weems invented it to teach a lesson.

Try this

For one day, keep a quiet tally of every small untruth you hear or say, such as "I'm fine" when you're not, or "I love it" about a gift. At bedtime, sort them into lies that protected someone's feelings and lies that protected the speaker. Talk with a grown-up about which ones, if any, were OK.
