How did M.C. Escher use maths in art?

An artist who struggled with maths at school ended up drawing staircases that climb forever and lizards that crawl out of the page, and mathematicians fell in love with his work.

The short answer

Maurits Cornelis Escher, born in the Netherlands in 1898, made prints that look like puzzles. Lizards crawl out of a flat pattern and back in. Monks walk up a staircase that never ends. Birds slowly turn into fish. Water flows downhill and arrives back where it started.

Behind each picture is a maths idea. He used tessellations, shapes that fit together with no gaps. He used symmetry, turning and flipping shapes. He played with infinity and drew impossible objects. Escher never studied maths and said he couldn't follow the formulas, yet he explored these ideas by drawing them.

Surprising but true

Escher said he never understood the formulas, yet a mathematician's diagram of curved space inspired his famous Circle Limit prints.

The palace that changed everything

As a student, Escher wasn't a star. He failed some of his school exams and dropped out of architecture school to study graphic art instead.

In 1922, and again in 1936, he visited the Alhambra in Granada, Spain. Its walls are covered with Islamic tile patterns that repeat endlessly in clever ways. Escher spent days copying them. The tile makers used only abstract shapes, because many Islamic artists avoided drawing living things. Escher wondered: what if the shapes were animals?

He spent years learning how. Mathematicians later showed that there are exactly 17 basic ways to repeat a pattern across a flat surface. Escher found his way to many of them without knowing the theory, by trial and error and lots of sketching. He filled notebooks with his own rules, colour-coded and numbered, like a scientist's lab book.

Stairs that go nowhere

Some of Escher's most famous prints are impossible. In Ascending and Descending (1960), monks march around a staircase that keeps going up, or down, forever. It's based on the "Penrose stairs", described by the father-and-son mathematicians Lionel and Roger Penrose in 1958.

The Penroses had seen Escher's work at a big maths conference in Amsterdam in 1954 and been inspired. They sent him their paper. Escher then used their impossible triangle to make Waterfall (1961). Ideas went back and forth like a game of catch. These drawings work as optical illusions: every small corner looks fine, and only the whole picture is impossible.

What are impossible shapes?You can draw a triangle made of three wooden beams that all meet at right angles. You just can't build it, because it could never exist.

Squeezing infinity into a circle

Escher wanted to show infinity inside a frame. In 1958, the Canadian mathematician H. S. M. Coxeter sent him a paper with a diagram of triangles that got smaller and smaller towards the edge of a circle. Escher couldn't follow the text, but the picture struck him at once. He later wrote that it gave him quite a shock.

He used it to create his Circle Limit prints, where fish or angels and devils shrink towards the edge without ever reaching it. Mathematicians call this hyperbolic geometry. Coxeter later checked one print and found that Escher's drawing was more accurate than the artist could have known.

Ants on a never-ending road

In Möbius Strip II (1963), nine red ants crawl around a twisted loop. Follow one and you'll see it covers both "sides" without crossing an edge, because a Möbius strip has only one side.

Escher made about 450 prints and more than 2,000 drawings before he died in 1972. Today his art appears on maths textbooks, album covers and film sets. The film Inception even featured a Penrose staircase. And the same tile-fitting tricks that Escher loved can be found under your feet, in floors made of hexagons, the shape bees also choose for their honeycombs.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Escher was a trained mathematician who used formulas to plan his art.

Truth: Escher had no maths training and did poorly in school maths. He worked out his patterns by drawing and experimenting, and later swapped ideas with real mathematicians like H. S. M. Coxeter and Roger Penrose.

Try this

Cut a square of card. Cut a curvy shape out of one side and tape it onto the opposite side. Trace the new shape on paper again and again so the pieces fit together with no gaps. Add eyes and legs, and you have your own Escher-style tessellation.
