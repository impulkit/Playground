What is the Valley of Flowers?

In 1931, three lost mountaineers stumbled into a Himalayan valley so thick with flowers that one of them wrote a whole book about it.

The short answer

In the summer of 1931, the British climber Frank Smythe and two friends were walking back from Mount Kamet when they lost their way. They came down into a high valley and found it covered in flowers, mile after mile. Smythe later wrote a book called The Valley of Flowers, and the name stuck.

The valley sits in the Himalaya of Uttarakhand, at about 3,500 m. That is more than 1,000 m higher than the hill town of Shimla. It is now a national park and, together with nearby Nanda Devi, a UNESCO World Heritage Site. For a few weeks each monsoon, it turns pink, blue, yellow and purple.

Surprising but true

For about seven months of the year the Valley of Flowers lies buried under snow, and nobody is allowed in.

A garden nobody planted

No gardener has ever worked here. The valley is a glacier-carved bowl, about 8 km long, with a river called the Pushpawati running through it. Pushpa means flower.

Scientists have counted more than 500 kinds of higher plants in the valley. Some grow nowhere else on Earth. Look closely and you might see blue poppies, wild geraniums and cobra lilies that look like a snake about to strike. There is also the Brahma kamal, a rare white flower that is sacred in Uttarakhand.

The flowers don't all bloom together. They work in shifts. One week a slope is yellow, a few weeks later the same slope is purple, as one set of plants finishes and the next begins.

Why so many flowers, so high up

Three things meet here. First, deep snow covers the valley all winter like a thick blanket, keeping the soil damp and protected. Second, the monsoon reaches this part of the Himalaya and soaks it in July and August. Third, the valley lies between the wet southern hills and the dry lands near Tibet, so plants from both sides grow side by side.

The growing season is short, maybe four months. So every plant is in a hurry to flower, attract pollinators such as bees and butterflies, and make seeds before the cold comes back. That rush is what makes the meadows explode with colour.

Why are flowers colourful?Many flowers that look plain yellow to you have bold patterns that only bees can see, like landing lights on a runway.

A botanist who stayed

In 1939, a botanist named Joan Margaret Legge came from Kew Gardens in London to study the valley's plants. While collecting flowers on a steep slope, she slipped and died. Her sister later put up a small stone memorial, and walkers still stop at her grave inside the valley today.

The park also shelters animals that are hard to spot: musk deer, Himalayan black bears, blue sheep called bharal, and the shy snow leopard. Its pugmarks have been found here, and camera traps have photographed it. Above them flies the Himalayan monal, a pheasant with feathers that shine green, blue and copper.

Getting there, on foot

There is no road into the valley. Visitors drive to Govindghat, then walk or ride a mule roughly 10 to 13 km uphill to the village of Ghangaria. From there it is about 3 km more into the valley. Many of the people on the same trail are Sikh pilgrims heading to Hemkund Sahib, a gurdwara beside a glacier lake even higher up.

Nobody may camp inside the park, and visitors must leave by evening. The rules keep the meadows from being trampled. Grazing by sheep and goats was stopped in the 1980s. That caused a surprise. Some tall weeds spread fast without animals to eat them, and park staff now pull them out by hand.

From November, the snow returns and the valley disappears again, the way it has for thousands of years, long before anyone gave it a name. Snow and flowers both depend on the tilt of the Earth, which is why the seasons here are so dramatic.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Valley of Flowers is full of flowers all year round.

Truth: It is under snow from about November to May and closed to visitors. Flowers carpet the valley only for a short summer window, and different kinds take turns blooming between June and September.

Try this

Make a "flower count" square. Lay four pencils on a patch of grass or a garden bed to make a small square. Count how many different kinds of plants grow inside it, then try a square somewhere else and compare. Botanists use the same trick, called a quadrat, to study meadows like the Valley of Flowers.
