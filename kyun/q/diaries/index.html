Why do people keep diaries?

In 1666, as the Great Fire of London spread, Samuel Pepys dug a pit in his garden and buried his wine and a wheel of Parmesan cheese. We know because he wrote it in his diary.

The short answer

People keep diaries to remember their lives, sort out their feelings and record things they think matter. A diary is a private space. You can be honest there in a way you might not be with anyone else.

Some diaries turn into history. Because diarists write things down as they happen, their pages show what ordinary days were like, long after everyone involved has gone. A boy's worries about exams in 1890 or a girl's notes during a war can tell historians more than an official report.

Surprising but true

Samuel Pepys wrote his diary in a kind of shorthand code so others couldn't read it easily. It wasn't fully decoded and published until more than a century after his death.

A record of the day

The word diary comes from the Latin dies, meaning day. Many people write a few lines each evening: what they did, who they met, what made them laugh or angry. Others write only when something big happens.

Diaries help memory. Most of what happens to us fades within weeks. Reading an old diary can bring back the smell of a classroom or the taste of a holiday meal you'd forgotten. Some scientists have also found that writing about worries can help people feel calmer, though not everyone finds it useful.

The man who buried his cheese

Samuel Pepys was a government official in London in the 1660s. For nearly ten years he kept a diary, in a shorthand that few people could read. He wrote about plays, food, arguments with his wife and gossip at work.

He also wrote about huge events. In 1665 he described the plague, and in 1666 the Great Fire of London. As flames spread, he buried his wine and a Parmesan cheese in his garden to save them. His diary is now one of the best sources historians have on life in London at that time.

A girl in hiding

Anne Frank was a Jewish girl living in Amsterdam during the Second World War. She got a red checked diary for her 13th birthday in June 1942. Weeks later, her family went into hiding from the Nazis in rooms behind her father's office.

For more than two years she wrote about fear, boredom, quarrels, hopes and growing up. Many entries begin "Dear Kitty". In 1944 the family was discovered, and Anne died in a camp the next year, during the Holocaust. Her father survived and published the diary in 1947. It has since been read by millions in dozens of languages.

Kings and explorers

Babur, the first Mughal emperor, wrote a memoir called the Baburnama in the early 1500s, partly based on day-by-day notes. He described battles, but also India's birds, fruits and rivers, and how much he missed the melons of Central Asia.

In America, Meriwether Lewis and William Clark kept journals as they crossed the continent between 1804 and 1806. Their notes on plants, animals and the lands they explored are still studied today.

Diaries today

Some people now keep diaries on phones or computers, or post short updates online. A private paper diary is different, because nobody else is reading. Many children start with a diary that has a little lock and key.

Writers often keep journals to collect ideas. Ruskin Bond, who has lived for decades in Landour, near Mussoorie, published one as Landour Days: A Writer's Journal. It is full of small things he noticed: rain on a tin roof, a bird at the window, a visit from a neighbour. Notes like these often grow into stories later.

Diaries also became a popular way to write stories. Jeff Kinney's Diary of a Wimpy Kid, first published in 2007, is told through a boy's doodle-filled journal, and it made millions of children want to start their own.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Anne Frank's diary is not real and was written by someone else.

Truth: Experts in the Netherlands examined the handwriting, paper and ink in the 1980s and confirmed that Anne wrote it. Her father edited the first published version, but the words are hers.

Try this

Keep a one-line diary for two weeks. Each night, write just one sentence about something that happened that day. At the end, read it back and see what you had already forgotten.
