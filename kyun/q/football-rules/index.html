How is football played?

Yellow and red cards were dreamed up by an English referee waiting at a traffic light in London.

The short answer

Football is played by two teams of 11 players on a rectangle of grass. Each team tries to kick or head the ball into the other team's goal. Only the goalkeeper may use their hands, and only inside their own penalty area. A match has two halves of 45 minutes. Whoever scores more goals wins.

In the USA it is usually called soccer, because "football" there means American football, a different game where players mostly carry and throw an oval ball. That's almost all you need to start. The full rule book, called the Laws of the Game, has 17 laws. It covers the pitch, the ball, fouls, offside, free kicks and penalties.

Surprising but true

In 1911, the barefoot players of Kolkata's Mohun Bagan beat a booted British army team to win the IFA Shield, and crowds across Bengal celebrated it as a victory for the freedom movement.

The pitch, the ball and the clock

A pitch for international matches is 100 to 110 metres long and 64 to 75 metres wide, big enough to hold more than 25 tennis courts. The ball is about 70 cm around and weighs about 430 grams, close to three cricket balls.

The referee keeps time. Stoppages for injuries, substitutions and goal celebrations are added at the end of each half, which is why you see "+4" on the screen. Each team may make up to five substitutions in a normal match. In knockout games that end level, there may be 30 minutes of extra time and then a penalty shoot-out.

Offside, the rule that confuses everyone

Without the offside rule, a striker could stand next to the other team's goalkeeper and wait for a long ball. So the rule says that when a teammate passes to you, you must not be nearer the goal line than the second-last defender. Usually the last defender is the goalkeeper. Being level counts as onside, and a player standing in an offside position isn't punished unless they get involved in the play.

It sounds tricky, but it shapes the whole game. Defenders step forward together to catch strikers offside. Strikers time their runs to stay level until the pass is made. Since 2018, World Cups have used the video assistant referee (VAR) to check close calls, sometimes with lines drawn on the replay to the nearest centimetre.

Fouls and cards

A foul is an unfair act, such as tripping, pushing or handling the ball. The other team gets a free kick. If a defender fouls inside their own penalty area, the attackers get a penalty: one shot from 11 metres with only the goalkeeper to beat.

For bad fouls the referee shows a yellow card as a warning. A second yellow, or one very serious foul, brings a red card, and the player must leave. Their team plays the rest of the match with one person fewer, which is a big handicap on a pitch that size. The cards were the idea of Ken Aston, an English referee. He saw how confusing spoken warnings were at the 1966 World Cup, and thought of the idea while stopped at a traffic light. They were first used at the 1970 World Cup in Mexico.

Where the rules came from

People kicked balls around in many countries for centuries, but each school or town had its own rules. In 1863, a group of clubs met in a London pub and formed the Football Association, which wrote the first shared rules.

British soldiers and traders carried the game around the world, including to India. In 1911, Mohun Bagan played barefoot against the East Yorkshire Regiment in the IFA Shield final in Kolkata, and won 2–1. Huge crowds turned out, and the win became a symbol of Indian pride. Today, more countries play football than any other team sport. The 2026 World Cup, in the USA, Canada and Mexico, was the first with 48 teams, and Spain won it by beating Argentina 1–0.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Standing in an offside position is against the rules.

Truth: Being in an offside position is not a foul by itself. It only counts if you are in that position at the moment a teammate passes the ball and you then get involved in play. You also can't be offside in your own half, or from a throw-in, goal kick or corner.
