How did people decode hieroglyphs?

For about 1,400 years, nobody on Earth could read Egyptian hieroglyphs. A broken stone found by soldiers in 1799 changed that.

The short answer

Hieroglyphs were the picture-like script of ancient Egypt, used for over 3,000 years. When Egypt came under Roman rule and later became Christian, fewer and fewer people learned them. By around 400 CE, nobody could read them, and their meaning was lost for about 1,400 years.

The key to reading them again was the Rosetta Stone. It carries the same message written three times: in hieroglyphs, in an everyday Egyptian script called Demotic, and in ancient Greek, which scholars could still read. By comparing the versions, and by using knowledge of Coptic, a late form of the Egyptian language, the French scholar Jean-François Champollion cracked the code in 1822.

Surprising but true

The last known hieroglyphic inscription was carved in 394 CE, on the temple of Philae. After that, the knowledge of how to read them died out completely.

A stone in a wall

In July 1799, French soldiers were rebuilding an old fort near the town of Rashid, which Europeans called Rosetta, in the Nile Delta. Napoleon had invaded Egypt the year before. Among the stones, an officer noticed a dark slab covered in writing.

The stone weighs about 760 kg, about as much as four heavy motorbikes, and part of it is broken off. When the British defeated the French in Egypt, the stone was handed over to them. It has been in the British Museum since 1802, and is now one of its most visited objects. Egypt has asked for it to be returned.

Same message, three ways

The text is a decree from 196 BCE, issued by priests to honour the young king Ptolemy V. Egypt was then ruled by Greek-speaking kings, so official notices were written in Greek as well as Egyptian.

Scholars read the Greek first. Now they had a translation. But matching it to the hieroglyphs was hard, because nobody knew whether each sign stood for a sound, a word or an idea. It was a puzzle for code-breakers with no key.

Names in loops

The English scientist Thomas Young made an important start. He noticed that some hieroglyphs were enclosed in oval loops, now called cartouches. He guessed these held royal names, and worked out that one spelled Ptolemy.

Champollion went much further. He compared cartouches from the Rosetta Stone with another one that held the name Cleopatra. The shared sounds, such as p, t, o and l, used the same signs. Then he read the name of the pharaoh Ramesses on a temple carving, using the Coptic word for the sun, ra. That showed sound-signs were used for Egyptian names too, and the whole system began to open up. A family story says that when he realised, he rushed to his brother's office, shouted "I've got it!" and fainted.

How hieroglyphs work

Hieroglyphs mix three kinds of signs. Some stand for single sounds, like the letters of an alphabet. Some stand for two or three sounds together. Others are pictures that show the meaning, such as a little seated man after a word for a person. Scribes did not write vowels, so we are not sure how many words were pronounced.

There were hundreds of signs in regular use. Writing them well took years of training, so Egyptians also used faster, joined-up scripts for everyday notes.

Scripts still being cracked

Decoding old scripts is still going on. In India, the Brahmi script used for Ashoka's edicts had been forgotten for centuries. James Prinsep, an official in Calcutta, worked it out in 1837 by comparing short, repeated donation inscriptions at Sanchi.

Some scripts remain unread. The signs on seals from the Indus cities have resisted every attempt so far. The inscriptions are short, and there is no Rosetta Stone with a translation beside them. Somewhere in a field or a museum drawer, the key may be waiting.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Hieroglyphs are just picture-writing, where each picture means the thing it shows.

Truth: Many hieroglyphs stand for sounds, like letters. Others stand for whole words, and some are silent signs that show what kind of word it is. That mix is why they were so hard to decode.
