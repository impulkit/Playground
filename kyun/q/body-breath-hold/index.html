How long can people hold their breath?

In 2025 a Croatian freediver held his breath underwater for 29 minutes and 3 seconds, longer than a whole episode of a TV cartoon.

The short answer

Most healthy people can hold their breath for somewhere between 30 seconds and about a minute and a half. Trained freedivers, who practise for years under careful supervision, can go much longer. The longest breath-holds on ordinary air recorded in competition are close to 12 minutes.

With a special trick, breathing pure oxygen for several minutes first, the Croatian freediver Vitomir Maričić held his breath for 29 minutes and 3 seconds in 2025, a Guinness world record. That is about the time it takes to bake a tray of biscuits. Records like these are set with safety divers watching every second. Doctors check the divers before and after, because the body is being pushed close to its limits.

Surprising but true

The desperate urge to breathe isn't caused by running out of oxygen. It is your body sensing a build-up of carbon dioxide.

Why you feel desperate

Every cell in your body uses oxygen and makes carbon dioxide. When you hold your breath, carbon dioxide piles up in your blood. Sensors near your brain notice it and push you, harder and harder, to breathe. That burning urge is a warning light, and it switches on long before oxygen runs low.

Freedivers learn to stay calm through the discomfort, because panic burns oxygen faster. Their training is also what makes the sport dangerous. If the warning is ignored for too long, the brain runs short of oxygen and a person can faint without feeling it coming.

Your secret diving switch

Humans have an old reflex that we share with seals and whales. When cold water touches your face, your heart slows down. Blood vessels in your arms and legs tighten, saving oxygen for the brain and heart. This is called the mammalian dive reflex.

Your spleen, an organ tucked under your left ribs, helps too. It stores spare red blood cells and squeezes them out during a long dive, adding more oxygen-carrying cells to your blood.

The sea nomads

The Bajau people of Southeast Asia have lived on boats and coasts for centuries, diving for fish and shellfish, some in the waters of Indonesia. Some divers spend hours a day underwater, on single breaths, reaching depths of 70 m.

A 2018 study found that Bajau people have spleens about 50 percent larger than their land-farming neighbours, and that genes linked to this have become common among them. It is a rare example of humans evolving to suit a way of life in recent history. In Korea and Japan, women divers called haenyeo and ama have worked the sea in a similar way for generations.

Champions of the deep

Compared with sea mammals, our best is tiny. Sperm whales dive for over an hour. In 2020 scientists reported a Cuvier's beaked whale that stayed under for 222 minutes, almost four hours. Seals and whales store huge amounts of oxygen in their muscles, using a protein that makes the muscles look almost black.

Even an emperor penguin can stay down for over 20 minutes. Sea turtles resting on the seabed in cold water can go for hours, because their bodies use oxygen so slowly. Some freshwater turtles survive whole winters under ice, taking in a little oxygen through their skin.

Breathing on mountains

The other way to test your oxygen supply is to go up. At the top of Everest, each breath holds only about a third of the oxygen it would at sea level. Climbers breathe fast and deep just to cope, which is the strange world of altitude.

Your own breath-holding record matters much less than knowing how your lungs and brain work together. They keep you breathing every few seconds for your whole life, including all night, without you ever having to remember.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Taking lots of fast, deep breaths before going underwater lets you stay down safely for longer.

Truth: Fast breathing lowers carbon dioxide, so the warning signal to breathe comes later, but your oxygen doesn't rise much. Swimmers can black out without warning. Never practise breath-holding in water.

Try this

Sit still and count how many breaths you take in one minute. Then do 30 star jumps and count again. Your body breathes faster to clear the extra carbon dioxide your muscles made. Don't do breath-holding contests, especially in water.
