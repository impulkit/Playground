What is the Victoria Memorial?

Kolkata's great white Victoria Memorial is built from the same Rajasthan marble as the Taj Mahal, for a queen who never once set foot in India.

The short answer

The Victoria Memorial is a huge white marble building in Kolkata, set in about 26 hectares of gardens and ponds. It was built between 1906 and 1921 to remember Queen Victoria of Britain, who had been Empress of India until she died in 1901.

It was never a palace or a home. From the start it was meant to be a memorial and a museum of British rule in India. Today it holds paintings, maps, coins, weapons and documents, and it's one of the most visited places in the city.

On winter evenings, families picnic on its lawns and children feed the fish in its ponds, much as they did a hundred years ago.

Surprising but true

The bronze angel on top of the dome, almost 5 metres tall, was built to turn in the wind like a giant weather vane.

A viceroy's big idea

When Victoria died in January 1901, India was ruled by Britain, and Calcutta, as Kolkata was then called, was the capital. The viceroy, Lord Curzon, wanted a grand monument for her. He imagined something to match the Taj Mahal.

The money came from donations: Indian princes, British officials and members of the public gave about 1 crore and 5 lakh rupees, a vast sum then. The Prince of Wales, later King George V, laid the foundation stone on 4 January 1906.

By the time it opened in December 1921, the capital had already moved to Delhi, in 1911. India's freedom struggle was growing fast.

Marble from Makrana

The architect, William Emerson, mixed styles. The great central dome and the layout echo European palaces. The corner domes, arches and carved details borrow from Mughal buildings. People call this mix Indo-Saracenic.

The white marble came from Makrana in Rajasthan, the same quarries that supplied the Taj Mahal three centuries earlier. It took fifteen years to raise and finish the building. The main building rises about 56 metres, about the height of an 18-storey block of flats.

The angel on the dome

On top of the main dome stands a bronze Angel of Victory, almost 5 metres tall, holding a bugle. It was designed by Emerson's assistant, Vincent Esch, and cast in England.

The angel was mounted so that it could swing around with the wind, like the cockerel on a church weather vane. Over the years it has often been stuck, and getting it turning again has needed careful repair work high above the ground. From the lawns below, the figure looks small. Up close it is taller than two grown adults standing on each other's shoulders.

What's inside

The memorial has 25 galleries. You can see oil paintings of Indian landscapes by the British uncle and nephew Thomas and William Daniell, who toured India in the 1780s and 1790s. There are portraits, rare books and weapons from the battles of the East India Company.

Later galleries tell the story of Kolkata itself and of the freedom movement, including the revolt of 1857 and the leaders who followed. A building made to honour an empire now also tells how that empire ended.

Kolkata's front garden

Most visitors come as much for the grounds as for the galleries. The gardens hold statues and ponds, and old trees, though Cyclone Amphan knocked several of them down in 2020. Cricket matches happen next door on the Maidan, a huge open park. In the early morning, walkers and joggers circle the outer paths. In the evening, snack sellers wait outside the gates for the crowds.

At night the dome is lit up, and on some evenings there's a light-and-sound show about the city. Not far away, the steel arches of Howrah Bridge span the Hooghly, the other famous shape on Kolkata's skyline.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Victoria Memorial was Queen Victoria's palace in India.

Truth: She never visited India, and nobody ever lived there. It was built after her death as a memorial and museum, and it still works as a museum today.

Fold a sheet of paper in half and draw one side of a building you know, such as your school, a temple or a mosque. Cut it out without unfolding. When you open it, you'll have a perfectly symmetrical front, the same trick architects used for the Victoria Memorial and the Taj Mahal.
