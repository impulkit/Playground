Did India's giant volcanoes help end the dinosaurs?

At the same time the asteroid came, western India was pouring out lava. Lots of it.

The short answer

Around the time of the asteroid, huge cracks in western India poured out lava again and again. The flows piled up more than 2 km thick.

These stacked flows are called the Deccan Traps. "Trap" comes from an old Swedish word for stairs. Look at the step-shaped hills of the Western Ghats.

The volcanoes released gases that changed Earth's climate. Some scientists think this made life harder before the asteroid hit. Others think it mattered much less.

Surprising but true

If you've been to Mahabaleshwar or Matheran, you've stood on lava from the age of dinosaurs.

Not a cone, a flood

These weren't volcanoes like a mountain with a crater. Lava gushed from long cracks and flooded the land in sheets, layer after layer. Geologists call this a flood basalt. Some single flows were large enough to run hundreds of kilometres.

Between eruptions there were quiet spells, sometimes long enough for soil to form and plants to grow. These in-between layers, called intertrappean beds, hold fossils of plants, frogs, fish, snails and insects. They show that life carried on in the gaps, until the next flood of lava arrived.

Why India?

As India drifted north, it passed over a hot spot deep in Earth's mantle, the same hot spot that today sits under Réunion Island in the Indian Ocean. Hot rock rose from deep below like a blowtorch under a moving sheet of metal. See India's route.

The eruptions may have helped India move too. Some scientists think the rising hot rock pushed India along faster for a while, making it one of the fastest-moving landmasses known.

The great argument

Here is the puzzle. The asteroid struck Mexico 66 million years ago. The Deccan eruptions had begun before that and carried on after it. Both happened within the same short slice of time, geologically speaking.

Volcanoes release carbon dioxide, which warms the planet over thousands of years. They also release sulphur gases, which can cool it quickly by blocking sunlight. If large pulses came before the asteroid, they might have stressed life already. In 2019, two teams dated the lava very precisely. One found a big pulse of eruptions just before the impact. The other found that most of the lava came out after it. Both studies were published in the same issue of the same journal, side by side.

Most scientists agree the asteroid was the main killer. The open question is whether the Deccan Traps made things worse, and by how much.

Living on old lava

Much of Maharashtra and parts of Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh and Karnataka sit on Deccan basalt. The dark rock is strong and easy to carve, which is why ancient artists chose it for the caves of Ajanta, Ellora and Elephanta. The Kailasa temple at Ellora was cut from a single basalt hill. Many forts in the Sahyadri hills, built by the Marathas and others, stand on flat-topped basalt ridges with steep cliffs on every side, ready-made walls left by the old lava.

What it left behind

The black basalt rock weathered into the rich black soil of the Deccan plateau, great for growing cotton. Farmers call it regur, or black cotton soil. It holds water well and cracks wide open in the dry season.

The layers also made the step-like hills of the Western Ghats. Dinosaurs like Rajasaurus walked across this land just before and during the eruptions, and some of their bones lie in rock just below the lava.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Deccan eruptions came from tall, cone-shaped volcanoes like Mount Fuji.

Truth: Most of the lava poured out of long cracks in the ground and spread out in flat sheets. There was no single giant mountain. The layers piled up into a thick plateau instead.

Try this

Stack several thin layers of different-coloured play dough, one at a time, pressing each flat. Then cut down through the stack with a blunt butter knife and look at the side. You'll see stripes, like the steps of the Western Ghats, where each layer is one old lava flow.
