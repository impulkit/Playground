Is photography art?

When photography was invented, some painters feared it would put them out of work. It took about a hundred years for big museums to hang photographs beside paintings.

The short answer

Most people today would say yes, photography can be art. Major museums collect photographs, art schools teach photography, and some photos sell for huge sums.

But it wasn't always so obvious. A camera records what is in front of it, so early critics argued it was a machine doing the work, not an artist. Photographers replied that the art lies in choosing: what to point at, how to frame it, when to press the button and how to print it. That argument has been going on since photography began, and in a way it still is. Not every photo is art, just as not every drawing is.

Surprising but true

The oldest surviving photograph taken with a camera, a view from a window in France made around 1826, needed hours of exposure, so the sun lit both sides of the buildings.

A machine that draws with light

The oldest surviving photograph taken with a camera was made in France around 1826 by Nicéphore Niépce. It shows rooftops seen from his window, and needed hours of exposure. In 1839 Louis Daguerre announced a faster process in Paris, and photography spread around the world within months. You can read more in How did the first photographs change the world?.

For portraits, a photograph was faster and cheaper than a painting. Many portrait painters lost work. Some critics worried that painting itself would no longer be needed.

How cameras changed painting

Instead of disappearing, painting changed. Since a camera could record exactly how things looked, painters began exploring what cameras couldn't do: colour, light, movement and feeling. The Impressionists borrowed from photography too, painting scenes cut off at odd angles, like snapshots.

Meanwhile, some photographers tried to make their pictures look like paintings, with soft focus and hand-worked prints, to prove photography was an art. Later photographers decided that photos should look like photos, sharp and clear.

How does a camera capture a picture?A camera is a dark box with a hole in it. Film and phone chips are just clever ways to catch the light.

Getting into the museum

In New York in the early 1900s, the photographer Alfred Stieglitz ran galleries that showed photographs alongside modern paintings. He argued hard that they belonged together. In 1940 the Museum of Modern Art opened a department of photography, one of the first in a major art museum.

In the United States, Ansel Adams became famous for black-and-white photographs of national parks like Yosemite, with huge mountains and glowing skies. He planned each picture carefully, from where to stand to how long to develop the print, so the final image matched what he had in mind.

Photography in India

Photography reached India in the 1840s. In the late 1800s Lala Deen Dayal photographed palaces, monuments and royal courts, and became court photographer to the Nizam of Hyderabad.

In the 1940s, Homai Vyarawalla became India's first woman photojournalist. She photographed the last days of British rule and the early years of independence, including the leaders who shaped the new country. Today many Indian photographers' work hangs in galleries across the world. Satyajit Ray, before becoming a great film-maker, also worked in design and illustration, and his films show a photographer's eye for framing.

Everyone is a photographer now

With smartphones, billions of people carry a camera all the time. Estimates suggest well over a trillion photos are taken every year. Most are quick snaps of friends, food and holidays, not art.

That flood of pictures has made the old question interesting again. If anyone can take a sharp, colourful photo, what makes one special? Most photographers would give the same answer as in the 1800s: the seeing, not the machine.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A photograph always shows exactly what was really there.

Truth: Photographers have staged, cropped and edited pictures since the 1800s. Choosing what to leave out of the frame already changes the story, and today digital editing makes changes even easier.

Try this

Choose one ordinary object, like a cup, a shoe or a plant. Take ten photos of it with a phone, with an adult's permission, each one different, such as very close, from below, in shadow, in bright light, half out of the frame. Pick your favourite and ask a friend what they feel when they look at it.
