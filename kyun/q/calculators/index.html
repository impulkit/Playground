How does a calculator do sums?

A pocket calculator can't really multiply. It adds and shifts numbers very fast, in a language with only two digits.

The short answer

A calculator turns every number you type into binary, a code that uses only 0s and 1s. Inside is a tiny chip containing thousands of electronic switches arranged into circuits called logic gates. Some circuits are wired to add two binary numbers. Everything else is built from that.

To subtract, the calculator uses a clever trick that turns subtraction into a kind of addition. To multiply, it adds and shifts numbers along, the way you multiply on paper by writing rows and adding them. To divide, it repeatedly subtracts. For square roots and trickier functions, it follows step-by-step recipes, called algorithms, that home in on the answer. When it's done, it turns the binary back into ordinary digits on the screen.

Surprising but true

The first microprocessor sold to the public, Intel's 4004 in 1971, was originally designed for a Japanese desktop calculator.

Beads, wheels and gears

People have used tools to help with sums for thousands of years. The abacus, with beads on rods, was used in ancient Mesopotamia, China, Rome and India, and skilled users can still beat a calculator on some sums.

In the 1640s, a French teenager named Blaise Pascal built a machine of gears and wheels to help his father, a tax official, add long columns of numbers. Turning one wheel a full circle nudged the next wheel along by one, just like carrying a digit. Only a small number of Pascalines were ever made, because each was hard to build and costly. A few decades later, the German mathematician Gottfried Leibniz built a machine that could also multiply.

In the 1820s, Charles Babbage designed huge calculating engines made of thousands of brass parts. He never finished them. In 1991, the Science Museum in London built his Difference Engine No. 2 from his drawings, and it worked perfectly.

Shrinking into your pocket

For over a century after Babbage, mechanical calculators clattered in offices, and they were heavy and noisy. The first electronic calculators in the 1960s were the size of a typewriter. Then engineers learned to fit many circuits onto one silicon chip.

In 1969, the Japanese company Busicom asked a young American firm called Intel to design chips for a new desktop calculator. Intel's engineers came up with a single general-purpose chip that could be programmed to do many jobs. It became the Intel 4004, released in 1971, the first microprocessor sold commercially. The same idea now powers every phone and computer.

By the late 1970s, pocket calculators had become cheap enough to turn up in schoolbags around the world.

Adding with switches

How can switches add? In binary, 0 + 0 = 0, 0 + 1 = 1 and 1 + 1 = 10, which means "write 0, carry 1". A small circuit of logic gates can be built to follow exactly these rules for one column. Line up many such circuits, each passing its carry to the next, and you can add long numbers.

Multiplying 13 by 5 might be done by adding 13 to itself in clever steps, using doubling, which in binary simply means shifting every digit one place to the left. The chip does this so fast that the answer seems to appear the instant you press "=".

The human calculator

Some people can do sums at astonishing speed. In 1980, at Imperial College London, the Indian mathematician Shakuntala Devi was given two 13-digit numbers chosen by a computer. She multiplied them in her head in 28 seconds, a feat recorded by Guinness World Records.

Methods like Vedic maths also teach shortcuts for mental arithmetic. But for most of us, the little machine of switches is faster, as long as we remember to check that its answer makes sense.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A calculator's answer is always exactly right.

Truth: Calculators follow their own rules and limits. Simple and scientific calculators can give different answers for the same keys, and because they store only a limited number of digits, answers like 1 ÷ 3 × 3 may come out as 0.9999999 instead of 1.

Try this

Multiply like a calculator. Work out 13 × 5 using only adding and doubling. Double 13 to get 26, double again to get 52 (that is 13 × 4), then add one more 13 to get 65. Calculators break hard sums into steps like these, only in binary and millions of times faster.
