Why do we throw up?

Just before you throw up, your mouth floods with extra spit. It's your body quickly coating your teeth against stomach acid.

The short answer

Throwing up is an emergency exit. When your body thinks there's something harmful in your stomach, like germs, spoiled food or a poison, it pushes it back out. Better out the way it came in than left to do more damage.

The order comes from your brain, not your stomach. A control area in the brainstem listens for warning signals. Some come from nerves in the gut, some from chemicals in the blood, and some from the balance organs in your inner ear. When enough warnings pile up, the brain starts a fixed routine, a bit like a fire drill.

Surprising but true

About half of all astronauts feel space sick in their first few days in orbit, and some throw up inside their helmets.

The fire drill

First you feel sick, the feeling doctors call nausea. Your mouth fills with extra saliva, which helps protect your teeth from the acid about to come up. You might go pale and sweaty.

Next, you take a deep breath and a flap closes over your windpipe so nothing goes into your lungs. Then the diaphragm, the big dome-shaped muscle under your lungs, and your belly muscles squeeze hard together, all at once. The stomach is pressed like a tube of toothpaste. A ring of muscle at its top relaxes, and out it all comes.

The stomach doesn't do most of the pushing. It is more like a balloon being squashed by the muscles around it.

Why cars and boats do it

Germs are not the only trigger. On a winding road or a rocking boat, your eyes may see a still car or cabin while your inner ear feels movement. Your brain gets two stories that don't match.

One old idea is that the brain treats this mismatch as a sign of poisoning, because some poisons make you dizzy. So it plays safe and empties the stomach. Scientists still debate whether that's the full reason, but it explains why motion sickness feels so much like food poisoning.

In space, the mismatch is even stronger. With no "up" or "down", roughly half of astronauts feel sick in their first days. NASA trains astronauts in a plane that dives to make short bursts of weightlessness. It earned the nickname "Vomit Comet".

What happens to the body in space?Astronauts come home a few centimetres taller, with weaker bones and puffy faces, and some have to relearn how to walk.

Animals that throw up on purpose

Humans aren't the only ones. Dogs and cats throw up often, sometimes after eating grass. Owls swallow mice whole and later cough up neat pellets of fur and bone.

Some animals use vomit as a weapon. Vultures can throw up foul-smelling food when threatened, which makes them lighter for take-off and puts attackers off. India's vultures belong to this group.

Rats and horses, oddly, can't throw up at all. Their bodies aren't built for it, which is one reason rat poison works and why horses must be fed carefully.

Feeling better afterwards

Most people feel a bit better after vomiting, because the source of trouble has gone. The biggest danger afterwards is losing too much water and salt, especially for small children. Sips of water or an oral rehydration drink help. Rest helps too.

Vomiting is the body's version of an alarm that goes off a little too easily. Better a false alarm than a real poison staying inside. And the strangest version of this trick belongs to a beetle that toads swallow and then throw up alive, thanks to the boiling spray of the bombardier beetle.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Throwing up always means something is seriously wrong.

Truth: Most vomiting is the body getting rid of something it doesn't like, such as a stomach bug or spoiled food, and it passes in a day or so. It is worth telling a trusted adult or doctor if it goes on, if there is blood, or if someone can't keep water down.

Try this

Sit on a spinning chair with your eyes closed while a friend turns you slowly a few times, then stop and open your eyes. Notice how the room seems to keep moving. That mismatch between your eyes and your inner ear is what causes motion sickness. Stop straight away if you feel queasy.
