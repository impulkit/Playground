How do dung beetles use the stars?

In 2013, scientists put tiny cardboard caps on dung beetles in a planetarium and found they steer by the Milky Way.

The short answer

Some dung beetles roll balls of dung away from a pile to eat or to lay eggs in. To escape thieves quickly, they need to roll in a straight line. During the day they use the Sun. On moonlit nights they use the pattern of polarised moonlight in the sky. And on moonless nights, at least one kind uses the bright band of the Milky Way.

Scientists proved this in 2013 at a planetarium in Johannesburg, South Africa. Beetles under a projected starry sky rolled straight. Beetles wearing tiny caps that blocked their view of the sky wandered in circles. They were the first animals known to steer by our galaxy.

Surprising but true

One kind of dung beetle can pull more than 1,100 times its own body weight, like a person dragging six double-decker buses.

A ball of food

When a herd of elephants passes through the Kalahari, the clean-up crew is quick. Within minutes of a fresh pile of dung landing, beetles arrive, sometimes thousands of them. They have sniffed it from far away.

A ball-roller shapes a lump of dung into a sphere, sometimes bigger than itself, and pushes it with its back legs while walking backwards on its front legs. It is a bit like a person doing a handstand while pushing a giant ball with their feet. Other beetles try to steal the ball, so speed and a straight escape route matter.

The beetle in the planetarium

The scientists, led by Marie Dacke from Sweden, worked with the African beetle Scarabaeus satyrus. They noticed that the beetles rolled straight even on dark nights with no Moon. So they took them into the Johannesburg Planetarium.

When the dome showed the full starry sky, the beetles rolled straight. When it showed only the Milky Way, they still rolled straight. When the scientists put little cardboard caps on the beetles' heads, the beetles were lost. Their eyes can't see individual stars, but the glowing stripe of the Milky Way is enough.

The dance on the ball

Before setting off, a dung beetle climbs on top of its ball and turns around in a little dance. Scientists think it's taking a mental snapshot of the sky. When it bumps into something or gets turned around, it climbs back up and dances again to check its course.

These beetles don't need to know where home is. They just need to go somewhere, fast, in one direction. A straight line is the quickest way away from a crowd of thieves.

Super strength

Dung beetles are among the strongest animals for their size. In one study, a male of the horned species Onthophagus taurus pulled about 1,141 times its own weight. For a person, that's like pulling six double-decker buses full of passengers.

The males use that strength in tunnel fights. They push and shove other males to protect a female in a burrow underground.

Cleaners of the world

Dung beetles bury huge amounts of dung. That feeds the soil, spreads seeds and cuts down on flies. In the 1960s, Australia had a problem. Its native beetles were used to dry kangaroo droppings, not big wet cow pats, and the pats were piling up and breeding flies. So scientists brought in dozens of kinds of dung beetle from Africa and Europe to do the job.

In ancient Egypt, people watched beetles rolling balls and thought of the Sun god Khepri rolling the Sun across the sky. They carved scarab charms by the million.

Many other animals travel by the sky too. Monarch butterflies use the Sun as a compass to fly thousands of kilometres to Mexican forests, and the monarch migration still has scientists puzzling over how first-time travellers know the way.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Dung beetles just wander around randomly looking for poo.

Truth: Ball-rolling dung beetles are expert navigators. They move away from a dung pile in a straight line using the Sun, polarised moonlight or the Milky Way, and they smell fresh dung from far away.

Try this

On a clear, dark night, ask an adult to take you somewhere away from street lights. Wait 15 minutes for your eyes to adjust and look for the pale band of the Milky Way. Notice that it stays in roughly the same direction for a while, which is why a beetle can use it as a guide.
