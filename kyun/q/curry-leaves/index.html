Why do South Indian dishes use curry leaves?

Drop a few curry leaves into hot oil and they crackle and spit for a second, and the whole kitchen smells different.

The short answer

Curry leaves give South Indian food much of its smell. They are small, shiny, dark green leaves from a little tree that grows wild in India and Sri Lanka and in countless backyards. Their scent is hard to describe: a bit citrus, a bit nutty, a bit like nothing else.

Cooks almost always fry them first. In a step called tadka, or tempering, a spoonful of oil is heated with mustard seeds, dried red chillies, a few lentils and a sprig of curry leaves. The leaves crackle, the seeds pop, and the flavoured oil is poured over sambar, rasam, chutney, upma or curd rice. Without curry leaves, many families would say the dish tastes unfinished.

Surprising but true

Curry leaves are a cousin of the orange and the lemon, and the United States keeps a close watch on imported fresh leaves because they can carry an insect that spreads a disease of orange trees.

A cousin of the lemon

The curry leaf tree belongs to the citrus family, along with oranges and limes. Its scientific name is Murraya koenigii. It has white flowers and small berries that turn black when ripe, and birds spread the seeds.

In Tamil the leaves are called kariveppilai. Veppilai means neem leaf, because the two leaves look rather alike. In northern India one common name is meetha neem, "sweet neem", even though the plants are not related to neem at all. In Hindi they are also called kadi patta, the leaf that goes into kadhi.

The tree is easy to grow from fresh seed or from the shoots that spring up around its roots, which is how many families share it with neighbours. It loves warmth and hates frost, so in colder places it lives in a pot indoors through the winter.

Why the oil matters

The smell of a curry leaf is locked inside tiny pockets of oil in the leaf. Hold one up to a window and you can sometimes see them as pale dots. Chopping, crushing or heating bursts the pockets.

Most of those scented chemicals dissolve better in fat than in water. So when a cook drops leaves into sizzling oil, the scent moves into the oil within seconds. Stirred through a dish, that oil carries the smell to every bite. Since much of what we call taste is really smell, this small step changes the whole meal.

Across India and beyond

Curry leaves are everywhere in Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh, and they travel well beyond the south. Cooks in Maharashtra put them in poha, Gujarati cooks add them to dhokla and kadhi, and Sri Lankan curries use them in great handfuls. Indian families who moved to Malaysia, Singapore, Fiji and South Africa took the plant with them.

Fresh leaves keep their scent for only a few days in the fridge. Dried leaves lose most of it, so cooks who live far from India often freeze them instead, or grow a tree in a pot on a sunny windowsill.

The orange-grove problem

In the United States, fresh curry leaves can be surprisingly hard to find. The tree can host the Asian citrus psyllid, a tiny insect that spreads a disease called citrus greening. The disease has damaged orange groves in Florida and threatens those in California. So American rules strictly control curry leaves and plants arriving from abroad, and some states limit moving them around.

What's in a name

The word "curry" itself probably comes from the Tamil word kari, used for spiced sauces and dishes. Portuguese and then British traders picked it up, and over time "curry" came to mean almost any spiced dish from South Asia. It is one of many borrowed words that travelled from India into English. The British even invented "curry powder" so they could make something like Indian food back home. Ironically, that powder rarely has the leaf that shares its name.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Curry powder is made from curry leaves.

Truth: Most curry powder is a mix of ground spices such as turmeric, coriander, cumin, chilli and fenugreek, and usually contains no curry leaves at all. The two simply share a name, which comes from a Tamil word for a spiced dish.

Try this

Ask an adult to help you crush a fresh curry leaf between your fingers and smell it. Then smell a dry, old leaf, or a packet of dried ones. Most of the scent in the dry leaf has gone. That is why cooks prefer fresh leaves straight from the plant.
