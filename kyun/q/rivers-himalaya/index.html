Where does the Ganga's water come from?

Follow a drop of water from an ice cave to the sea.

The short answer

The Ganga starts as melting ice from the Gangotri glacier in the Himalayas.

But most of its water comes from monsoon rain falling on the plains and hills.

The mud it carries built the huge flat plains of north India, some of the richest farmland on Earth.

Surprising but true

The Ganga and Brahmaputra together carry so much mud they built the world's biggest delta.

From ice cave to Devprayag

High in Uttarakhand, about 4,000 m up, meltwater pours out from under the snout of the Gangotri glacier. The opening is called Gaumukh, meaning "cow's mouth". The stream that rushes out is the Bhagirathi, named after a king who, in Hindu tradition, prayed to bring the goddess Ganga down from the heavens to Earth.

The Bhagirathi tumbles down steep valleys. At Devprayag, it meets the Alaknanda, another river from the high mountains. From that point, the joined river is called the Ganga. It leaves the mountains at Rishikesh and Haridwar and spreads out onto the plains. In 1968, the Beatles stayed at an ashram in Rishikesh, on its banks.

Rain does the heavy lifting

A glacier gives the Ganga a steady trickle, even in the dry months. But the big flows come from the monsoon. From June to September, rain falls across the whole basin, an area covering about a quarter of India. Streams swell into rivers, and rivers rush to join the Ganga.

The Yamuna joins at Prayagraj. Big rivers from Nepal, such as the Ghaghara, Gandak and Kosi, join further east. The Kosi carries so much sand and shifts its course so often that it has been called the "Sorrow of Bihar". By the time the Ganga reaches Bangladesh, it is several kilometres wide in places.

Mud that built the plains

Rivers coming down from the Himalayas carry huge loads of crushed rock and mud. When they reach the flat plains and slow down, they drop it. Over millions of years, this fine mud, called alluvium, has built the wide Indo-Gangetic plain, hundreds of metres deep in places.

Near the sea, the Ganga joins the Brahmaputra, and together they spread into hundreds of channels. The land they built there is the largest delta in the world, shared by India and Bangladesh. Its southern edge is the Sundarbans, the world's biggest mangrove forest.

Life in the river

The Ganga is home to the Ganges river dolphin, India's national aquatic animal. It is nearly blind and lives in muddy water, so it finds its way and its food by sound, sending out clicks and listening for echoes, a skill called echolocation.

The river also holds the gharial, a crocodile with a long, thin snout made for catching fish, and many kinds of turtles. In the delta, tigers swim between islands in the mangroves.

A river many people share

Hundreds of millions of people live in the Ganga's basin, more than in most countries. They use the river for drinking water, farming, fishing and transport. For many Hindus, the Ganga is a sacred river, and cities such as Varanasi and Haridwar have stood on its banks for centuries.

All that use has made parts of it badly polluted by sewage and factory waste. In 2014, India launched a large clean-up programme called Namami Gange. Scientists measure the water regularly to track whether it is getting cleaner.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Ganga's water comes mostly from melting Himalayan glaciers.

Truth: Glacier melt starts the river and keeps it flowing in the dry months, but most of the water in the Ganga comes from monsoon rain falling across its huge basin, along with the many rivers that join it.

Try this

Fill a clear jar with water and add two spoonfuls of garden soil. Screw on the lid, shake it hard, then leave it still overnight. In the morning, look at the layers on the bottom, with the heaviest bits lowest and fine mud on top. Rivers drop their mud the same way when they slow down, building flat plains year after year.
