How do countries pay for healthcare?

In 1948, Britain opened a health service that let anyone see a doctor without paying at the door. Countries have argued about how to pay for health ever since.

The short answer

Healthcare is expensive. A single heart operation can cost more than a family earns in a year. So most countries try to share the cost, so that people don't have to pay everything themselves at the moment they fall ill.

There are three main ways. A country can pay for care through taxes, as Britain does. It can make people and employers pay into insurance funds, as Germany does. Or people can buy their own insurance or pay at the clinic, which happens a lot in India and the USA. Most countries mix all three. The World Health Organization calls the goal "universal health coverage": everyone getting the care they need without being pushed into poverty.

Surprising but true

Germany's health insurance system dates back to 1883, when Chancellor Otto von Bismarck brought in insurance for workers, partly to win them away from rival political parties.

Bismarck and Bevan

In 1883, Germany's chancellor Otto von Bismarck brought in compulsory health insurance for many workers, paid for by both workers and employers. It was among the first national schemes of its kind, and he was partly trying to win support away from socialist rivals. Many European countries and Japan now run systems based on this idea.

In 1948, after the Second World War, Britain's health minister Aneurin Bevan launched the National Health Service. It was paid for out of taxes, and anyone could see a doctor or go to hospital without paying at the door. Some doctors opposed it at first. It is now one of the largest employers in the world.

How insurance works

Insurance is a way of pooling risk. Picture a thousand families each paying a small amount into a fund every year. Most of them stay healthy, but a few need costly treatment, and the fund pays for them. Nobody knows in advance who will need it, so everyone is protected.

The maths behind this is probability. Insurers estimate how many people are likely to fall ill and set the price so the fund doesn't run dry.

India's path

India has free government hospitals and health centres, but many are crowded, and lots of families choose private clinics. For years, a large share of health spending came straight out of people's pockets. A serious illness could push a family into debt.

In 2018, India launched Ayushman Bharat PM-JAY, which covers hospital treatment up to ₹5 lakh per family per year for poorer households. In 2024 it was opened to everyone aged 70 and above. Many states also run their own schemes.

Other countries offer different models. Canada's public insurance grew from a plan started in the province of Saskatchewan in 1962. Thailand brought in universal coverage in 2002, and for years a hospital visit there cost just 30 baht, less than a US dollar. For older people, health costs and pensions are often planned together, because both grow as a population ages.

America's mix

The USA has no single national system. About half of Americans get insurance through their job. Since 1965, the government programme Medicare has covered people aged 65 and over, and Medicaid helps many people with low incomes. A 2010 law, often called Obamacare, helped millions more buy insurance.

Even so, about 8% of Americans had no health insurance in 2023. Americans disagree about how far the government should go. Some want a single public plan like Canada's, while others prefer private choice. Every country is still adjusting the balance between cost, choice and fairness.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In countries with "free" healthcare, nobody pays for it.

Truth: Doctors, nurses and medicines still cost money. In those countries, people pay through taxes or compulsory insurance instead of paying at the hospital desk.
