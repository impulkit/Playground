Who were the Phoenicians, the sailors who spread the alphabet?

The letters you are reading right now descend from a short alphabet used by sea traders on the coast of today's Lebanon about 3,000 years ago.

The short answer

The Phoenicians were a seafaring people who lived along a narrow strip of coast at the eastern end of the Mediterranean Sea, in what is now mostly Lebanon. They were never one big empire. Instead they lived in proud, separate city-states, such as Tyre, Sidon and Byblos, each with its own king.

What made them famous was trade. Their ships carried wood, cloth, glass, metals and dye all over the Mediterranean, and they set up trading towns as far away as Spain and North Africa. Along the way they spread a short, simple alphabet that later became the Greek, Latin, Hebrew and Arabic scripts.

Surprising but true

The word "Bible" comes from Byblos, a Phoenician port. The Greeks bought so much papyrus there that they named books after the city.

Cedar, snails and the colour of kings

Behind the Phoenician coast rose mountains covered in tall cedar trees. Cedar wood was strong and smelled sweet, and the treeless lands of Egypt and Mesopotamia wanted it badly for ships, temples and palace beams.

The most prized Phoenician product came from a sea snail called the murex. Crushed in huge numbers and left to rot in vats, the snails gave a deep purple dye that did not fade. It took thousands of snails to dye a single cloak. That made purple cloth so costly that in later centuries, Roman emperors wore it as a sign of power. The Greek name for these people may come from a word for this purple-red colour.

The alphabet that travelled

Writing in Egypt and Mesopotamia used hundreds of picture-signs and took years to learn. The Phoenicians used a script of just 22 letters, one for each consonant sound. A trader could learn it quickly and write down a deal on the spot.

The Greeks borrowed these letters around 800 BCE and added vowels. The Romans then adapted the Greek letters into the Latin alphabet you use for English. The Phoenician letter "aleph", which began as a picture of an ox's head, slowly turned into our letter A. Turn a capital A upside down and you can still see the horns.

Ships and colonies

Phoenician ships had a large square sail and rows of oars for calm days. Sailors steered by the stars at night, and the Greeks are said to have called the Pole Star the "Phoenician star" for that reason.

They founded trading posts, or colonies, across the Mediterranean. The most famous was Carthage, in today's Tunisia, which grew so rich that it fought long wars with Rome. Phoenicians also sailed past the Strait of Gibraltar into the Atlantic and built a port at Cadiz in Spain.

Around Africa?

The Greek historian Herodotus told a story about Pharaoh Necho II of Egypt, who ruled around 600 BCE. Necho is said to have sent Phoenician sailors south down the Red Sea with orders to come home through the Mediterranean. They supposedly took three years and returned from the west.

Herodotus did not believe one detail: the sailors said that in the far south, the Sun was on their right as they sailed west. Today we know that is just what you would see south of the equator. Some historians think this detail makes the story more believable. Others say there is not enough evidence to be sure.

Clever with glass and metal

Phoenician craftspeople were famous for more than dye. They made fine glass beads and small glass bottles for perfume, and they carved ivory into delicate panels for furniture. Their metalworkers hammered silver and bronze bowls decorated with scenes of hunting and gods, mixing Egyptian and Mesopotamian styles.

Much of what we know about these crafts comes from objects found far from Phoenicia, in Greek tombs, Assyrian palaces and Spanish towns. That tells archaeologists how widely Phoenician goods travelled. Their own libraries, probably written on papyrus, have mostly rotted away in the damp coastal air.

What happened to them

The Phoenician cities were conquered in turn by the Assyrians, Babylonians, Persians and finally Alexander the Great, who besieged Tyre in 332 BCE. Their language slowly faded. Their letters lived on, carried by the people who had borrowed them.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Phoenicians invented writing.

Truth: People in Mesopotamia and Egypt were writing more than 1,500 years earlier. What the Phoenicians spread was a short alphabet, where each sign stood for one sound, and that idea was easy for other peoples to borrow.

Try this

Write a short sentence using only consonants, the way the Phoenicians did, for example "Th ct st n th mt" for "The cat sat on the mat". Give it to a friend and see how quickly they can read it. Then try a sentence where the missing vowels make it confusing.
