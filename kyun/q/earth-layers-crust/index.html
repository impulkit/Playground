How thick is Earth's crust?

If Earth were an apple, the crust we live on would be thinner than its skin, and nobody has ever drilled all the way through it.

The short answer

Earth's crust, its rocky outer skin, is surprisingly thin. Under the oceans it is only about 5 to 10 kilometres thick, roughly the distance a Mumbai local train covers in ten minutes. Under the continents it is thicker, usually 30 to 50 kilometres, and up to about 70 kilometres under the Himalaya and Tibet.

Compared to the whole planet, that is very little. Earth's radius is about 6,371 kilometres, so the crust makes up only about one per cent of its volume. Below it lies the mantle, a thick layer of hot rock, and then the core. Everything we have ever mined, drilled or built sits in that thin top layer.

Surprising but true

The deepest hole ever drilled, in Russia, went down about 12 kilometres and still only got about a third of the way through the crust there.

Two kinds of crust

The crust under the oceans is mostly basalt, a dark, heavy volcanic rock. It forms at ridges in the middle of the oceans, where melted rock rises and cools. It is young by Earth standards. The oldest parts are less than about 200 million years old.

Continental crust is thicker but lighter. It is mostly made of rocks like granite, full of light-coloured minerals. Because it is light, it floats higher on the mantle, a bit like a big block of wood floats higher than a thin one. That is why continents stand above the sea. Some continental rocks are about 4 billion years old.

How we know without digging

Nobody has drilled through the crust, so how do scientists measure it? The answer is earthquakes. When the ground shakes, waves travel through the planet. They speed up or slow down depending on what rock they pass through.

In 1909, the Croatian scientist Andrija Mohorovičić studied waves from an earthquake near Zagreb. He noticed some waves arrived sooner than expected, as if they had taken a faster path through deeper rock. He had found the boundary between crust and mantle. It is now called the Mohorovičić discontinuity, or the Moho for short.

The deepest hole

People have tried to drill down to the Moho. In 1970, Soviet scientists started the Kola Superdeep Borehole in the far north of Russia. By 1989 it reached about 12.3 kilometres, deeper than Mount Everest is tall. At the bottom the rock was about 180°C, much hotter than expected, and drilling became too hard.

The hole is only about 23 centimetres wide and is now capped with a rusty metal lid. Even so, it went only about a third of the way through the crust there. Scientists in Japan have a ship called Chikyu designed to drill into thin ocean crust, hoping to one day reach the mantle.

Thick crust under mountains

Crust is thickest under big mountain ranges. When India's plate pushed into Asia, the crust crumpled and stacked up, raising the Himalaya. Just as an iceberg has a big chunk hidden underwater, high mountains have deep roots of crust pushing down into the mantle.

That is why the crust under Tibet is about twice as thick as under most continents. The roots also help hold the mountains up.

A cracked shell

The crust is not one solid piece. Together with the top of the mantle, it is broken into giant plates that move a few centimetres a year, about as fast as your fingernails grow. Where plates pull apart, new crust forms. Where they collide, crust gets pushed down, crumpled into mountains or melted in volcanoes.

So the ground under your feet is moving right now, though far too slowly to feel. Over millions of years, that slow motion has opened oceans, closed them again and carried India all the way from near Antarctica to Asia.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Just below the crust is an ocean of liquid lava.

Truth: The mantle under the crust is almost entirely solid rock. It is so hot that it can flow very slowly over millions of years, like extremely thick toffee, but only small pockets of it melt to form magma.

Try this

Take a hard-boiled egg and gently crack the shell with a spoon. The thin cracked shell is like Earth's crust broken into plates, sitting on the softer white below. Notice how the pieces can slide a little against each other when you press. Then peel and eat your model.
