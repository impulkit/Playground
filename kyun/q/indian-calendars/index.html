How do Indian calendars work?

India has an official national calendar, yet most families plan festivals using a different one, and which one depends on where they live.

The short answer

Most traditional Hindu calendars are lunisolar. Their months follow the Moon, but they add an extra month now and then to keep in step with the Sun and the seasons.

A lunar month runs from one new Moon to the next, or in some regions from one full Moon to the next. Twelve lunar months are about 11 days shorter than a solar year. So, roughly every 32 or 33 months, an extra month called adhik maas is slipped in. This keeps festivals like Holi in spring and Diwali in autumn.

Other parts of India, including Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Odisha, Punjab and West Bengal, use solar calendars. There, a new month begins when the Sun appears to move into a new sign of the zodiac.

Surprising but true

Many Hindu calendars add a whole extra month, called adhik maas, about once every three years, to keep the Moon and the seasons in step.

Days measured by the Moon

Hindu calendars count lunar days called tithis. A tithi is the time it takes for the Moon to move 12 degrees further from the Sun in the sky. Thirty tithis make a lunar month, split into a bright half, as the Moon grows, and a dark half, as it shrinks.

Because the Moon's speed changes, a tithi can be shorter or longer than an ordinary day. Sometimes a tithi is skipped, and sometimes one stretches over two sunrises. Working all this out needs careful astronomy, the kind studied by mathematicians such as Aryabhata about 1,500 years ago.

The panchang, or almanac, lists five things for each day: the tithi, the weekday, the nakshatra (the Moon's star group), and two more measures called yoga and karana.

Why festivals move

Festivals tied to the Moon move around on a wall calendar. Diwali falls on a new Moon night, so it can be anywhere from mid-October to mid-November. Holi comes on a full Moon in February or March.

Festivals tied to the Sun hardly move at all. Makar Sankranti marks the Sun entering the sign of Makara (Capricorn), and it falls on about 14 January almost every year. In Gujarat that day is Uttarayan, when the sky fills with kites. Pongal in Tamil Nadu and Vishu in Kerala are also set by the Sun.

Why is Diwali called the festival of lights?Millions of small oil lamps glow on the darkest night of the month.

Many new years

Because different regions use different calendars, India has many new year days. Ugadi in Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh and Telangana and Gudi Padwa in Maharashtra come in March or April, at the start of a lunar year. Puthandu in Tamil Nadu, Vishu in Kerala, Baisakhi in Punjab and Pohela Boishakh in Bengal usually fall around 14 April, at the start of a solar year.

Years are counted from different starting points, called eras. The Vikram Samvat, used in much of north and west India and as an official calendar in Nepal, is about 57 years ahead of the Gregorian count. The Saka era is 78 years behind it.

One calendar for the whole country

After Independence, the Government of India asked a committee of scientists, led by the astrophysicist Meghnad Saha, to study the country's calendars. They found about 30 in use. In 1957, the government adopted a national calendar based on the Saka era.

Its year begins on 22 March, or 21 March in a leap year, with the month of Chaitra. Its months have a fixed number of days, so its dates line up with the Gregorian calendar every year. You can see Saka dates on government notices, the Gazette of India and news broadcasts on All India Radio.

Many calendars, side by side

Indian Muslims use the Islamic lunar calendar to fix Ramadan and Eid. Parsis use their own Zoroastrian calendar. Sikhs mark gurpurabs using a calendar that has been the subject of debate, with groups disagreeing over whether to follow the Moon or fixed solar dates.

Most families use the Gregorian calendar for school and work, and check the panchang for festivals and ceremonies.

Myth or fact?

Myth: India has one traditional calendar that everyone follows.

Truth: India has many calendars. Some regions use months based on the Moon, others on the Sun, and they start the year at different times. Muslims follow the Islamic calendar, and the government uses both the Gregorian and the national Saka calendar.

Try this

Find a printed Hindu calendar or panchang at home, in a temple or online with an adult. Look for today's tithi and nakshatra. Then check when the next full Moon (purnima) and new Moon (amavasya) are, and watch the sky to see if it agrees.
