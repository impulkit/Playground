What was life like for a gladiator?

Roman gladiators were nicknamed "barley men", because bones from a gladiator graveyard show they ate mostly grain and beans, not meat.

The short answer

Gladiators were trained fighters who battled in Roman arenas to entertain huge crowds. Most were enslaved people, prisoners of war or criminals. Some were free men who signed up for money and fame. They lived in special training schools, ate a carefully planned diet and practised every day.

Life was hard and dangerous, but it wasn't always the bloodbath of films. Gladiators were costly to train, so many fights stopped before anyone died. A popular gladiator could become a celebrity, with fans scratching his name on walls. A few even won their freedom.

Surprising but true

Gladiators drank a mix of plant ash and vinegar after training, probably to strengthen their bones, a bit like a calcium tonic.

Inside the gladiator school

Gladiators lived in barracks called ludi. Next to the Colosseum stood the biggest, the Ludus Magnus, with its own small practice arena. Trainers taught each fighter a style, with specific weapons and armour. They practised with wooden swords against posts and each other.

Doctors looked after them. The famous doctor Galen began his career treating gladiators in what is now Turkey. He wrote that good care meant fewer deaths, which pleased the owners.

The "barley men"

In 1993, archaeologists found a gladiator graveyard at Ephesus, an ancient city in Turkey. Studies of the bones in 2014 showed that gladiators ate mostly barley and beans. Roman writers called them hordearii, or "barley men".

Their bones also had lots of strontium, which suggests they drank a tonic made of plant ash. Roman writers mention this drink. It may have helped strengthen bones that took a beating. A diet rich in grains might also have added a layer of fat, which could have protected them from cuts.

Matched fighters

Each type of gladiator had strengths and weaknesses, and they were paired to make a fair fight. The retiarius fought almost unarmoured, with a net and a trident, like a fisherman. He was fast. His usual opponent, the secutor, wore a smooth helmet with tiny eyeholes so the net couldn't catch it. He was protected but tired quickly.

The murmillo had a big shield and a fish-shaped crest. The thraex carried a small shield and a curved sword. Referees watched the bouts and could stop a fight if a gladiator raised a finger to give up.

What happened in the Colosseum?Under the Colosseum floor was a maze of tunnels and lifts that could raise a lion straight up into the arena, as if by magic.

A day at the games

A big show began in the morning with animal hunts. Lions, bears and even elephants were brought from across the empire. At midday, criminals were executed. The main gladiator fights came in the afternoon.

The Colosseum opened in 80 CE and could hold about 50,000 people, more than many cricket grounds in India today. A huge awning shaded the crowd. Free food and prizes were sometimes thrown into the stands.

Fame and freedom

A gladiator who survived years of fights might be given a wooden sword called a rudis, a sign of freedom. Some stayed on as trainers. Fans wrote graffiti about their favourites on the walls of Pompeii, praising their skill and good looks.

Not every gladiator accepted his fate. In 73 BCE, a Thracian gladiator named Spartacus broke out of a school in Capua with about 70 others. Their revolt grew into an army of tens of thousands before Rome crushed it in 71 BCE.

Women in the arena

There were a few women gladiators. A carving from Halicarnassus, now in the British Museum, shows two women fighters named Amazon and Achillia. Emperor Septimius Severus banned female gladiators around 200 CE.

The games faded as the empire became Christian and grew poorer, and they ended in the early 400s. Fighting for crowds didn't disappear, though. In India, traditional wrestling in mud pits, called kushti, has drawn cheering crowds for centuries.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The crowd gave a thumbs down to call for a gladiator's death.

Truth: Romans did use thumb signals, but nobody knows exactly what they meant. Some historians think a turned thumb meant death and a hidden thumb meant mercy. The famous "thumbs down" comes mostly from a 19th-century painting.
