How does a photocopier work?

The photocopier works using static electricity, the same force that makes a balloon stick to your hair, plus light, powder and heat.

The short answer

A photocopier makes a copy in a few seconds by using light, static electricity, a fine powder called toner, and heat.

Inside is a drum, a metal cylinder coated with a special material. First the drum is given a static charge all over. Then a bright light shines onto your page, and the light reflected off it falls on the drum. Wherever light hits, the charge leaks away. Only the dark parts, the shapes of the words and pictures, stay charged.

Toner powder sticks to those charged parts. The drum rolls the powder onto a sheet of paper, and hot rollers melt it in place. Out comes your copy, still warm.

Surprising but true

The first automatic plain-paper copier, the Xerox 914 of 1959, could overheat and catch fire, so it came with a small fire extinguisher.

A man tired of copying

Before photocopiers, copying a document was slow. People retyped it, traced it, or used messy carbon paper, which made a faint copy as you typed.

Chester Carlson knew this first-hand. He worked in the patent office of an electronics company in New York, and he spent long days copying drawings and papers by hand. He also had arthritis, which made his hands hurt.

So he set out to invent a copying machine. He worked in his kitchen, and later in a small room behind a beauty salon in Astoria, a part of New York City.

The first copy

On 22 October 1938, Carlson and his helper, Otto Kornei, wrote "10.-22.-38 ASTORIA." in ink on a glass slide. They rubbed a zinc plate coated with sulphur to give it a static charge, pressed the slide to it and shone a bright lamp on it.

Next they dusted the plate with a fine powder. The powder clung only where the writing had been. They pressed waxed paper onto it and warmed it. The words appeared on the paper. It was the world's first xerographic copy.

For years, more than 20 companies turned Carlson down. They thought carbon paper was good enough. Finally a small firm called Haloid agreed to develop his idea. It later renamed itself Xerox.

The machine that caught fire

In 1959 Xerox launched the 914, the first machine that could quickly make copies on ordinary paper at the press of a button. It weighed close to 300 kg and was too big to fit on a desk.

It could also overheat, and if paper got stuck near the hot parts, it might start to smoke or burn. So each machine came with a small fire extinguisher. Even so, offices loved it. Suddenly anyone could copy a letter in seconds. Xerox grew into a giant company, and "to xerox" came to mean "to photocopy" in many countries, including India.

Inside a modern copier

Today's copiers work on the same idea, but with digital parts. A scanner bar with a row of tiny light sensors slides under the glass and turns the page into a digital image. Then a laser draws that image onto the charged drum, just as in a laser printer. In fact, many office machines are printer, scanner and copier in one.

Colour copiers use four toners: cyan, magenta, yellow and black. They lay down each colour in turn.

The xerox shop

In India, the small "xerox shop" near schools, colleges and government offices is a familiar sight. Students copy notes, and families copy forms, ID cards and certificates.

Photocopiers changed how information spread. Before them, a rare book or document could be read only where it was kept, often in a library. After them, pages could be copied and shared widely. Chester Carlson became rich from his invention and gave most of his fortune away to charities before he died in 1968.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A photocopier takes a photograph of the page and prints it with ink.

Truth: A copier uses light to make a pattern of static charge on a drum. Dry powder sticks to the charged parts and is then melted onto paper. There is no film and usually no liquid ink.

Try this

Blow up a balloon and rub it on a woollen jumper or your hair. Then hold it over a little pile of pepper or tiny bits of torn paper on a plate. The specks jump up and cling to the balloon. That is the same pull a photocopier uses to make toner stick to the right places.
