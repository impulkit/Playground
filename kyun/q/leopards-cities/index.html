How do leopards live near Mumbai?

Inside the limits of one of the world's biggest cities, about 54 wild leopards live in a forest ringed by apartment blocks, roads and railway lines.

The short answer

Right in the middle of Mumbai is a forest called Sanjay Gandhi National Park. It covers about 104 square kilometres, roughly the size of 15,000 football fields, with crores of people living around its edges. About 54 leopards live in the park and the green Aarey area next to it, according to a camera-trap survey reported in 2025.

Leopards survive here because they are very good at sharing space. They hide by day, hunt mostly at night, eat almost anything they can catch, and usually avoid people. Most Mumbaikars living next to the park have never seen one, though the leopards have certainly seen them.

Surprising but true

Scientists estimate that Mumbai's leopards eat many hundreds of stray dogs a year, which may mean fewer dog bites for the people living nearby.

A city cat

Leopards are the most adaptable of India's big cats. They live in rainforests, deserts, mountains, sugarcane fields and tea gardens. They can climb trees with prey in their jaws, and their spotted coats vanish in dappled shadow. The pattern of spots, called rosettes, is different on every leopard, which is how researchers tell individuals apart in camera-trap photos.

Around Mumbai they are mostly nocturnal. Camera traps catch them walking along park roads, crossing hillside slums after midnight, and sometimes wandering onto housing colonies and the campus of IIT Bombay in Powai. By dawn they are usually back in thick cover.

What's for dinner

Leopards eat deer, wild pigs, monkeys and porcupines, but around Mumbai a large share of their food is stray dogs. Dogs gather wherever rubbish piles up, and the forest edge has plenty of both.

In 2018 a team of scientists estimated that the park's leopards were eating hundreds of stray dogs every year. That may mean fewer dog bites and fewer cases of rabies, a deadly disease spread by bites. Nobody planned it, but the leopards may be quietly helping with public health.

Learning to live together

Things were not always calm. In the early 2000s, leopard attacks on people near the park caused fear and anger, and many leopards were trapped and moved away. That didn't solve the problem. Some found their way back, and new leopards moved into the empty spaces.

In 2011, forest officers, scientists such as Vidya Athreya and local residents started a project called Mumbaikars for SGNP. They visited schools and housing societies and shared simple advice. Don't let small children out alone at night near the forest. Keep paths lit, carry a torch and talk loudly when walking after dark. Clear away rubbish so dogs, and the leopards that follow them, stay away. Attacks have become much rarer since then.

The leopard who walked to Mumbai

One famous leopard, nicknamed Ajoba, was rescued from a well near Malshej Ghat, fitted with a radio collar and released. Over the following weeks he walked around 120 kilometres, crossing highways, farms and railway lines, until he reached Sanjay Gandhi National Park. His journey showed scientists that Mumbai's forest is still linked to the wild hills of the Western Ghats.

Big cats in other cities

Mumbai is not alone. In Los Angeles, a mountain lion known as P-22 lived for about ten years in Griffith Park, surrounded by freeways, and became a local celebrity. To help wild cats move safely, California is building a large wildlife bridge over a highway there.

These cities show the same thing. Big cats can live close to people, as long as they have enough forest to hide in and people know how to behave around them. Across India, the leopard population was estimated at about 13,900 in 2022, and many of them live outside protected forests. Mumbai's leopards are a small part of a much larger story of urban wildlife finding room among us.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A leopard seen near homes has strayed out of the forest, and the best answer is to catch it and move it far away.

Truth: Leopards near Mumbai live there all the time. Studies in Maharashtra found that leopards moved far away often tried to return, and conflict sometimes got worse. Safer habits, like clearing rubbish and lighting paths, work better.
