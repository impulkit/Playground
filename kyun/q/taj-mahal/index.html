How was the Taj Mahal built so perfectly?

It took about 20 years, around 20,000 workers and white marble carried from Rajasthan.

The short answer

The Taj Mahal in Agra was built by the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan as a tomb for his wife, Mumtaz Mahal, who died in 1631. Work began around 1632 and took about 20 years, with around 20,000 workers: stone-carvers, masons, inlay artists and calligraphers.

Its white marble came from Makrana in Rajasthan, more than 300 km away. Elephants, bullocks and long earth ramps helped move the stone. The result looks almost weightless, a white dome floating above the Yamuna river.

Surprising but true

The Taj Mahal looks pink at dawn, white at noon and golden in moonlight. The marble reflects the changing colours of the sky.

Perfect balance

The Taj is almost perfectly symmetrical. Its left side mirrors its right, the four minarets stand at equal distances, and a long pool in the garden reflects the whole building. The garden itself is a charbagh, a Persian-style garden split into four parts by water channels.

There is one famous break in the pattern. Mumtaz Mahal's tomb lies exactly at the centre. When Shah Jahan died in 1666, he was buried beside her, slightly off to one side. It is the only thing in the whole design that isn't balanced. Even the red sandstone buildings on either side match. A mosque stands on the west, and an almost identical building on the east, called the jawab or "answer", is there mainly to balance it. The same love of symmetry appears in a kolam drawn at a South Indian doorstep.

Who designed it

The chief architect is usually named as Ustad Ahmad Lahauri, though many experts worked on the plan. The building blends Persian, Central Asian and Indian ideas: the tall arched gateway called an iwan, the swelling onion-shaped dome, and small domed pavilions called chhatris from Indian architecture.

Writing was part of the design. Verses from the Quran, inlaid in black stone, run around the great arches. The calligrapher, Amanat Khan, made the letters near the top larger than those lower down, so that from the ground they all appear the same size. It is a trick of perspective in stone.

Stones set in stone

The flowers on the walls aren't painted. They are thin slices of coloured stone, such as carnelian, jasper and lapis lazuli, cut to shape and fitted into hollows carved in the marble. This art is called pietra dura in Italian and parchin kari in Persian and Hindi.

Some single flowers contain dozens of pieces, fitted so tightly that the joins are hard to see. Some stones came from far away, such as lapis lazuli from Afghanistan, the same blue stone that the painters of Ajanta used centuries earlier. Families of craftspeople in Agra still practise the art, making tabletops and boxes for visitors. Their skills link them to the Mughal workshops of nearly 400 years ago.

Keeping it white

Marble is a soft stone that reacts with acids. Dust, soot and polluted air slowly stain it yellow and brown. In the 1990s, India's Supreme Court ordered many industries around Agra to switch to cleaner fuels or move away, and polluting vehicles are kept away from the monument.

To clean the walls, restorers spread a paste of multani mitti, a fine clay also used in face packs, and leave it to dry. When it is washed off, it pulls dirt out of the marble's tiny pores. The Taj became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1983, and millions of people visit every year. Keeping it white is a job that never quite ends.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Shah Jahan had the workers' hands cut off so they could never build anything as beautiful again.

Truth: This is a popular legend, and historians have found no evidence for it. Ustad Ahmad Lahauri, the architect usually credited with the Taj, is also credited with Shah Jahan's later Red Fort in Delhi.
