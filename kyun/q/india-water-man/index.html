Who is the Waterman of India?

In a dry corner of Rajasthan, a river that had vanished for years began to flow again, because villagers dug thousands of simple mud ponds.

The short answer

The "Waterman of India" is Rajendra Singh, who has spent about 40 years helping villages in Rajasthan catch rainwater. With his organisation, Tarun Bharat Sangh, he and the villagers revived old ways of building small mud dams called johads.

The johads caught monsoon rain and let it soak into the ground. Wells that had dried up filled again. Farmers could grow crops once more. And a few small rivers in Alwar district, including the Arvari, began flowing again after years of being dry for most of the year. In 2015 he won the Stockholm Water Prize, sometimes described as the Nobel Prize for water.

Surprising but true

Once the Arvari river started flowing again, the villages along it set up their own "river parliament" to make rules about fishing, farming and how much water anyone could take.

A dry land

Alwar is in eastern Rajasthan, on the rocky Aravalli hills. Rain comes mostly in the monsoon months, and some years hardly at all. For centuries, villages there stored water in johads and ponds.

By the 1980s, many of these had broken or silted up. Forests on the hills had been cut, so rain rushed off the bare slopes instead of sinking in. Wells went dry. Young men left to look for work in cities. Women walked for hours to fetch water.

A doctor who picked up a shovel

Rajendra Singh trained in ayurvedic medicine and worked for the government. In 1985, he and a few friends quit their jobs and went to live in a village in Alwar, planning to run health and education work.

An old farmer there told him the village didn't need more schooling. It needed water. He told Singh to dig. So Singh began clearing out an old johad, carrying mud in baskets. When the monsoon came, the pond filled, and nearby wells that had been dry for years started to hold water.

What is rainwater harvesting?One millimetre of rain falling on one square metre of roof gives you exactly one litre of water, and over a monsoon that adds up to tankers' worth.

How a johad works

A johad is a curved wall of earth and stones built across a slope or a seasonal stream. It is usually no taller than a grown-up. During the monsoon, it traps the water that would have rushed away.

Some water is used directly by animals and farmers. Most of it slowly seeps down into the groundwater, like water into a sponge. A single johad helps a few wells. Hundreds of them, spread across a valley, can lift the water level for an entire area.

Villagers did the work themselves, giving land, money and labour. Tarun Bharat Sangh says thousands of johads have been built or repaired across Rajasthan and nearby states.

Rivers that came back

The Arvari river had flowed only during the rains for years. As johads multiplied along its catchment, the area of land that drains into it, the groundwater rose. By the 1990s, the river was flowing for much more of the year.

Tarun Bharat Sangh says several other small rivers in the area, such as the Ruparel and Sarsa, have revived too. Tigers and other animals in the nearby Sariska Tiger Reserve also gained from the extra water.

When fishing contractors arrived to make money from the river, the villagers objected. They formed the Arvari River Parliament, where representatives from dozens of villages make rules for using the river fairly.

Spreading the idea

Singh has walked across India to talk about water, and has worked in other countries facing droughts. He argues that water belongs to communities, and that old Indian knowledge, from village ponds to grand stepwells, still has lessons for today.

His story sits alongside other Indians who changed their land with their own hands. In Bihar, a labourer known as the Mountain Man spent 22 years cutting a path through a rocky ridge with a hammer and chisel.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Once a river dries up, it is gone for good.

Truth: Many small rivers depend on groundwater to keep flowing after the rains. When forests are cut and wells overused, they can dry up. But when the ground is refilled, as in Alwar, some rivers have started flowing again.

Try this

Test how water soaks in. Pour one cup of water quickly onto a slope of hard, dry soil and another cup onto the same slope behind a little wall of pebbles and mud. Watch which water runs away and which water stays and sinks in. The little wall is a mini johad.
