Who made the Easter Island statues?

The giant stone heads of Easter Island are not just heads. Most of them have whole bodies hidden under the soil.

The short answer

The statues on Easter Island, called moai, were carved by the Rapa Nui, the Polynesian people of the island. Their ancestors sailed across the Pacific Ocean in large canoes and settled the island, probably around 1200 CE.

Over the next few hundred years, they carved about 900 moai from volcanic rock. Most were placed on stone platforms near the coast, facing inland, with their backs to the sea. They are thought to represent important ancestors, watching over the villages and their people. The island is tiny, only about 160 square kilometres, and it is one of the most remote places on Earth where people live.

Surprising but true

Experiments have shown that a team of people pulling ropes can rock a replica statue from side to side so that it "walks" upright, which matches old Rapa Nui stories that the statues walked.

Sailors across the Pacific

Polynesians were some of the greatest navigators in history. Without compasses or maps, they read the stars, the swell of the waves, the flight of seabirds and the shapes of clouds to find islands across thousands of kilometres of open ocean.

Easter Island lies about 3,500 km off the coast of Chile and about 2,000 km from the nearest inhabited island. Finding it at all was an achievement.

Carving the moai

Almost all the moai came from one quarry, Rano Raraku, an old volcano crater made of soft rock called tuff. Carvers used hard stone tools, called toki, to shape each figure while it was still attached to the hillside. Then they cut it free and slid it down the slope.

An average moai is about 4 m tall and weighs about as much as two elephants. The largest one ever finished stands about 10 m tall. One unfinished giant, still lying in the quarry, would have been about 21 m, as tall as a seven-storey building. Some moai were later given red stone topknots, called pukao, made from a different volcanic rock.

How are statues made?Sculptors make statues in four basic ways. They can take material away, add it on, pour it into a mould, or build it up from pieces.

How did they move?

The statues had to travel up to about 18 km from the quarry to the coast. Rapa Nui legends say the moai walked there with the help of spiritual power, called mana.

In 2012, archaeologists Terry Hunt and Carl Lipo, with others, tested an idea. They built a 4-tonne concrete copy and tied ropes to its head. Teams pulling from either side rocked it forward, step by step, and it really did "walk". Other researchers think the statues were dragged lying down on wooden sledges. The debate is not settled, but the walking idea fits the statues' shape and the old stories surprisingly well.

What happened to the people?

When Dutch sailors arrived on Easter Sunday in 1722, giving the island its European name, the palm forests were mostly gone. For years, a popular explanation was that the islanders cut down all their trees, causing war and collapse.

More recent research tells a more complicated story. Rats that arrived with the first settlers probably ate many palm seeds. The worst disasters came after Europeans arrived. In 1862, slave raiders took around 1,500 islanders to Peru, and diseases followed. By 1877, only about a hundred Rapa Nui were left on the island.

Rapa Nui today

Easter Island became part of Chile in 1888. Today about 7,700 people live there, many of them Rapa Nui, who keep their language, dances and festivals alive. The island is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

The islanders also had a script called rongorongo, carved on wooden tablets. No one can read it today, and it is one of the few writing systems still undeciphered.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Easter Island statues are only giant heads.

Truth: Most statues have full bodies with arms, carved down to the hips. Many of the famous ones on the slopes of the quarry are buried up to their shoulders in soil that washed down over the centuries.

Try this

Stand a tall, empty plastic bottle on the floor. Tie two strings around its neck and, with a friend on each side, gently pull in turn so it tips and twists forward. With practice, the bottle "walks". That is the idea behind one theory of how the statues moved.
