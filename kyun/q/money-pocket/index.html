How should you manage pocket money?

Save ₹100 a week and in 15 weeks you have ₹1,500, enough for a good cricket bat. Spend it as it comes and you have a pile of wrappers.

The short answer

Pocket money is practice. It lets you make small money decisions, and small mistakes, long before the big ones arrive. The basic idea is simple. Before you spend, decide what the money is for.

Many families use three parts: some to spend now, some to save for something bigger, and some to share, such as a gift for a friend or a donation. Keep a note of what comes in and what goes out. Learn the difference between needs and wants. And give yourself time before buying things on impulse. These habits sound boring, but they are the same ones that grown-ups with steady finances use.

Surprising but true

There's a quick trick called the rule of 72. Divide 72 by an interest rate and you get roughly how many years it takes your money to double.

Spend, save, share

Splitting money as soon as you get it makes decisions easier. If ₹200 arrives and you put ₹100 in Save, ₹60 in Spend and ₹40 in Share, you already know your limit for the week.

The Save jar works best with a goal. "Saving" for nothing in particular is hard to stick to. Saving ₹1,500 for a cricket bat, a book set or a football is much easier, because you can see the finish line. At ₹100 a week, you reach it in 15 weeks. Drawing a chart and colouring it in each week makes it feel real.

Needs, wants and the waiting rule

A need is something you really must have, like school shoes that fit. A want is something nice, like a fancier pair with lights in the soles. Wants aren't bad. They just come after needs.

Shops, apps and games are designed to make you want things right now. Advertising uses bright colours, famous faces and "only today" deals to rush you. One defence is a waiting rule: when you want to buy something that isn't a need, wait a day or two. Often the urge fades. If you still want it, you can buy it knowing you really chose it.

Keeping track

Write down every bit of money that comes in and goes out, in a notebook or a simple app. After a month, add it up. Many people are surprised by how much went on small snacks or in-game coins.

This is a budget: a plan for money, made in advance. Families make one for groceries and school fees. Governments make one for roads and hospitals. Yours might only be a few hundred rupees or dollars, but the skill is the same.

When money is invisible

More and more payments happen on phones, through UPI in India or cards and apps in the USA. That is quick and safe, but it can make spending feel less real. Handing over a ₹500 note feels like losing something. Tapping a phone hardly feels like anything.

If you use digital money, check your balance often. Some families keep part of a child's pocket money in cash, so it's easier to see it shrink. Never share a PIN or one-time password with anyone, even if they say they are from a bank. Real banks never ask.

Letting money grow

Money in a piggy bank stays the same. Money in a savings account earns interest, a small payment from the bank for using your money. Interest is shown as a percentage of what you have.

The magic comes when interest earns interest. That is called compound interest. A handy shortcut is the rule of 72: divide 72 by the interest rate to find roughly how many years it takes to double. At 6%, about 12 years. At 9%, about 8. Starting young gives that snowball more time to roll. In India, children over 10 can open and run a basic savings account in their own name, under Reserve Bank of India rules. Many banks in the USA offer accounts for children that a parent helps manage.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Money in a bank just sits in a safe until you take it out.

Truth: Banks lend most of the money people deposit to others, who pay it back with extra. The bank shares a small part of that extra with savers as interest. Your balance is still there for you when you need it.

Try this

Find three jars or envelopes and label them Spend, Save and Share. For one month, split every bit of pocket money or gift money between them, perhaps half, a third and the rest. Write a saving goal on the Save jar and draw a bar that you colour in as you get closer.
