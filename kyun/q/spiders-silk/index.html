How strong is spider silk?

Weight for weight, the safety line a spider dangles from is stronger than steel, and it can stretch.

The short answer

Very strong, for its size. The dragline silk that a spider uses as its safety rope and as the frame of its web is about as strong as high-grade steel of the same thickness. Because silk is about six times lighter than steel, it is far stronger weight for weight.

It is also tough. Silk can stretch a long way before it snaps, so it soaks up the energy of a flying insect smacking into the web. By some measures, it is tougher than Kevlar, the material in bulletproof vests.

All of this comes from a thread thinner than a human hair, made from nothing more than protein, the same kind of stuff as your skin and muscles.

Surprising but true

A web-building spider often eats its old web and uses the protein to spin a new one.

How a spider spins

Inside a spider's belly are silk glands full of liquid protein, a bit like runny glue. The spider squeezes it out through tiny nozzles at the tip of its body, called spinnerets.

As the liquid is pulled out, the long protein molecules line up side by side and lock together. In a fraction of a second, the liquid becomes a solid, flexible thread. The spider usually pulls the thread with its back legs or lets its own weight drag it out as it drops.

A garden orb-weaver can make up to seven kinds of silk from different glands. There is strong silk for the frame, stretchy silk dotted with glue for the sticky spiral, soft silk for egg sacs and wide bands for wrapping up prey.

The toughest silk on Earth

In 2010, scientists described a spider from Madagascar that they named Darwin's bark spider. It strings webs right across rivers, with bridge lines up to about 25 m long, about the length of a tennis court.

Its silk is the toughest biological material ever measured, about twice as tough as other spider silks. Each morning, insects flying along the river crash into the web like cars into a net stretched across a road.

Spiders don't waste their silk. Many orb-weavers eat their old web before building a fresh one, recycling the protein. A new web can take less than an hour to spin.

Spiders that fly

Baby spiders can travel hundreds of kilometres without wings. A spiderling climbs to a high point, lifts its body and lets out fine threads of silk. The breeze, and even Earth's natural electric field, lifts it into the air. This is called ballooning.

In 1832, Charles Darwin was sailing off the coast of Argentina aboard HMS Beagle when thousands of tiny spiders landed on the ship's ropes. The nearest land was many kilometres away.

Golden cloth

Spider silk is so hard to collect that very little cloth has ever been made from it. In Madagascar, two textile makers, Simon Peers and Nicholas Godley, spent years gathering silk from wild golden orb-weaver spiders. Workers drew silk gently from each spider, then released it.

It took silk from over a million spiders to weave a shimmering golden shawl, shown at the American Museum of Natural History in New York in 2009, and later a cape. Silkworm silk, by comparison, is farmed by the tonne.

Scientists dream of using spider silk for medical stitches, light ropes and strong fabrics. They have put spider silk genes into bacteria, yeast, silkworms and even goats to make the protein. Getting the threads to spin as well as a spider's has proved much harder.

For years, spider silk did one useful job already. Its fine threads were used as crosshairs in telescopes and gun sights, because few other materials were so thin and even.

Myth or fact?

Myth: All spiders spin webs to catch their food.

Truth: Every spider makes silk, but only about half of them build webs to trap prey. Jumping spiders, wolf spiders and many others hunt by stalking or ambushing, and use silk for safety lines, nests and egg sacs.

Try this

On a misty or dewy morning, look for an orb web on a bush or fence, with an adult. Water droplets make every thread shine. Count the spokes and the turns of the spiral, but don't touch the web or the spider. If there's no dew, a very light mist from a plant sprayer held well back works too.
