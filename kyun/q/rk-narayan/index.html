Who was R.K. Narayan?

He invented a small South Indian town called Malgudi so completely that, decades later, a real railway station became a museum named after it.

The short answer

R.K. Narayan was one of the first Indian writers in English to become famous around the world. He lived from 1906 to 2001 and wrote fifteen novels and hundreds of short stories, most of them set in Malgudi, a small town he made up.

His stories are about ordinary people: a schoolboy who hates arithmetic, a tourist guide who is mistaken for a holy man, a sweet seller worried about his son. Narayan wrote in plain, gentle English with a lot of quiet humour. He rarely judged his characters. He watched them, the way a neighbour might.

Surprising but true

Narayan's first novel was rejected by publishers until a friend showed it to the English novelist Graham Greene, who liked it and helped get it published.

Growing up between two cities

Narayan was born in Madras, now Chennai, and spent much of his childhood with his grandmother there. Later he joined his family in Mysore, now Mysuru, where his father was a headmaster. He was not a keen student and failed his university entrance exam once.

He tried teaching and gave it up after a short time. He decided to become a writer, which in 1930s India was not an obvious way to earn a living, especially in English.

A lucky friendship

Narayan's first novel, Swami and Friends, follows a ten-year-old boy, Swaminathan, and his gang of friends in Malgudi. Several British publishers turned it down.

A friend studying at Oxford, Kittu Purna, took the manuscript to the English novelist Graham Greene. Greene liked it, recommended it to a publisher, and suggested the shorter pen name "R.K. Narayan". The book came out in 1935. Greene and Narayan stayed friends and wrote letters to each other for the rest of their lives.

The town that wasn't there

Malgudi has a river called Sarayu, a market road, a railway station, a mission school and a banyan tree. Readers can picture it so clearly that many assume it is real. Narayan said he first thought of the name, and of a railway station, before he had a single character.

The town changes slowly across the books, just as real Indian towns did in the 1900s. Cars, cinemas and new ideas arrive, while old habits stay put.

How did railways come to India?India's first passenger train ran for about 34 km in 1853. Today Indian Railways makes close to 2 crore passenger journeys on a typical day.

The Guide and Malgudi Days

Narayan's novel The Guide (1958) is about Raju, a railway-station guide who is mistaken for a holy man. It won the Sahitya Akademi Award, India's top literary prize, in 1960. A Hindi film version, Guide, starring Dev Anand, came out in 1965 and is still loved for its songs.

In 1986, the actor and director Shankar Nag turned Narayan's short stories into a television series, Malgudi Days, for Doordarshan. Much of it was filmed in Agumbe, a rainy village in the hills of Karnataka. Its hummed theme tune is instantly recognised by millions of Indians who grew up with early Indian television.

Brothers with a pen

Narayan's younger brother, R.K. Laxman, became one of India's best-loved cartoonists. For decades his Common Man, a silent figure in a checked coat, watched politics from the front page of The Times of India. Laxman drew the pictures for Malgudi Days.

Narayan wrote in English, and some critics in his lifetime asked whether an Indian writer should. He felt English was simply the language he wrote best in, and he used it to describe Indian life without explaining it for foreigners. He received the Padma Vibhushan in 2000.

The old building of Arasalu, a small railway station in Karnataka that appeared in the TV series, has since been turned into the Malgudi Museum. It celebrates a town that only ever existed in books.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Malgudi is a real town somewhere in South India.

Truth: Malgudi is imaginary. Narayan built it from bits of Mysuru, Chennai and other places he knew. An old station in Karnataka became a Malgudi museum in honour of the books, but the town in the stories exists only on the page.
