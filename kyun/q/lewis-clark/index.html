Who were Lewis and Clark?

In 1804 a small team set off up the Missouri River to find a water route to the Pacific. There wasn't one, but what they did find changed the map of North America.

The short answer

Meriwether Lewis and William Clark led an expedition across the western part of North America from 1804 to 1806. President Thomas Jefferson sent them after the United States bought a vast area called Louisiana from France in 1803, roughly doubling the country's size.

Their team, called the Corps of Discovery, travelled by boat, on foot and on horseback from near St. Louis to the Pacific coast and back. They mapped rivers, recorded hundreds of plants and animals new to science, and met dozens of Native nations. They did not find the easy water route Jefferson hoped for, but they showed Americans what lay to the west.

Surprising but true

Only one member of the expedition died in more than two years of travel, probably from a burst appendix, which no doctor at the time could have cured.

Why Jefferson sent them

In 1803 France sold Louisiana to the United States for 15 million dollars. It stretched from the Mississippi to the Rocky Mountains. France had lost interest partly because of the revolution in Haiti, which cost Napoleon his richest colony.

Few Americans had seen this land. Jefferson wanted to know what was there, and whether a boat could travel by river to the Pacific Ocean. He chose Lewis, his private secretary, who then asked his old army friend Clark to share the command.

Up the Missouri

The team of about 30 to 40 men left Camp Dubois, near St. Louis, in May 1804. They pushed, rowed and dragged a big keelboat up the Missouri River against the current. Some days they managed only a few kilometres.

They spent their first winter among the Mandan people in what is now North Dakota. There they met Sacagawea, a young Lemhi Shoshone woman, and her French-Canadian husband, Toussaint Charbonneau. They joined as interpreters. Sacagawea had a baby, Jean Baptiste, in February 1805, and carried him the rest of the way.

Across the mountains

In August 1805 the expedition reached the Shoshone. By an astonishing chance, their chief, Cameahwait, turned out to be Sacagawea's brother, whom she had not seen since she was kidnapped as a child. The Shoshone traded horses the team badly needed.

Crossing the Bitterroot Mountains was the hardest part. Snow fell, game was scarce, and the men ate candles and some of their horses. When they stumbled out, hungry and ill, the Nez Perce people fed them and looked after their horses. In November 1805 they finally saw the Pacific. Clark wrote in his journal, "Ocian in view! O! the joy."

The whole team

The expedition included York, a man enslaved by Clark. He hunted, scouted and voted with the others on where to spend the winter. Yet after they returned, Clark refused for years to free him. Lewis also brought his big black Newfoundland dog, Seaman, who caught squirrels for the cooking pot and guarded the camp at night.

What they brought back

The team returned to St. Louis in September 1806, long after many people had given them up for dead. Their journals described about 178 plants and 122 animals unknown to Western science, including the grizzly bear and the prairie dog. Clark's maps were used for decades.

In spring 1805 the team even sent a live prairie dog back east by boat. It made the journey of thousands of kilometres and arrived alive at Jefferson's home, the White House, along with skins, bones and seeds.

For Native nations, the journey marked the start of something harder. Settlers, traders and soldiers soon followed the route, and many nations lost their lands. In 2000 the United States put Sacagawea on a one-dollar coin.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Lewis and Clark explored empty, unknown land.

Truth: Every place they went was already home to Native nations, who knew it well. The expedition survived because many of those nations gave food, horses, maps and advice.

Try this

Keep an explorer's journal for one week. Each day, sketch one plant, insect or bird you see near home or school, describe it in words, and note the weather. Lewis and Clark did exactly this, and their notes are still read by scientists.
