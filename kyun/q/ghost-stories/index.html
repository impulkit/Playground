Why do people love ghost stories?

About 1,900 years ago, a Roman writer named Pliny the Younger wrote to a friend about a haunted house in Athens. People have been scaring each other on purpose ever since.

The short answer

People love ghost stories because they let us feel fear while we are safe. Your heart beats faster, your skin prickles and your breath catches, yet you are tucked in bed or sitting by a campfire with friends.

That mix of danger and safety is a thrill, like a roller coaster for the mind. When the story ends, your body relaxes and you feel a rush of relief.

Ghost stories also bring people together. They are told in groups, often at night, and the shared shiver is part of the fun. And they ask big questions, about death and memory and the things we can't explain.

Surprising but true

Scientists at a "Recreational Fear Lab" in Denmark have studied people in haunted houses and found that most enjoy being scared just the right amount, not too little and not too much.

Very old shivers

Ghost stories are among the oldest stories we have. Around 100 CE, the Roman writer Pliny the Younger wrote a letter about a house in Athens that nobody would live in. At night came the clank of chains and a thin, old ghost.

A philosopher named Athenodorus rented the house anyway, because it was cheap. When the ghost beckoned, he followed it to a spot in the courtyard. Workers dug there the next day and found old bones. Once the bones were buried properly, the haunting stopped. Many later ghost stories follow the same pattern: a restless spirit and a wrong put right.

Ghosts in Indian storytelling

India has a deep store of spooky tales. In Vikram and Betal, a king must carry a corpse possessed by a ghost. Each time, Betal tells a story with a riddle at the end, and whenever the king answers, the ghost flies back to its tree.

Grandparents across India tell of bhoots with backward feet, of churails and of the brahmarakshas who haunts old trees. Bengali writers, including Satyajit Ray, wrote ghost stories for young readers. Ruskin Bond set many of his in the misty hills around Mussoorie, where a ghost might be a lonely boy or a friendly old man.

Your body on a scary story

When a story frightens you, a small part of your brain called the amygdala raises the alarm. Your body releases adrenaline. Your heart speeds up and your muscles get ready to run, even though you are only listening.

But another part of your brain knows you are safe. So the fear never gets out of hand. Some scientists think this is useful practice, letting us try out scary feelings without real danger. At Aarhus University in Denmark, a team called the Recreational Fear Lab studies people at haunted house attractions to learn more.

It also explains why ghost stories work best in the dark. With less to see, your brain fills the gaps with guesses about what might be there, and when you are already on edge, those guesses tend to be scary ones. A coat on a hook becomes a figure. A creak becomes a footstep.

Stories for a long night

Ghost stories often belong to the darkest, coldest time of year. In Victorian Britain, families told them at Christmas. Charles Dickens wrote A Christmas Carol in 1843, in which three spirits visit a mean old man named Scrooge and change him for good.

One Cambridge scholar, M. R. James, wrote new ghost stories and read them aloud to friends on Christmas Eve, by the light of a single candle.

In the USA, ghosts come out at Halloween, with haunted hayrides and costumes. In India, some families tell spooky tales on rainy monsoon nights when the power goes out. In each case the weather outside makes the warm room feel safer, and the story feels scarier.

Do ghosts exist? There is no scientific evidence that they do. But the stories are real, and so is the shiver they give you.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Bhangarh Fort is officially recognised as a haunted place.

Truth: The Archaeological Survey of India's sign at Bhangarh bans entry between sunset and sunrise, a rule found at many protected ruins. The haunted reputation comes from local legends and from travel shows.

Try this

Tell a spooky story with a friend, one sentence each, in a room with the lights low. Try to scare each other using only sounds and small details, like a creak or a cold draught, with no monsters allowed. Notice which sentences made you both jump.
