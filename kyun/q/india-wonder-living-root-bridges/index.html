How do trees become bridges in Meghalaya?

In one of the rainiest places on Earth, villagers grow bridges out of living tree roots, and the oldest ones get stronger every year.

The short answer

In the hills of Meghalaya, in north-east India, Khasi and Jaintia villagers grow bridges from the roots of a tree called the rubber fig. This tree sends long roots down from its branches and trunk. Builders guide these young roots across a stream, often along a hollow bamboo or palm trunk, until they reach the far bank and root into the soil.

Over the years, more roots are added, twisted and woven together. Stones fill the gaps to make a walkway. After about 15 years or more, people can cross. The bridge keeps growing for as long as the tree lives, and some are thought to be well over 100 years old.

Surprising but true

A living root bridge can take 15 years or more to be ready to walk on, so the people who start one often build it for their grandchildren.

Why grow a bridge

This region is wet. Cherrapunji and nearby Mawsynram are among the rainiest places on Earth, with more than 11 metres of rain in a year. Stacked up, that much water would rise higher than a three-storey house. In the monsoon, quiet streams turn into roaring rivers.

A wooden bridge rots in a few years in such damp. Steel rusts, and carrying it up steep jungle paths is hard. A living bridge needs no factory and no lorry. It just needs a tree, time and patient hands. The roots also grip the soil of steep banks, so a root bridge helps stop the stream from washing the path away.

How the roots are trained

The rubber fig is a cousin of the banyan, and like the banyan it grows aerial roots, roots that hang in the air before reaching the ground. Young roots are soft and bendy. Builders lay them along guides of bamboo or split areca palm stems laid across the stream, and gently tie them in place.

Where two roots press together, they slowly fuse into one. Scientists call this inosculation. Over decades, the roots form a thick net that can carry dozens of people at once. Some bridges stretch more than 20 m, longer than a cricket pitch.

How do bridges hold up so much weight?Every bridge is a tug-of-war between two forces, a push and a pull. Good engineers decide which parts get which.

The double-decker

The most famous root bridge is near the village of Nongriat, below Tyrna. It has two levels, one root bridge above another, crossing the same stream. Visitors reach it by climbing down thousands of stone steps through the forest, and then, harder, climbing back up.

Some villages have also grown root ladders up cliffs and root platforms along slopes. The bridges are part of a whole way of life in which forests, streams and farms are looked after together.

A design the world is studying

Engineers usually build bridges that are strongest on the day they open and slowly wear out. A root bridge does the opposite. Researchers from Germany studied dozens of Meghalaya's bridges and published their findings in 2019. They are interested in using living plants in city buildings, an idea some architects call "tree architecture".

Local groups have counted root bridges in more than 70 villages. In 2022, India added them to UNESCO's tentative list of World Heritage Sites, the first step towards full recognition.

Visitors and worries

The bridges now attract thousands of tourists, which brings money to villages but also litter and wear. Too many feet can damage young roots, and some villages have started charging small fees and limiting crowds.

There is another worry. As roads and concrete bridges arrive, fewer young people learn the old skill. So some communities and local groups now teach children how to start and care for a new bridge. The trees are part of something bigger. The hills around them hold forests full of frogs, orchids and birds found almost nowhere else, as rich in places as the great rainforests near the equator.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The root bridges grew that way by themselves.

Truth: People shape every bridge. Builders guide young roots across a stream for years, tying, twisting and weaving them together. Without that care, the roots would simply hang down or wander.

Try this

Plant two money plant or pothos cuttings in a pot and give them a stick or string to climb. Each week, gently guide the new growth along the path you choose by loosely tying it. You are doing a tiny version of what Khasi bridge builders do over many years.
