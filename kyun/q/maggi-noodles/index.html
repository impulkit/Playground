How did instant noodles take over India?

Instant noodles were invented in a backyard shed in Japan by a man who wanted to feed hungry people after a war.

The short answer

Instant noodles are noodles that have been cooked and then fried or dried at a factory, so they need only a few minutes in hot water. They were invented in Japan in 1958. In India, Nestlé launched Maggi 2-minute noodles in 1983, and the yellow packet soon became a part of growing up for millions of children.

They caught on because they were cheap, quick, filling and easy enough for a hungry child or a tired hostel student to make. Clever advertising helped. So did the masala tastemaker, which gave the noodles a flavour that felt familiar to Indian tongues. Today almost every hill-station stall and college canteen in India has a version on the menu.

Surprising but true

Instant noodles are already cooked when you buy them. They were steamed, then fried or dried at the factory, so boiling water only has to soften them.

A shed in Osaka

After the Second World War, Japan was short of food. A businessman named Momofuku Ando saw long queues for bowls of ramen in Osaka and wondered whether noodles could be made that anyone could cook at home in minutes.

He experimented for about a year in a small shed behind his house. The breakthrough came when he watched his wife frying tempura. Frying drove the water out of the batter and left it full of tiny holes. He fried seasoned noodles the same way, and in 1958 he sold the first packets of Chikin Ramen. In 1971 he followed it with Cup Noodles, sold in a cup you could eat from. Today, according to the World Instant Noodles Association, people eat more than 100 billion servings of instant noodles a year, with China, Indonesia and India among the biggest eaters.

Two minutes, more or less

Maggi was a Swiss brand long before it made noodles. Julius Maggi began selling ready-made soups and seasonings in Switzerland in the 1880s, partly to give factory workers quick, cheap, nourishing meals. The company later became part of Nestlé.

When Maggi noodles arrived in India, the adverts promised a snack ready in two minutes, made by children for themselves after school. In practice, most people take a little longer. Families invented their own versions too: with peas and onions, with an egg, with extra chilli, or with cheese. On mountain treks in Himachal and Uttarakhand, a steaming plate of Maggi at a roadside stall is almost a tradition.

The ban of 2015

In June 2015, India's food regulator, the FSSAI, ordered Maggi noodles off shop shelves. It said tests had found lead above the allowed limit, and objected to a label that said "no added MSG". Nestlé disagreed, but withdrew and destroyed huge stocks of noodles already in shops.

In August 2015, the Bombay High Court overturned the ban, and fresh tests found the noodles within safe limits. Maggi returned to shops in November that year. The episode made many families read food packets more carefully.

Something to eat sometimes

Instant noodles are mostly refined flour, oil and salt. A single packet can hold close to half of the salt that health experts suggest for an adult in a whole day, and not much fibre, vitamins or protein.

That does not make them poison. Nutritionists usually suggest treating them as an occasional snack rather than a daily meal, and adding vegetables, an egg or paneer to make a bowl more filling. The noodles took over India because they solved a real problem: getting hot food on the table quickly, for very little money.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Instant noodles have a coating of wax that stays in your stomach for days.

Truth: There is no wax. The shine comes from the oil used to fry the noodles. Your body digests them like other foods made of flour and oil.

Try this

Read the packet of any instant noodles at home. Find the salt (sodium) per serving and compare it with an adult's daily limit, which the WHO sets at under 2,000 mg of sodium. Then work out what share of a day's salt one packet holds.
