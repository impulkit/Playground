How are maths and music connected?

Halve a guitar string and it plays the same note, only higher. That simple fact links music to maths, from ancient Greece to a tabla player's tihai.

The short answer

Music is full of patterns, and patterns are what maths describes. Every musical note is a vibration with a frequency, a number of shakes per second. Notes that sound good together usually have frequencies in simple ratios, like 2 to 1 or 3 to 2.

Rhythm is maths too. Beats are counted, grouped and divided, like fractions. A tabla player landing perfectly on the first beat after a long, tricky phrase is doing a kind of calculation, even if it feels like pure music. People have noticed these links for at least 2,500 years, in Greece, India, China and elsewhere.

Surprising but true

The number pattern now called the Fibonacci sequence was described in India centuries before Fibonacci, by scholars counting the rhythms of Sanskrit poetry.

Strings and simple ratios

An old story says that the Greek thinker Pythagoras noticed that strings of certain lengths sounded good together. Whether or not he did it himself, his followers studied the numbers.

They found that if one string is exactly half the length of another, it plays the same note, but higher. Today we call that an octave. A string two-thirds as long plays a note called a fifth, which sounds very harmonious with the first. Three-quarters gives a fourth. The simpler the ratio, the smoother the two notes sound together.

Modern science explains why. Halving a string doubles its frequency. The A note that orchestras tune to vibrates 440 times a second, and the A one octave higher vibrates 880 times. Your ears hear these doublings as the same note at a different height.

Twelve equal steps

On a piano, each octave is split into 12 steps called semitones, counting the black keys and the white keys. In the tuning most pianos use, called equal temperament, every step multiplies the frequency by the same amount: the twelfth root of 2, which is about 1.0595. Twelve steps multiply it by exactly 2.

This is a compromise. Pure ratios like 3:2 are slightly out with equal steps, but the trade-off lets musicians play in any key. Indian classical music works differently. It traditionally speaks of 22 shrutis, fine shades of pitch, and singers often slide between notes rather than jumping.

Counting the beat

Indian classical music has detailed rhythm cycles called tala. Teentaal, common in Hindustani music, has 16 beats split into four groups of four. Adi tala, common in Carnatic music, has 8 beats grouped 4 + 2 + 2.

Players love rhythmic puzzles. A tihai is a phrase repeated three times so that it ends exactly on sam, the first beat of the cycle. To make it land, a tabla player must work out the length of the phrase and the gaps so that three copies fit perfectly. Audiences often clap when the last stroke hits sam.

Fibonacci, from poetry

Sanskrit poetry uses short and long syllables. A long one takes twice the time of a short one. Scholars asked: how many different rhythms can fill a line of a given length? The answers are 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, and so on, with each number the sum of the two before.

Indian scholars such as Virahanka, perhaps in the 7th century, and Hemachandra, around 1150, described this pattern. In Europe it is named after Fibonacci, who wrote about it in 1202 in a puzzle about rabbits. The same numbers turn up in pine cones and sunflowers.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You have to be good at maths to be good at music.

Truth: Many brilliant musicians never think about numbers at all. Music and maths share patterns, and studying one can help you notice things in the other, but neither is needed for the other.

Try this

Stretch a rubber band tightly around an open box or a lunchbox and pluck it. Then press it down exactly in the middle with a pencil and pluck one side. Then try pressing it one-third of the way along. Listen to how the notes relate. Do any of them sound good together?
