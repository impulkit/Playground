Did whales once walk on land?

Whale ancestors had four legs, and some of the clues were found in India.

The short answer

Yes. About 50 million years ago, some small four-legged mammals started wading into water to find food and to hide from danger. Over millions of years, their descendants became better and better swimmers. Their front legs turned into flippers, their back legs shrank away and their tails grew flat flukes.

Those animals became today's whales and dolphins. Whales still breathe air and feed their babies milk, just like other mammals.

Some of the most important clues come from India. Indohyus, a key whale relative, was found in Jammu and Kashmir. Its bones were collected by the Indian geologist A. Ranga Rao.

Surprising but true

A whale's closest living relative on land is the hippo.

A bone in the ear

Whales have an unusual ear bone, thick and heavy with a curled lip. No other mammals have one quite like it. So when scientists found the same kind of ear bone in fossils of land animals, they knew they had found early relatives of whales.

The first was Pakicetus, a wolf-sized animal found in Pakistan in the early 1980s. Then came Indohyus. Ranga Rao had collected its fossils near Kalakot decades earlier. After he died, his family gave the rocks to the American scientist Hans Thewissen. In his account, a lab worker accidentally cracked open one skull, and there was the telltale ear bone.

Indohyus was about the size of a raccoon and looked a bit like a tiny deer. Its bones were thick and heavy, like a hippo's, which would help it stay down while wading.

From legs to flippers

Over about 10 million years, a parade of animals moved further into the sea. Ambulocetus, the "walking whale", had big feet and probably swam like an otter. Later kinds had shorter legs and stronger tails. Basilosaurus, about 40 million years ago, was fully at home in the ocean, with back legs so tiny they were useless for walking.

Their noses moved slowly back up the head and became blowholes. Their backbones kept bending up and down, the way a running dog's does, instead of side to side like a fish.

An Indian story

Many key early whale fossils come from India and Pakistan. That is because, back then, this region was the coast of a warm, shallow sea called the Tethys. As India drifted north and pushed into Asia, as told in India's drift, that sea closed up, and its seabed rose into hills full of fossils.

In Kutch, in Gujarat, the Indian palaeontologist Sunil Bajpai and his colleagues have found other early whales, including a small, otter-like one named Kutchicetus.

Whale bones in strange places

In the 1830s, huge bones turned up on farms in Louisiana and Alabama in the USA. One scientist thought they came from a giant sea reptile and named it Basilosaurus, meaning "king lizard". It turned out to be a whale. Today it is Alabama's state fossil.

In Egypt's desert, the Wadi Al-Hitan, or "Valley of the Whales", is covered with Basilosaurus skeletons, some with their tiny legs still in place. It is now one of the UNESCO World Heritage Sites.

Living whales still carry hints of their past. Many have small hip bones buried in their bodies. Whale embryos briefly grow little bumps where back legs would be. In 2006, fishermen in Japan even caught a dolphin with an extra pair of small fins near its tail, in the place where back legs once grew. Its cousins, like the blue whale, have travelled a long way from those first waders.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Whales evolved straight from fish.

Truth: Fish crawled onto land hundreds of millions of years ago and eventually gave rise to mammals. Whales come from land mammals that later went back to the water, so their nearest fishy relatives lived very long ago.

Try this

Lie on your tummy on a carpet and wiggle like a fish, bending from side to side. Now try moving like a dolphin, bending your body up and down. Whales swim up and down because they come from land mammals, whose backbones bend that way when they run.
