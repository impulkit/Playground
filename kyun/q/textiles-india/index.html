How did Indian cloth amaze the world?

Words like calico, chintz, dungarees and bandana all came into English from Indian cloth.

The short answer

For more than two thousand years, Indian cloth was some of the most wanted in the world. Indian weavers made cotton so fine it floated like mist, and dyers made colours that stayed bright after washing. Traders carried Indian textiles to Rome, Egypt, China, Southeast Asia, Africa and Europe.

Indian cotton changed what the world wore. Even the English language is full of words for cloth that came from India. The story also has a hard side. When British factories began making cheap cloth with machines, India's handweavers suffered badly, and that became a big part of India's freedom struggle.

Surprising but true

Britain once passed laws banning people from wearing printed Indian cotton, because it was so popular that British wool and silk makers feared they would go out of business.

Cotton from the Indus

India is one of the first places where people grew cotton and spun it into thread. At Mohenjo-daro, one of the great Indus Valley cities, archaeologists found a scrap of cotton cloth over 4,000 years old. It had been dyed red with madder, a plant root.

Cotton is tricky to spin, because its fibres are short. Indian spinners learned to twist them into long, strong threads by hand. Weavers then turned that thread into cloth on wooden looms.

Cloth for emperors

The finest Indian cloth was muslin, especially from around Dhaka, in what is now Bangladesh. The best muslins were so thin and light that they seemed almost see-through. Some had poetic names that meant "running water" or "evening dew".

Ancient Romans bought so much Indian cloth, spice and jewellery that the writer Pliny the Elder complained, nearly 2,000 years ago, that Rome's gold was flowing east to pay for it. Much later, Mughal emperors and their courts wore Indian silks and muslins, and gave them as precious gifts.

Which English words came from India?Every time you put on pyjamas, wash with shampoo or talk about a jungle, you are borrowing from Indian languages.

Colours that didn't fade

Indian dyers were masters of colour. They used plants like indigo for blue, madder for red and turmeric or pomegranate rind for yellow. The secret was the mordant, a substance that helps dye bond to cotton. With mordants, Indian colours stayed bright after many washes.

Indigo was so linked with India that its name comes from a Greek word meaning "Indian". When Europeans saw printed and painted Indian cottons in the 1600s, they went wild for them. They called them calico, after the port of Calicut (Kozhikode), and chintz, from the Hindi word chhint, meaning spotted or sprinkled.

Too popular for Britain

The East India Company shipped huge amounts of Indian cotton to Britain. Soon everyone wanted calico curtains, dresses and bedspreads. British wool and silk weavers were furious. In 1700 and 1721, Parliament passed laws that banned importing or wearing most printed Indian cottons.

British inventors then built machines to spin and weave cotton cheaply, a key part of the Industrial Revolution. Raw cotton was shipped from India to British mills, and machine-made cloth was sold back to India.

What happened to India's weavers

Machine-made cloth was cheaper than handwoven cloth. Under British rule, Indian weavers could not compete, and many lost their living. Towns that had been famous for weaving shrank.

In the early 1900s, leaders of the freedom movement urged Indians to buy Indian cloth instead of British. Gandhi went further, spinning his own thread and asking everyone to wear handmade khadi. Today India's handloom weavers still make famous fabrics, from Banarasi silk to Pochampally ikat, and many of them have legal protection for their names.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Fine cotton cloth was a European invention of the Industrial Revolution.

Truth: Indian weavers had been making fine cotton cloth for thousands of years. British factories in the 1700s and 1800s mostly copied Indian designs and used machines to make similar cloth faster and cheaper.
