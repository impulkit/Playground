What is empathy?

In a 2011 experiment, rats learned to open a cage to free a trapped companion, even when a pile of chocolate chips sat in the next cage.

The short answer

Empathy is the ability to understand and share the emotions someone else is feeling. When your friend loses a match and your own stomach sinks, that's empathy. So is noticing that a classmate has gone quiet and guessing something is wrong at home.

Psychologists often split empathy into parts. Emotional empathy is catching some of another person's feeling, like flinching when you see someone stub their toe. Cognitive empathy is working out what someone feels and why, almost like stepping into their shoes. Compassion goes one step further, adding the wish to help. Together, these let people live, play and work alongside each other.

Surprising but true

Newborn babies cry more when they hear another baby crying than when they hear a recording of their own cry, an early hint of feelings spreading from one person to another.

Babies, toddlers and tears

Empathy starts early. Studies going back to the 1970s found that newborns in hospital cry more when they hear another baby cry. They cry less at a recording of their own crying, so it's not just noise that sets them off.

By around 18 months, toddlers start to help. In a well-known 2006 study in Germany, a grown-up dropped a clothes peg or struggled to open a cupboard while carrying books. Most toddlers waddled over to help, without being asked or rewarded. Young chimpanzees did something similar.

Over childhood, the thinking side grows. Children learn that others can have different feelings, wishes and knowledge from their own, and that a smile can hide sadness. Psychologists call this understanding "theory of mind", and most children have a good grip on it by about the age of five.

What happens in the brain

Scientists have scanned people's brains while they watched someone they love receive a mild electric shock. Some of the same areas that respond to a person's own pain lit up when they saw a partner in pain. They weren't feeling the shock, but part of the brain seemed to rehearse it.

In the 1990s, researchers in Italy found neurons in monkeys that fired both when a monkey grabbed food and when it watched someone else grab food. They were named mirror neurons. Some people claimed they explained human empathy. Most scientists now think they are only part of a more complicated story.

Beyond humans

Empathy-like behaviour shows up in other animals. In a 2011 experiment in the USA, rats learned to open a little door to free a trapped cage-mate. Many did this before eating chocolate that was also on offer, and some saved chocolate for the rat they had freed.

Asian elephants in Thailand have been seen comforting upset companions, touching them with their trunks and making soft chirping sounds. Chimpanzees hug and groom friends who have lost a fight. Dogs often come close when their owners cry.

Where empathy falls short

Empathy has limits. People tend to feel it most for those who look like them, live near them or belong to their group. One sad story about a single named child often moves people more than news about thousands who are suffering.

Some thinkers, like the psychologist Paul Bloom, argue that empathy can even lead us astray, and that calm, fair kindness is a better guide for big decisions. Others reply that without empathy we wouldn't care in the first place. Both sides agree that feeling for others is a starting point, and that kindness is what turns it into action.

Growing it

Empathy can get stronger. Listening without interrupting, asking "How did that feel?" and reading stories about people unlike yourself all seem to help. A Canadian programme called Roots of Empathy, started in 1996, brings a baby and parent into classrooms so children can watch the baby grow and talk about its feelings. Studies have linked it to less fighting and bullying among students.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You're either born with empathy or you aren't, and it can't change.

Truth: Most people are born with the ability, but how much they use it grows with practice, experience and good examples. Listening carefully, reading stories and spending time with people different from you can all strengthen it.

Try this

Watch two minutes of a film or cartoon with the sound turned off. Pause on a character's face and guess what they are feeling and why. Then turn the sound on and check. Reading faces and situations like this is a big part of cognitive empathy, and it gets better with practice.
