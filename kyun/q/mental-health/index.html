What is mental health?

Everyone has mental health, the same way everyone has a heart, and it has good days and bad days too.

The short answer

Mental health is how you're doing on the inside: how you think, how you feel and how you cope with life. It affects how you sleep, learn, play and get along with people. The World Health Organization describes good mental health as being able to handle the normal stresses of life, learn and work well, and feel part of your community.

Everyone has mental health, just as everyone has physical health. Both go up and down. A bad week after a fight with a friend is normal. When sad, worried or angry feelings stay for weeks and get in the way of everyday life, it may be a mental health condition, and help is available.

Surprising but true

According to the World Health Organization, about half of all mental health conditions begin by the age of 14.

A body and a mind, wired together

Your feelings come from your brain, which is part of your body. So it makes sense that the body and mind affect each other. Poor sleep can make worries feel bigger. Exercise, sunlight and time with friends often lift people's mood. A frightening event can make the heart race and the stomach churn, which is your stress system switching on. Hunger, illness and even a long spell of hot weather can change how people feel.

The most common conditions are anxiety and depression. Anxiety is worry that is too strong or won't switch off. Depression is more than feeling sad. It can make people feel flat, tired and uninterested in things they used to enjoy, for weeks at a time.

When champions spoke up

For a long time, many people kept mental health problems secret. That has started to change, partly because famous people have spoken openly. At the Tokyo Olympics in 2021, the American gymnast Simone Biles withdrew from several events. She said her mind and body were not in sync, which is dangerous when you are twisting high in the air. Many people praised her for putting her health first. She returned to win a bronze medal on the balance beam a few days later.

In India, the film actor Deepika Padukone talked publicly in 2015 about her depression and later set up a foundation to fight the shame around it. Stories like these help others realise they are not alone.

Getting help

If you feel low or worried for a long time, the first step is to tell an adult you trust: a parent, a teacher or a school counsellor. Doctors can help too. Treatment might mean talking regularly with a trained counsellor, learning ways to handle difficult thoughts, and sometimes medicine prescribed by a doctor.

Many countries now run free phone helplines. In India, the government launched Tele-MANAS, on 14416, in 2022. In the USA, people can call or text 988. Both connect callers with trained people who listen. Asking for help is a sensible thing to do, the same as seeing a doctor for a broken arm.

Small things that help

Some everyday habits support mental health for most people. Getting enough sleep is one of the biggest. Moving your body, eating regular meals, spending time outdoors and having a few good friendships all help.

So does noticing your feelings and putting them into words. Scientists have found that naming an emotion, even just saying "I feel nervous", can make it a little easier to manage. These habits don't stop hard times from coming. They're more like a good raincoat: they help you get through the weather.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Mental health problems are a sign of weakness, and people could snap out of them if they tried.

Truth: Conditions like depression and anxiety are health problems, linked to the brain, the body and life events. Nobody chooses them, and strong, successful people get them too. Like other health problems, they usually get better with the right support.

Try this

Make a feelings weather chart for a week. Each evening, draw the weather that matches your mood, such as sun, clouds, rain or wind, and write one thing that helped or didn't. At the end of the week, look for patterns, like how sleep, games or time with friends changed your weather.
