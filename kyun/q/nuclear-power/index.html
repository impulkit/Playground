How does a nuclear power plant work?

The first nuclear reactor was built in 1942 under the seats of a sports stadium in Chicago, out of graphite bricks and lumps of uranium.

The short answer

A nuclear power plant boils water, just like a coal power plant. The difference is how it makes the heat. Instead of burning fuel, it splits atoms of uranium.

The centre of a uranium-235 atom, its nucleus, is big and a little unstable. When a tiny particle called a neutron hits it, the nucleus can split into two smaller pieces. This is called fission. It releases a burst of heat and two or three more neutrons. Those neutrons can split more uranium atoms, which release more neutrons, and so on. That's a chain reaction.

In a reactor, the heat from this chain reaction warms water to make steam. The steam spins a turbine, and the turbine spins a generator that makes electricity.

Surprising but true

The tall, curved towers people picture at nuclear plants release clouds of water vapour, not smoke. Many nuclear plants don't have them at all.

Under the stadium

The first nuclear reactor was built at the University of Chicago, in a squash court beneath the stands of a sports field. A team led by the Italian-born physicist Enrico Fermi stacked tens of thousands of graphite blocks into a rough pile, with lumps of uranium tucked inside.

On 2 December 1942, they slowly pulled out cadmium rods that soaked up neutrons. The chain reaction started and kept itself going for about four minutes before they shut it down. This happened during the Second World War, and the same science was soon used to build atomic bombs. After the war, many countries turned to using it for electricity.

Keeping it under control

A power reactor must never let its chain reaction run away. Control rods, made of materials that soak up neutrons, slide in and out of the fuel. Push them in and the reaction slows. Pull them out and it speeds up. Huge flows of water carry heat away and keep the core cool.

The fuel itself comes as small ceramic pellets, each about the size of a fingertip, packed into long metal tubes. A single pellet can release roughly as much energy as a tonne of coal. Thick steel and concrete walls surround the reactor to hold in radiation.

Nuclear power in India and the USA

Homi Bhabha founded India's atomic energy programme in the 1940s and 1950s. India's first nuclear power station opened at Tarapur, on the coast north of Mumbai, in 1969. Today India runs over 20 reactors, and plans more, but they make only about 3% of its electricity.

The USA has more nuclear reactors than any other country, over 90 of them. Together they make close to a fifth of American electricity.

Benefits and worries

Nuclear plants run day and night in any weather and give off almost no carbon dioxide, the main gas warming our planet. That's why some people see them as part of the answer to climate change.

They also have serious risks. Accidents at Chernobyl in the Soviet Union in 1986 and at Fukushima in Japan in 2011 forced many thousands of people to leave their homes. Used fuel stays dangerously radioactive for thousands of years, and countries are still working out where to store it safely. Its slow fading is described by its half-life. New plants are also very expensive and slow to build.

Scientists are also trying to master fusion, joining small atoms together the way the Sun does. If it works, it could one day give power with far less long-lived waste.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The big towers at nuclear power plants pour out radioactive smoke.

Truth: Those are cooling towers, and the white clouds are water vapour, like steam from a kettle. They cool water from the steam side of the plant, which never touches the reactor core. Coal plants use the same kind of tower.

Try this

Model a chain reaction with dominoes. Stand them in a single line and knock the first one over. One falls, then the next. Now set them up in a branching pattern, where each domino knocks over two more. Notice how much faster the falling spreads. Then take a few out of the middle, as control rods do, and see the reaction stop.
