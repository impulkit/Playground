Why does bread rise?

Every fluffy loaf of bread is full of gas breathed out by billions of tiny living fungi.

The short answer

Most bread rises because of yeast, a living fungus made of single cells far too small to see. When you mix yeast into dough, it wakes up and feeds on sugars in the flour. As it feeds, it gives off bubbles of carbon dioxide, the same gas you breathe out.

Those bubbles would simply escape if it weren't for gluten. When wheat flour is mixed with water and kneaded, two proteins link up into a stretchy net. The net traps the gas like millions of tiny balloons, and the dough swells. In the oven, the heat makes the bubbles grow bigger, then sets the dough firm around them.

Surprising but true

The oldest bread crumbs ever found, in Jordan, are about 14,400 years old, older than farming itself.

Tiny workers in the dough

Bakers' yeast is a type of fungus, a distant cousin of mushrooms. A single teaspoon of dried yeast holds billions of cells. In warm, wet dough, each cell eats sugar and buds off a new cell every couple of hours.

Flour doesn't contain much sugar at first. It's mostly starch. Natural enzymes in the flour slowly snip the starch into sugars, feeding the yeast. Along with gas, yeast makes a small amount of alcohol and dozens of flavour chemicals. That's why dough left to rise slowly overnight tastes richer.

Kneading builds the net

Kneading isn't just mixing. Every push and fold lines up the gluten proteins into long, stretchy strands. You can test it: a well-kneaded dough can be pulled so thin you can almost see light through it.

Wheat has lots of gluten, which is why wheat makes tall loaves. Rye, millet and rice have little or none. Breads made from them are flatter or need other tricks. Indian rotis need no yeast at all. They puff up on a hot tawa because water inside turns to steam.

What the oven does

In a hot oven, bread gets one last burst of growth that bakers call "oven spring". The gas in every bubble expands in the heat, and the yeast works frantically for a few minutes. At about 60 °C the yeast dies. Then the starch and proteins set, locking the bubbles in place.

The outside dries and browns into a crust, as sugars and proteins react to make new colours and smells. The holes you see in a slice of bread are the frozen ghosts of those gas bubbles.

Rising without yeast

Not every fluffy food uses yeast. Baking soda and baking powder are chemical raising agents. When they meet something acidic, like curd, lemon juice or buttermilk, or get hot, they release carbon dioxide straight away, with no waiting. That's why cake batter, dhokla and soda bread can go into the oven or steamer almost as soon as they're mixed.

Steam can do the job too. Puris swell into golden balloons in hot oil, as water inside the dough suddenly boils.

A very old trick

In 2018, scientists studying an ancient fireplace in Jordan found charred bread crumbs about 14,400 years old. They were flatbreads made from wild grains, baked thousands of years before anyone farmed wheat.

Nobody knows who first made bread rise, but Egyptian bakers were doing it thousands of years ago. Wild yeast floats in the air, so dough left out would have started to bubble on its own. Sourdough still works this way, using wild yeast and helpful bacteria. In the 1850s and 1860s the French scientist Louis Pasteur showed that fermentation is caused by living things.

In India, soft breads like pav arrived with Portuguese bakers in Goa. But the strangest part? South India's dosa and idli batter rises overnight with no added yeast at all, puffed up by wild microbes in the rice and urad dal.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Yeast is a chemical powder, like baking soda.

Truth: Yeast is alive. Each grain of dried yeast holds sleeping single-celled fungi that wake up in warm water and start feeding. Baking soda is a chemical that makes gas through a reaction, with nothing living involved.

Try this

Put a teaspoon of dried yeast and a teaspoon of sugar into a small bottle with half a cup of warm (not hot) water. Stretch a balloon over the mouth and wait 30 minutes in a warm spot. The balloon slowly stands up, blown up by gas breathed out by the yeast.
