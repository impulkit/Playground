How do braces straighten teeth?

Braces work because your jawbone is alive. Push a tooth gently for months and the bone in front of it melts away while new bone fills in behind.

The short answer

Braces straighten teeth by pushing on them gently, all day and all night, for months or years. Small metal or ceramic pieces called brackets are glued to the front of each tooth. A springy wire runs through all the brackets and is bent into the shape of a perfect smile.

The wire keeps trying to spring back into that shape. As it does, it pulls and pushes each tooth a tiny bit.

A tooth isn't fixed in solid rock. It sits in a socket of living bone, held by a thin cushion of fibres. Steady pressure makes the bone slowly rebuild itself around the tooth, letting it travel to a new place.

Surprising but true

The bone around your teeth is rebuilt all through your life, which is why adults can wear braces too and why teeth can drift back if you stop wearing a retainer.

Bone that moves out of the way

Your bones look hard and still, but they are busy. Special cells keep tearing down old bone and laying down new bone, all your life.

When a brace pushes a tooth, the fibres on one side get squashed and the fibres on the other side get stretched. On the squashed side, cells called osteoclasts start dissolving bone, opening up room for the tooth to move into. On the stretched side, cells called osteoblasts build fresh bone to fill the gap left behind.

A tooth usually moves about a millimetre a month. That sounds slow, but over two years it can add up to more than the width of a whole tooth.

Why do teeth grow crooked at all? Sometimes the jaw is a little too small for the teeth, so they crowd and twist. Sometimes the top and bottom jaws grow at different rates, and habits like long-term thumb-sucking can push front teeth forward. Often it simply runs in families.

Wires, elastics and tightening days

Many braces today use wires made of nickel and titanium. This metal can be bent at room temperature and then, warmed in the mouth, it tries to return to its first shape. So it keeps up a gentle pull for weeks.

Every month or two, a dentist who specialises in straightening teeth, called an orthodontist, changes or adjusts the wire. Teeth often ache for a day or two afterwards, which is a sign the bone cells have work to do.

Tiny rubber bands hooked between the top and bottom teeth help line up the bite, the way the jaws close together. Wearers often pick a new colour of band at each visit.

A long history

People have tried to straighten teeth for a very long time. In 1728 a French dentist, Pierre Fauchard, described a curved strip of metal tied to the teeth to widen the arch. In the late 1800s and early 1900s an American dentist named Edward Angle sorted bites into types and designed many brace parts, and he is often called the father of modern orthodontics.

Clear plastic aligners arrived in the USA in 1998. Instead of a wire, you wear a series of thin trays, each shaped a fraction differently from the last. Computers plan the steps, and many trays are made using 3D printers.

Keeping teeth where they are

When braces come off, the job isn't over. The fibres around each tooth remember where it used to be and slowly tug it back. So most people wear a retainer, a plastic tray or a thin wire glued behind the teeth, for years afterwards.

Braces also make cleaning harder. Food gets stuck around the brackets, and if it isn't brushed away it can lead to tooth decay and white marks on the teeth. Orthodontists often give wearers small brushes to clean between the wires. And no, braces won't set off an airport metal detector or pick up radio stations.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Braces are only for children and teenagers.

Truth: Bone is rebuilt at every age, so adults can have braces too. Treatment can take a little longer because adult bone changes more slowly, but many people get braces in their twenties, thirties or later.

Try this

Push a lolly stick into a tray of damp sand and lean on it gently and steadily with one finger for a minute. Watch the sand pile up on one side and a gap open on the other. Now press hard all at once and see how differently the stick behaves.
