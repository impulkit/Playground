Why are cricket bats made of willow?

Of all the woods in the world, cricket chose willow.

The short answer

Cricket bats are made from a special willow tree, Salix alba caerulea, often called cricket bat willow. Its wood is light for its size, tough and a little springy. When a hard ball hits it, the wood squashes slightly and springs back, sending the ball away fast without the bat breaking.

The wood is pressed and oiled so it's strong enough for hard balls. The handle is made from cane, a springy plant related to palms, glued into a V-shaped slot in the blade.

Many bats are made in Kashmir and in Meerut, Uttar Pradesh, as well as Jalandhar in Punjab. The best and most expensive bats usually use English willow grown in eastern England.

Surprising but true

After a player turned up with a bat as wide as the stumps in 1771, cricket made a rule limiting bat width.

Light, tough and springy

A good bat has to do three jobs. It must be light enough to swing fast, strong enough to survive balls bowled at over 140 km/h, and springy enough to send the ball flying. Heavy woods like oak are too slow to swing. Very light woods would dent and split.

Willow fibres run in long straight lines down the blade, and the wood holds a lot of air. That gives it its spring. The best bats show straight, even grain lines on the face. A bat works like a lever: the batter's hands move a little, while the far end of the blade swings fast.

From tree to bat

Cricket bat willow trees are grown for about 15 years or more before they are cut down. The trunk is sawn into short logs and then split into wedge-shaped blocks called clefts. These are dried for months, then shaped, pressed between rollers to harden the face, and fitted with a handle.

English willow is softer and lighter, with a lot of spring. Kashmir willow is harder and heavier, and bats made from it cost much less. That is why many young players in India learn on Kashmir willow. In the Anantnag district of Kashmir, families have made bats for generations.

The rules of the bat

The Laws of Cricket have controlled the size of bats since the 1700s. In 1771, a player called Thomas White walked out with a bat as wide as the wicket, making him almost impossible to bowl out. The rule-makers quickly limited the width, to 4¼ inches, about 10.8 cm, which is still the limit today.

Today a bat may be no longer than 96.52 cm. Since 2017 there has also been a limit on how thick the edges and the middle can be. The reason: bats had grown so chunky that even badly timed shots could clear the boundary.

The aluminium bat

In December 1979, during a Test match in Perth, the Australian bowler Dennis Lillee came out to bat with an aluminium bat. Each time he hit the ball, it made a loud clang. The England captain complained that the metal was damaging the ball, and after a long argument Lillee threw the bat away and took a wooden one. The Laws were soon changed to say a bat's blade must be made of wood.

Bats and legends

Players are often very attached to their bats. Sachin Tendulkar was known for using a heavy bat, about 1.5 kg, much heavier than most players use. Heavier bats can hit harder but are slower to swing, so every player chooses a different balance.

Batters also oil their bats with a little linseed oil to stop the wood drying and cracking, and some wrap the face in a thin protective sheet. Look after a good willow bat, and it can last for years of games, from the gully to the stadium.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A new bat is ready to use straight out of the shop.

Truth: Most new willow bats need "knocking in" first. The face is tapped with a wooden mallet for hours to squash and harden the soft fibres, so a hard ball does not crack it.

Try this

Find the sweet spot of a cricket bat. Hold the bat loosely by the handle, blade hanging down, and ask a friend to tap the face gently with a ball at different heights. Where the tap stings your hands least and sounds clearest, you have found the sweet spot, usually a bit below the middle of the blade.
