What do fuses and circuit breakers do?

A fuse is a tiny piece of wire designed to fail, so that the rest of your home's wiring never has to.

The short answer

Fuses and circuit breakers are safety guards for your home's wiring. Every wire can carry only so much electric current before it gets dangerously hot. If too much current flows, a fuse melts or a breaker switches off. That opens the circuit, the current stops, and the wires don't get hot enough to start a fire.

They step in for two main problems. One is an overload, when too many appliances run on one circuit at once. The other is a short circuit, when current finds a shortcut, perhaps through a damaged wire, and rushes through with almost nothing to slow it down.

Surprising but true

A circuit breaker can switch off a short circuit in about a hundredth of a second, faster than you can blink.

How a fuse works

When current flows through a wire, it heats it. That's how a toaster or an old filament bulb works. In most wires the heat is small. But a fuse contains a deliberately thin wire that melts at a lower temperature. If the current rises too high, the fuse wire melts first and breaks the circuit.

Older Indian homes often had rewirable fuses called "kit-kat" fuses: white porcelain holders with a thin wire across them. When one blew, someone pulled it out and fitted a new piece of wire. Electrical plugs in Britain and many appliances in the USA still contain small glass or ceramic cartridge fuses. A fuse works only once, then must be replaced.

Breakers that switch themselves off

Most new homes use miniature circuit breakers, or MCBs, instead. They look like a row of small switches in the board. Inside each are two clever parts. A strip made of two different metals bends as it warms up, because one metal expands more than the other. During an overload it slowly bends far enough to flip the switch off.

The second part is a coil that acts as an electromagnet. In a short circuit, the current jumps so high that the magnet snaps the switch open almost instantly, often in about a hundredth of a second. Once the problem is fixed, you can simply push the switch back up. That is far easier than finding fuse wire with a torch during a power cut.

The two parts work at different speeds on purpose. A motor in a fridge or a mixer draws a quick surge of current when it starts, which is harmless. The slow metal strip ignores that short burst, so the breaker doesn't trip every time you make a milkshake.

Guarding people, not just wires

Fuses and MCBs protect the wiring, but a small current through a person can still be dangerous. That's the job of an RCCB (residual current circuit breaker), sometimes called an ELCB. It constantly compares the current going out along one wire with the current coming back along the other. Normally they match exactly.

If some current goes missing, it may be leaking through faulty equipment, wet ground or someone's body. When the difference reaches a small amount, often 30 milliamps, the RCCB cuts power within a fraction of a second. In the USA, bathroom and kitchen sockets have their own version, called GFCI outlets, with little "test" and "reset" buttons on the front.

A switch is simpler

An ordinary light switch does something much simpler. It is a gap in a wire that you can close and open by hand. Press it one way and two metal contacts touch, completing the circuit. Press it the other way and a spring pulls them apart.

The idea of fuses goes back to the early days of electric lighting. Thomas Edison patented a fuse in 1890 as part of his system for bringing power to homes. Today, a switchboard is one of the quietest parts of a house. Most of the time nothing happens there at all, and that is exactly what it is for. Grown-ups should be the ones to reset a breaker, and if one keeps tripping, an electrician should check why.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If a fuse keeps blowing, you should put in a thicker fuse wire so it stops happening.

Truth: A fuse that keeps blowing is warning you about a real problem, such as a faulty appliance or too many things on one circuit. A thicker wire can let the walls' wiring overheat and start a fire. An electrician should find and fix the cause.

Try this

With a grown-up, open the door of your home's switchboard (don't touch anything inside). Read the labels and count the breakers. Notice which room or appliance each one is for, and look for a bigger switch marked RCCB or ELCB. Draw a simple map of which breaker controls which part of your home.
