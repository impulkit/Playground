Why do countries do science together?

Some machines are so big and so costly that no single country can build them alone. So countries that may disagree about almost everything else end up sharing a lab.

The short answer

Countries do science together because some questions are too big, too costly or too complicated for one nation to answer alone. By sharing money, machines and brains, they can build giant labs, test big ideas and solve problems that cross borders, like disease and climate change.

Teaming up also helps scientists learn from each other, and it builds trust between countries.

It saves money too. If ten countries each built the same giant machine, they would spend ten times as much. Sharing one machine lets each country pay a part and still use the whole thing. The results are usually published openly, so the whole world can learn from them.

Surprising but true

The International Space Station has been lived in without a break since November 2000. Astronauts from rival countries have shared it, even in years when their governments were quarrelling on Earth.

How it works

There are a few common ways countries work together.

Shared giant machines. Countries chip in to build one huge instrument and share its use. Each sends scientists and engineers, and each gets parts of the work to do at home.

Shared funding pots. A group of countries creates one big pot of money. Researchers from member countries form teams across borders and compete for grants by putting forward the best ideas.

Shared data. Scientists in many places collect information, such as weather readings or gene sequences, and pool it so everyone can study the whole picture.

Think of a cricket team. One player cannot bat, bowl and keep wicket all at once. A good team has openers, spinners and fielders, each doing what they do best. Big science works the same way, with each country bringing its own strengths to test a shared hypothesis.

Where it came from

After the Second World War, much of Europe's science lay in ruins, and many top scientists had moved away. In 1954, twelve European countries set up CERN, near Geneva, to study the smallest bits of matter, even smaller than an atom. It became one of the world's leading labs.

In 1959, countries signed the Antarctic Treaty, setting aside a whole continent for peaceful science. Today, research stations from dozens of countries, including India, sit side by side there.

In 1998, the first part of the International Space Station was launched, built by the USA, Russia, Europe, Japan and Canada. In the 1990s, scientists from several countries also teamed up to read the whole human genetic code. In 2007, work began in France on ITER, a giant machine to test nuclear fusion, the kind of power that lights the Sun.

India's story

India has joined many of these teams. It became an associate member of CERN in 2017, and Indian scientists helped build parts of its giant detectors. India is one of seven members of ITER and is making some of its biggest parts, including a huge steel shell called a cryostat, whose pieces were made in Gujarat.

In 2015, scientists detected gravitational waves, tiny ripples in space, for the first time. Dozens of Indian scientists were among the authors of that discovery. India is now building its own detector, LIGO-India, in Hingoli district of Maharashtra, as part of a worldwide network.

India also helps build some of the giant telescopes of the future. Through its space agency, it works with NASA on a satellite called NISAR that watches Earth's surface. NISAR can spot tiny movements of the ground, helping track earthquakes, landslides and melting ice.

In November 2026, India finished talks to join Horizon Europe, the European Union's research programme worth €93.5 billion, a deal expected to begin in 2027.

The numbers that matter

  • - €93.5 billion: the budget of Horizon Europe for 2021 to 2027, more than ₹9 lakh crore. It is one of the biggest research funds in the world.
  • - More than 20: non-EU countries in Horizon Europe, including the UK, Canada, Japan and South Korea. India is set to join them.
  • - 7: members of ITER, the fusion project in France: the EU, China, India, Japan, South Korea, Russia and the USA. Together they include more than half the world's people.
  • - Over 1,000: authors listed on the paper announcing gravitational waves in 2016, from many countries.
  • - Since 2000: people have lived on the International Space Station without a gap, a record for living in space.

What people argue about

Supporters say joining big programmes gives scientists money, top equipment and partners they could never get at home. It can also help a country's companies learn new skills and win contracts.

Others raise questions. Joining often costs money, and some ask whether that money would do more good spent on labs at home. Some worry about "brain drain", where the best scientists move abroad. Others reply that it can become "brain gain", when people return with new skills and links.

There are also tricky questions about who owns new inventions, or patents, from shared projects, and about keeping sensitive technology safe. Every agreement has to settle these rules carefully.

Myths and mix-ups

"International science only happens in Europe and America." Countries across Asia, Africa and South America run and join big projects, including India, Japan, China, South Africa and Brazil.

"Only the richest country gets the credit." Credit goes to the teams who do the work, and results are usually published for everyone.

"Joining means a country gives up its own research." Countries keep their own labs and programmes, from ISRO's rockets to university teams studying DNA. Joining adds partners and funding; it does not replace home science.

What it means for you

When countries share science, discoveries reach people faster. Vaccines, weather forecasts, internet technology and medical scans have all come partly from scientists working across borders.

For a student in India today, these deals mean more chances to work on world-class projects, perhaps without even leaving the country. Indian universities and labs can lead teams with partners in Europe and beyond.

If you enjoy asking "why?", a science career can now be truly global. The next big discovery might come from a team with members in Bengaluru, Berlin and Busan, all working on the same puzzle.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Scientists from different countries mostly compete and keep their discoveries secret.

Truth: Much of science is open. Most results are published for anyone to read, and many big discoveries, like gravitational waves, had over a thousand authors from dozens of countries.

Try this

With friends, try to solve a big jigsaw puzzle in two ways. First, each person works on their own pieces in a separate corner. Then everyone works on one table, sharing pieces. Which is faster? Which is more fun? Big science works a lot like the second way.
