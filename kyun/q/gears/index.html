What do gears do?

Gears can't make energy, but they can swap speed for strength, which is how you pedal a bicycle up a steep hill.

The short answer

A gear is a wheel with teeth around its edge. When two gears mesh, the teeth of one push the teeth of the other, so turning one turns the other.

Gears do three useful jobs. They can change speed, making something turn faster or slower. They can change force, making it easier or harder to turn. And they can change direction, because two gears that mesh always turn opposite ways.

The key is size. When a small gear drives a big one, the big one turns more slowly but with more force. When a big gear drives a small one, the small one spins faster but with less force. You can't have both at once.

Surprising but true

A lump of corroded bronze pulled from a Greek shipwreck turned out to hold more than 30 gears, built over 2,000 years ago to predict eclipses.

Counting teeth

Engineers describe gears by their ratio, which you can work out by counting teeth. If a 10-tooth gear drives a 40-tooth gear, the ratio is 1 to 4. The small gear must turn four times to turn the big one once.

In exchange, the big gear turns with four times the force. It works a lot like a lever: a long handle lets you lift something heavy, but you have to move your end further. Gears are levers that keep going round and round. The same maths of comparing numbers, called ratios, turns up in recipes, maps and music.

Gears on a bicycle

On a bicycle, a chain links a big gear at the pedals to smaller gears at the back wheel. In a low gear, the chain sits on a large back gear. Each push of the pedal turns the wheel only a little, so climbing a hill feels easier, though you go slowly. In a high gear, the chain moves to a small back gear. The wheel spins quickly with every pedal stroke, which is great on a flat road but hard work uphill.

Racing cyclists may have more than 20 gear combinations to choose from. Car gearboxes do the same job for an engine, which works best at certain speeds. Low gears get a heavy car moving. High gears let it cruise without the engine racing.

The oldest known gears

In 1901, sponge divers near the Greek island of Antikythera found a corroded lump of bronze in an old shipwreck. Decades later, X-ray scans revealed what it was: a machine with more than 30 interlocking gears, built about 2,000 years ago.

Turned by a handle, the Antikythera mechanism showed the positions of the Sun and Moon, the Moon's phases and the dates of eclipses. Nothing as complex has been found from the next thousand years. Every one of its tiny triangular gear teeth was made by hand.

Gears in fields and clocks

In villages across India and Pakistan, farmers once used the Persian wheel, or rahat, to lift water from wells. An ox walked in a circle, turning a horizontal wooden wheel with pegs for teeth. Those pegs turned an upright wheel carrying a loop of pots, which scooped water up and tipped it into a channel. The gears changed the ox's slow, flat circle into a turning wheel standing on its edge.

Mechanical clocks use whole families of gears. One gear turns once a minute for the second hand. A chain of gears slows that down to one turn an hour for the minute hand, and slower again for the hour hand. Gears turning wind turbines, cranes and food mixers all follow the same simple rule of counting teeth.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Gears give a machine extra energy or power for free.

Truth: Gears can't create energy. They trade one thing for another. A gear that makes turning easier makes it slower, and a gear that makes it faster makes it harder to turn. The energy in stays the same as the energy out, minus a little lost to friction.

Try this

Turn a bicycle upside down with an adult's help, so it rests on its seat and handlebars. Put it in its lowest gear and turn the pedal once by hand, counting how many times the back wheel goes round. Then try the highest gear. Count the teeth on the front and back gears too and see if the numbers match.
