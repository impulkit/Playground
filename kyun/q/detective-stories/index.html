Who are the world's most famous detectives?

Sherlock Holmes never once says "Elementary, my dear Watson" in any of the 60 stories Arthur Conan Doyle wrote about him.

The short answer

The most famous detective of all is probably Sherlock Holmes, the sharp-eyed London detective created by Arthur Conan Doyle. Close behind come Agatha Christie's Hercule Poirot and Miss Marple. In India, readers love Byomkesh Bakshi and Feluda from Bengal.

Fictional detectives have a lot in common. They notice small things others miss. They ask odd questions. And at the end they gather everyone together and explain who did it, and how they knew.

Detective stories, also called mysteries or whodunits, are a puzzle the reader tries to solve alongside the hero. That game is a big part of the fun.

Surprising but true

Bengal has two of India's best-loved detectives, Byomkesh Bakshi and Feluda. Feluda was created by the film director Satyajit Ray for a children's magazine his grandfather had founded.

Where it began

Many people call Edgar Allan Poe's "The Murders in the Rue Morgue" the first modern detective story. Poe, an American writer, published it in 1841. His detective, C. Auguste Dupin, solves a baffling crime in Paris by careful reasoning.

Dupin had a friend who told the story, and he loved to explain his thinking at the end. Later writers copied both ideas. Poe wrote only three Dupin stories, but he set the pattern for almost every fictional detective who followed.

The man from Baker Street

In 1887 a young doctor named Arthur Conan Doyle published A Study in Scarlet. It introduced Sherlock Holmes and his friend Dr Watson, who share rooms at 221B Baker Street in London.

Holmes reasons from tiny details. On meeting Watson, he can tell he has just come back from Afghanistan, from his tanned face, his stiff arm and his soldierly manner. Conan Doyle based this habit partly on a real teacher of his, the Scottish surgeon Joseph Bell, who could guess a patient's job from their hands.

Conan Doyle grew tired of Holmes and killed him off in 1893, sending him over a waterfall. Readers were so upset that he brought Holmes back. In all he wrote four novels and 56 short stories.

The queen of crime

Agatha Christie, an English writer, published her first mystery in 1920. It introduced Hercule Poirot, a fussy Belgian detective with a neat moustache who trusts his "little grey cells". Later she created Miss Marple, an elderly woman in a quiet village who understands people better than any police officer.

Guinness World Records lists Christie as the best-selling fiction writer of all time, with about two billion books sold. Her play The Mousetrap has run in London almost without a break since 1952.

India's detectives

Bengal has a strong love of mysteries. In 1932 Sharadindu Bandyopadhyay wrote the first story about Byomkesh Bakshi, who called himself a "truth-seeker" rather than a detective. Byomkesh solves crimes in Kolkata with his friend Ajit, who tells the stories.

In 1965 Satyajit Ray, the great film director, began writing about Feluda for Sandesh, a children's magazine his grandfather had started. Feluda is tall, smart and fond of logic. His young cousin Topshe narrates, and a writer of thrillers named Jatayu often tags along for comic relief.

Both detectives have been made into many films and TV series. Other Indian languages have their own detectives too, from Tamil and Hindi pulp novels to the TV detective Karamchand.

Detectives for younger readers

Many children start with detective stories written for them. In the USA, the Hardy Boys first appeared in 1927 and Nancy Drew in 1930, and both are still in print. In Britain and India, generations grew up on Enid Blyton's Famous Five and Secret Seven, groups of children who solve mysteries in the holidays.

Real detectives rely more on science than on clever guesses, using fingerprints, cameras and DNA. But the heart of a mystery story hasn't changed since Poe: notice what others miss, and think it through.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Sherlock Holmes often said "Elementary, my dear Watson."

Truth: That exact line never appears in Conan Doyle's stories. Holmes says "Elementary" once and "my dear Watson" many times, but the famous phrase was put together later in films and plays.

Try this

Set up a mystery for your family. Hide a small object, then leave three clues around the house, like a footprint drawn on paper, a riddle and a smudge of chalk. Watch how they work it out, and notice which clue they found most useful.
