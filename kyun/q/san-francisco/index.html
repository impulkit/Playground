Why is the Golden Gate Bridge orange?

The US Navy wanted the Golden Gate Bridge painted in black and yellow stripes. An architect fought for orange, and won.

The short answer

The Golden Gate Bridge is orange because an architect named Irving Morrow loved the colour. When the steel arrived, it was coated in a reddish-orange primer, the first layer of paint that protects metal from rust. Morrow thought it looked beautiful against the brown hills, blue sea and grey fog. It was also easy to see.

Others had different ideas. The US Navy wanted black and yellow stripes so ships could spot it, and some suggested plain grey or silver. Morrow won, and the bridge was painted a shade now called International Orange. It opened in May 1937.

Surprising but true

The Golden Gate was named about 90 years before the bridge was built, and the name has nothing to do with the Gold Rush or the paint.

Bridging the gate

The Golden Gate is a narrow strait, about 1.6 km wide, where San Francisco Bay meets the Pacific Ocean. The explorer John C. Frémont named it Chrysopylae, or Golden Gate, in about 1846, comparing it to a harbour in Istanbul. That was before gold was found in California and long before the bridge.

For years, people said a bridge here was impossible. The water is deep, the currents are strong, winds are fierce and fog rolls in almost daily. Engineer Joseph Strauss led the project, and Charles Ellis and Leon Moisseiff did much of the hard maths behind the design.

Hanging from cables

The Golden Gate is a suspension bridge. Two tall towers hold up two giant cables, and the road hangs from those cables on thinner steel ropes. Each main cable is about 92 cm thick and made of over 27,000 thin wires bundled together. Laid end to end, the wires in both cables would wrap around Earth more than three times.

When it opened, its main span of about 1,280 m was the longest suspension span in the world. Workers wore hard hats, and a safety net under the deck saved 19 men during building. Even so, 11 workers died.

Why so much fog

San Francisco's famous fog comes from the sea. A cold ocean current runs down the California coast, and deep cold water rises near the shore. When warm, damp air blows over that cold water, it cools and turns into fog. In summer, the fog often pours through the Golden Gate in the afternoon. People in the city have even given it a nickname, Karl.

The day the city shook

San Francisco sits close to the San Andreas Fault, where two huge plates of Earth's crust slide past each other. Early on 18 April 1906, a powerful earthquake struck. Fires broke out across the city and burned for days. Much of San Francisco was destroyed, and thousands died. Today buildings and bridges are built to sway and survive shaking.

Gold, cable cars and new ideas

In 1848, gold was found in the hills east of the city. San Francisco grew from a village of about 1,000 people to a busy city of about 25,000 in a couple of years. Workers came from across the world, including from China. The city's Chinatown is one of the oldest in North America.

San Francisco's hills are so steep that horses struggled to pull carts up them. In 1873, Andrew Hallidie started cable cars, pulled along by a moving cable under the street. They still run, and they are the last cable cars of their kind in the world still worked by hand.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Painters repaint the Golden Gate Bridge from end to end every year, starting again as soon as they finish.

Truth: The bridge is never painted all at once. A team of painters touches up the parts that need it all year round. The whole bridge was given a new coat of paint only once, slowly, between 1965 and 1995.
