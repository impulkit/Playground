Why do phone batteries wear out?

Every time a phone charges, tiny particles squeeze into a block of carbon and out again, and each trip leaves a little damage behind.

The short answer

Phones run on lithium-ion batteries. Inside, tiny charged particles called lithium ions shuttle between two layers. When you charge, they pack into one side, a layer of carbon called graphite. When you use the phone, they flow back to the other side, a metal oxide, and that flow pushes electrons round the circuit to power the phone.

Each trip is a little imperfect. Some lithium gets stuck in side reactions and can never move again. The layers swell and shrink, and tiny cracks form. Over hundreds of charges, the battery can hold less and less. After about 500 to 1,000 full charges, many phone batteries keep only about 80% of what they could hold when new.

Surprising but true

Heat harms a phone battery more than almost anything else. Leaving a phone on a car dashboard in summer can age the battery faster than months of normal use.

A rocking-chair battery

Scientists sometimes call lithium-ion a "rocking-chair" battery because the ions rock back and forth. Between the two layers is a liquid called the electrolyte, which lets ions through, and a thin plastic sheet, the separator, which keeps the two sides from touching.

The idea took decades of work, nearly two centuries after the first battery. Stanley Whittingham made an early version in the 1970s. John Goodenough found a better metal-oxide side in 1980. Akira Yoshino in Japan built the first safe, practical design in 1985. Sony sold the first lithium-ion batteries in 1991, and in 2019 the three scientists shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Goodenough was 97, the oldest person ever to win a Nobel.

What wears it out

Charge cycles. One full cycle is using 100% of the battery, whether in one go or as two halves on different days. Every cycle adds a little wear.

Heat. Warmth speeds up the harmful reactions. A battery kept at 40°C ages much faster than one at room temperature. Phones left in hot cars or charging under a pillow suffer most.

Being very full or very empty. A battery sitting at 100% for hours is under strain, like a stretched rubber band. Draining to zero is hard on it too. Many phones now offer an "optimised charging" setting that holds at 80% overnight and finishes just before you wake.

Fast charging. Pushing energy in quickly makes heat. Phones slow down charging once they pass about 80% to protect the cells.

Why your old phone dies at 20%

As a battery ages, it also finds it harder to deliver a sudden burst of power. When you open a heavy game or the camera, the voltage dips. If it dips too low, the phone switches off to protect itself, even though the screen said there was charge left. Some phone makers slow down old phones slightly to avoid these surprise shutdowns.

Bigger cousins

The same chemistry powers laptops, e-scooters and electric cars. Car makers wrap their batteries in cooling systems and stop drivers from using the very top and bottom of the charge, so the packs last many years. That's why an electric car can keep most of its range for well over 100,000 km.

The lithium in your pocket

Lithium is a soft, light metal, mined in places such as Australia, Chile and Argentina. Phone batteries also contain cobalt, nickel or other minerals, and some of these are mined in difficult conditions. That makes it worth keeping a battery healthy, and recycling it properly when it's finished. Throwing one in the bin is dangerous: a crushed lithium battery can catch fire in a rubbish truck, which is why old phones belong in e-waste collection.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You should always let a phone battery run down to zero before charging, or it will "forget" its full size.

Truth: That was true of old nickel-cadmium batteries, which had a "memory effect". Lithium-ion batteries don't. Running them down to zero often actually wears them faster.

Try this

Ask an adult to check the battery health setting on a phone (many phones show it in the battery menu). Compare an older phone with a newer one. Then, for one week, notice where phones are left to charge, and whether any of those places get warm in the sun.
