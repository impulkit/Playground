Who was Frederick Douglass?

Frederick Douglass secretly taught himself to read while enslaved, escaped to freedom at 20 and became one of the most powerful speakers in American history.

The short answer

Frederick Douglass was born enslaved in Maryland, in the United States, around 1818. He secretly learned to read, escaped to freedom in 1838 and became one of the most famous Americans of his time. He spoke and wrote against slavery, published his own newspaper and advised presidents.

His autobiography, published in 1845, showed thousands of readers what slavery was really like. He also fought for women's right to vote, for equal rights after the Civil War and for an end to unfair treatment of Black Americans. He died in 1895.

Surprising but true

Douglass was probably the most photographed American of the 1800s. He sat for more than 160 portraits, believing that photos could show the world the dignity of Black Americans.

Learning in secret

Douglass was born Frederick Bailey. He never knew his exact birthday. As a small child he was separated from his mother, who worked on a plantation miles away.

At about eight, he was sent to work for a family in Baltimore. The wife began teaching him the alphabet until her husband stopped her, saying reading would make a slave unfit for slavery. Douglass realised that reading was exactly the key he needed. He traded bread with poor white boys in the street for reading lessons, and taught himself to write by copying letters on walls and fences.

He bought a book of speeches with money he earned shining shoes. Reading about freedom made him determined to win his own.

The escape

In September 1838, aged about 20, Douglass borrowed the papers of a free Black sailor and dressed like one. He boarded a train in Baltimore, then a steamboat and more trains. Within a day he reached New York City.

He married Anna Murray, a free Black woman who had helped plan the escape and paid for part of it, and they settled in Massachusetts. To avoid slave catchers, he changed his surname to Douglass.

What makes a speech famous?Abraham Lincoln's most famous speech took about two minutes. The man who spoke before him talked for two hours, and almost nobody remembers his words.

A voice nobody could ignore

In 1841, Douglass spoke at an anti-slavery meeting. People were stunned by his power. He began touring as a speaker. Some listeners refused to believe such a brilliant man had ever been enslaved.

To prove it, he wrote Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, published in 1845. It named real people and places, which put him in danger of recapture. He went to Britain and Ireland for nearly two years. Supporters there raised money to buy his freedom legally.

Back home, he started a newspaper, The North Star, in 1847. In 1852 he gave a famous speech asking, "What to the slave is the Fourth of July?" It pointed out that Americans celebrated freedom while millions were still enslaved.

War and after

During the Civil War, Douglass pushed Abraham Lincoln to free enslaved people and let Black men fight. When Black soldiers were finally allowed to join the Union army, two of his sons enlisted. He met Lincoln at the White House three times. Douglass had often criticised him for moving too slowly, but he came to respect him.

After the war, slavery was abolished. Douglass held government jobs, including US Marshal for the District of Columbia and US minister to Haiti.

Rights for everyone

In 1848, Douglass was one of the few men at the Seneca Falls Convention in New York, the first big meeting for women's rights in the US. He spoke in favour of women's right to vote. He worked with campaigners like Susan B. Anthony, though they disagreed sharply after the war over whether Black men should get the vote before women.

He died at his home in Washington, DC, on 20 February 1895, after speaking at a women's rights meeting that day. The house, called Cedar Hill, is now a museum run by the National Park Service.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Abraham Lincoln and Frederick Douglass always agreed with each other.

Truth: Douglass criticised Lincoln strongly at first for moving too slowly against slavery. They met three times at the White House, and Douglass came to respect him, but he never stopped pushing for faster change.
