How much skin do you shed?

While you read this sentence, a few thousand flakes of your skin have quietly floated off into the air.

The short answer

You lose skin all the time, without feeling a thing. Scientists estimate that a person sheds roughly 30,000 to 40,000 skin cells every minute. That adds up to tens of millions of cells a day. Each one is far too small to see. Clumps of them form the tiny flakes that drift through sunbeams.

This isn't a sign that something is wrong. It's how skin works. New cells are always being made underneath, pushing old ones up to the surface. The old ones dry out, die and fall away. It's a little like a conveyor belt that ends in a cliff.

Surprising but true

The outer layer of your skin replaces itself roughly once a month, so the skin you see today is not the skin you had a few weeks ago.

A month-long climb

Your skin has layers. The top one, the epidermis, is thinner than a page in most places. At its bottom, cells divide again and again, making new ones.

Each new cell then begins a slow climb to the surface. On the way, it fills up with a tough protein called keratin, the same stuff in your hair and nails. It flattens like a pancake and dies. By the time it reaches the top, it's a dead, flat scale, stacked with many others like roof tiles. These tiles keep water in and germs out.

The whole climb takes about four weeks. So the outer skin on your hands right now was deep inside you about a month ago. In older people, the trip can take longer.

Where does it all go?

Most flakes fall onto your clothes, bed, chair and floor. Some get washed away in the shower. Some float in the air, which is why you can sometimes see them sparkle in sunlight.

Plenty of creatures are happy about this. Microscopic dust mites, relatives of spiders, live in beds, pillows and sofas and eat skin flakes. A single used mattress can be home to thousands of them. They're harmless to most people, though their droppings can trigger allergies.

Clues for detectives

Each skin flake can carry traces of DNA, the code that makes you, you. Forensic scientists can sometimes pick up "touch DNA" from things a person handled, like a doorknob or a pen. It doesn't always work, because the amount is tiny and often mixed with other people's.

Skin cells even end up in space. Astronauts on the International Space Station shed skin too, and with no gravity, flakes float around until air filters catch them. Astronauts have said that thick skin on the soles of their feet peels off after weeks of not walking.

Animals that shed in style

Humans shed in tiny bits, all day long. Other animals do it differently. Snakes peel off their whole outer skin in one piece, often starting at the lips and rolling it off inside out like a sock. A shed snake skin sometimes shows every scale, even the clear caps over the eyes.

Lizards and geckos shed in patches, and some eat their old skin. Crabs and insects must climb out of a hard shell when they grow. And one lizard has skin that does something far stranger than flaking: it changes colour. Tiny crystals inside it bend light, which is how a chameleon can turn from green to yellow in minutes.

Should you worry?

Not at all. Shedding is healthy skin doing its job. Dry, flaky patches, or flakes in the hair called dandruff, are common and usually harmless, though a doctor can help if skin is itchy, sore or red. Your skin is your body's largest organ, covering about 2 square metres in an adult, and it rebuilds itself quietly every single month.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Most house dust is dead human skin.

Truth: Skin is part of house dust, but studies suggest much of it comes from outside, carried in on shoes and through windows as soil and other particles. Fibres from clothes, carpets and furniture make up a big share too.

Try this

On a sunny morning, close the curtains so a single beam of light comes into a quiet room. Sit still and watch the specks dancing in it. Some are fibres from clothes and carpets, some are soil from outside, and a few are tiny flakes of skin.
