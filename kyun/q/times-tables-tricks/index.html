What are some tricks for times tables?

Your ten fingers can do the whole nine times table for you, and the trick works because of a neat pattern hiding in the answers.

The short answer

Times tables get much easier once you see the patterns in them. The biggest trick is that multiplication works both ways: 4 × 9 is the same as 9 × 4. That cuts the 100 facts in a 10 by 10 table almost in half.

Then come the easy tables. Anything times 1 stays the same. Times 10 adds a zero. Times 2 is doubling. Times 5 is half of times 10. Once those are sorted, you are left with only about 21 tricky facts, such as 6 × 7 and 7 × 8. And even those can be worked out from facts you already know, while you are still learning them by heart.

Surprising but true

If you know your tables up to 10, you only really need to learn about 21 facts. The rest are swaps of each other or come from the easy tables of 1, 2, 5 and 10.

Nines on your fingers

Hold both hands up with your fingers spread. To work out 9 × 4, fold down the fourth finger from the left. Count the fingers to the left of it: three. Count the fingers to the right: six. The answer is 36.

It works for 9 × 1 up to 9 × 10. The reason is a pattern: each step up the nine times table adds one to the tens and takes one from the ones. Look at 18, 27, 36, 45. The digits always add up to 9, at least up to 90. That makes it easy to check an answer too. If you think 9 × 6 is 56, the digits make 11, so something has gone wrong.

Doubling and halving

Many tables are just doubles of others. The 4 times table is the 2 times table doubled. The 8 times table is the 4 times table doubled. So for 8 × 7, you can go 2 × 7 = 14, double to 28, double again to 56.

For the 5 times table, multiply by 10 and halve. 5 × 8 is half of 80, which is 40. For the 6 times table, take the 3 times table and double it. These tricks are part of what people call mental maths, and they build real number sense, not just memory.

Friendly neighbours

If you know one fact, you know the ones next to it. Say you are sure that 7 × 7 = 49. Then 7 × 8 is just one more seven: 56. And 7 × 6 is one less seven: 42.

You can also split numbers. For 6 × 7, think of 6 × 5 = 30 and 6 × 2 = 12, then add them to get 42. Teachers sometimes call this breaking apart. Indian systems such as Vedic maths and Japanese abacus training use similar ideas, though with their own methods.

Elevens and twelves

The 11 times table starts with a gift: 11, 22, 33, all the way to 99. For bigger numbers, there is a cute trick with two-digit numbers. For 11 × 23, split the 2 and the 3, then put their sum, 5, in the middle. The answer is 253. If the middle sum is 10 or more, you carry one to the left.

For 12, use 10 and 2. To get 12 × 7, do 10 × 7 = 70 and 2 × 7 = 14, then add to get 84.

Why practice still helps

Tricks are a ladder, not a lift. At first you work out 7 × 8 using doubling or neighbours. After you do it enough times, your brain starts to remember the answer straight away, which frees up space for harder problems like long division or fractions.

Short, regular practice works better than long cramming sessions. Some children chant tables as songs, like the "do ekam do, do dooni chaar" chants heard in many Indian classrooms, or the times-table raps American teachers play. Rhythm and tune help your memory hold on. Card games and dice games, where you multiply the numbers you roll, can turn practice into play.

Myth or fact?

Myth: People who are good at maths just memorise everything.

Truth: Most fast calculators lean on patterns and shortcuts, like doubling, halving and using a fact they know to find one they don't. Memory helps, but understanding how numbers fit together helps more.

Try this

Make a 10 by 10 grid on paper, with the numbers 1 to 10 along the top and down the side. Colour in every square you already know for sure. Then colour the matching square across the diagonal, since 3 × 7 is the same as 7 × 3. Count how many squares are left. That is your real list to learn.
