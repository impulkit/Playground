How did carrom become one of India's favourite board games?

Carrom players sprinkle powder on the board so the pieces glide, then win or lose by the flick of a single finger.

The short answer

Carrom is a tabletop game where you flick a heavy disc, the striker, to knock smaller wooden pieces into the four corner pockets of a square board. One player sinks the white pieces, the other the black, and both chase a single red piece called the queen.

It became an Indian favourite because it needs very little: one board, a set of coins, a bit of powder and two or four people. It fits on a floor or a verandah, works in a power cut and keeps whole families busy on a rainy monsoon afternoon. Many Indian homes, offices and housing societies have a board, often with the paint worn pale in the corners.

Surprising but true

Pocketing the red queen is not enough. You must "cover" it by sinking one of your own pieces straight after, or the queen goes back to the centre.

Where did it begin?

Nobody knows for sure. Most stories say carrom came from India, perhaps played in royal courts, and spread through South Asia. Some people link its name to a Portuguese word, carambola, also used for a type of shot in billiards. Firm records are hard to find, so the true start remains a mystery.

Similar flicking games appeared elsewhere too. Canada has crokinole and Latvia has novuss, played on bigger boards with cues. What is clear is that carrom became hugely popular in India, Sri Lanka and their neighbours in the 1900s. It then travelled with South Asian families to Britain, the Gulf and North America.

The board and the powder

A standard board has a playing area about 74 cm on each side, roughly the width of a school desk, with a thick wooden frame around it. Players dust it with fine powder. The powder fills tiny bumps in the wood and lowers friction, the force that slows sliding things down.

Too little powder and the coins drag. Too much and they skid off in every direction. Serious players fuss over it the way cricketers fuss over a pitch.

A good board is made of smooth plywood on a solid wooden frame, and it must stay perfectly flat. Players judge a board by how evenly the coins glide and how cleanly the striker bounces off the edge. Many families wipe it with a dry cloth and store it standing up, so it doesn't warp in the damp monsoon air.

Flicks, angles and the queen

You sit at one side and place the striker on your baseline. You can only flick it with a finger, usually the index or middle finger, or the thumb. In doubles, four players sit around the board, with partners facing each other across it. The trick is in angles. A coin near the edge can be bounced, or banked, off the frame into a pocket, the same idea snooker players use.

The queen is worth extra points, but only if you cover it by pocketing one of your own pieces straight after. Sinking the striker by accident is a foul, and you have to give back one of your pieces. Like chess, it rewards thinking a few moves ahead. Good players also leave the coins in awkward spots for their opponent, the way a snooker player leaves the cue ball tucked behind another ball.

From verandahs to world championships

In 1988 players from several countries formed the International Carrom Federation in Chennai, with agreed rules and board sizes. World championships and international tournaments followed, with India and Sri Lanka usually near the top.

Indian players such as Rashmi Kumari have won world titles. At the same time, carrom stays a home game, played next to ludo and cards in summer holidays. In 2020, when the COVID-19 lockdowns kept families indoors, plenty of boards came down from the tops of cupboards and were dusted off again, powder and all.

Quick facts

Pieces
9 white, 9 black, 1 red queen and a heavier striker
Playing surface
about 74 cm square, with a pocket in each corner
Powder
used to cut friction so pieces slide
Governing body
International Carrom Federation, founded 1988 in Chennai
Played widely in
India, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh, Pakistan, Nepal and the Maldives

Myth or fact?

Myth: Carrom is a game of luck for passing time on holidays.

Truth: Top players plan bank shots off the edges, judge angles and control spin. National and world championships are played in many countries, and the best players train for years.

Try this

Make a flat-surface carrom test. On a smooth table, flick a coin with your finger and mark where it stops. Sprinkle a tiny pinch of talcum powder on the same patch, wipe it thin, and flick again with the same force. Compare the distances, then clean the table.
