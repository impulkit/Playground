Why do chess pieces move the way they do?

The most powerful piece on a chess board was once the weakest, able to shuffle just one square at a time.

The short answer

Chess pieces move the way they do because the game is a frozen army from ancient India. About 1,500 years ago, people played chaturanga, a battle game with four parts of an army: foot soldiers, horses, elephants and chariots. Each piece moved a bit like the real thing.

As the game travelled to Persia, then the Arab world, then Europe, players kept changing the rules. Some pieces got new names. A few got new powers. The moves you learn today are the result of that long relay, settled in Europe only about 500 years ago.

Surprising but true

The bishop began life as an elephant, and in Spanish it is still called alfil, from the Arabic word for elephant.

An army on a board

In chaturanga, the foot soldiers crept forward slowly, just like pawns. The horse jumped in an odd bent path, because horses could leap over people in battle. That knight's L-shaped jump is one of the oldest rules in any game still played. It has barely changed in 15 centuries.

The chariot raced in straight lines, the way a chariot needs a clear road. It became the rook. The word comes from the Persian rukh, meaning chariot, which is why a rook looks nothing like a bird. The king, slow and precious, could move one square at a time. That has not changed either.

The elephant that became a bishop

The strangest change happened to the elephant. In chaturanga it probably jumped exactly two squares diagonally. Arab players called it al-fil, the elephant. Spanish players still call the bishop alfil today.

In Europe, few people had seen an elephant. The piece's two little bumps, which were meant to be tusks, looked like a bishop's pointed hat. In England the elephant became a churchman. In France it became a jester. Its jump turned into a long diagonal glide around the same time the queen changed.

Other pieces got new identities too. The Persian name for the king, shah, gave us the word "check". The cry shah mat, often translated as "the king is helpless", became "checkmate". So every time a player says it, they are speaking a little Persian. In Russian the bishop is still called slon, the elephant, so the old animal never fully left the board.

The queen's great upgrade

The queen started as a male minister, the mantri in India and the ferz in Persia. He stood beside the king and stepped one square diagonally. He was so weak that a single knight was worth far more.

Then, in the late 1400s, players in Spain and Italy tried something new. They let the queen move any distance in any straight line. Games that once took hours of slow shuffling became sharp and fast. Some called the new game "the queen's chess" or even "mad queen chess". Within about 50 years it had swept across Europe, and the old rules faded away.

Little rules added later

A few rules are newer still. Pawns gained the right to step two squares on their first move, to speed up the slow opening. Castling, where the king tucks behind a rook in one move, became standard a few centuries ago. Even the shape of the pieces was fixed late. The design used in almost every tournament, the Staunton set, appeared in London in 1849.

Chess also came home. India now produces some of the strongest players on Earth, and the story of Viswanathan Anand and the young grandmasters who followed him is a good one. Chaturanga's elephants may have lost their tusks, but every game still starts with an ancient Indian army. And the board hides one more secret. Put one grain of rice on the first square and double it on each square after. By the last square you need more rice than the whole world grows in centuries, which is the trick behind the famous chessboard rice puzzle.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The queen has always been the most powerful piece in chess.

Truth: For most of chess history she was one of the weakest. Her long-range moves only arrived in Europe around 500 years ago.

Try this

Play a game with a friend using the old rules. Your queen may move only one square diagonally, and your bishops jump exactly two squares diagonally. Notice how much slower and more careful the game becomes.
