How do people say hello around the world?

In parts of Tibet, sticking your tongue out at someone has long been a polite way to say hello.

The short answer

People greet each other in hundreds of ways. Some touch, some don't. Indians often say namaste with palms pressed together. Americans shake hands or wave. Japanese people bow. French friends kiss on the cheek. Maori in New Zealand press noses and foreheads. In parts of Tibet, people traditionally stick out their tongues.

Each greeting says the same basic things: I see you, I'm friendly, I mean no harm. The way it's done depends on history, religion, climate, closeness and who is older. Even within India, one family may say vanakkam, another sat sri akal, another salaam or khamma ghani.

Surprising but true

The Maori hongi, pressing noses and foreheads together, is said to share the "breath of life" between two people.

Hands together, hands out

Namaste comes from Sanskrit and is often explained as "I bow to you". Palms are pressed together near the chest, sometimes with a small bow. It is common across India and Nepal, and during the COVID-19 pandemic people around the world started using it because it needs no touching.

In Thailand, the wai looks similar, but the height of the hands matters. The higher they go, the more respect is shown. Monks and elders get the highest wai.

The handshake is common in Europe, America and offices everywhere. A popular theory says it began as a way to show that your hand held no weapon, but historians aren't sure. More of that puzzle lives in the story of the handshake.

Bows, kisses and noses

In Japan, bowing is a whole language. A small nod for a friend. A deeper bow of around 30 degrees for a teacher. An even deeper, slower bow to say a serious sorry. Japanese culture has many rules like this, learned from childhood.

In France, friends greet with la bise, touching cheeks and making a kissing sound. How many kisses? It depends on the region: one, two, three or even four.

The Maori hongi is a press of noses and foreheads. Tradition says it shares the ha, the breath of life. Visitors to official events in New Zealand, including prime ministers and royals, often receive a hongi.

Why do people shake hands?Nearly 3,000 years ago, an Assyrian sculptor carved two kings shaking hands on a throne base, one of the oldest pictures of a handshake ever found.

The tongue out hello

In Tibet, showing the tongue has long been a sign of respect. A story often told says a cruel 9th-century king, Langdarma, had a black tongue. People showed their tongues to prove they weren't him reborn. It's a legend, but the custom is real, mostly among older people in villages.

Muslims around the world often greet each other with As-salaam alaikum, "peace be upon you". It is answered with Wa alaikum as-salaam, "and upon you be peace". Hands may be placed on the heart.

Getting it wrong

Greetings can cause confusion. A firm handshake that feels confident in the USA can seem pushy elsewhere. In some places, men and women don't touch when they greet unless they are family. Looking someone in the eye is friendly in some cultures and rude in others when facing an elder.

Travellers learn to watch first and copy. Business guides teach people how to greet before they visit another country. Getting a greeting right is a small sign of respect for other cultures.

Saying hello without sound

Deaf people greet each other in sign language, which has its own word for hello, different in every sign language. Indian Sign Language and American Sign Language are not the same. In many places, a simple wave, a smile or raised eyebrows does the job without any words at all.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Inuit people kiss by rubbing noses, which is why it's called an "Eskimo kiss".

Truth: The Inuit kunik is a gesture of affection where you press your nose and upper lip against someone's cheek or forehead and breathe in. It is mostly shared within families, not used as an everyday greeting with strangers, and it isn't the same as rubbing noses.
