Who are some heroes of the environment?

A man in Assam planted a forest on a bare sandbar, one seedling at a time, until tigers and elephants moved in.

The short answer

Environmental heroes are people who noticed something going wrong with nature and refused to look away. Some were scientists. Some were village women, farmers or a man with a bag of seedlings.

In the USA, John Muir campaigned to protect wild valleys, and Rachel Carson warned that pesticides were poisoning birds. In India, Saalumarada Thimmakka planted banyan trees along a road and Jadav Payeng grew a forest on a sandbar. The women of the Chipko movement stood between loggers and their trees.

None of them had special powers. They had patience, and they kept going when others laughed.

Surprising but true

The bald eagle, the national bird of the USA, nearly vanished from most of the country because of a pesticide. A book by Rachel Carson helped get it banned, and the eagles came back.

The man who grew a forest

In 1979, a teenager named Jadav Payeng saw snakes lying dead on a bare sandbar in the Brahmaputra river in Assam, killed by the heat after a flood. There was no shade anywhere.

He began planting bamboo, then other trees, watering them by hand. He kept going for decades. Today that forest, called Molai after his nickname, covers hundreds of hectares. Deer, rhinos, tigers and elephants have been seen there. In 2015 he received the Padma Shri, one of India's highest awards. People call him the Forest Man of India.

Trees as children

In Karnataka, Saalumarada Thimmakka and her husband had no children. So they planted banyan saplings along the road between the villages of Hulikal and Kudur and cared for them like children, carrying water over long distances.

About 385 banyan trees now line roughly 4 km of road. Her name, "Saalumarada", means "row of trees" in Kannada. She received the Padma Shri in 2019.

The book that saved the eagles

Rachel Carson was an American biologist. In 1962 she published Silent Spring, which showed how a pesticide called DDT was building up in animals and killing birds. Chemical companies attacked her, but the book made millions of people pay attention.

The USA banned most uses of DDT in 1972. The bald eagle, whose eggs had been breaking, slowly recovered.

How did the bald eagle come back from the edge?A chemical made their eggshells so thin they cracked. Banning it saved them.

Mountains and birds

A century earlier, the Scottish-American writer John Muir walked the Sierra Nevada mountains in California and wrote about them with joy. His articles helped make Yosemite a national park in 1890.

In India, Salim Ali spent his life watching birds. His Book of Indian Birds got generations of children looking up, and he helped save wetlands like Bharatpur in Rajasthan, now Keoladeo National Park.

Ordinary people, real science

You don't need a degree to help. Every February, thousands of people in India and the USA take part in the Great Backyard Bird Count, noting the birds they see for at least 15 minutes. Scientists use these counts to track which birds are thriving and which are disappearing.

In cities like Bengaluru, groups of neighbours have cleaned and revived dying lakes, removing rubbish and stopping sewage flowing in. In American towns, volunteers pick litter off beaches and riverbanks and record what they find, which helps show where plastic comes from.

Heroes like Payeng and Thimmakka started small, with one sapling. Lots of small efforts, added up, can change a landscape.

Heroes today

Young people have joined in too. In 2018, a Swedish schoolgirl named Greta Thunberg sat outside her country's parliament every Friday to demand action on climate change. Millions of students around the world copied her school strike. Whether by protest, science or a spade, people keep finding their own way to help.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Planting trees is enough to fix climate change on its own.

Truth: Trees soak up carbon dioxide, and planting them helps. But scientists say the biggest step is burning far less coal, oil and gas. Trees and cleaner energy are both needed.

Try this

Do a bird count. Sit by a window or in a park with an adult for 15 minutes and tally every bird you see or hear. Do it at the same time each week for a month. Real scientists use counts like this, and India and the USA both hold a yearly Great Backyard Bird Count that anyone can join.
