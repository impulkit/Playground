How do food trucks work?

A food truck is a whole restaurant kitchen squeezed into a vehicle about the size of a school minibus, and it can move to wherever the hungry people are.

The short answer

A food truck is a small kitchen built inside a van or lorry. It has a stove or grill, a fridge, a sink and a hatch in the side where customers order. The cooks drive to a busy spot, such as an office area at lunchtime, a college gate or a park on a Sunday, open the hatch and start serving.

The big advantage is that the kitchen moves. A restaurant has to wait for people to walk in. A food truck can go where the crowds are, and change its spot from one day to the next.

Surprising but true

In 1866 a Texas cattle rancher named Charles Goodnight turned an old army wagon into a moving kitchen for cowboys, and food trucks still borrow his idea.

Fitting a restaurant into a van

Space is the hardest problem. The cooking area is often only 3–5 metres long, about the length of a car, and two or three people work inside it. Everything is planned so that a cook can reach the fridge, the grill and the serving window with a step or two.

Power comes from a generator, from gas cylinders, or from a plug point when the truck is parked somewhere with electricity. Most trucks carry two water tanks: one for clean water and one for the grey water that comes out of the sink. A small menu helps too. A truck selling six dishes needs far fewer ingredients than a restaurant selling sixty.

Cowboys, carts and cooks

People have sold food from wheels for a long time. In 1866, a Texas rancher called Charles Goodnight fixed a wooden box of shelves and drawers to the back of an old army wagon. It carried beans, flour, coffee and a cook on long cattle drives. Cowboys called it the chuckwagon.

Later came lunch wagons outside factories and ice cream vans that played tunes. In India, the same idea lives on in handcarts selling chaat, bhutta and kulfi, which are really food trucks without engines.

How a tweet changed everything

For years, trucks in the USA had a reputation for cheap, dull food. Then, in 2008, a cook named Roy Choi started a truck in Los Angeles that sold Korean barbecue folded into tacos. The truck kept moving, so the team posted its location on Twitter. Crowds followed it around the city.

Other cooks copied the idea. Soon there were trucks selling dumplings, dosas and gourmet burgers, and finding the truck became part of the fun. Indian cities such as Bengaluru, Pune and Delhi saw a wave of food trucks in the 2010s, often run by young people who could not afford a full restaurant.

The rules of the road

A food truck has to follow two sets of rules: the ones for food and the ones for vehicles. In India, food businesses need a licence or registration from the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India. In the USA, each city has its own permits, and some cities limit how many trucks may operate, so there can be long waiting lists.

Then there is parking. Many cities say where trucks can stop and for how long, so they don't block traffic or crowd the doors of restaurants that pay rent. The best spot in town is worth a lot, and truck owners sometimes arrive before sunrise to get it.

Why cooks love them

A truck costs much less than a restaurant to start, so a cook can test a new idea without borrowing huge sums. If a dish doesn't sell in one neighbourhood, the truck can try another. Customers tell the cook what they think right at the window, which is faster feedback than any survey. The hard part is the long day: shopping at dawn, cooking for hours in a hot metal box, then cleaning every surface before driving home. Several famous restaurants began as a single truck with a handwritten menu taped to the window.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Food from a truck is less safe than food from a restaurant.

Truth: In India, the USA and many other places, food trucks need the same kind of food licence and inspections as restaurants. A clean truck can be just as safe as a clean restaurant.
