How can an app tell how old you are?

When an app asks for your birthday, you could type anything. So how can a website really know whether you are 10, 15 or 40?

The short answer

Most apps simply ask for your birthday and trust the answer. That is easy to fool.

Stronger checks ask for more proof. It might be a photo of an ID card, a selfie that software uses to guess your age, or approval from a parent. Each method is harder to trick, but it also asks you to share more about yourself.

Surprising but true

Some age checks use a selfie. Software guesses your age from your face in a second or two, and good systems are usually right to within a couple of years for adults.

Why apps ask

Most big social media apps say users must be at least 13. That comes from a 1998 American law that limits how companies collect personal data from younger children. India's 2023 data law says companies need a parent's consent to handle the personal data of anyone under 18.

Some countries go further and ban social media for younger teens. Australia's ban for under-16s began in December 2025, and Greece passed a ban for under-15s in October 2026. Supporters say heavy scrolling harms sleep, mood and screen habits. Critics worry about privacy and whether bans can really work.

Ways to check

Self-declaration. You type your age. Quick and private, but anyone can lie.

ID checks. You upload an ID card or log in with an official ID. Accurate, but it hands over sensitive information, and not every child has an ID.

Face age estimation. An algorithm looks at a selfie and guesses your age, using the same kind of maths as face recognition. Good systems can delete the photo straight away. They are less accurate close to the age limit.

Parent approval. A parent confirms the account from their own phone, often linked to their own verified age.

How can you stay safe online?Most online tricks don't need clever hacking. They work by getting someone to hand over a password, a photo or a code without thinking.

The privacy puzzle

The stronger the check, the more personal data changes hands. If an app stores your ID and is then hacked, that data could leak. This is why privacy experts prefer methods that prove only one fact, such as "over 15: yes", without revealing your name or birthday, often protected by encryption.

Some governments are building digital "age tokens" that do exactly this. It is a new and fast-changing area. Learning how apps use your data helps you understand what you are sharing, whichever check you meet.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Age limits on apps are just about stopping kids from having fun.

Truth: Many age limits come from laws about collecting children's personal data, and from worries about what young people see online. They are meant to protect privacy and safety, though people disagree about where the lines should be.

Try this

With a grown-up, open the sign-up page of an app or game you know. Look at how it asks for your age. Does it just ask for a birthday, or does it ask for more? Talk about which method you think is fairest.
