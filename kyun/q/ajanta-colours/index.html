Where did ancient artists get their colours?

No paint shops, no tubes, no brushes from a store. So the artists of Ajanta painted with rocks, soil and soot.

The short answer

About 1,500 years ago, the painters of Ajanta had no tubes of paint and no shops to buy them from. They made their colours from the earth. Reds and yellows came from ochre, soil stained by iron rust. White came from lime or a soft white clay. Black was soot scraped from oil lamps.

Each mineral was ground into a fine powder, called a pigment, then mixed with a sticky glue made from plants or animals. The painters brushed it onto walls coated with mud plaster and a thin skin of lime.

One colour came from much farther away. The brilliant blue was lapis lazuli, a stone thought to have travelled from the mountains of Afghanistan along ancient trade routes.

Surprising but true

Ajanta's bright blue is thought to have come from lapis lazuli, a stone mined in the mountains of Afghanistan.

Carved into a cliff

Ajanta is a row of about 30 caves cut by hand into a horseshoe-shaped cliff above the Waghora river in Maharashtra. The oldest were carved around the 2nd century BCE. Most of the famous paintings date from the 5th century CE, when the region was ruled by the Vakataka kings.

The caves were Buddhist prayer halls and monasteries, where monks lived, studied and prayed. Their walls tell stories from the life of the Buddha and from the Jataka tales, which describe his earlier lives as animals and people. There are princes and dancers, elephants and monkeys, palaces and boats, all painted with a sure, flowing line.

Preparing the walls

Before any paint went on, the rough rock needed a smooth skin. Workers pressed on a layer of mud mixed with rock grit, plant fibres and rice husk, which held it together like straw in the walls of a mud hut. On top went a very thin coat of lime.

Ajanta's pictures are often called frescoes, but strictly they are not. In a true fresco, paint goes onto wet plaster and soaks in. At Ajanta, the painters waited for the plaster to dry and used glue to hold the colour in place. That is one reason some paint has flaked off over the centuries, leaving bare patches of brown mud.

Painting in the dark

The caves face out over the river, but their inner halls are dim. How the painters saw what they were doing is still a puzzle. Some historians think they used mirrors or sheets of polished metal to bounce sunlight deep inside. Others think oil lamps were enough, which fits with the lamp black in the paint itself.

The colours we see now are not quite the colours that were painted. Some pigments have darkened with age. Varnish brushed on by later restorers turned yellow, and smoke from visitors' torches stained some walls. Getting the paint to mix and last was a craft learned over many years.

Lost and found

For more than a thousand years, the caves sat almost forgotten behind thick forest. In 1819, a British army officer named John Smith was hunting a tiger when he spotted a cave entrance across the gorge. He scratched his name and the date onto a painting in Cave 10, and the scratch is still there.

Artists soon came to copy the murals. In the early 1900s, a team that included the young Indian painter Nandalal Bose spent months copying them. Years later, Bose and his students decorated the original handwritten Constitution of India. Today, scientists study tiny flakes of Ajanta paint to learn how to protect what is left, using the methods of art detectives.

Try this

Make your own earth paint. Collect a spoonful of red or yellow garden soil and a stick of white chalk. Crush each into a fine powder in a bowl with the back of a spoon, then mix in a few drops of water and a little flour-and-water paste as glue. Paint on rough paper and see which colour sticks best.
