Who was Saalumarada Thimmakka?

A village woman who never went to school planted 385 banyan trees along a highway in Karnataka, and watered them by hand.

The short answer

Saalumarada Thimmakka was a farm labourer from Karnataka who became one of India's best-loved tree planters. With her husband, Chikkaiah, she planted banyan saplings along the road between the villages of Hulikal and Kudur, near Bengaluru. Today 385 of those trees line about 4.5 km of highway, a green tunnel of shade over the traffic.

She never went to school and earned a few rupees a day working in a stone quarry. The name people gave her, Saalumarada, means "row of trees" in Kannada. She died in November 2025 at an age she gave as 114, honoured with a state funeral.

Surprising but true

Thimmakka and her husband carried water to their saplings in pots, walking kilometres along the road, because there was no tap or pump nearby.

Children of a different kind

Thimmakka married Chikkaiah when she was young. Years passed and they had no children, which in their village brought sadness and unkind talk. So the couple decided to raise trees instead.

They chose the banyan, India's national tree, partly because cuttings from it root easily. A banyan sends down roots from its branches, and these roots thicken into new trunks. The Great Banyan in Kolkata's botanical garden is about 250 years old and covers an area larger than two football fields. One tree can look like a whole grove.

Pots, thorns and patience

The road had no water supply. The couple filled pots and carried them along the highway, walking kilometres each day after work. They planted new saplings in the monsoon, when rain did some of the work, and guarded them through the dry months.

Accounts of her life say they began small, with about ten saplings in the first year, and added more each year after that. The row grew a little longer every monsoon. Banyans grow fast once their roots are settled, but the first few summers are fragile, and a single dry month can kill a sapling.

Hungry goats and cattle were the biggest danger. The couple wrapped each young plant in thorny bushes to keep animals away. Year by year, the saplings grew taller than the people who watered them. Chikkaiah died in 1991, and Thimmakka carried on.

Why one row of trees matters

A big banyan can lower the ground temperature beneath it by several degrees on a hot afternoon. Its leaves pull carbon dioxide from the air through photosynthesis, and its figs feed birds, bats and monkeys. A single old banyan can be home to dozens of kinds of animals.

Roads in India are often widened, and trees are the first things cut down. In 2019, a road plan threatened Thimmakka's banyans. She appealed, and the state government said it would look at other options. The trees still stood.

Fame without changing

Thimmakka was invited to Delhi in 2019 to receive the Padma Shri, one of India's highest civilian honours. Photos from that day show her giving the President a blessing, a touch on the forehead, which made people smile across the country. In 2016 the BBC named her one of its 100 most influential women.

Her fame reached far beyond India. An environmental education group in California is named after her. She went on planting trees and visiting schools well into old age, often in a simple green sari.

Planters across the world

Thimmakka was not alone. In Assam, Jadav Payeng planted a forest on a sandbar in the Brahmaputra. In Kenya, Wangari Maathai started a movement in 1977 that has planted tens of millions of trees, and won a Nobel Peace Prize for it. None of them began with money or power. They began with one plant and a pot of water, and the strangest part is how long those small acts last: a banyan can keep growing for hundreds of years.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Once you plant a tree, it looks after itself.

Truth: Young trees need water and protection for their first few years, and many planted saplings die without it. Thimmakka's trees survived because she looked after them every day.

Try this

With an adult, pick a young tree near your home or school. Tie a piece of string around its trunk at your knee height and mark the length. Check it once a month, and water the tree in dry weeks. After a year, compare the strings.
