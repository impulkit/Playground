How did tennis get its strange scoring?

In tennis, zero is called love and the scores go 15, 30, 40. Nobody is completely sure why.

The short answer

Tennis scoring goes love, 15, 30, 40, game, because of habits that grew up in France around 600 years ago. The most popular explanation is a clock. Scorers may have used a clock face with a hand moving a quarter of the way round, to 15, 30 and 45, for each point. The game ended when the hand reached 60.

Over time 45 was shortened to 40, perhaps because it was quicker to say. Nobody wrote the reasons down, so this is a good guess, not a certain answer. The word "love" for zero is even more mysterious.

Surprising but true

The longest tennis match ever, at Wimbledon in 2010, lasted 11 hours and 5 minutes over three days. The last set alone ended 70–68.

A game played with the palm

Tennis began as jeu de paume, "game of the palm", in medieval France. Players at first hit a ball with their bare hands, then with gloves, then with rackets. It was played indoors in long stone halls, and kings loved it. Hundreds of courts were built in and around Paris.

The name tennis probably comes from the French "tenez", meaning "take this" or "here you go", which a server called out. That version, now called real tennis, is still played on a few dozen indoor courts in Britain, France, the USA and Australia.

How does a clock keep time?Every clock, from a sundial to an atomic clock, has two parts: something that repeats steadily, and something that counts.

Love, deuce and other puzzles

From 40–40, a player must win two points in a row. This score is called deuce, probably from the French "à deux", "at two". The player one point ahead has the advantage.

Zero is called love. One story says it comes from "l'oeuf", the French word for egg, because an egg looks like a zero. Most experts find the "nothing" explanation more convincing: to play for love was to play for no reward. Either way, scoring like this turns each game into a mini-drama, because you can be one point from losing and still come back. If the clock theory is right, tennis is one more thing shaped by the old habit of counting time in sixties.

From halls to lawns

In 1874 a British army officer, Walter Clopton Wingfield, patented an outdoor game for garden lawns. He gave it the Greek name "sphairistike", which almost nobody could pronounce. People simply called it lawn tennis.

In 1877 the All England Croquet Club in Wimbledon, London, held a lawn tennis tournament to raise money for a new roller for its croquet lawns. About 200 people watched the final. Wimbledon is now the oldest tennis tournament in the world.

The longest match

Long games were always a risk with this scoring, because a set could go on and on. At Wimbledon in 2010, John Isner of the USA and Nicolas Mahut of France played for 11 hours and 5 minutes over three days. The last set ended 70–68, and the electronic scoreboard stopped working at 47–47.

Tie-breaks, which end a set at 6–6 with a quick count of points, were introduced in the 1970s. In 2022, all four Grand Slams agreed to use a tie-break in the final set too.

India's tennis stars

India has a proud tennis history. Ramanathan Krishnan reached the Wimbledon semi-finals in 1960 and 1961. Vijay Amritraj was one of the world's top players in the 1970s and 1980s.

At the 1996 Atlanta Olympics, Leander Paes won a bronze medal in singles. With Mahesh Bhupathi and others he won many Grand Slam doubles titles. Sania Mirza became India's most successful woman player, winning six Grand Slam doubles and mixed-doubles titles. Sports scientists now measure serves at over 250 km/h, more than twice a cheetah's top speed, but the score is still called in a language from a medieval French hall.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Love means zero because it comes from the French word l'oeuf, meaning egg, which looks like a 0.

Truth: This is a popular story, but there is little evidence for it. Many language experts prefer the idea that "love" meant playing for nothing, just for the love of the game.

Try this

Play a game of table-top tennis with a scrunched paper ball and books as nets. Score it like real tennis, saying "15, 30, 40" out loud. Then try scoring 1, 2, 3 instead. Which feels more exciting, and does the strange scoring change how you play?
