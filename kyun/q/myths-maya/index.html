What did the Maya believe about the world?

According to an ancient Maya story, the gods tried to make people three times. Mud people melted, wooden people had no hearts, and only people made of maize dough came out right.

The short answer

The ancient Maya believed the world had layers. Above were 13 levels of heaven. In the middle was the Earth, where people lived. Below were 9 levels of a dark underworld called Xibalba, "the place of fear".

At the centre of everything stood a giant world tree, often shown as a ceiba tree, linking the heavens, the Earth and the underworld. The Maya believed gods controlled rain, maize, the Sun and the Moon, and that people had to honour them to keep the world running. They told these beliefs in stories, painted them on pots and carved them in stone.

Surprising but true

The Maya did not disappear. Millions of Maya people live in Mexico, Guatemala, Belize and Honduras today, and many still speak Mayan languages.

People made of maize

The most famous Maya stories come from the Popol Vuh, the sacred book of the K'iche' Maya of Guatemala. It begins with the gods in a quiet, dark sea, deciding to make the world.

They wanted beings who would speak and remember them. First they made people of mud. These crumbled and dissolved in water. Next they carved people of wood. These could talk, but had no hearts or minds, and forgot their makers. A great flood swept them away. Some survivors, the story says, became monkeys.

Finally, the gods ground yellow and white maize into dough and shaped the first true humans. Maize was the Maya's most important food, so this story made people and their crop part of the same family.

The Hero Twins

The longest part of the Popol Vuh tells of the Hero Twins, Hunahpu and Xbalanque. Their father and uncle had been tricked and killed by the lords of Xibalba after a noisy ball game disturbed the underworld.

The twins went down to face the same lords. They passed through the House of Gloom, the House of Knives, the House of Cold, the House of Jaguars and the House of Bats. Each time, they survived through tricks.

In the end, the twins pretended to perform a magic act. The lords were so impressed that they asked to try it themselves, and the twins defeated them. The brothers then rose into the sky as the Sun and the Moon.

The ball game

The Maya ball game was more than a sport. Players had to keep a heavy rubber ball in the air using hips, thighs and arms, not hands. Stone courts were built in many cities, some with rings high on the walls.

The game echoed the twins' story, the movement of the Sun and the battle between life and death. Rubber balls, made from the sap of local trees, were in use in the region thousands of years before Europeans had ever seen rubber.

Time that goes round and round

The Maya were brilliant at tracking time. They used several calendars at once. One had 260 days, another had 365. Together they made a cycle of 52 years.

For longer periods, they used the Long Count, which counted days from a starting date in 3114 BCE. To write it, they needed a symbol for nothing, so they used zero, shown as a shell. They were among the first peoples in the world to use zero, centuries before it reached Europe from India.

The Maya saw time as cycles that repeat, like seasons. That is why the end of one Long Count cycle in 2012 did not mean the end of the world, only the start of a new count.

A living culture

Spanish soldiers arrived in the 1500s. Priests burned many Maya books, and only four are known to survive today. The Popol Vuh survived because Maya writers wrote it down in the Latin alphabet in the 1550s. Around 1701, a Spanish priest, Francisco Ximénez, copied it, and that copy is the one we read.

Today, millions of Maya people live in Mexico and Central America and speak about 30 Mayan languages. Many still plant maize with prayers and keep the 260-day calendar. Their stories sit alongside those of peoples further north, from the turtle-backed world to Coyote's tricks, in Native American stories.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Maya predicted that the world would end in 2012.

Truth: A cycle of the Maya Long Count calendar ended in December 2012, like a car's odometer rolling over. Maya writings never said the world would end. Some inscriptions even mention dates thousands of years after 2012.
