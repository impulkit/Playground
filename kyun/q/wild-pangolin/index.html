Why is the pangolin the world's most trafficked mammal?

A pangolin's scales can stop a lion's bite, yet over a million pangolins are thought to have been taken from the wild in a single decade.

The short answer

Pangolins are the only mammals covered in large hard scales. They are shy, slow, eat ants and termites, and live in parts of Asia and Africa. They are also hunted and smuggled more than any other wild mammal. Conservation groups estimate that over a million were taken from the wild between 2000 and 2013, and tonnes of their scales are still seized every year.

There are two main reasons. People in some countries eat pangolin meat as an expensive luxury, and their scales are used in some traditional medicines, especially in East Asia. Because a pangolin defends itself by rolling into a ball, a poacher can simply pick it up. All eight species are now protected by an international ban on trade.

Surprising but true

A pangolin's tongue can be longer than its body. When it isn't licking up ants, the tongue is tucked away in a pouch in its chest.

Built like a walking pine cone

A pangolin's body is covered with overlapping scales, a bit like the tiles on a roof or the scales on an artichoke. The scales are made of keratin, the same material as your fingernails. They make up about a fifth of the animal's weight, and their edges are sharp enough to cut a curious paw.

When danger comes, the pangolin rolls into a tight ball, tucking its head inside its tail. Lions and leopards have been filmed batting at a curled-up pangolin and finally giving up. The name comes from a Malay word, pengguling, meaning "one who rolls up".

A tongue as long as its body

Pangolins have no teeth. They rip open ant nests and termite mounds with strong front claws, then lick up the insects with a long, sticky tongue. In the giant pangolin, the tongue can reach about 40 cm, longer than the head and body together in smaller species. It is anchored near the pelvis and stored in a pouch in the chest.

Small stones and sand in the stomach help grind food. Scientists estimate that a single pangolin can eat tens of millions of insects a year, which makes them free pest control for farmers and forests.

India's pangolins

India has two species. The Indian pangolin lives across much of the country, from the Western Ghats to dry forests in central India. The Chinese pangolin lives in the Himalayan foothills and the northeast.

Both are protected under India's Wildlife Protection Act, at the highest level, the same as tigers. Even so, Indian forest officials regularly catch smugglers carrying live pangolins or bags of scales towards borders. Many of them are rescued and returned to the wild.

Catching the smugglers

In 2016, countries meeting under CITES, the treaty that controls wildlife trade, voted to ban all international commercial trade in all eight species. The ban came into force in 2017. In 2019, officials in Singapore seized more than 25 tonnes of pangolin scales in two shipments in one week, taken from tens of thousands of animals.

In 2020, China raised the protection level for its native pangolins and removed their scales from its official list of traditional medicines. Conservationists hope demand will fall, but smuggling continues.

Why it matters

Pangolins breed slowly. A mother usually has one baby a year, and the baby rides on her tail, clinging to her scales. Losing even a few adults from an area can take years to recover from.

They are also hard to keep in zoos, because they need special diets and get stressed easily. That means saving them in the wild is the only real option. World Pangolin Day, held on the third Saturday of February, spreads the word. And the creatures they eat, the ants and termites, have their own hidden superpowers.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pangolin scales are powerful medicine.

Truth: Pangolin scales are made of keratin, the same material as human fingernails and rhino horn. There is no good scientific evidence that they cure anything, and China removed them from its official list of traditional medicines in 2020.

Try this

Cut 20 leaf or fish shapes out of thick paper and tape them in overlapping rows onto a cardboard tube, starting from one end like roof tiles. Bend the tube and watch how the scales slide over each other but still cover everything. That is how a pangolin stays protected while it curls.
