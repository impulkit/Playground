What is Arizona like?

Arizona has cactuses taller than a house, a crater left by a space rock, snowy mountain towns, and clocks that never change.

The short answer

Arizona is a state in the south-west of the USA, next to Mexico. It became the 48th state in 1912, the last of the 48 states that touch each other on the map. Much of the south is the Sonoran Desert, home to giant saguaro cactuses and summer days that can pass 45°C in the capital, Phoenix.

But Arizona is not all low desert. The north is a high plateau with pine forests, snow and the Grand Canyon, carved by the Colorado River. The state is also home to 22 Native American tribes, including the Navajo Nation, the biggest reservation in the country. Arizona packs deserts, mountains, canyons and dark starry skies into one state a little smaller than Rajasthan.

Surprising but true

Most of Arizona does not change its clocks for daylight saving time, but the Navajo Nation inside it does, so you can change time zones several times on one drive.

The giant cactus

The saguaro (say "sa-WAH-ro") grows wild only in the Sonoran Desert, in Arizona and north-western Mexico. A big one can be 12 metres tall, as high as a four-storey building, and weigh several tonnes when full of water. One heavy rain can let it soak up hundreds of litres through roots that spread out just under the surface.

Woodpeckers drill nest holes in the trunk. Later, tiny elf owls move into the empty holes. In late spring, white flowers open at night on the tips of the arms, and bats, bees and doves drink their nectar. The Tohono O'odham people have long harvested the red fruit to make syrup and jam.

A hole made by space

About 50,000 years ago, an iron meteorite about 50 metres across slammed into northern Arizona. It blasted out a bowl more than a kilometre across and about 170 metres deep. Today it is called Meteor Crater.

In the 1960s, scientists studying the crater found minerals that only form under the huge pressure of an impact. That helped prove that craters like this, and many on the Moon, were made by falling space rocks rather than volcanoes. Apollo astronauts trained nearby before going to the Moon.

Dark skies and a lost planet

In 1930, a young astronomer named Clyde Tombaugh was working at Lowell Observatory in Flagstaff. He compared photos of the same patch of sky taken nights apart, looking for a dot that moved. He found one: Pluto.

Flagstaff has worked hard to keep its skies dark, with rules about outdoor lights. In 2001 it became the first International Dark Sky City. On a clear night there, the Milky Way stretches across the sky like a pale cloud.

Copper, cotton and clocks

Arizonans once spoke of the "five Cs" that built the state: copper, cattle, cotton, citrus and climate. Copper still matters. Arizona mines around two-thirds of all the copper produced in the USA, metal that ends up in wires, pipes and phones.

Water is the thing Arizona never has enough of. Phoenix gets only about 20 cm of rain a year, less than Mumbai can get in a single wet day. So a concrete canal more than 500 km long, the Central Arizona Project, carries water from the Colorado River across the desert to Phoenix and Tucson. As the river shrinks in long droughts, the state has had to cut back on how much it takes.

Then there are the clocks. Summer days in Phoenix are hot enough without an extra hour of evening sun, so in 1968 the state chose to skip daylight saving time. The Navajo Nation, which spreads into Utah and New Mexico, does change its clocks. Inside it lies the Hopi reservation, which doesn't. Drive across the north-east of the state in summer and your phone may change its mind several times.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Deserts like Arizona are always hot.

Truth: Arizona's high country gets real winters. Flagstaff, at over 2,100 m, is one of the snowiest cities in the USA, and the rim of the Grand Canyon is often white in January.

Try this

Find a pleated sports bottle or fold a strip of paper like a fan, then stretch and squeeze it. Saguaros have folds like this down their sides. After rain they swell up as they fill with water, and in dry months they slowly shrink back.
