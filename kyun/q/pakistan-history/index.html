What do India and Pakistan share?

India and Pakistan were one land for thousands of years, so they share a river, a cuisine, a love of cricket and a language that only looks different on paper.

The short answer

For thousands of years, the lands that are now India and Pakistan had one long, shared history. In 1947, when British rule ended, the subcontinent was divided into two countries. India and Pakistan have been neighbours ever since, and their governments have often disagreed, sometimes fought.

But people on both sides still share a great deal. They share the oldest cities of the region, the Indus River, the history of the Mughals and much more. They speak closely related languages and cook many of the same dishes. They watch the same films and songs, and both are passionate about cricket.

Surprising but true

Mohenjo-daro, one of the most famous cities of the Indus Valley Civilisation that Indian children learn about in school, is in Pakistan's Sindh province.

The oldest shared story

About 4,500 years ago, one of the world's first great civilisations grew along the Indus River and its neighbours. Its people built cities with straight streets, brick houses, covered drains and huge granaries. You can explore them in Indus cities.

Harappa and Mohenjo-daro are in Pakistan. Dholavira, Lothal and Rakhigarhi are in India. Archaeologists from both countries study the same civilisation, sometimes reading the same mysterious, still undeciphered script on the same kind of small stone seals.

How did the Indus cities have drains 4,500 years ago?Long before many cities had sewers, Indus towns had covered drains along their streets.

Partition

In August 1947, British India was split into India and Pakistan. Pakistan then included two parts, West and East, with East Pakistan later becoming Bangladesh. The new border cut through Punjab and Bengal, through farms, villages and families.

Around 1.5 crore people crossed the new borders, Hindus and Sikhs moving to India and Muslims moving to Pakistan. It was one of the largest migrations in history. There was terrible violence, and estimates of the dead range from a few hundred thousand to about two million. Many families on both sides still have grandparents who remember leaving a home they never saw again.

One language, two scripts

If a person from Delhi and a person from Lahore chat about cricket or food, they will understand each other easily. Everyday Hindi and Urdu are so close that some people call the shared spoken language Hindustani. Film songs from Mumbai are loved in Pakistan for exactly that reason.

The difference shows on paper. Hindi is written in Devanagari, from left to right. Urdu is written in a flowing Perso-Arabic script, from right to left. In formal speech, Hindi often borrows words from Sanskrit, and Urdu from Persian and Arabic. Punjabi, too, is spoken on both sides, written in Gurmukhi in India and in Shahmukhi in Pakistan.

Food, buildings and a game

Kebabs, biryani, parathas, lassi and sweet milky chai are loved in both countries. Each side argues happily about whose biryani is best.

The Mughal emperors ruled from both Delhi and Lahore, as told in the Mughals. Lahore Fort and the Badshahi Mosque in Pakistan, and the Red Fort and Jama Masjid in India, were built by the same dynasty. Ancient Taxila, near Islamabad, was a famous centre of learning linked by tradition with Chanakya, the adviser of Chandragupta Maurya.

When India and Pakistan play cricket, streets on both sides fall quiet. These matches are among the most watched sporting events on Earth.

Meeting at the border

At the Attari-Wagah crossing between Amritsar and Lahore, soldiers from both countries have held a flag-lowering ceremony at sunset for decades. It is full of high kicks, stamping and loud cheers from crowds on each side.

Many families still have relatives across the border, and some write, call and visit when they can.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Hindi and Urdu are completely different languages.

Truth: In everyday speech they are so close that speakers easily understand each other. They differ mainly in their scripts and in formal words, which Hindi often takes from Sanskrit and Urdu from Persian and Arabic.

Try this

Write the word "kitaab" (book) or "duniya" (world) and ask family members whether it is a Hindi word or an Urdu word. Then look at how the same word is written in Devanagari and in the Urdu script. Same sound, two different scripts.
