What does music do to your brain?

When a favourite song reaches its best bit, your brain releases dopamine, a chemical linked to pleasure. It even starts releasing it a few seconds early, in anticipation.

The short answer

Music switches on more of your brain at once than almost anything else you do. Sound is picked up by hearing areas on both sides of the brain. Areas for movement get busy, which is why your foot taps. Emotion centres react, and memory areas link songs to people and places.

When you hear music you love, your brain releases dopamine, a chemical linked to pleasure and reward. That's part of why music can make you happy, excited, calm or tearful. It can also help people who are ill, from stroke patients to people with memory loss.

Surprising but true

Some people who lose the ability to speak after a stroke can still sing the words of songs they know, because singing uses different brain pathways.

A brain-wide workout

Sound waves enter your ears and become signals in your hearing nerves. The hearing areas of your brain pick out pitch, loudness and rhythm. Then the signals spread.

Areas that plan movement start predicting the beat, even if you sit still. That's why it's so hard not to nod along. Your brain also keeps guessing what note comes next. When a song surprises you, with a sudden key change or a pause before the chorus, those guesses are broken, and you feel a jolt.

This starts early. Babies only a few months old wriggle and bounce more to music with a clear beat than to ordinary speech. Every human culture that scientists have studied makes music, from lullabies in Assamese villages to work songs in the American South. Many researchers think our brains are tuned for it from birth.

The pleasure chemical

In 2011 scientists at McGill University in Canada asked people to bring their favourite pieces of music. They scanned their brains while they listened. At the best moments, the brain released dopamine, the same chemical involved in enjoying food.

The surprise was the timing. Dopamine also rose a few seconds before the big moment, as people waited for it. Many listeners got goosebumps or a shiver down the spine right then. Your brain enjoys the build-up almost as much as the payoff.

Songs and memories

Hear a song from a family wedding or a school trip, and you can be pulled straight back to that day. Music and memory are tightly linked, partly because music stirs up emotions, and emotional moments are easier to remember.

This link is strong enough to help people with Alzheimer's disease, which damages memory. Some patients who struggle to recognise relatives can still sing every word of songs from their youth. Care homes in many countries, including India and the US, use music to calm and comfort residents.

Healing with rhythm

Music therapists use songs and rhythm to help people recover. After a stroke, some patients can no longer speak, but they can sing. Therapists use singing to slowly rebuild speech.

A steady beat also helps people with Parkinson's disease, which makes walking hard. Walking in time to a rhythm can make their steps steadier. Even premature babies in some hospitals are played gentle music or lullabies, which may help them settle.

The Mozart question

In 1993 a small study suggested that college students did slightly better on a folding-paper puzzle after hearing Mozart. The boost lasted only about 10 to 15 minutes. Newspapers still called it the "Mozart effect". Toy companies sold Mozart CDs for babies, and in 1998 the governor of Georgia in the US even proposed giving one to every newborn.

Later research found the effect was tiny, short and possibly just a sign of being in a good mood. Learning to play an instrument is different. It trains listening, attention and fine finger control, and musicians' brains show some differences from non-musicians'. Scientists are still arguing about whether it makes children better at other subjects.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Listening to Mozart makes children smarter.

Truth: The "Mozart effect" came from a 1993 study of college students that found a tiny, short boost on one puzzle task. Bigger studies haven't backed it up. Learning to play an instrument is a different question, and scientists are still studying it.

Try this

Make two short playlists, one of calm songs and one of fast, bouncy ones. Listen to each for five minutes while you sit still, then count your pulse at your wrist for 30 seconds. Notice whether your heart rate or mood changes.
