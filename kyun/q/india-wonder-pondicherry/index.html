Why does Puducherry feel French?

In one Indian city, the police wear red French caps, the streets are called "rue", and some residents vote in French elections.

The short answer

Puducherry feels French because it was ruled by France for most of the time between 1674 and 1954, much longer than the British ruled most of India. The French laid out its old town, built its churches and government buildings, and left behind their language, food and even street signs.

Walk through the old seaside quarter and you see mustard-yellow villas with white shutters, bougainvillea over the gates, and street names like Rue Romain Rolland. Policemen wear red caps called kepis, a French style. A French school, a French consulate and bakeries selling croissants sit a few streets from Tamil temples.

It became part of India in 1954, seven years after independence, and its name changed from Pondicherry to Puducherry in 2006.

Surprising but true

Puducherry's territory is split into four separate pieces spread across three states, hundreds of kilometres apart.

How the French got here

In the 1600s, European trading companies raced to buy Indian cotton, pepper and indigo. The French East India Company arrived late. In 1674, a French official named François Martin set up a trading post at a fishing village on the Tamil coast. The village grew into a fortified town.

The Dutch captured it once, and the British captured it several times during their wars with France. Each time, it was handed back by a peace treaty. In the 1740s and 1750s, the French governor Dupleix even tried to build a French empire across southern India. The British East India Company beat him, and France was left with only a few small towns.

Who else tried to rule parts of India?In 1741, a Kerala king named Marthanda Varma defeated a Dutch army at Colachel, and Dutch power in India never recovered.

Four pieces in three states

Puducherry is not one block of land. It is four separate pieces. Puducherry town and Karaikal sit inside Tamil Nadu. Mahe is on the Kerala coast, on the other side of India. Yanam is in Andhra Pradesh, more than 600 km from Puducherry town.

Each was a French trading post, and they stayed together after 1954. Mahe and Yanam are small enclaves, surrounded by other states. That's why Puducherry's government uses five languages: Tamil, Malayalam and Telugu for its three regions, plus English and French.

The French who stayed

When France handed over Puducherry, people living there were given a choice. They could become Indian citizens or keep French citizenship. Several thousand chose France. Their families still live in Puducherry today, carrying French passports.

So during French presidential elections, polling booths open in Puducherry, and voters line up in a small Indian town to elect the leader of a country more than 7,000 km away. Some French-Tamil families have relatives in Paris, Lyon and Marseille. Many older people in the town still speak French at home.

The town by the sea

The old French quarter is built in a tidy grid, a plan that was unusual for Indian towns at the time. A canal once divided it into a European quarter by the sea and a Tamil quarter inland. The canal is still there, though the old names have faded.

Along the seafront, the long Promenade Beach is closed to traffic every evening, and families walk along it as the sea breeze comes in. Near it stands a statue of Joan of Arc, the French heroine, and a war memorial to soldiers from Puducherry who fought for France in the First World War.

Just north of the town, Auroville, a township founded in 1968, brings together people from dozens of countries. Puducherry is not the only place in India that feels European. Down the west coast, Goa was ruled by Portugal until 1961, even longer, and its churches, food and words still show it. And not far north lies Chennai, which began as a British fort only a few decades before the French arrived here.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Puducherry became part of India in 1947 like the rest of the country.

Truth: In 1947 Puducherry was still ruled by France. It was handed over to India in 1954, and the treaty making it fully official took effect in 1962.
