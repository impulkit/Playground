How do sand artists make pictures?

Some of the world's most careful artworks are made from sand and then swept away on purpose, sometimes after weeks of work.

The short answer

Sand artists use the same grains you find on a beach, but in very different ways. Sand sculptors pack wet sand into huge blocks and carve them, like stone. Sand painters pour coloured sand in thin streams to draw on the ground. Sand animators sprinkle sand on a lit glass table and push it around with their fingers, making pictures that change in front of an audience.

What links them is control. A grain of sand is tiny and hard to steer, so the artist has to learn exactly how it flows, packs and falls.

Surprising but true

A sandcastle is strongest with only a little water in it. Too much water and it slumps, too little and it crumbles.

Why wet sand stands up

Dry sand pours like water. Add a little water and something odd happens. A thin film clings to each grain, and where two grains touch, the water forms a tiny bridge that pulls them together. Millions of bridges make the sand stiff enough to carve.

Scientists tested this in 2012 and found sand works best with just a little water, around 1 part in 100. The ancient Egyptians may have known a similar trick. A 2014 study suggested that wetting the sand in front of a sledge halved the force needed to drag a heavy stone, which may help explain how the pyramids were built.

The giant castles

Sculptors start by building tall wooden or plastic frames, filling them with sand and water, and pounding it down hard, layer by layer. Then they remove the frames and carve from the top down, so falling sand never ruins finished work.

In 2021, a team in Blokhus, Denmark, built a sandcastle about 21 metres tall, roughly the height of a seven-storey building. In India, Sudarsan Pattnaik of Puri in Odisha builds huge sculptures on the beach, often about news events. He received the Padma Shri in 2014, and his sculptures have won prizes in competitions around the world.

Why do some beaches have black sand?Some of the white sand on tropical beaches has passed through the gut of a parrotfish, a reef fish that grinds up coral and poops out sand.

Art made to disappear

Some sand art is meant to be destroyed. Tibetan Buddhist monks make sand mandalas, circular designs packed with symbols, by tapping coloured sand through thin metal funnels, a few grains at a time. A large mandala can take a team several days or even weeks.

When it is done, the monks sweep it away and pour the sand into flowing water. The ceremony is a reminder of a teaching of the Buddha: that nothing lasts forever.

In the American Southwest, Navajo healers make sandpaintings from coloured sand, crushed rock and charcoal as part of healing ceremonies. The painting is wiped away afterwards. Families in the Navajo Nation treat these designs as sacred, and photographs of real ceremonial paintings are usually not allowed.

Pictures that move

Sand animation is a newer art. The artist works on a glass table lit from below. Where sand is thick, the light is blocked and the glass looks dark. Where the artist sweeps sand away, light shines through. A camera projects the picture on a big screen, and one scene melts into the next.

In 2009, a Ukrainian sand artist told a war story this way on a TV talent show, and the video was watched by millions online.

Sand at your doorstep

Indian homes have their own floor art. Many families draw rangoli or kolam each morning with rice flour, coloured powder or sand, letting it trickle through their fingers into patterns of lines and dots. Footsteps and wind wipe it away by evening, and the next morning it starts again. The rice flour has a second job too: ants and birds eat it, so a kolam feeds small creatures as well as decorating the door.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Sand sculptures need glue or cement to stay up.

Truth: Most competition sand sculptures are just sand and water, packed down very hard. Some artists spray a thin coat of watered-down glue at the end to protect the surface from wind, but the shape holds itself up.

Try this

Fill three cups with sand. Leave one dry, add a spoonful of water to the second and lots of water to the third. Pack each one firmly, turn it upside down on a tray and lift the cup. Which tower stands best?
