How does soundproofing work?

The foam squares on a music studio's walls barely stop sound escaping. Keeping noise out takes weight, gaps and good seals.

The short answer

Sound is a vibration. When someone plays a drum, the skin wobbles, pushes on the air, and that wobble spreads outwards until it shakes your eardrum. It can also travel through walls, floors and windows, which is why you can hear the neighbour's television.

Soundproofing makes it harder for those vibrations to get through. Builders use four main tricks. Heavy materials are hard to shake. Soft, spongy materials soak up sound. Gaps between layers stop vibrations passing straight across. And sealing cracks stops sound sneaking through the air.

Good soundproofing uses all four together. Leave out one, and noise finds a way. Engineers measure loudness in decibels. Every 10 decibels more sounds roughly twice as loud to our ears.

Surprising but true

In a very quiet test room called an anechoic chamber, many people can hear their own heartbeat and the blood moving in their ears.

Heavy is quiet

A thin sheet of paper flutters at the slightest breath. A thick concrete wall hardly moves at all. Sound works the same way. The heavier a wall is, the more energy it takes for sound to shake it, so less comes out the other side.

That's why recording studios and cinemas have thick walls, often made of several layers. Doubling a wall's weight helps, but not as much as you might hope. A smarter trick is to build two separate walls with an air gap between them. Vibrations that shake the first wall then have to cross the gap before they reach the second. Double-glazed windows near airports work like this, with two panes of glass and a space in between.

Water leaks, so does sound

Sound travels through air, so any crack lets it through, the way water finds a hole in a bucket. A gap under a door can let in a surprising amount of noise. Electric sockets, air vents and badly fitted windows are common leaks.

Soundproofers seal them with rubber strips and thick, flexible sealant. Studio doors are heavy and close tightly against rubber edges, a bit like a fridge door. Sometimes the cheapest way to make a bedroom quieter is a draught stopper along the bottom of the door.

How do noise-cancelling headphones work?Noise-cancelling headphones fight sound with more sound. They play a mirror-image copy of the noise around you, and the two wipe each other out.

Soft surfaces and echoes

Foam panels, curtains, carpets and cushions do something different. They are full of tiny air pockets. When sound enters, it bounces around inside, rubs against the fibres and turns into a very small amount of heat. This stops sound bouncing off hard walls and creating echoes.

A room with bare walls and a tiled floor sounds loud and boomy. Add rugs, sofas and bookshelves, and it sounds softer. Musicians who record music at home often hang heavy blankets around them for this reason. But the neighbours will still hear the drums.

The quietest rooms on Earth

Some laboratories have rooms called anechoic chambers, which means "without echoes". Their walls, floors and ceilings are covered in deep foam wedges, and the room sits on springs inside a heavy concrete shell. Engineers use them to test microphones, phones and headphones. One such room at Microsoft's offices near Seattle was named the quietest place on Earth by Guinness World Records in 2015. In 2021, a lab in Minneapolis won the record back, and it still held it in 2024.

Being inside one is strange. With no echoes, a voice sounds flat and dead. Many visitors notice sounds they never normally hear, such as their own breathing and heartbeat. Too much noise, on the other hand, can harm hearing, which is why factories and building sites need soundproofing for workers' ears.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Covering walls with egg boxes or foam soundproofs a room.

Truth: Soft, light materials absorb echoes so a room sounds less boomy inside. Stopping sound from getting through needs heavy, airtight layers. That's why a studio has foam on the walls and also thick, double walls behind them.

Try this

Put a ticking clock or a phone playing quiet music in a shoebox. Listen from a metre away. Then try wrapping the box in a towel, then putting it inside a second box with a gap, then leaving a crack in the lid. Which change made the biggest difference?
