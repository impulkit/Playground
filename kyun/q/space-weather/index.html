What is space weather?

In May 2024, a storm from the Sun painted the night sky red over Ladakh, and Indian astronomers caught it on camera.

The short answer

Space weather is what happens when the Sun throws energy and particles out into space and some of it reaches Earth. Like weather on Earth, it can be calm or stormy.

The Sun gives off a steady stream of charged particles called the solar wind. Sometimes it also has explosions. A solar flare is a sudden flash of energy. A coronal mass ejection, or CME, is a giant cloud of hot, charged gas, called plasma, flung into space. When a big CME hits Earth's magnetic field, it causes a geomagnetic storm. Storms like this can make beautiful auroras, but they can also disturb satellites, power lines, radio signals and GPS.

Surprising but true

During a huge solar storm in 1859, some telegraph operators unplugged their batteries and kept sending messages using only the electricity the storm produced in the wires.

Flares and clouds from the Sun

The Sun is a huge ball of plasma, crossed by twisted magnetic fields. Around sunspots, those fields can tangle and suddenly snap into a new shape, releasing energy.

A solar flare's light travels at the speed of light, so it reaches Earth in about 8 minutes. It can disrupt radio signals on the side of Earth facing the Sun. A CME is slower. It can take one to three days to cross the 15 crore km to Earth. That gives forecasters a little time to warn satellite operators and power companies.

Earth's shield

Earth has a magnetic field, as if a giant bar magnet were buried inside it. This field, along with our atmosphere, shields us from most of the Sun's particles. Earth's magnetic field steers many of them towards the poles.

When they crash into gases high in the atmosphere, they make the air glow. That is the aurora, the northern and southern lights. Oxygen glows green and red, and nitrogen adds blue and purple. In a strong storm, auroras spread much farther from the poles than usual.

The Carrington Event

On 1 September 1859, the English astronomer Richard Carrington was sketching sunspots when he saw two bright flashes of white light. About 17 hours later, a huge geomagnetic storm hit Earth.

Auroras lit up the sky as far south as the Caribbean. People in some places got up thinking it was dawn. Telegraph wires sparked, some operators got electric shocks, and some found they could send messages with their batteries unplugged. It remains the strongest geomagnetic storm on record.

Storms in our time

In March 1989, a geomagnetic storm caused a blackout across the Canadian province of Quebec. Millions of people lost power for about nine hours.

In February 2022, SpaceX launched 49 new Starlink satellites just before a minor storm. The storm warmed and puffed up the upper atmosphere, increasing drag. Around 38 of the satellites fell back and burned up.

In May 2024, the strongest storm in about two decades brought auroras to places where they're almost never seen. In India, cameras at the Indian Astronomical Observatory in Hanle, Ladakh, recorded a red aurora low on the horizon. People in the southern United States saw them too.

Watching the Sun

Forecasters keep watch with spacecraft and ground observatories. In the US, NOAA's Space Weather Prediction Center in Boulder, Colorado, issues alerts and rates storms from G1 (minor) to G5 (extreme).

India launched its own Sun observatory, Aditya-L1, in September 2023. It sits about 15 lakh km from Earth, at a point where it can watch the Sun without Earth ever getting in the way. It studies flares, CMEs and the solar wind.

The Sun's 11-year beat

The Sun's activity rises and falls in a cycle of about 11 years. At solar maximum there are more sunspots, flares and storms. NASA and NOAA announced in October 2024 that the Sun had reached the maximum phase of its current cycle. Over the next several years, activity should slowly calm down, before rising again in the 2030s.

Astronauts on the International Space Station have a sheltered area to move to during a strong storm. For the rest of us, a big solar storm mostly means a chance to look up at the right moment.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Solar storms are dangerous to people on the ground.

Truth: Earth's magnetic field and atmosphere protect us, so solar storms can't hurt people on the surface. The risks are to satellites, power grids, radio and GPS, and to astronauts in space.

Try this

Place a compass on a table and slowly bring a fridge magnet towards it from different sides. Watch the needle swing. Earth's magnetic field guides a compass the same way, and during a big solar storm, sensitive instruments can see that field shiver.
