What was the Forbidden City?

For almost 500 years, ordinary people in China could be severely punished for entering the Forbidden City without permission. Now millions walk through it every year.

The short answer

The Forbidden City is a vast palace in the middle of Beijing, the capital of China. It was the home of China's emperors and the centre of their government from 1420 until 1912. It was called "forbidden" because nobody could enter or leave without the emperor's permission.

It covers about 72 hectares, about 100 football fields, and is surrounded by a 10-metre wall and a wide moat. Inside are around 90 courtyards and about 8,700 rooms, with golden-yellow roofs and red walls. Since 1925 it has been a museum, called the Palace Museum, and it is one of the most visited places on Earth.

Surprising but true

A famous story says the Forbidden City has 9,999 rooms, one fewer than a palace in heaven. A survey in the 1970s counted about 8,700.

Built for an emperor

In 1402, a prince seized the throne of the Ming dynasty and became the Yongle Emperor. He decided to move the capital north from Nanjing to Beijing, and ordered a new palace. Work began in 1406.

Huge numbers of workers took part, often said to be around a million, including some 100,000 skilled craftsmen. Giant logs of rare nanmu wood were dragged from forests in the south-west. Massive stone slabs were slid along roads that workers flooded with water in winter, so it froze into ice tracks. The palace was finished in 1420.

A city of symbols

Everything in the Forbidden City was laid out with meaning. Buildings line up along a north–south line through the middle. The most important halls face south, which in Chinese tradition was the direction of warmth and good fortune. Rooms and courtyards are arranged in matching pairs, showing balance and order.

Colours mattered too. Yellow, the emperor's colour, covers almost every roof. Red walls stood for good luck. Small statues of animals line the ridges of the roofs. The more statues, the more important the building. The Hall of Supreme Harmony, where emperors held big ceremonies, has the most, ten in all.

Life behind the walls

The emperor lived with his family, hundreds of servants, officials and guards. In the inner court, where the imperial family lived, the only grown men allowed apart from the emperor were usually palace servants called eunuchs. At some times, thousands of them worked in the palace.

Few outsiders ever saw the emperor. Officials who did had to kneel and touch their heads to the ground. Those who broke the palace's rules could be severely punished.

The last emperor

In 1644, the Ming dynasty fell, and the Manchu rulers of the Qing dynasty took over the palace. In total, 24 emperors lived there over nearly 500 years.

In 1912, after the revolution that made China a republic, the last emperor, a six-year-old boy named Puyi, gave up the throne. He was allowed to keep living in the inner palace until 1924, when he was forced to leave. A year later, the palace opened to the public as a museum.

Treasures and visitors

The Palace Museum holds more than 1.8 million objects, including paintings, porcelain, clocks, jade and bronze. During the 1930s, when Japan invaded China, staff packed many treasures into crates and moved them away from Beijing. Some never came back. Part of the collection is now in the National Palace Museum in Taipei, Taiwan.

The Forbidden City now limits how many people can visit each day, and you must book a ticket ahead with your ID. Crowds are biggest during China's national holidays in early October, when lines of visitors stretch across the courtyards. It is closed on most Mondays for cleaning and repairs. With the Great Wall, it is one of the top sights in Beijing for visitors from around the world.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Forbidden City has exactly 9,999 and a half rooms.

Truth: That is a popular legend, linked to the idea that only heaven could have 10,000 rooms. A careful count in the 1970s found about 8,700 rooms.
