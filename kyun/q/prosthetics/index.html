How do bionic arms and legs work?

A bionic hand can be moved by thought, by picking up tiny electrical signals from the muscles that are still there.

The short answer

A prosthetic is an artificial body part, and a "bionic" one has motors and electronics. Many bionic arms are myoelectric: they read the tiny electrical signals your muscles make when you tighten them. Someone who has lost a hand still has muscles in their forearm. When they try to close the hand, those muscles twitch, and sensors resting on the skin inside the socket pick up the signal.

A small computer turns that signal into a command, and motors in the hand open or close the fingers. Bionic legs work differently. They use sensors to feel the ground and microprocessors to adjust the knee or ankle many times a second, so walking downhill or up stairs feels steadier.

Surprising but true

One of the oldest known artificial body parts is a wooden and leather toe found on an Egyptian mummy, about 3,000 years old, made to help its owner walk.

Thousands of years of spare parts

People have been making replacement body parts for a very long time. A wooden toe with leather straps, found on the foot of an Egyptian woman's mummy, is about 3,000 years old and shows signs of wear, so it was probably used for walking. Another, made of linen, glue and plaster, is in the British Museum. Ancient Egyptians cared about walking well, and they cared about being buried whole.

For most of history, artificial legs were wooden pegs and artificial hands were hooks. Wars, which left many soldiers injured, pushed inventors to make better ones, with joints, springs and straps.

The Jaipur Foot

In 1968–69, in Jaipur, an orthopaedic surgeon named Dr P.K. Sethi and a craftsman named Ram Chandra Sharma designed a new kind of artificial foot. Western prosthetic feet of the time were stiff and costly, and didn't suit people who walked barefoot, squatted or sat cross-legged on the floor.

The Jaipur Foot is made of rubber and wood, looks like a real foot, and can bend and twist. It lets people walk in fields, climb trees and work in paddy fields. A charity in Jaipur, Bhagwan Mahaveer Viklang Sahayata Samiti, fits it free of charge. It has helped more than a million people, in India and at fitting camps in many countries of Asia and Africa.

Blades for running

Look at sprinters at the Paralympics and you'll see curved blades instead of feet. They are made of carbon fibre, a light, strong material that bends like a spring as the runner lands and springs back as they push off.

They're designed for running only. Standing still on them is wobbly, so athletes switch to a different leg for walking. Scientists still debate whether blades give an advantage over natural legs or not, and sports bodies decide case by case.

Feeling and thinking

The hardest problem is feeling. Your real hand sends a stream of messages up your nerves, telling your brain how hard you are gripping and whether something is slipping. Most prosthetic hands send nothing back, so users have to watch what they are doing.

Researchers are testing ways to fix this. In one approach, surgeons move nerves that once served the lost hand into other muscles, giving clearer signals to control a bionic arm. In another, tiny electrodes on the remaining nerves send buzzes back to the brain when the hand's fingertips touch something. Some people can now tell a soft grape from a hard ball with their eyes closed.

Cheaper hands

A top bionic arm can cost as much as a car. To bring costs down, volunteers and small companies use 3D printers to make simple plastic hands, especially for children, who outgrow them quickly. A child can get a new hand in a favourite colour, or one that looks like a superhero's, for far less than the price of a standard prosthetic.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Bionic limbs work just like real ones, or even make people stronger than everyone else.

Truth: Modern prosthetics can do a lot, but none yet match a natural limb's feeling, speed and control. Most users can't feel what their prosthetic hand touches. Running blades help athletes, but scientists still debate whether they give any advantage.

Try this

Try making a sandwich, buttoning a shirt or tying a shoelace using only one hand, with the other tucked in a pocket. Notice which jobs are hardest. Then think about which movement you would most want a prosthetic hand to do well.
