Why did people pay fortunes for tulips?

In 1630s Holland, a single striped tulip bulb could cost about as much as a fine house beside an Amsterdam canal.

The short answer

In the 1630s, tulips became a status symbol in the Dutch Republic, now the Netherlands. The country was rich from trade, and owning rare, beautiful flowers showed off taste and wealth. The most prized tulips had striking flame-like stripes, and nobody could grow them on demand.

Prices climbed and climbed. By the winter of 1636–37, people were trading contracts for bulbs still buried in the ground, sometimes reselling them several times in a day. In February 1637 buyers suddenly stopped turning up, and prices collapsed. The episode became famous as "tulip mania", often called the first big price bubble in history.

Surprising but true

The most prized "broken" tulips, with flame-like stripes, were actually sick. Their patterns came from a virus spread by tiny insects called aphids.

A flower from far away

Tulips are not originally Dutch. They grew wild in the mountains of Central Asia and were grown in gardens across the Ottoman Empire. In Turkish, the tulip is lale, and it decorated tiles, clothes and palaces in Istanbul.

In the 1590s, a botanist named Carolus Clusius planted tulips in the university garden at Leiden. They were new and dazzling in northern Europe, with colours stronger than any local flower. Wealthy collectors wanted them, and some bulbs were stolen from Clusius's garden.

The sick flowers everyone wanted

The most valuable tulips were called "broken". Their petals had feathery stripes of red or purple on white or yellow, like flames. The most famous was Semper Augustus, red and white. Only a handful were known.

Nobody could predict which bulbs would break. A plain tulip might grow striped the next year, or never. Then the striped ones tended to grow weaker. In the 1920s scientists discovered why: the stripes were caused by a virus, spread from plant to plant by aphids. The disease made them beautiful and fragile at the same time.

Buying the wind

Tulips flower in spring, and bulbs can only be dug up safely in summer. So deals made in autumn and winter were promises: I will pay you this much for that bulb when it comes out of the ground. Traders met in taverns, where contracts changed hands over drinks.

People called it windhandel, the "wind trade", because nothing real changed hands. Prices for some bulbs rose many times over in a few months. A single Semper Augustus bulb was reportedly priced at thousands of guilders, about what a skilled worker earned in many years.

The crash

In early February 1637, at a bulb auction in Haarlem, buyers simply didn't bid. Word spread fast. Within days, nobody wanted to pay the old prices. People who had promised to buy bulbs at high prices refused to pay. Courts were unwilling to enforce the contracts, and many deals were settled for a small fraction or cancelled.

This is how many bubbles end, with prices driven by supply and demand and by people expecting someone else to pay more later. When that belief vanishes, prices fall fast.

Legend versus history

For centuries, tulip mania was told as a warning tale. A popular 1841 book by Charles Mackay described people selling homes and farms, and a sailor who ate a priceless bulb thinking it was an onion. Much of this came from moralising songs and pamphlets of the 1630s.

The historian Anne Goldgar searched Dutch records and found few signs of mass ruin. Buyers were mostly fairly well-off, and the Dutch economy carried on growing.

Tulips today

The Netherlands is still famous for tulips, grown in huge colourful fields. Keukenhof gardens near Leiden plant around 7 million bulbs each year. India has its own tulip spectacle. In Kashmir, the Indira Gandhi Memorial Tulip Garden in Srinagar, Asia's largest, displayed about 1.7 million tulips in 2025. And the country that grows the most is the same one that has pushed back the sea, as the story of how the Dutch live below sea level explains.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Tulip mania ruined the Dutch economy and drove many people to despair.

Truth: Historians who studied records from the time found little sign of widespread ruin. Most buyers were fairly well-off merchants and craftspeople, and many contracts were simply cancelled. The Dutch economy kept growing through the 1600s.

Try this

Run a tulip market with friends. Give each person 10 paper coins and make five paper tulips, with one special striped one. Hold an auction and see how high the striped one goes when everyone wants it. Then announce that nobody will buy tulips any more and watch what the prices are worth.
