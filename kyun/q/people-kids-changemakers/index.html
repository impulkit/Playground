Which kids have changed the world?

The reading system used by millions of blind people around the world was invented by a 15-year-old boy in Paris.

The short answer

Plenty of children and teenagers have changed the world. Some invented things, like Louis Braille, who created a reading system for blind people at 15. Some stood up to unfair rules, like Claudette Colvin and Ruby Bridges in the USA. Some spoke out for education, like Malala Yousafzai in Pakistan, or for the planet, like Greta Thunberg in Sweden.

They didn't have money or power. What they had was a problem they couldn't ignore and the stubbornness to keep going. Several were also helped by brave parents, teachers and friends.

Surprising but true

Nine months before Rosa Parks refused to give up her bus seat, a 15-year-old girl named Claudette Colvin did the same thing in the same city.

A teenager who gave blind people books

Louis Braille lost his sight after an accident when he was three. At a school for blind children in Paris, books used big raised letters that were slow to read and heavy to carry. A former army captain had shown the school a code of raised dots meant for soldiers to read at night.

Braille, aged about 15, made it far simpler. His system used just six dots, arranged like the six on a domino, to stand for every letter. That was in 1824. Today braille appears on lift buttons, medicine boxes and railway signs, and blind readers can feel a whole word with one fingertip.

Two girls who refused to give in

In 1955, buses in Montgomery, Alabama, made Black passengers give up seats to white passengers. On 2 March that year, a 15-year-old schoolgirl, Claudette Colvin, refused. She was arrested. Nine months later, Rosa Parks did the same, and her arrest sparked a famous bus boycott. Colvin later became one of the young people whose court case helped end bus segregation.

In 1960, six-year-old Ruby Bridges became the first Black child at William Frantz Elementary, an all-white primary school in New Orleans. Federal officers walked her past angry crowds every day. For most of that year she was the only child in her class.

Marching for the right to be a child

In 1903, many American children worked long hours in cotton mills instead of going to school. The campaigner Mary Harris Jones, known as Mother Jones, led a march of child workers and adults from Philadelphia towards the New York home of President Theodore Roosevelt. He refused to meet them. But newspapers covered the march, and it helped build support for laws against child labour.

In India, the fight against child labour carries on. Kailash Satyarthi, who shared the 2014 Nobel Peace Prize with Malala, has spent decades rescuing children from factories.

A schoolgirl, a blog and a Nobel Prize

Malala Yousafzai grew up in the Swat Valley of Pakistan. At 11, she began writing a blog about life under a group that banned girls from school. In 2012, at 15, she was attacked on her school bus because of her campaign. She survived and kept speaking out. In 2014, at 17, she became the youngest person ever to win a Nobel Prize.

Inventors, climbers and strikers

Some young changemakers break limits. In May 2014, Malavath Purna from a village in Telangana reached the top of Mount Everest at 13. She became the youngest girl to climb it, about 60 years after the first climbers stood there.

In 2018, 15-year-old Greta Thunberg started sitting outside the Swedish parliament on school days, holding a sign about climate change. Within a year, millions of students in many countries had joined school strikes. In 2020, Gitanjali Rao, a 15-year-old Indian-American from Colorado, was named TIME magazine's first Kid of the Year. She had built a small device to test drinking water for lead.

Children have also invented everyday things, from earmuffs to the ice lolly, as the story of inventions by kids shows.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You have to be a grown-up to change anything important.

Truth: Many big changes started with young people. A teenager invented braille, child workers marched against factory work in 1903, and schoolchildren have pushed governments on civil rights and the climate.

Try this

Make a braille name card. Look up the braille alphabet, then push the tip of a pencil into a sheet of paper resting on a folded towel to make raised dots for each letter of your name. Close your eyes and try to feel the letters.
