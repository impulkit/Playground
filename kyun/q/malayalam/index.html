What is unique about Malayalam?

Spell "Malayalam" backwards and you get Malayalam again. It is one of the very few language names that is a palindrome.

The short answer

Malayalam is the language of Kerala and Lakshadweep, spoken as a first language by about 3.5 crore people in India and by many more in the Gulf countries and beyond. It is a Dravidian language, a close relative of Tamil.

A few things make it stand out. Its name is a palindrome. Its script has a large set of letters, with rounded shapes that look like loops and waves. It borrowed so many Sanskrit words that poets once wrote in a blend of the two. And Kerala, where it is spoken, has the highest literacy rate of any big Indian state, so Malayalam has an unusually large reading public.

Surprising but true

In the 1300s, poets in Kerala wrote in a mixture of Malayalam and Sanskrit called Manipravalam, which means "rubies and coral", because the two languages were strung together like beads in a necklace.

Splitting from Tamil

Around 2,000 years ago, people along the Kerala coast spoke an early form of Tamil. The tall Western Ghats mountains separated them from Tamil speakers to the east, and over hundreds of years their speech drifted in its own direction.

The oldest known writing in Malayalam is the Vazhappally copper plate, from about 830 CE. It records a royal grant to a temple. By the 1200s, poems such as Ramacharitam were being written in a language clearly on its way to Malayalam.

Malayalam also traded words with the many visitors who came for Kerala's pepper. Arab, Jewish, Chinese and later Portuguese and Dutch traders landed at ports like Kochi. The Malayalam word for a window, janala, comes from Portuguese.

Rubies and coral

In medieval Kerala, poets loved to mix Malayalam with Sanskrit. They called this style Manipravalam, meaning "rubies and coral". The idea was that red rubies and red coral, strung together, make a beautiful necklace.

In the 1500s, the poet Thunchaththu Ezhuthachan retold the Ramayana and Mahabharata in Malayalam. He is often called the father of modern Malayalam, and his version of the Ramayana is still read aloud in many Kerala homes during the monsoon month of Karkidakam.

Malayalam stories also live on stage. Kathakali, Kerala's famous dance-drama, uses songs in a style close to Manipravalam.

A script full of curves

The Malayalam script comes from Grantha, a script once used to write Sanskrit in South India. It has more than 50 letters in its full set, and many consonants can join together into special combined shapes.

In 1872, a German missionary named Hermann Gundert published a Malayalam-English dictionary that is still respected. In the 1970s, the Kerala government simplified the script so it was easier to print on typewriters. Today you can see both older and newer letter forms on the same street. When computers arrived, volunteers in Kerala worked to bring back the older, more traditional letter shapes in free digital fonts, so that the script could look the way many readers preferred.

A language of readers

In 2013, Malayalam became the fifth language to be declared a classical language by the Government of India. Kerala's 2011 literacy rate was about 94%, the highest among India's states. Newspapers sell in huge numbers, and there are public libraries in villages across the state. Malayalam films are watched far beyond Kerala, often with subtitles. Malayalam writers have won the Jnanpith, India's highest literary award, several times, starting with the poet G. Sankara Kurup, who received the very first one in 1965.

Because so many Keralites work in the Gulf, Malayalam can be heard in shops and offices in Dubai, Doha and Muscat. Each year in late summer, from Kerala to the Gulf, families greet each other at Onam with the words Onashamsakal.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Malayalam is a dialect of Tamil.

Truth: Malayalam began as a western form of early Tamil, but it became a separate language more than a thousand years ago. It has its own script, grammar and literature, and its speakers cannot fully understand Tamil without learning it.
