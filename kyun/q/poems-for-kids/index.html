What are some poems every child should know?

Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star was written in 1806 by Jane Taylor, a young woman in England, and children on every continent still sing it.

The short answer

There is no official list, and every family has its own favourites. But some poems keep turning up in homes and classrooms in India and America, because they are fun to say and easy to remember. A few are silly, a few are brave, and a few are quietly beautiful.

Here are some worth knowing, why they last, and the people who wrote them. Many of them are short enough to learn in a week.

Surprising but true

The word "runcible" in Edward Lear's poem The Owl and the Pussy-cat was made up by Lear. Dictionaries now list it anyway.

Rhymes that start it all

Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star began life as a poem called "The Star" by Jane Taylor, published in England in 1806. It is sung to an old French tune that also carries the ABC song. Most people only know the first verse, but there are five.

Nursery rhymes like this last because of rhythm. A strong beat and rhyming words act like hooks for your memory, which is why you can still sing rhymes you learned before you could read.

Every Indian language has its own first rhymes. Many Hindi-speaking toddlers learn "Machli jal ki rani hai", about a fish who is queen of the water and dies if taken out of it. In Tamil Nadu, small children hear "Nila nila odi vaa", which begs the Moon to come running down to play. Nobody is sure who first made up most of these. They passed from parent to child long before anyone printed them.

Nonsense that makes perfect sense

In 1871 Edward Lear wrote "The Owl and the Pussy-cat", about an owl and a cat who sail away "in a beautiful pea-green boat" and eat with a runcible spoon. Nobody knows exactly what a runcible spoon is, because Lear invented the word.

Bengal has its own king of nonsense. Sukumar Ray filled his 1923 book Abol Tabol with strange creatures, such as a duck crossed with a porcupine. Children still recite it in Kolkata, and his son, the film director Satyajit Ray, grew up surrounded by it. In America, Dr. Seuss and Shel Silverstein carried the same spirit. Silverstein's Where the Sidewalk Ends (1974) is full of poems about kids who refuse to take out the rubbish.

Poems with courage

Subhadra Kumari Chauhan's Hindi poem "Jhansi ki Rani", written in the 1930s, tells the story of Rani Lakshmibai, who fought the British in 1857. Its refrain, "khoob ladi mardani, woh to Jhansi wali rani thi", roughly means "she fought bravely, the queen of Jhansi". Generations of schoolchildren in north India have learned it by heart.

In the United States, Langston Hughes wrote a tiny poem called "Dreams". It asks readers to hold fast to their dreams, because a life without them is "a broken-winged bird that cannot fly".

What makes something a poem?The oldest writer in history whose name we know was a poet, a priestess in Mesopotamia about 4,300 years ago.

Poems that make you think

Rabindranath Tagore wrote "Where the mind is without fear" as part of Gitanjali, the collection that helped him win the Nobel Prize in 1913. It describes a country where knowledge is free and people are not divided by narrow walls. Many Indian schools use it in assemblies.

Robert Frost's "Stopping by Woods on a Snowy Evening" (1923) is about a rider pausing to watch snow fall in a dark wood. It ends by repeating one line, "And miles to go before I sleep". That simple repeat is why so many people remember it.

How to find your own

The best poem for you might not be on anyone's list. Try a library, a poetry app or a grandparent who knows old songs in Tamil, Marathi or Spanish. Read poems aloud, because poetry was spoken long before it was written down. If two lines stay in your head all day, you've found one worth keeping.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ring a Ring o' Roses is secretly about the plague.

Truth: Experts on nursery rhymes have found no evidence for this. The rhyme was first printed in the 1880s, centuries after the great plague, and the plague story only became popular in the twentieth century.

Try this

Pick a short poem you like and read it aloud three times, once whispering, once slowly and once like a news reader. Notice which words you start to stress. Then try learning four lines by heart and say them at dinner without looking.
