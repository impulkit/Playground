What is the Door to Hell?

In the desert of Turkmenistan, a crater as wide as a jumbo jet is long has been on fire for about 50 years.

The short answer

The Door to Hell is a burning hole in the Karakum Desert of Turkmenistan, in Central Asia. Its proper name is the Darvaza gas crater. It is about 60 to 70 metres wide, roughly the length of a jumbo jet, and about 30 metres deep, as deep as a ten-storey building is tall.

Natural gas, mostly methane, leaks up from underground through cracks in the crater's walls and floor. The gas has been on fire for decades. At night, the glow can be seen from far away, and the heat at the rim is strong enough to make you step back.

Tourists camp in yurts nearby to watch it after dark. How it started is a bit of a mystery.

Surprising but true

In 2013, an explorer climbed down to the floor of the burning crater and found bacteria living in the hot soil.

The most famous story

The usual tale goes like this. In 1971, Soviet engineers were drilling in the desert, looking for oil and gas. Their drilling rig sat on top of a hollow space underground. The ground gave way, and the rig fell into a crater.

Gas began pouring out. Methane on its own is not poisonous, but gas fields often contain other harmful gases, and a cloud of them could choke people and animals. So, the story says, the engineers decided to set it alight and let it burn off. They expected the fire to burn out within weeks.

It didn't. The gas field below is huge, and it kept feeding the flames.

Some geologists in Turkmenistan tell it differently. They say the crater formed in the 1960s and was only lit in the 1980s. Old records are missing, so nobody can say for sure.

A trip to the bottom

In 2013, the Canadian explorer George Kourounis became the first known person to reach the crater floor. Wearing a heat-reflecting suit and breathing equipment, he was lowered on ropes into the fire.

He collected soil samples from the bottom. Back in a laboratory, scientists found bacteria living in that hot, gassy soil. None of them were found in the surrounding desert. It was a small sign of how tough life can be. Scientists who search for life on other planets were interested, because it shows microbes can survive in places that look hopeless.

Why not just put it out?

Turkmenistan has some of the largest natural gas reserves in the world, the fuel that formed from ancient life just like coal and oil. A fire wastes gas that could be sold. In 2022 the country's leader ordered officials to find a way to end the fire.

But simply smothering the flames would cause a different problem. Unburned methane would then leak into the air. Methane traps far more heat than carbon dioxide over a few decades, so it is a big worry for climate change.

Instead, engineers drilled wells nearby to pull gas out before it reached the crater. In 2025 officials said the fire had shrunk to about a third of its earlier size. One analysis of heat measurements found the fire's heat had dropped by more than three-quarters in three years, though small pockets of flame were still burning in late 2025.

Fires that won't go out

The Door to Hell is not the only fire that has burned for years. In Jharia, in Jharkhand, coal seams underground have been burning since around 1916. In the US, a coal fire under the town of Centralia, Pennsylvania, has smouldered since 1962, and almost everyone moved away.

Some natural gas fires are far older. In Turkey, flames called the Chimaera have flickered from rocks on a hillside since ancient times. And the strangest part? India has its own everlasting flames, which burn from the rock at the Jwalamukhi temple in Himachal Pradesh and are honoured as sacred.

Myth or fact?

Myth: We know for certain that Soviet engineers lit the crater in 1971 and expected it to burn for only a few weeks.

Truth: That is the most popular story, but records are missing. Some geologists from Turkmenistan say the crater collapsed in the 1960s and was only set alight in the 1980s.

Try this

Look at the flame on a gas stove with an adult. Notice its blue colour and how steady it is. That blue flame is burning methane, the same gas that feeds the Door to Hell, just on a much smaller scale.
