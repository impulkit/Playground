Was there a lost river called Saraswati?

An ancient hymn praises a mighty river flowing to the sea. Today its likely bed is a dry, sandy strip, kilometres wide, across Rajasthan.

The short answer

The Rigveda, one of the oldest texts composed in India, praises a great river called Saraswati that flows from the mountains to the sea. One verse calls her the best of mothers, the best of rivers and the best of goddesses. Later texts say the river disappears into the desert. Today no river by that name reaches any sea.

Many researchers link it to the Ghaggar-Hakra, a river in Haryana, Rajasthan and Pakistan that flows mostly in the monsoon. Its dry bed is in places several kilometres wide, much too wide for the trickle it carries now. So something big once flowed there. The arguments are about when, and what fed it.

Surprising but true

A river now seen only in the monsoon has a dry bed that in places is several kilometres wide, far too big for the water it carries today.

A river that vanished in the stories

In the Mahabharata, Balarama travels along the Saraswati on a long pilgrimage. The epic names a place called Vinashana, meaning "the vanishing", where the river sinks into the sand. That detail matters. Even 2,000 years ago, storytellers seem to have known a river that had shrunk.

Many Hindus today believe the Saraswati still flows unseen, underground. In this belief, it joins the Ganga and the Yamuna at the Triveni Sangam in Prayagraj, the meeting point where the Kumbh Mela gathers crowds bigger than most countries. For believers, the river is real whether or not a map shows it.

Clues seen from space

Around 1980, a team including the scientist Yash Pal studied pictures from the American Landsat satellites. They traced long, pale bands across the Thar region: the ghosts of old river channels, buried under sand and fields. Later work by ISRO mapped these channels in more detail.

Some wells dug along the old courses strike water deep underground. The land itself seems to remember where the river ran.

The towns on its banks

Here is the twist that made archaeologists sit up. Hundreds of settlements of the Harappan civilisation lie along the Ghaggar-Hakra and its old branches. At Kalibangan in Rajasthan, diggers found a ploughed field about 4,800 years old, its furrows still visible in the soil. Rakhigarhi in Haryana, one of the biggest Harappan sites ever found, lies on the same plain.

People do not build towns in a waterless desert. So during Harappan times, roughly 4,600 to 3,900 years ago, this valley must have had water and good farmland.

What the scientists argue about

A 2012 study led by Liviu Giosan suggested that, in Harappan times, the river was fed by monsoon rain, not by Himalayan glaciers. In 2017, researchers from IIT Kanpur and Imperial College London found that the mighty Sutlej had once run down this valley, then shifted west about 8,000 years ago. The cities grew later, beside a gentler river that flooded less fiercely.

Other scientists argue that glacier water reached the valley for longer than that. Most agree that the monsoon weakened around 4,200 years ago and the river dried, around the time many Harappan towns emptied.

The argument today

Matching a poem to a riverbed is tricky, because poets do not draw maps. Some historians are sure the Ghaggar-Hakra is the Saraswati of the Vedic age. Others say the hymns may describe a different river, or a memory blended from several. The Haryana government has even dug channels to bring water back along parts of the old course.

There is one more problem. The Rigveda was composed over centuries and passed down by memory long before anyone wrote it down, so nobody can pin its hymns to an exact year. Without a firm date, it is hard to say which version of the river the poets saw.

And rivers still wander. The Kosi in Bihar has shifted more than 100 kilometres west in about two centuries. It is a reminder that the rivers tumbling out of the Himalaya never promise to stay put.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Saraswati is only a legend, with no real river behind it.

Truth: A wide, dry riverbed really does run across north-west India and into Pakistan, and many ancient towns sat beside it. Whether it is the river of the hymns is the part people still debate.
