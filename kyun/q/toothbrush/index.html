What did people use before toothbrushes?

Long before plastic toothbrushes, people across India chewed neem twigs every morning, and many still do.

The short answer

For thousands of years, people cleaned their teeth with chew sticks. They chewed the end of a twig until it frayed into soft bristles, then rubbed it over their teeth. In India this is called datun, and it is often made from neem or babool twigs. In the Middle East and Africa, people use miswak twigs from a shrub called the toothbrush tree.

People also used powders made from crushed shells, ash, salt or charcoal, rubbed on with a finger or a cloth. The bristle toothbrush came later, first recorded in China, and spread worldwide only in the last few hundred years.

Surprising but true

One of the first mass-produced toothbrushes in England was dreamed up in a prison cell around 1780, using a bone and some bristles.

Twigs with a bite

A good chew stick is more than a scrubber. Neem twigs are bitter, and neem contains substances that may slow the growth of some mouth bacteria. Miswak sticks have natural fibres and a little fluoride.

Chewing the end of the stick makes a brush that reaches between teeth. In many Indian villages, people still start the day with a fresh datun. Studies suggest chew sticks can clean teeth fairly well when used properly, though dentists say a toothbrush with fluoride toothpaste is more reliable.

Powders and pastes

Ancient Egyptians mixed tooth powders from ash, crushed eggshells and myrrh, among other things. Ancient Greek and Roman writers describe cleaning teeth with crushed bones and shells.

In India, people used powders of charcoal, salt and herbs. Some of these old powders were very rough and could scratch teeth. Modern toothpaste is gentler and contains fluoride, which makes tooth enamel stronger. Fluoride toothpaste became common in the second half of the 20th century.

The brush arrives

Chinese brush makers were setting pig bristles into handles of bone or bamboo by the 1400s. Travellers carried the idea to Europe, where some people used brushes of horsehair or feathers instead.

In England, around 1780, a man named William Addis was in prison. The story goes that he saved a small bone from a meal, drilled tiny holes in it and tied bristles into them. After his release, he started a company making toothbrushes. It went on to sell them for well over a century.

Plastic takes over

Animal bristles were stiff but soaked up water and held on to germs. In 1938 an American company began selling toothbrushes with nylon bristles, a new plastic. Nylon dried quickly and lasted longer.

During World War II, American soldiers were told to brush their teeth every day, and they brought the habit home. Today almost everyone uses a plastic toothbrush, which creates a problem of its own: billions are thrown away each year. Some people now choose bamboo handles to cut down on plastic.

Why we brush at all

Your mouth is home to many bacteria. They feed on sugar and starch left on your teeth and make acids that eat into enamel, creating holes called cavities.

Brushing sweeps away the sticky film of bacteria before it can do much harm. Dentists recommend brushing gently for about two minutes, twice a day, with a pea-sized amount of fluoride toothpaste. It takes only a few minutes a day, but it helps keep your teeth for life.

Old habits, new ideas

Some old ideas are being looked at again. Researchers have tested neem and miswak extracts in mouthwashes and toothpastes, with mixed results. Electric toothbrushes now buzz thousands of times a minute. The tools keep changing, but the morning ritual of cleaning your teeth is one of the oldest habits people share.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Brushing harder gets your teeth cleaner.

Truth: Scrubbing hard can wear away enamel and hurt your gums. Dentists recommend a soft brush, gentle circles and about two minutes, twice a day.

Try this

Put an eggshell in a glass of vinegar and another in a glass of plain water. Leave them overnight. Eggshell is made of calcium, a bit like tooth enamel. In the morning, compare the two shells to see what acid can do to a hard surface over time.
