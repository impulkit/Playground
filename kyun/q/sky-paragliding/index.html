How do paragliders stay up so long?

A paraglider has no engine at all, yet skilled pilots have flown more than 550 km in a single day, farther than from Delhi to Jaipur and back.

The short answer

A paraglider is a soft fabric wing with no engine. In still air, it always sinks slowly, like a paper plane. Pilots stay up by finding air that rises faster than they sink.

The main sources are thermals, columns of warm air rising from sun-heated ground, and ridge lift, where wind hits a hill and is pushed upwards. Ride a strong thermal and you can climb hundreds of metres in minutes. Glide to the next one, climb again, and keep going. Good pilots stay up for hours and travel hundreds of kilometres in a day.

Surprising but true

Bir Billing in Himachal Pradesh is one of the world's best-known paragliding spots, and it hosted the Paragliding World Cup in 2015.

A wing made of cloth

A paraglider wing is made of thin, strong nylon, often about 25 m² in area, roughly the size of a small room. It weighs only about 4 to 6 kg and can be folded into a backpack.

The wing is made of many tubes called cells, open at the front. As the pilot runs down a slope, air rushes into the cells and inflates the wing into a firm, curved shape. Hundreds of thin lines connect it to the pilot's harness. The curved top makes air flow faster over the wing than under it, which creates lift, just like on an aeroplane.

Gliding, not falling

In calm air, a typical paraglider moves about 8 to 10 m forward for every 1 m it drops. Launch from a hill 1,000 m high and, with no rising air at all, you could glide roughly 8 to 10 km before landing. That is the distance of a long autorickshaw ride across a city.

Pilots steer by pulling on brake lines that bend the back edge of the wing. Pull the left brake and you turn left.

Hunting for thermals

When the Sun heats the ground, some patches warm faster than others: ploughed fields, rocky slopes, car parks. Air above them warms, gets lighter and rises in a bubble or column.

Pilots can't see thermals, so they look for clues. A puffy cumulus cloud often forms at the top of one. Dust swirls, rising leaves and circling birds give it away too. Vultures are experts. They can soar all day hardly flapping their wings, and pilots often share thermals with them.

A small device called a variometer beeps faster as the pilot climbs. The pilot then circles tightly to stay inside the rising air.

Ridges and long flights

On windy days, pilots fly along hills and cliffs. Wind blowing against the slope gets pushed up, and the glider rides that rising air like a surfer on a wave, sometimes for hours.

The longest flights happen over flat, hot land with strong, steady thermals and wind behind the pilot. In northeast Brazil and in Texas, pilots have covered more than 550 km in one day. That is farther than a straight line from Bengaluru to Hyderabad.

Bir Billing and beyond

India has some excellent flying sites. At Bir Billing in Himachal Pradesh, pilots launch from a meadow at about 2,400 m and land in Bir, about 1,000 m lower. The site hosted the Paragliding World Cup in 2015. Kamshet near Pune and the hills of Sikkim are popular too.

Many visitors try tandem flights, sitting in front of a trained pilot on a double harness.

The danger in the clouds

Thermals can also turn dangerous. Under a big thundercloud, the air rises so fast that a glider can be sucked up. In 2007, a German pilot, Ewa Wiśniewska, was pulled to nearly 10,000 m during a competition in Australia. She passed out in the freezing, thin air and woke up covered in ice, still flying. She survived and landed safely.

That is why pilots study the weather before every flight and avoid towering storm clouds. Learning to read the sky is half of flying, and the shapes of clouds tell pilots more than any weather app.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A paraglider is just a parachute that floats slowly down.

Truth: A parachute is built to fall slowly. A paraglider is a true wing. It moves forward and creates lift, so a pilot can steer, glide for many kilometres and even climb thousands of metres in rising air.

Try this

See warm air rise. With an adult, hold a tissue strip or a light feather a little above a radiator, a sunny window sill or a mug of hot water (not touching it). Watch it lift and flutter. That rising air is a tiny thermal, the same kind of lift a paraglider rides.
