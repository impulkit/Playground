What happens to your body when it is freezing?

When you get very cold, your body quietly decides that your fingers and toes matter less than your heart and brain, and starts sending your warm blood inward.

The short answer

Your body works best at about 37 °C on the inside, and it has clever ways of keeping that temperature steady. When you get cold, it fights to protect that temperature. First, blood vessels in your skin narrow so that less heat escapes. Then your muscles start shivering to make extra heat. You may also get goosebumps and feel the urge to curl up.

If you stay cold for too long, those defences can't keep up. Your inside temperature starts to drop. Below 35 °C, doctors call it hypothermia, and it is a medical emergency.

Surprising but true

Shivering is your muscles working hard without moving you anywhere. Strong shivering can make several times as much heat as sitting still.

Saving the middle

Your brain has a built-in thermostat, part of a region called the hypothalamus. When sensors in your skin report cold, it sends out orders. The first is to squeeze the small blood vessels near your skin.

This is why cold hands look pale or even bluish. Your body is choosing to keep warm blood around your heart, lungs and brain and let your fingers, toes, nose and ears cool down. It is a smart trade, but it is also why those parts are the first to get numb, clumsy and, in very harsh cold, frostbitten.

Shivering and goosebumps

If narrowing the blood vessels isn't enough, your muscles start to shiver. They tighten and relax many times a second. All that work produces heat, and strong shivering can raise heat production several times above normal. It also burns through energy fast, which is one reason people get very hungry in the cold.

Goosebumps come from tiny muscles that pull each hair upright. In furry animals, raised hair traps a thicker layer of warm air. Humans have too little body hair for that to help much, so goosebumps are a leftover from our hairy ancestors.

Babies have a special tissue called brown fat that burns energy to make heat without shivering. Adults keep a little of it, mostly around the neck and shoulders.

When cold becomes dangerous

Mild hypothermia makes people shiver hard, feel tired and fumble with simple things. As the body cools further, shivering may actually stop, speech becomes slurred and the person gets confused. That is why mountaineers and soldiers are trained to watch each other for these signs.

Frostbite happens when skin and the flesh beneath it freeze. Ice crystals form inside the tissue and damage it. Indian Army soldiers on the Siachen Glacier, where winter temperatures can drop below −40 °C, wear special boots, gloves and face masks to avoid it. In places like Alaska and Minnesota, schools sometimes close on days when the wind chill is dangerously low.

If someone seems badly chilled, the right thing to do is get them somewhere warm and dry and tell an adult straight away, so that help can be called.

Why wind and wet make it worse

Still air is a good insulator. Wind blows away the thin layer of warm air next to your skin and replaces it with cold air, which is why a windy day feels colder than the thermometer says. Weather forecasters call this "wind chill".

Water is even worse. It pulls heat out of the body about 25 times faster than air at the same temperature. Wet clothes or a fall into icy water can chill someone very quickly.

Dressing for the cold

Layers work better than one thick coat, because each layer traps air. A thin inner layer keeps sweat off your skin, a fluffy middle layer holds warm air, and an outer layer stops wind and rain. People in Ladakh and other cold places have known this for centuries, wearing thick woollen robes and felt boots. Warm drinks and regular meals help too, because your body needs fuel to keep its fire burning.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You lose most of your body heat through your head.

Truth: Your head loses heat at about the same rate as any other uncovered part of your body. A hat helps because your head is often the only part left uncovered, not because it is special.

Try this

Hold an ice cube in a napkin against the back of your hand for 30 seconds, then look at the skin. It will probably look paler, because the blood vessels there have narrowed. Watch how long it takes for the normal colour to come back as the skin warms up.
