How does Bharatanatyam tell a story?

One dancer, no costume changes, and a whole story with gods, demons and a lovesick heroine. All on one small stage.

The short answer

Bharatanatyam tells stories through abhinaya, the art of expression. A dancer acts out the words of a song with her face, eyes and hands, while her feet keep a precise rhythm. One dancer can play every character, switching from a god to a demon to a worried mother in a heartbeat.

The dance comes from Tamil Nadu in southern India. It is famous for its sharp lines and its half-sitting position, called araimandi, with knees bent out to the sides. A live group of musicians plays Carnatic music as the dancer performs, often for an hour or more.

Surprising but true

Bharatanatyam got its name less than a hundred years ago. Before the 1930s, the dance was mostly known as sadir.

Steps in a set order

A Bharatanatyam concert usually follows a set order called the margam. This order was shaped in the early 1800s by four brothers known as the Thanjavur Quartet, musicians at the court of the Maratha king of Thanjavur.

It begins with alarippu, a short warm-up that wakes up the eyes, neck, arms and feet. Pure dance pieces follow. The centrepiece is the varnam, often the longest item, mixing fast footwork with long passages of storytelling. Slower pieces called padams are mostly acting. The concert ends with the tillana, a bright burst of rhythm.

Telling a story with no words

Here is how a dancer might show a single line about the god Krishna stealing butter. Her hands become a pot. Her eyes dart left and right, checking that nobody is watching. She dips her fingers in and licks them, grinning.

Then she switches. Now she is Krishna's mother, Yashoda, hands on hips, eyebrows raised. A moment later she is Krishna again, eyes wide and innocent. The song may repeat the same line many times, and each time the dancer shows a new shade of meaning. Audiences who know the stories enjoy spotting each fresh idea.

Carved in stone

Around 1010 CE, the Chola king Rajaraja I completed the Brihadisvara Temple in Thanjavur. Its walls carry carvings of dance poses described in the ancient Natya Shastra. At the Nataraja temple in Chidambaram, the gateway towers show all 108 of these poses, called karanas.

These carvings show how close dance and temple life once were. Dancers today still visit these temples to study the poses.

A dance with two histories

For centuries, the dance was kept alive mainly by women called devadasis, who served in temples and danced at courts. In the early 1900s, reformers attacked the devadasi system, and temple dancing was later banned by law in the Madras Presidency.

Rukmini Devi Arundale learned the dance and helped rename and reshape it as Bharatanatyam. In 1936, she founded Kalakshetra in Chennai. Others, like the great hereditary dancer T. Balasaraswati, argued that the old traditions should be kept closer to their roots. Historians still discuss both sides today: one view says the revival saved the dance, another that it pushed out the families who had carried it.

Bells, cymbals and a busy December

A Bharatanatyam dancer wears salangai, strings of small bells tied around the ankles. A leader called the nattuvanar keeps time with small cymbals and calls out rhythm syllables, like "tha dhi thom nam". The dancer's feet answer, sound for sound.

Every December, Chennai hosts a huge season of music and dance, with hundreds of concerts in a few weeks. Bharatanatyam is also one of the most popular dances among Indian-American children, many of whom train for years for their arangetram. It is one of eight classical dances recognised in India.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Bharatanatyam has always been called by that name.

Truth: For a long time, the dance was called sadir or dasi attam. The name Bharatanatyam, linking it to the ancient sage Bharata, became popular in the 1930s.

Try this

Choose a short story you know well, such as a crow dropping pebbles into a pot. Act it out without words, using only your face and hands. Play the crow, then the pot, then the water. Ask a friend to guess each character as you switch.
