How are video games made?

A big video game can take hundreds of people several years to make, and every frame you see is redrawn about 60 times a second.

The short answer

A video game is a computer program, plus art, sound and a lot of careful design. Writers and designers decide what the game is about and what makes it fun. Artists draw characters and worlds. Sound designers and composers make music and noises. Programmers write the code that ties it all together.

Then comes testing, and lots of it. People play unfinished versions again and again, looking for bugs, confusing bits and boring parts. A small phone game might take a few people a few months. A huge adventure game can take hundreds of people several years.

Surprising but true

One of the first video games was made in 1958 by a physicist who wanted to make visitors' day at his science laboratory less boring. It was a tennis game played on a lab screen.

The game loop

Inside almost every game is a short set of steps that repeats very fast. Programmers call it the game loop. First, the game checks what you pressed. Next, it updates the world: the car moves forward a little, the enemy turns, a coin spins. Then it draws everything on the screen.

That loop usually runs 30 to 60 times a second. Each picture it draws is called a frame, made of millions of tiny coloured dots called pixels. Because the frames change so quickly, your brain sees smooth movement, the same trick that makes cartoons work.

How are cartoons made?Every blink of a cartoon character is drawn or moved by someone, one frame at a time.

Engines and building blocks

Few teams start from nothing. Most use a game engine, a big toolkit of ready-made code. An engine already knows how to draw 3D scenes, play sounds, make objects fall and bounce, and work on different machines. The team then adds its own world and rules on top.

Inside the game, characters are often moved by small programs. A guard in a stealth game might follow a simple rule: walk this path, and if you see the player, chase them. Clever games mix lots of simple rules like that, and players experience it as a character that seems to think.

Games also need physics, the rules for how things move. A ball must fall, bounce and roll in a way that feels right. Programmers write simple versions of real laws of motion, then often bend them. Many game characters can jump far higher than any athlete, because that is more fun.

A tennis game in a lab

In 1958, a physicist named William Higinbotham worked at Brookhaven National Laboratory in New York. Each year the lab held a visitors' day, and he thought the displays were dull. So he wired up a lab screen to show a bouncing ball and a net, with two controllers. He called it Tennis for Two. People queued to play it.

In 1972 the company Atari released Pong, a simple bat-and-ball game, in arcades across the United States. It was a giant hit and helped start the games industry. Home consoles that plugged into the family television soon followed. Early games had to fit into tiny amounts of memory, so their makers became experts at squeezing a whole world into a few simple shapes and beeps.

Games from India

India now has its own game studios, and hundreds of millions of people in India play games, mostly on phones. Some Indian games draw on stories and settings from home, such as myths, cricket and city streets.

Many popular games also borrow from much older ones. Snakes and ladders, carrom and chess, which grew from the Indian game chaturanga, all have digital versions. A good game, old or new, still needs the same thing: rules that are simple to learn and hard to master.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Video games are mostly played by children.

Truth: Surveys by the Entertainment Software Association find that the average American player is an adult in their thirties. In India too, millions of adults play games, mostly on their phones.

Try this

Design a level on paper. Draw a simple map with a start, a goal, three obstacles and one secret. Then give it to a friend or family member to "play" by tracing a route with a finger while you explain the rules. Watch where they get stuck. That is exactly what game testers do.
