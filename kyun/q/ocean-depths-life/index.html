How do animals survive in the deepest sea?

At the bottom of the deepest ocean trench, the water presses down with the weight of a small car on every square centimetre, and small shrimp-like animals still scuttle about.

The short answer

The deep sea is cold, dark and under enormous pressure. Sunlight fades away within the first few hundred metres, so below about 1,000 metres it is pitch black forever. The water is close to freezing. Food is scarce, because nothing grows without light.

Yet the deep is full of life, and the animals there have found clever ways to cope. Their bodies are soft and watery, so pressure doesn't crush them. Many make their own light. They save energy by moving slowly, and they eat whatever falls from above, or get food from bacteria that live on chemicals instead of sunlight.

Surprising but true

The deepest fish ever filmed was a small, pale snailfish swimming at 8,336 metres below the surface off Japan in 2022.

Pressure that doesn't crush

Every 10 metres you go down, the weight of water above adds about one more "atmosphere" of pressure. At the bottom of Challenger Deep, in the Mariana Trench, it is more than 1,000 atmospheres. That's like having a small car resting on each square centimetre of your skin.

Humans can only go there inside thick metal spheres. A fish is a different story. Its body is mostly water, which hardly squeezes, so the pressure inside it matches the pressure outside. Deep-sea fish also make a special chemical called TMAO that helps their proteins keep their shape. Some scientists think fish can't live much below about 8,200 to 8,400 metres, because they can't pack in enough of this chemical.

Food from above and below

Most deep animals live on "marine snow", a steady drizzle of dead plankton, droppings and other scraps sinking from the sunlit waters. It can take weeks to reach the bottom. Animals there have huge mouths, stretchy stomachs and slow lives, so they can survive on rare meals.

When a whale dies and sinks, it becomes a feast called a whale fall. Hagfish, crabs, worms and bacteria can feed on a single whale skeleton for decades.

In a few places, there is another food source. Around hot springs on the sea floor, called vents, bacteria use chemicals from the Earth to make food without sunlight. Tubeworms, crabs and shrimp crowd around them, as busy as any coral reef.

Making light in the dark

In the deep, many animals glow. This light, called bioluminescence, is made by chemical reactions in their bodies. Some use it to find mates. Some use it to scare off attackers or confuse them with a flash.

The anglerfish dangles a glowing lure in front of its mouth, like a fishing rod with a lamp. Small fish swim closer to look, and the anglerfish snaps them up. Other fish have glowing spots on their bellies that match the faint light from above, so hunters below can't see their outline.

The deepest fish

In 2022, a team from Australia and Japan lowered cameras into the Izu-Ogasawara Trench south of Japan. At 8,336 metres, they filmed a young snailfish, a small, pale fish with a big head and a body like soft jelly. It is the deepest fish ever recorded on video.

Snailfish look fragile, but that softness is their strength. They have no hard scales and no air bladder. Their skeletons are only partly hardened, so their bodies flex with the pressure instead of fighting it.

Down to the very bottom

In 1960, Jacques Piccard and Don Walsh became the first people to reach Challenger Deep, in a craft called the Trieste. Through the window, they reported what they thought was a flatfish, though many scientists now doubt it. The film-maker James Cameron dived there alone in 2012.

Robotic landers and crewed dives since then have found life all the way to the bottom. There are amphipods the size of a thumb, sea cucumbers drifting over the mud, and giant single-celled creatures called xenophyophores. Scientists think most deep-sea species have never been seen by anyone at all.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Nothing can live at the very bottom of the deepest ocean trench.

Truth: Explorers have found life even in Challenger Deep, nearly 11 km down. Tiny shrimp-like amphipods, sea cucumbers, single-celled creatures and many kinds of microbe live there, feeding on scraps that drift down from above.

Try this

Fill one plastic bottle to the very top with water and screw the lid on tight. Leave a second bottle empty with the lid on. Squeeze both. The air-filled bottle crumples, but the water-filled one barely gives, which is why watery bodies cope with the deep.
