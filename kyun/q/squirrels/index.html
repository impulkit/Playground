How did the Indian squirrel get its stripes?

A much-loved Indian story says Lord Rama stroked a little squirrel and left three stripes on its back. Scientists have a different explanation.

The short answer

The small striped squirrels you see across India are palm squirrels. Their stripes are part of their fur pattern, passed down from parents to babies through their genes, just like a tiger's stripes or a zebra's.

Scientists think the stripes help squirrels blend in. Light and dark lines break up the outline of the body, so a squirrel on a branch dappled with sunlight is harder for a hawk or a cat to spot.

But many Indian children first hear a different answer, from a story. It has nothing to do with genes, and a lot to do with kindness.

Surprising but true

Some of the oaks and walnut trees in American forests were planted by grey squirrels, which bury nuts to eat later and never come back for a share of them.

The story of the helpful squirrel

In the Ramayana, Rama's army of monkeys and bears builds a bridge across the sea to Lanka, to rescue Sita. In one much-loved retelling, a little squirrel wants to help too.

It rolls in the sand on the beach, runs onto the bridge and shakes the sand off between the stones. It does this again and again. Some of the monkeys laugh at such a tiny helper.

Rama picks up the squirrel and gently strokes its back with three fingers. The marks of his fingers become its stripes. The story is told to show that even the smallest effort matters. It isn't in every version of the Ramayana, but it is told in homes across India.

Three stripes or five

There are two common kinds. The three-striped palm squirrel lives mostly in southern India and Sri Lanka. The five-striped palm squirrel is more common in the north, from Gujarat to Bengal, and also in Nepal and Pakistan.

Both kinds have three bold pale stripes down the back. The five-striped squirrel has two extra fainter stripes along its sides. Look closely at the one on your wall or mango tree and count.

Palm squirrels are busy in the daytime. They eat seeds, fruit, flowers and insects, and they often live close to people. Their high, chattering call, often with a flick of the tail, warns others of a cat or a crow.

India's giant squirrels

India also has squirrels that look nothing like the little striped ones. The Indian giant squirrel lives in forests in the Western Ghats and central India. Its fur is a mix of maroon, brown and cream, and from nose to tail tip it can be about a metre long, as long as a cricket bat.

It is the state animal of Maharashtra, where it is called shekru. It rarely comes to the ground and can leap about 6 m between trees.

India has flying squirrels too. They don't really fly. They stretch out folds of skin between their legs and glide from tree to tree at night.

The gardeners of America

In much of the USA, the squirrel everyone knows is the eastern grey squirrel. It has no stripes. Each autumn it buries thousands of acorns and nuts, one at a time, in shallow holes.

Scientists once thought squirrels just sniffed around for nuts in winter. Experiments show they remember many of their hiding places and use smell to find the exact spot. Some squirrels even pretend to bury a nut when another animal is watching, then hide it somewhere else.

Still, some buried nuts are never dug up. They sprout into seedlings, and a few grow into oak and walnut trees. In this way squirrels help forests spread, which makes them some of the busiest tree planters around.

Unlike chipmunks and ground squirrels, tree squirrels don't hibernate. On cold days they curl up in a leafy nest called a drey and wait for better weather.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Squirrels forget where they hide almost all of their food.

Truth: Studies of grey squirrels show they remember many of their hiding places, sometimes for weeks. They do lose some nuts, and that small share is what helps forests spread.

Try this

Sit quietly near a tree in a park or garden for ten minutes and watch for squirrels. Count how often one stops, flicks its tail or makes a sharp chirp. These are warning signals to other squirrels, and you may be the thing they are warning about.
