Who invented the crossword?

The first crossword appeared in a New York newspaper in December 1913, shaped like a diamond and called a "word-cross".

The short answer

The crossword was invented by Arthur Wynne, a journalist from Liverpool who had moved to the United States. On 21 December 1913, he needed a new puzzle for the Christmas edition of the New York World's Sunday fun section. He drew a diamond-shaped grid, wrote a list of clues and called it a "word-cross".

A few weeks later, a typesetting slip swapped the words, and "cross-word" stuck. Within about ten years, the puzzle had spread across America and to Britain, and soon to newspapers all over the world, including India.

Surprising but true

In the 1920s, crosswords were so popular in the US that some people called the craze a waste of time, and the New York Times grumbled about them for years before starting its own puzzle in 1942.

The first puzzle

Wynne's grid had no black squares. The words simply crossed in a diamond around an empty middle. The very first clue was "What bargain hunters enjoy", and the answer was SALES. Most clues were simple, though one asked for "the fibre of the gomuti palm". The answer was DOH, which hardly anyone knew.

Readers wrote in asking for more. Wynne kept making them, and soon readers were sending in their own puzzles too. The grids changed shape over the next few years, from diamonds to squares, and black squares appeared to separate the words.

The craze of the 1920s

In 1924, two young men named Richard Simon and Max Schuster published a book of crosswords. It came with a pencil attached. It sold so well that it launched their publishing company, Simon & Schuster, which still exists.

Soon crosswords were everywhere. Dictionaries sold out. Train passengers filled in puzzles on their way to work. Some newspapers complained the craze was making people lazy. The New York Times called crosswords a "sinful waste" in 1924, then started its own Sunday puzzle in 1942, during the Second World War. People at the paper argued that readers needed something to take their minds off the bad news.

How a grid is built

Most modern American crosswords follow neat rules. The grid is usually 15 squares by 15 on weekdays. Every white square belongs to two words, one across and one down. The pattern of black squares looks the same if you turn the grid upside down. That's called rotational symmetry.

The setter usually starts with a few long theme answers, then fills the gaps. This is the hard part. Each new word has to fit letters already placed, which is a bit like building a tower of cards where every card touches four others. Many setters now use computer word lists to help, though the clues are still written by people.

Cryptic crosswords and Indian puzzles

British newspapers developed the cryptic crossword, where each clue is a little riddle. A clue might hide the answer inside other words, or scramble letters in an anagram. For example, "Silent, mixed up, will pay attention (6)" points to LISTEN, because the letters of SILENT can be rearranged into it.

Indian English newspapers, such as The Hindu, have run cryptic crosswords for decades, and puzzles also appear in Hindi, Tamil, Bengali and other languages. A crossword in Hindi is trickier than it looks, because one letter of Devanagari can carry a vowel sign too.

Why people keep solving

A crossword gives you small wins, one word at a time, and each answer helps with the next. Puzzles like sudoku work in a similar way with numbers. Solvers also pick up odd words: setters love short words full of vowels, like ERA, ALOE and OREO.

Researchers have studied whether puzzles protect the brain as people age. The results are mixed. Puzzles certainly make people better at puzzles, and that alone has kept crosswords in newspapers for more than a hundred years.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Crosswords are an ancient puzzle, as old as word games themselves.

Truth: Word squares, where words read the same across and down, go back to Roman times. But the crossword with clues and a grid of crossing answers was invented in 1913 by Arthur Wynne.

Try this

Make a mini crossword for a friend. Draw a 5-by-5 grid on squared paper, fit in four or five words that cross each other, and shade the empty boxes black. Write one clue for each word, then swap puzzles and race.
