Who was Jyotirao Phule?

In 1873 Jyotirao Phule of Pune dedicated his book Gulamgiri, meaning Slavery, to Americans who had fought to end slavery in their own country.

The short answer

Jyotirao Phule, often called Mahatma Phule or Jyotiba, was a social reformer, writer and thinker from Pune in the 1800s. He fought against caste discrimination, the unfair treatment of people based on the community they were born into. He also worked for the education of girls and of children who had been denied schooling.

With his wife Savitribai Phule, he opened a school for girls in 1848. In 1873 he founded a society called the Satyashodhak Samaj, or Truth-Seekers' Society, to work for equal rights.

Surprising but true

Phule opened the water tank at his own house to people who were not allowed to use the village wells, at a time when this shocked many of his neighbours.

A gardener's son

Jyotirao was born in 1827 in a family of gardeners and flower sellers. The family name Phule comes from the Marathi word for flowers. His mother died when he was a baby, and his father, Govindrao, brought him up.

He went to a Scottish mission school in Pune, where he did well and read widely. One book that moved him was Rights of Man by Thomas Paine, an English-American writer who argued that all people are born with equal rights. Jyotirao began to ask why Indian society treated people so unequally.

A turning point

A story he told later describes a moment in 1848. He joined a friend's wedding procession, but some guests insulted him because of his caste and told him to leave. He was hurt and angry, and he decided to spend his life fighting such unfairness.

He believed education was the key. That same year, he and Savitribai began teaching girls. Soon they opened schools for children from communities that had been kept out of classrooms. Many people opposed them, and at one point they had to leave his father's home.

The couple also took on problems few others would touch. Young widows at the time were often shunned, and some were driven to despair. In 1863 the Phules opened a home where such women could give birth in safety. Jyotirao also spoke up for widows' right to remarry, which many people then considered shameful.

Slavery, here and there

In 1873 Phule published Gulamgiri, meaning Slavery. It argued that the caste system kept millions of Indians in a kind of bondage. Phule had followed news of the American fight against slavery, which had ended with the Civil War only eight years earlier.

He dedicated the book to the good people of the United States, praising those who had worked to free enslaved people. It was an unusual link to draw at the time, between struggles on opposite sides of the world.

Truth-seekers and farmers

The Satyashodhak Samaj, founded the same year, welcomed people of all castes and religions. It encouraged simple weddings without expensive priests and pushed for schooling for all. Its members held meetings in villages and wrote in plain Marathi so farmers could follow their ideas. Its members spread across Maharashtra.

Phule also wrote about farmers, who were often deep in debt. His book Shetkaryacha Asud, or The Cultivator's Whipcord, described how hard their lives were. He once opened the water tank at his house to people who had been refused water from public wells.

How he is remembered

In 1888, at a public meeting in Bombay, he was given the title Mahatma, meaning "great soul". He died in Pune on 28 November 1890. After his death, Savitribai carried on leading the Satyashodhak Samaj.

His ideas shaped later leaders. B.R. Ambedkar, who helped write India's Constitution, named Phule as one of his three great teachers, along with the Buddha and the poet-saint Kabir. Today schools, universities, roads and markets across Maharashtra carry his name, including Mahatma Phule Mandai, a famous old market in Pune. His birthday, 11 April, is marked across India.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Mahatma Gandhi was the first Indian to be called "Mahatma".

Truth: Jyotirao Phule was given the title Mahatma at a public meeting in Bombay in 1888, when Gandhi was still a teenager. The title means "great soul" and has been given to several people.
