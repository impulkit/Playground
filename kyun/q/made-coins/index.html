How are coins made?

A coin press can stamp hundreds of coins a minute, each one squeezed with enough force to flatten a car.

The short answer

Coins start as huge rolls of metal sheet, as thick as the coin will be. A machine punches round discs out of the sheet, like a cookie cutter in a kitchen. These plain discs are called blanks.

The blanks are heated to soften them, washed until they shine, and given a raised rim. Then each one goes into a press. Two steel dies, one for each side, slam together with tremendous force and stamp the design into the metal in a fraction of a second. Out drops a finished coin. In India, this happens at four government mints: in Mumbai, Kolkata, Hyderabad and Noida.

Surprising but true

By 2024, each American one-cent coin cost almost four cents to make, so in 2025 the US Mint stopped making pennies for everyday use.

The press that never gets tired

Coin presses are fast. Modern ones at the US Mint can strike up to about 750 coins a minute, more than twelve every second. To push the metal into every tiny groove of the design, the dies press with a force of many tonnes. Picture the weight of a loaded truck focused on a disc smaller than your thumbnail.

The metal doesn't melt. It flows, a little like modelling clay squeezed in a mould. That's why the letters on a coin are so crisp. The dies themselves wear out after hundreds of thousands of strikes and must be replaced.

After striking, coins pass over screens and under cameras that pick out bent, cracked or off-centre ones. Rejects are melted down and the metal is used again. The good coins are counted by machine, sealed in bags or boxes, and sent to banks, which pass them on to shops and to you.

What's inside your pocket

Coins are rarely made of a single pure metal. Mints use alloys, mixtures of metals chosen to be hard, cheap and hard to fake. Many Indian coins today are made of ferritic stainless steel, which is tough and doesn't rust. A one-rupee coin weighs under 4 grams.

In the USA, the penny was about 97.5% zinc with a thin copper skin. By 2024, each one cost about 3.7 cents to make. So in November 2025, the US Mint struck its last pennies for everyday use, after more than 230 years. Many shops now round cash prices to the nearest five cents.

How are banknotes made?A banknote isn't really paper. Most are made of cotton, which is why a forgotten note can survive a trip through the washing machine.

Coins are very old

The first coins we know of were made about 2,600 years ago in Lydia, in what is now Turkey. They were lumps of electrum, a natural mix of gold and silver, stamped with a lion's head.

India has its own early coins. Punch-marked coins of silver, stamped with symbols like the sun, an elephant or a tree, were used across the Ganga plains from about 2,500 years ago, including in the Mauryan Empire. Rather than one design, each coin got several separate punches, like rubber stamps.

Ridges, holes and forgers

Have a look at a coin's edge. The ridges there have a history. In the 1600s, people clipped silver from coin edges. In England, Isaac Newton became warden of the Royal Mint in 1696 and hunted forgers and clippers himself. Coins with ridged or lettered edges made clipping easy to spot.

Holes have a purpose too. Coins with holes, used in China for about 2,000 years and in places like Japan today, could be strung together. In 2019, India released coins with different shapes and sizes so that people who are blind can tell them apart by touch. And if you drop one on a beach, its metal is exactly what a metal detector is listening for.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Coins are made of the same metal they're named after, so a copper coin is solid copper.

Truth: Most modern coins are cheap metals or alloys. Many Indian coins are ferritic stainless steel. The US penny was about 97.5% zinc with a thin copper coat, and the US "nickel" is mostly copper.

Try this

Lay a sheet of thin paper over a coin and rub a pencil lead sideways across it. Watch the design appear. Try the edge too, and compare coins of different values. You may find that some have ridges or bumps that help blind people tell them apart.
