What colour is a sunset on Mars?

On Earth, sunsets are orange. On Mars...

The short answer

On Mars, the colours of the sky are roughly the reverse of ours. In the daytime the sky is a dusty pinkish-orange, often described as butterscotch. At sunset, a cool blue glow spreads around the Sun.

The reason is dust. Mars has a thin atmosphere that is full of very fine reddish dust, finer than flour. Those dust grains scatter sunlight differently from the gas molecules in Earth's air. NASA rovers have photographed blue Martian sunsets many times.

Surprising but true

Mars has blue sunsets and a pink daytime sky. It's Earth's colours, flipped.

Why Earth's sky is blue

To understand Mars, start at home. Sunlight looks white, but it is a mix of all the colours of the rainbow. When it passes through Earth's air, tiny gas molecules bounce blue light around much more than red light. That scattered blue comes at you from every direction, so the whole sky looks blue.

At sunset, the light has to travel through much more air to reach you. Most of the blue gets scattered away before it arrives. What's left is orange and red, which is why Earth's sunsets glow red.

Why Mars flips it

Mars's air is about one hundredth as thick as ours, so its gas scatters very little light. The dust does the work instead. The grains are much bigger than gas molecules. Their iron-rich colour soaks up some of the blue, and they scatter reddish light widely across the sky. So in daytime the whole sky takes on the dust's warm colour.

Blue light behaves differently with grains that size. More of it carries on almost straight ahead, in the direction the sunlight was already travelling. So the sky right around the Sun looks blue. At sunset, when the light passes through the most dust, that blue halo shows up best.

In April 2015, the Curiosity rover filmed one of these sunsets from Gale Crater. The Sun looked small and pale, wrapped in a soft blue glow.

A colour mistake

When NASA's Viking lander sent back the first colour pictures from the surface of Mars in 1976, the first versions showed a blue sky like Earth's. Scientists soon realised the colours had not been balanced properly. Once they corrected them, the sky turned pinkish-tan. Getting colour right from a camera millions of kilometres away is harder than it sounds.

Why Mars is red

Mars is nicknamed the red planet because its dust and rocks are rich in iron oxide, the same chemical as rust on an old bicycle. Winds lift that dust high into the air, so the planet's colour fills its sky too.

Sometimes the dust gets out of hand. In 2018, a storm grew until it covered the whole planet and blocked so much sunlight that the solar-powered Opportunity rover shut down for good.

Looking up from Mars

From Mars, the Sun looks about two-thirds as wide as it does from Earth, because Mars is farther away. Two small moons, Phobos and Deimos, cross the sky. Phobos zips across so quickly that it rises in the west and sets in the east, twice a day.

And Earth? From the Martian surface, our whole planet looks like a bright evening or morning star. Curiosity has photographed it, a small dot of light hanging in the Martian sky.

Mars has clouds too, thin wisps made of water ice, and high up, even of frozen carbon dioxide. Rovers have photographed them drifting overhead. Some shimmer with pale rainbow colours just after sunset, like mother-of-pearl.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Mars has no air at all.

Truth: Mars has a thin atmosphere, mostly carbon dioxide, about one hundredth as thick as Earth's. It is enough for wind, thin clouds and dust storms that can cover the whole planet.

Try this

Fill a clear glass with water and stir in a few drops of milk. In a dark room, shine a torch through the side of the glass. Look at the glass from the side, then look straight through it at the torch. The colours change depending on where you stand, because tiny particles scatter different colours in different directions.
