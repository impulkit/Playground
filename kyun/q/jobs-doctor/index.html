What does a doctor do all day?

A doctor's day can include a newborn baby, a broken arm, a pile of paperwork and a 3 a.m. phone call, sometimes all before lunch.

The short answer

Doctors find out what is wrong with people and help them get better or stay well. A lot of that is detective work. They ask questions, listen, examine the body and order tests like blood tests or X-rays. Then they decide on a treatment and explain it.

But there is no single doctor's day. A family doctor in a small town might see 40 patients in a morning, most with coughs, fevers and aches. A surgeon might spend six hours on one operation. A doctor in a village health centre might be the only one for many kilometres. All of them also write notes, sign forms and talk to worried families.

Surprising but true

Much of a doctor's work happens without touching the patient at all. Listening carefully to someone describe how they feel often gives the most important clues.

Morning rounds

In a hospital, many doctors start the day with ward rounds. A senior doctor walks from bed to bed with a team of junior doctors, nurses and students. At each bed they check how the patient slept, look at charts and test results, and decide what happens next.

Rounds are also how young doctors learn. A senior might ask a student to explain why a patient's temperature rose overnight. Then the team plans the day: who needs a scan, who can go home, who needs a specialist.

The outpatient queue

In India, the outpatient department, or OPD, is where people come without staying overnight. In big government hospitals the queues can start before sunrise. A doctor there may have only a few minutes for each patient. They have to listen fast, think fast and still be kind.

In the US, many people see a family doctor in a small clinic by appointment, often for 15 to 20 minutes. In both countries, the doctor's first tool is the question "What brings you here today?"

Listening is the main skill

Doctors have a saying: listen to the patient, and they will tell you what is wrong. How a pain started, what makes it worse, whether others at home are sick: these clues often matter more than any machine.

Then comes the examination. A stethoscope lets a doctor hear the heart and lungs. A torch shows the back of the throat. A tap on the knee tests the reflexes. Only after this do most doctors decide which tests are worth doing. Good doctors also say "I don't know yet" when that's the truth.

Many kinds of doctor

After basic training, many doctors specialise. Paediatricians look after children. Cardiologists treat hearts, and surgeons operate. Radiologists read X-rays and scans. Psychiatrists care for mental health. Some surgeons now work with robots that hold tools steadier than any human hand.

Becoming a doctor takes a long time. In India, students sit an entrance exam called NEET, then study for about 5½ years for an MBBS degree, including a year as an intern. Many then train for three more years in a specialty. In the US, students usually finish a four-year college degree first, then four years of medical school, then three to seven years of hospital training.

Night shifts and hard days

Illness doesn't keep office hours, so hospitals need doctors around the clock. Young doctors often work long night shifts. They also see sad things, and learning to cope with that is part of the job.

Doctors work in teams. Nurses, pharmacists, lab technicians and ambulance crews do much of the care a patient receives. In India, people like the surgeon Sushruta, who described operations about 2,000 years ago, are remembered as early teachers of medicine. India celebrates National Doctors' Day on 1 July, the birthday of Dr B. C. Roy, a doctor who also became chief minister of West Bengal.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Antibiotics will cure a cold or the flu faster.

Truth: Colds and flu are caused by viruses, and antibiotics only work against bacteria. Doctors often spend time explaining this, because taking antibiotics when they aren't needed helps bacteria become resistant to them.

Try this

Find your pulse by pressing two fingers gently on the inside of your wrist, below your thumb. Count the beats for 15 seconds and multiply by four. Then do 20 jumping jacks and count again. Doctors use this simple check every day.
