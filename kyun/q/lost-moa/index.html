What was the moa?

New Zealand was once ruled by giant birds taller than a basketball hoop, with no wings at all. They were hunted by an eagle big enough to kill them.

The short answer

Moa were giant flightless birds that lived only in New Zealand. There were nine kinds. The biggest, the South Island giant moa, could reach about 3.6 metres tall with its neck stretched up, taller than a basketball hoop. It could weigh about 230 kg, as much as a grand piano.

Moa ate leaves, twigs, seeds and fruit, and filled the role that deer or antelope play on other continents. They lived for millions of years. But within roughly 200 years of people arriving in New Zealand, around 1300 CE, they were all gone.

Surprising but true

Moa had no wing bones at all, not even tiny ones. Every other flightless bird alive today, such as the ostrich and kiwi, still has at least small wings.

Birds without wings

Many birds can't fly, like ostriches, emus and penguins. But they all still have wings, even if they're tiny. Moa had none. Their skeletons show no wing bones at all.

That's unusual among birds. Scientists think moa lost their wings because they had no need for them. New Zealand had no land mammals hunting on the ground, apart from bats. Birds could walk, nest and feed safely, so many of them became flightless.

Giant females

In the largest kinds of moa, the females were much bigger than the males. Females could be about 1.5 times taller and nearly three times heavier.

For years, scientists thought the big and small moa bones belonged to different species. DNA tests in the early 2000s showed they were the same species, just mothers and fathers. Big females probably ruled their patches of forest.

Moa eggs were huge too. Some were around 24 cm long, much bigger than a coconut. Their shells were thin, so scientists think lighter males may have sat on them.

The giant eagle

The moa had one big enemy before people arrived: Haast's eagle. It was the largest eagle known, with a wingspan of up to about 3 metres and talons as large as a tiger's claws.

Like other eagles, it relied on speed and surprise. It hunted moa by swooping down, grabbing the bird's back or neck and striking hard. When moa disappeared, the eagle lost its main food, and it died out too. Māori stories may remember it as a giant bird called Pouakai or Te Hōkioi.

People arrive

Polynesian voyagers, ancestors of the Māori, sailed to New Zealand around 1300 CE in great canoes. They were some of the greatest navigators in history, finding islands across thousands of kilometres of open ocean.

They found a land full of large, slow-breeding birds that had never seen humans. Moa were hunted for meat, their bones were carved into tools and jewellery, and their eggshells were used as water containers. Forests were also burned to clear land.

Moa laid only one or two eggs at a time and took years to grow up. So hunting even a small number each year was enough to tip them towards extinction. By about 1400 to 1450, they were gone.

What we know from their remains

Moa left plenty behind. Scientists have found complete skeletons, eggs, feathers and even dried muscle and skin in caves. Their gizzard stones, swallowed to grind tough plants, have been found in piles.

Ancient DNA from moa bones has revealed their family tree. Surprisingly, their closest living relatives are tinamous, small birds from South America that can fly. That means the moa's ancestors probably flew to New Zealand millions of years ago, then grew big and lost their wings.

Could moa come back?

In July 2025, the American company Colossal Biosciences announced a project to try to bring back the moa. It is working with the Ngāi Tahu Research Centre, linked to a South Island Māori tribe, and the film director Peter Jackson.

Many scientists are doubtful. A "new" moa would be a modern bird with some moa genes, not the real thing, and it's unclear where it would live. Some argue the money would be better spent saving living birds, like New Zealand's endangered kākāpō. The moa's story sits alongside the dodo as one of the clearest warnings about how quickly people can wipe out an animal.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Moa died out long before people arrived, like the dinosaurs.

Truth: Moa were alive when the first Polynesian settlers reached New Zealand around 700 years ago. They vanished within a couple of centuries, mainly from hunting and changes to the forests.

Try this

Measure 3.6 metres up a wall with a tape measure, using a chair only with an adult holding it. Put a sticky note at the top. That's how tall a big female moa could stretch. Then stand beside it and see how many of you it would take to match.
