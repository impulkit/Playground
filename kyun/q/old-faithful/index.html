Why does Old Faithful shoot water into the sky?

Every hour or so, a fountain of boiling water blasts up to 55 m high. The heat comes from magma hidden under the ground.

The short answer

Under Yellowstone National Park in the USA there is hot magma. Rain and snowmelt soak into the ground and are heated by it. In a geyser, the water collects in a narrow underground pipe.

Deep down, the weight of the water above squeezes the hot water, so it can't boil even above 100°C. When a little water near the top turns to steam and escapes, the squeeze drops, and all the super-hot water flashes into steam at once. Whoosh.

Surprising but true

Yellowstone has about half of all the geysers on Earth, around 500 of them.

A pressure cooker in the ground

It works a bit like a pressure cooker. In a sealed cooker, extra pressure lets water get hotter than 100 °C without boiling. Inside a geyser, the pressure comes from the column of water above. Even a few metres of water adds enough squeeze to raise the boiling point.

As heat keeps flowing up from below, the deep water gets hotter and hotter. Eventually some of it boils anyway. Bubbles push water out of the top, the weight above drops, and the super-hot water below suddenly boils in a rush. The eruption keeps going until the pipe is nearly empty. Then it slowly refills, and the cycle starts again.

Faithful, but not exact

Old Faithful got its name because it erupts so regularly: about every 60 to 110 minutes. Each blast lasts a few minutes and reaches up to about 55 metres, taller than a 15-storey building. A single eruption throws out roughly 14,000 to 32,000 litres of water, enough to fill a small tanker truck.

It was named in 1870 by members of an expedition exploring the area, who were struck by how reliably it went off. Today park rangers predict each eruption, and crowds gather on wooden boardwalks to watch.

Why geysers are rare

A geyser needs three things at once: plenty of underground heat, a steady supply of water, and a narrow, sealed pipe of rock. The pipe is often lined with a hard mineral called silica, left behind by the hot water itself. Without all three, you get a gentle hot spring or a bubbling mud pot instead.

That's why fewer than a thousand geysers exist on Earth, and about half are in Yellowstone. The word "geyser" comes from Geysir, a famous spouting spring in Iceland. India has many hot springs, such as Manikaran in Himachal Pradesh, but no geysers like Old Faithful.

A sleeping giant

Yellowstone sits on top of one of the world's largest volcanic systems. Its last huge eruption happened roughly 630,000 years ago. Scientists watch it closely every day.

In 1872, the USA made Yellowstone the world's first national park, protecting its geysers, forests and wildlife for everyone. Nearby, Steamboat Geyser is the tallest active geyser in the world, sometimes shooting water over 90 m high, though it can go years between big eruptions.

Living colours

Some of Yellowstone's hot springs are ringed with bright bands of orange, yellow and green. These colours come from microbes, tiny living things that thrive in water hot enough to scald you.

In the 1960s, a scientist named Thomas Brock found one of them, a bacterium called Thermus aquaticus, in a Yellowstone hot spring. Years later, a substance from it became the key ingredient in a test that copies DNA, used in labs everywhere, including for COVID-19 tests. Even the smallest living things can turn out to be helpful.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Old Faithful erupts exactly once an hour, like clockwork.

Truth: The gap between eruptions varies, usually from about an hour to nearly two. Park rangers predict the next one from how long the last eruption lasted. A long eruption empties the pipe more, so the wait afterwards is longer.
