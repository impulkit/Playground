How do Roman numerals work?

The Colosseum in Rome still has numbers carved over its arches, so that 50,000 Roman spectators could find the right gate.

The short answer

Roman numerals use seven letters to stand for numbers. I is 1, V is 5, X is 10, L is 50, C is 100, D is 500 and M is 1,000. To write a number, add letters together, starting with the biggest. So XVII is 10 + 5 + 1 + 1 = 17.

There is one twist. When a smaller letter comes before a bigger one, you subtract it. IV is 5 minus 1, which is 4, and XC is 100 minus 10, which is 90. The Romans had no zero, which made big sums hard. That is why these numerals faded from everyday use, but you can still spot them on clocks, buildings and film credits.

Surprising but true

Many clocks with Roman numerals show four as IIII, not IV, and the Romans themselves often wrote it that way.

From tally marks to letters

The Roman system grew out of tally marks, the scratches people make when keeping count. One stroke, I, was one. Many historians think V for five may come from the shape of an open hand, with the thumb and fingers spread, and that X looks like two Vs joined point to point. Over time the symbols came to look like Latin letters.

Ancient Rome borrowed ideas from the Etruscans, who lived in Italy before the Romans grew powerful. The Etruscans used similar marks. Around the empire, from Britain to Egypt, the same numerals appeared on coins, milestones, buildings and tombstones.

Reading the numbers

The rules are simple once you know them. Write the biggest values first and add. Never repeat a letter more than three times in a row in the modern style. Use subtraction only in six pairs: IV (4), IX (9), XL (40), XC (90), CD (400) and CM (900).

So 1994 is M (1000) + CM (900) + XC (90) + IV (4), written MCMXCIV. The biggest number you can write with these basic rules is MMMCMXCIX, which is 3,999. For bigger numbers, Romans sometimes drew a bar over a numeral to multiply it by 1,000.

Sums without zero

Try adding CXXIII and XLIX in your head. It is not easy. The Romans did not do sums with numerals. They used counting boards and a kind of abacus with pebbles in grooves. The Latin word for pebble, calculus, gives us the English words "calculate" and "calculus".

Roman numerals have no zero and no place value. In our system, the 2 in 200 means something different from the 2 in 20. In Roman numerals, X always means ten, wherever it sits. That makes long multiplication almost impossible. When Indian digits with zero reached Europe in the Middle Ages, merchants slowly switched over, though some cities resisted for a long time.

Where to find them today

At the Colosseum in Rome, the arches around the outside were numbered so crowds could find their seats. You can still read LII, 52, above one of them.

Today Roman numerals often appear where something should look grand or old-fashioned. They label kings and queens, like Elizabeth II, as well as book chapters, the copyright dates at the end of films and many clock faces. The Super Bowl, the final of American football, is numbered in Roman numerals. In 2016 the organisers made one exception and called it Super Bowl 50 instead of Super Bowl L, because they thought a lone L looked odd. In India, you may see them on old buildings from British times and on some school certificates.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Romans always wrote 4 as IV and 9 as IX.

Truth: Romans often wrote IIII and VIIII. The rule of putting a smaller numeral before a larger one to subtract was used, but it became standard mainly in the Middle Ages and later. Clocks showing IIII are following an old habit, not making a mistake.

Try this

Write your age, your house number and the year you were born in Roman numerals. Then add two of them together using only Roman numerals, without changing them into ordinary digits. You will quickly see why Romans used counting boards for sums.
