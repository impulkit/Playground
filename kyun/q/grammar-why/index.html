Why do languages have grammar?

"Dog bites man" is a boring headline. "Man bites dog" is big news. Same words, different order: that's grammar at work.

The short answer

Grammar is the set of patterns a language uses to show how words fit together. Without it, you'd have a pile of words but no way to say who did what to whom, when it happened, or whether you are asking or telling.

Every human language has grammar, including sign languages and languages that have never been written down. It isn't a list of rules invented by teachers. Speakers follow most of it without thinking, and children pick it up long before they go to school.

Languages do the job in different ways. Some rely on word order, some on word endings, and some on small helper words.

Surprising but true

More of the world's languages put the verb at the end of a sentence, as Hindi and Tamil do, than in the middle, as English does.

Who did what to whom

In English, word order carries much of the meaning. "The cat chased the mouse" and "The mouse chased the cat" use exactly the same words, but the order tells you who is running away. English usually goes subject, verb, object.

Hindi often puts the verb at the end and uses small helper words to mark roles: Billi ne chuhe ko pakda, "The cat caught the mouse". Ne marks who did it and ko marks who it happened to, so the words can sometimes shuffle without confusion. Tamil and Japanese also put the verb last. In fact, subject, object, verb is the most common order among the world's languages, with English's order a close second.

Endings and genders

Other languages pack information into the ends of words. In Sanskrit, nouns change their endings depending on their job in a sentence, so the word order is very free. Panini's Ashtadhyayi, written over 2,000 years ago, described these patterns in about 4,000 compact rules.

Many languages sort nouns into groups called genders. In Hindi, a book (kitab) is feminine and a room (kamra) is masculine, and that changes the words around them: kitab achchhi hai, but kamra achchha hai. Bengali, Turkish and Finnish have no gender for nouns at all. English has mostly lost the genders that Old English once had, keeping only he, she and it.

Built in, or learned?

Children everywhere learn grammar at about the same pace, without lessons. A three-year-old who says "I goed" instead of "I went" isn't copying anyone. They have worked out the rule "add -ed for the past" and applied it a little too well.

In the 1950s and 1960s, the American linguist Noam Chomsky argued that humans are born with a "universal grammar", a kind of built-in blueprint for language. Others argue that children are simply excellent pattern-spotters who learn grammar from the speech around them. The debate is still going.

One striking clue came from Nicaragua, where deaf children brought together in new schools created a sign language with its own grammar in just a few years.

Grammar for machines

Computers need grammar too, but far stricter than ours. A programming language has exact rules, and a single missing bracket can stop a program from running. People, by contrast, understand each other even when sentences are messy, half-finished or full of "umm".

Machine translation used to struggle because human grammar is so flexible. Modern systems learn patterns from huge amounts of text instead of following hand-written rules. They can still stumble on a sentence like "Time flies like an arrow; fruit flies like a banana", where the grammar quietly changes halfway through.

That sentence is easy for a ten-year-old and hard for a machine, which says a lot about how good human brains are at grammar.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Some languages have no grammar.

Truth: Every human language ever studied has grammar, including languages that have never been written down and sign languages. Some just use word endings or helper words where others use word order.
