Why is New York called the Big Apple?

New York's nickname started at the racetrack. In the 1920s, a sports writer heard stable hands call the city's big races "the big apple", the prize every jockey wanted.

The short answer

New York got its nickname, the Big Apple, from horse racing. In the 1920s, a newspaper writer named John J. Fitz Gerald heard stable workers call the big New York races "the big apple", the biggest prize in the sport. He used the phrase in his column. Jazz musicians picked it up in the 1930s, meaning that playing New York was the top.

The name faded, then came back in 1971, when the city's tourism office used it in a campaign to bring visitors back. It has stuck ever since. Today New York has about 8.5 million people, more than any other US city.

Surprising but true

Hundreds of languages are spoken in New York City, making it one of the most language-rich places on Earth.

From Lenape land to New Amsterdam

Long before the city, the Lenape people lived on the islands and shores here, fishing, farming and hunting. In 1624, Dutch traders arrived and built a settlement at the tip of Manhattan called New Amsterdam. They built a wall to protect it, and the path beside it later became Wall Street.

In 1664, English ships sailed into the harbour and took the town without a battle. They renamed it New York, after the Duke of York. In 1898, five areas called boroughs joined to form today's city: Manhattan, Brooklyn, Queens, the Bronx and Staten Island.

The golden door

For many people, New York was their first sight of America. The Statue of Liberty, a gift from France, was dedicated in 1886 in the harbour. From 1892 to 1954, about 12 million immigrants passed through the inspection station on nearby Ellis Island. Many stayed in the city, crowding into apartments and building new lives.

Why is the Statue of Liberty green?It was shiny copper-brown when it arrived from France. Air and rain turned it green.

Immigrants still shape the city today. Hundreds of languages are spoken in New York. Queens is one of the most varied places on Earth, and Jackson Heights has streets of Indian, Bangladeshi, Nepali and Tibetan shops. You can find biryani, momos and dosas a few blocks apart.

Upwards

Manhattan is a narrow island, so builders went up. The invention of safe elevators and steel frames made tall buildings possible. The Empire State Building, finished in 1931, was the tallest building in the world for nearly 40 years. Today the tallest is One World Trade Center, about 541 m including its spire.

The skyscrapers stand on hard rock called schist, which comes close to the surface in parts of Manhattan and makes a strong base. Many of the workers who built them were immigrants. Mohawk ironworkers from Canada and New York State became famous for walking along narrow steel beams hundreds of metres up, and several generations of their families helped raise the city's towers.

A city underground and in the park

The New York City Subway opened in 1904. It runs all day and all night, with about 470 stations, more than any other metro system in the world. Millions of people ride it every day.

In the middle of Manhattan is Central Park, about 340 hectares of lawns, lakes and woods, all designed by hand in the 1850s. That is more than seven times the size of Vatican City.

Where the world meets

New York is home to the United Nations headquarters, where 193 member countries meet. It is a world centre of banking, publishing, theatre and art. It is also where hip-hop began, at block parties in the Bronx in the 1970s. People call New York "the city that never sleeps", and on a weekday night in Times Square, it is easy to believe.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Dutch bought Manhattan from Native Americans for 24 dollars' worth of beads.

Truth: Records from 1626 say the Dutch paid goods worth 60 guilders. The "24 dollars" figure was worked out in the 1800s. Historians also think the Lenape probably saw the deal as sharing use of the land, not selling it forever.
