Where did Mars's two little moons come from?

Mars has two moons shaped like lumpy potatoes, and nobody is sure how it got them.

The short answer

Mars has two small moons, Phobos and Deimos. Phobos is a lumpy rock only about 22 km across, small enough to walk around in a few days. Deimos is even smaller.

Nobody knows for sure where they came from. They may be asteroids that Mars caught, or rubble thrown up when something huge smashed into Mars long ago. The best way to find out is to bring a piece back to Earth.

Surprising but true

Phobos may be dusted with tiny bits of Mars itself. Scientists think crashes on Mars long ago blasted rock into space, and about 0.1 percent of Phobos's surface could be Martian dust.

Two ideas

The first idea is capture. Phobos and Deimos are dark and rocky, a lot like some asteroids. Perhaps they wandered too close and got trapped by Mars's gravity.

The second idea is a giant crash. Something big may have hit Mars, flinging a ring of rubble into space that slowly clumped into moons. That is similar to how scientists think our own Moon formed.

The two ideas would leave different clues in the rock. An asteroid would look like the meteorites that fall to Earth. Rubble from Mars would look much more like Mars itself.

A strange little moon

Phobos goes round Mars about three times a day, in a very low orbit. Seen from the Martian surface, it rises in the west and sets in the east. Its biggest dent, a crater called Stickney, is about 9 km wide, almost half the size of the whole moon.

Phobos is also slowly spiralling inwards. In tens of millions of years, Mars's pull may tear it apart into a ring.

Scooping a moon

Japan's space agency, JAXA, has done tricky sample trips before: its Hayabusa2 spacecraft brought back dust from an asteroid in 2020. In 2026 it launched MMX to Phobos, aiming to collect at least 10 grams of rock and dust, including some from just under the surface.

A small rover called Idefix was built to test the ground first, a bit like the rovers that explore Mars. If the sample comes home, scientists can study it with powerful lab machines and finally see which story is true.

Myth or fact?

Myth: All moons are round like ours.

Truth: Big moons are pulled into balls by their own gravity. Small moons like Phobos, only about 22 km across, are too light for that, so they stay lumpy, a bit like a potato.

Try this

Gently drop a marble into a tray of flour from about 30 cm up. Look at the round dent and the splash of flour around it. That is a tiny model of a crater, and of how crashes on Mars could have sprayed bits of Mars out into space.
