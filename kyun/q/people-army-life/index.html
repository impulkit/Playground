What is life like in the Indian Army?

Some Indian soldiers live on a glacier more than 6,000 metres up, where the air has half as much oxygen and winter nights fall to around minus 50°C.

The short answer

Life in the Indian Army is a mix of hard training, strict routine, long postings far from home and a very close bond with the people you serve beside. A soldier might spend two or three years at a base in the plains, then be sent to guard a snowy border post or a forest in the Northeast.

The Indian Army is one of the largest in the world, with about 12 lakh active soldiers. They come from every state and many languages. Most of their time is spent not fighting but training, guarding India's long borders and helping during disasters.

A day in uniform

At most army bases, the day starts before sunrise with a bugle call called reveille. Then comes physical training: running, push-ups, rope climbing and drills. After breakfast, soldiers practise their skills, look after their weapons and vehicles, and study.

Meals are eaten together in a mess or a langar. Evenings often include games of volleyball or football. Army life runs on timetables, uniforms and orders, and discipline matters because in a crisis everyone must know exactly what to do.

Officers usually train at the National Defence Academy near Pune, joining at around 16 to 19, then at the Indian Military Academy in Dehradun. Since 2022, girls can join the National Defence Academy too.

Regiments with long memories

Soldiers belong to regiments, many with histories going back centuries. The Madras Regiment, the oldest infantry regiment, traces its roots to the 1750s. Others include the Sikh Regiment, the Gorkha Rifles, the Rajputana Rifles and the Assam Regiment.

Each has its own traditions, songs and battle cry. The Gorkhas shout "Ayo Gorkhali" ("the Gorkhas are here"). Sikh regiments cry "Jo Bole So Nihal". These traditions help soldiers feel like a family, which matters when they are far from their own.

Living on the edge of the map

Some postings are among the toughest anywhere. On the Siachen Glacier in the Karakoram, Indian posts sit above 6,000 metres. At that altitude, each breath holds about half the oxygen it would at sea level. Temperatures can drop to around minus 50°C, and storms can last for days.

Soldiers there wear layers of special clothing, live in insulated huts and must be swapped out after a few months, because the body cannot cope for long. Supplies come by helicopter, or on foot and by mule along icy tracks.

Other soldiers guard hot deserts in Rajasthan, where summer days pass 45°C, or the thick forests of the Northeast.

Families on the move

Army families move often, every two or three years. Children may go to a new school each time, often a Kendriya Vidyalaya or Army Public School, where most classmates are also army kids. Many say they grew up with friends in every corner of India.

Bases, called cantonments, have their own shops, hospitals and parks. When a parent is posted to a remote border, the family usually stays behind, and phone calls may be rare.

Since 2022, many young people join through a scheme called Agnipath, serving for four years. A share of them are then chosen to stay on for a full career. Women have served as army officers for decades and, since a 2020 Supreme Court ruling, can serve full careers as officers too.

Helping in hard times

When floods, earthquakes or landslides strike, the army is often among the first to arrive. Its engineers build temporary bridges, its doctors run field hospitals and its trucks carry food. In 2024, after deadly landslides in Wayanad, Kerala, army engineers built a 190-foot steel bridge in about 31 hours so rescuers could reach cut-off villages.

Indian soldiers also serve abroad as UN peacekeepers. India has been one of the biggest contributors of troops to UN missions for decades. And every year on 26 January, soldiers from many regiments march down Kartavya Path in Delhi in the Republic Day parade, each regiment in its own colours.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Soldiers only fight wars.

Truth: Most of a soldier's time is spent training, guarding borders and keeping equipment ready. The army is also called in after floods, earthquakes and landslides, and Indian soldiers serve as UN peacekeepers in other countries.

Try this

Fill a backpack with books until it feels heavy but comfortable, and time how long it takes you to walk up and down a flight of stairs five times, with an adult nearby. Then do it without the bag. Soldiers in the mountains carry loads like this in thin air, where every step is harder.
