Can a whole ecosystem be endangered?

We know tigers and turtles can be endangered. But what about a whole coral reef, a mangrove forest or a grassland, with every creature living in it?

The short answer

Yes. An ecosystem is a community of living things and the place they share, like a coral reef, a mangrove swamp or a grassland. Just as a species can become endangered, so can a whole ecosystem.

Scientists judge an ecosystem by how much of it is shrinking and how badly it is changing. If it is likely to stop working as a home for its creatures, it is listed as threatened.

Surprising but true

The coral reefs around the western Galápagos Islands are listed as already collapsed. Their corals died after a powerful El Niño warmed the sea in 1982 and 1983.

Lists for species, lists for places

Since 1964, the IUCN, a worldwide group of scientists and conservationists, has kept the Red List of Threatened Species. It sorts animals and plants from Least Concern up to Critically Endangered and Extinct. Many endangered animals you have heard of are on it.

In 2014, the IUCN agreed a second list, the Red List of Ecosystems. Instead of asking "how many tigers are left?", it asks questions like "how much of this forest is left?" and "is it still working as a forest?".

How an ecosystem is judged

Scientists look at five kinds of evidence. Is the ecosystem shrinking? Is it found in only a few small places? Is the land, water or climate around it changing? Are the living things in it, and the links between them, breaking down? And what do computer models say about its future?

The ratings run from Least Concern to Critically Endangered. The last step is Collapsed, when the ecosystem has changed so much that it no longer works as it did. For example, a reef where the corals have died and been replaced by seaweed.

The case of coral reefs

In 2026, the IUCN finished the first global check of coral reef ecosystems, covering reefs in more than 80 countries. It found that 96% of the shallow-water reef ecosystems it assessed were Critically Endangered, with a very high risk of collapse by 2075.

The biggest threat is heat. Warmer seas cause coral bleaching, when corals lose the tiny algae that feed them. Catching too many grazing fish, like parrotfish, also lets seaweed take over. Reefs from the Great Barrier Reef to the Andaman Islands face these pressures. The good news: reefs can recover when the water cools and fishing is managed, so scientists say there is still a window to act.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A reef or forest is only in trouble once it has completely disappeared.

Truth: Ecosystems can stop working long before they vanish. A reef can still look like a reef while its corals die, its fish leave and seaweed takes over. Scientists try to spot these warning signs early, while there is still time to help.

Try this

Draw a food web for a place near you, such as a pond, park or garden. Write down five living things and draw arrows from what is eaten to what eats it. Now cross one out. How many arrows break? That is why losing one piece can harm a whole ecosystem.
