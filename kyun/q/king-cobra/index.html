Why does the king cobra build a nest?

It's the only snake in the world known to build a nest.

The short answer

Most snakes lay eggs and leave. The king cobra mother builds a nest and guards it.

Rotting leaves in the nest give off warmth, like a compost heap, keeping the eggs cosy.

King cobras are shy and avoid people. Never go near one. Always tell an adult.

The nest is built in the wet forests of India and South-East Asia, often before the monsoon. Inside a mound of leaves, the eggs stay warm and damp but protected from flooding, while the mother keeps egg-eating animals away.

Surprising but true

The king cobra isn't a true cobra. It's in a group all by itself.

Building with no hands

A king cobra has no arms or claws, so she builds with her body. She loops her coils around fallen leaves and drags them into a pile, sometimes on a slope or at the foot of a bamboo clump. The finished nest can be over a metre across, with a chamber for eggs inside and a layer on top where she often rests.

She lays about 20 to 40 white, leathery eggs. Then she guards the nest for weeks, chasing off monitor lizards, mongooses and wild pigs. She usually leaves shortly before the eggs hatch, about two to three months after they were laid. The babies are already about 50 cm long and carry venom from the moment they hatch, so they can look after themselves.

A snake that eats snakes

King cobras hunt other snakes, including rat snakes, kraits, pythons and even other venomous species. They can follow a scent trail with their flicking forked tongue and swallow prey longer than a metre.

Because snakes are slippery and hard to catch, king cobras don't eat often. After a big meal, one may go weeks without food. Their venom acts mainly on the nerves, stopping prey from moving or breathing.

How does snake venom work?A snake's venom is really a kind of spit, turned over millions of years into one of nature's most powerful mixtures.

More than one king

For a long time, scientists thought every king cobra belonged to one species, Ophiophagus hannah. But snakes from different regions looked slightly different, and their DNA told the same story. In 2024, a team of researchers split them into four species.

The king cobra of the Western Ghats got its own name, Ophiophagus kaalinga. Research at the Agumbe Rainforest Research Station in Karnataka, founded by the herpetologist Romulus Whitaker, helped show this. There, scientists have tracked king cobras with small radio tags to learn how far they travel, where they nest and how they live alongside farms and villages.

Living with snakes

India has more snakebite deaths than any other country, tens of thousands every year. Almost all of them are caused by four snakes: the spectacled cobra, the common krait, the Russell's viper and the saw-scaled viper. King cobras live mostly in forests, avoid people, and bite rarely.

In many villages in Karnataka and Kerala, snakes are respected and even worshipped, and people call trained rescuers to move a king cobra rather than kill it. If you ever see a snake, stay still, back away slowly and tell an adult. Snakes don't chase people.

Reptile parents

Caring for eggs is unusual among snakes but not among reptiles. Mother crocodiles guard their nests and carry their babies to the water. Some dinosaurs probably buried their eggs in warm mounds of soil and plants, much like the king cobra's leaf pile, and birds, the dinosaurs' living descendants, are famous nest builders.

Myth or fact?

Myth: King cobras chase people and attack without warning.

Truth: King cobras are shy and usually slip away from people. When cornered, they rise up, spread their hood and give a low growl as a warning. Bites are rare. Most snakebite deaths in India come from four other snakes, not the king cobra.

Try this

Build a model leaf nest. Gather a big pile of dry leaves in a corner of a garden or a large box. Push them together into a mound using only your forearms, with no hands, the way a king cobra uses its coils. Put a thermometer in the middle for a day and compare it with the air outside to see if the pile stays warmer.
