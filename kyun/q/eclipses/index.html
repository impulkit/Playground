What happens during an eclipse?

The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon, and also about 400 times farther away. That coincidence gives us total eclipses.

The short answer

An eclipse happens when the Sun, Earth and the Moon line up so that one blocks light from another.

In a solar eclipse, the Moon passes between the Sun and Earth, and its shadow falls on part of our planet. If you are in the very centre of that shadow, the Sun disappears completely for a few minutes. In a lunar eclipse, Earth passes between the Sun and the Moon, and Earth's shadow slides across the Moon, often turning it a deep coppery red.

Solar eclipses can only happen at new moon, and lunar eclipses only at full moon.

Surprising but true

Helium was discovered by studying the Sun during an eclipse seen from Guntur in Andhra Pradesh in 1868, decades before anyone found it on Earth.

A lucky fit

The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon. It is also about 400 times farther away. So from Earth they look almost exactly the same size, and the Moon can cover the Sun like a coin covering a coin.

When it does, day turns to deep twilight. Stars and planets appear. Birds go quiet, and the temperature can drop by several degrees. Around the black disc of the Moon, the Sun's thin outer layer, the corona, glows in pale silvery streamers. It is normally invisible because the rest of the Sun is far too bright.

The Moon's shadow on Earth is narrow, usually less than a couple of hundred kilometres wide. That is why a total solar eclipse is only seen along a thin path, while people outside it see the Sun only partly covered.

Why the Moon turns red

During a total lunar eclipse, the Moon doesn't vanish. It glows a dull orange or red. That is because some sunlight bends through Earth's atmosphere on its way past. The air scatters away blue light, which is also why the sky is blue, and lets red light through.

So the red light on the Moon is the glow of every sunrise and sunset happening on Earth at that moment. Lunar eclipses are completely safe to watch. A total one was seen across India in September 2025.

A new element from Guntur

On 18 August 1868, a total solar eclipse crossed India. The French astronomer Jules Janssen set up his instruments at Guntur, in today's Andhra Pradesh. He split the light from the glowing gas around the Sun into its colours and saw a bright yellow line that no known element could explain.

The English astronomer Norman Lockyer later named the mystery element helium, after helios, the Greek word for the Sun. It was not found on Earth until 1895.

Eclipses helped science again in 1919. Astronomers photographed stars near the darkened Sun and showed that its gravity bends starlight, just as Albert Einstein had predicted.

What did Albert Einstein discover?A young clerk in a Swiss patent office wrote four papers in one year that changed how we understand light, time and energy.

Watching safely

Never look straight at the Sun, even when most of it is covered. Its light can damage the back of your eyes without any pain. Ordinary sunglasses are nowhere near dark enough. Use certified eclipse glasses, or make a pinhole projector and look at the image on paper instead.

Eclipses have been watched with awe for thousands of years. In Hindu stories, the demon Rahu swallows the Sun or Moon, which soon slips free again. Ancient astronomers in Babylon, China and India learned to predict eclipses long before anyone knew why they happened.

On 8 April 2024, a total eclipse crossed Mexico, the United States and Canada. Millions of people stood outside in the sudden dusk and cheered when the Sun came back.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Food cooked or left out during an eclipse becomes harmful to eat.

Truth: The light reaching Earth during an eclipse is ordinary sunlight, just less of it. Food is not affected. The one real danger is looking straight at the Sun without proper eye protection.

Try this

In a dark room, use a torch as the Sun, a football as Earth and a small ball as the Moon. Move the small ball between the torch and the football and watch its shadow fall on the "Earth". Then move it behind the football into Earth's shadow to make a lunar eclipse.
