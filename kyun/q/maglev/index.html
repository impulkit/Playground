How do maglev trains float?

In Shanghai, a train with no wheels on the track carries airport passengers at up to 300 km/h, hovering on magnets about a centimetre above the rail.

The short answer

A maglev train floats because powerful magnets push or pull it up off its track, with a gap of somewhere between about 1 and 10 centimetres. The word maglev is short for magnetic levitation. Levitation just means rising into the air without support.

With nothing rolling on a rail, there is no wheel friction and very little shaking. The train is pushed forwards by more magnets. Electricity running through coils in the track creates a magnetic wave that travels along the line. The train is pulled with it, a bit like a surfer riding a wave. Speed up the wave, and the train speeds up too.

Surprising but true

Japan's test maglev, which has floated at 603 km/h, still has rubber wheels, because at low speeds it can't float yet.

Two ways to float

Engineers use two main tricks. The first relies on attraction. The bottom of the train wraps around under a steel rail, and electromagnets on the train pull upwards towards it. Pull too hard and the train would clamp onto the rail, so computers adjust the magnets thousands of times a second to hold a gap of about 1 cm. Shanghai's maglev works this way.

The second relies on repulsion. Japan's system uses super-cold magnets on the train. As they rush past coils in the walls of a U-shaped guideway, they create a pushing force that lifts the train about 10 cm. This only works once the train is moving fast, so it rolls on rubber wheels until it reaches about 150 km/h, then lifts off like a plane.

The Shanghai airport ride

The world's best-known working maglev links Shanghai's Pudong airport with the edge of the city, a line of about 30 km. It opened for regular passengers in 2004, using German technology. For years it ran at up to 431 km/h in daily service. Since 2021 it has been held to 300 km/h, which still makes the trip in about eight minutes. A digital sign inside the train shows the speed climbing. Outside, cars on the highway seem to go backwards.

It's a thrilling ride, but it has not been copied widely. The line cost a great deal, it only goes a short distance, and passengers still have to change to the metro to reach the city centre.

Japan's 603 km/h train

Japan already runs some of the world's best bullet trains on ordinary steel wheels. Even so, it is building a maglev line, called the Chuo Shinkansen, between Tokyo and Nagoya. Much of it runs in tunnels, including under the Japanese Alps.

On its test track, a seven-car maglev reached 603 km/h in 2015, a world record for trains. The finished line should make the Tokyo to Nagoya trip in about 40 minutes, less than half today's time. Construction has been delayed, and it is not expected to open before the 2030s.

Why aren't they everywhere?

Maglev trains can't use ordinary railway tracks. Every kilometre of guideway must be built from scratch, very precisely, and that is expensive. Ordinary high-speed trains, which reach about 300 to 350 km/h, can share existing stations and tracks, and are good enough for most journeys.

Still, the idea keeps returning. China has tested new maglev trains designed for 600 km/h, and engineers in India have studied it too. Supporters point out that maglevs are quiet, smooth and need fewer repairs, because no wheels grind against rails. Critics answer that the money could build many more kilometres of ordinary fast railway. For now, if you want to float across the countryside, Shanghai is the place to go.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Because maglev trains don't touch the track, nothing slows them down once they are moving.

Truth: There's no rolling friction on the rails, but air still pushes back hard. At 400 km/h, air resistance is the main thing the train fights, so it needs plenty of power to keep going.

Try this

Take two ring-shaped fridge magnets or ring magnets and slide them onto a pencil so they repel each other. The top one floats. Press it down gently and feel the push. Now flip it over and see what happens when the poles attract instead.
