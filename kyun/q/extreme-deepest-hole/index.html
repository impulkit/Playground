What is the deepest hole ever dug?

Soviet scientists drilled 12 km into the Arctic rock, deeper than Everest is tall, and the hole is narrower than a dinner plate.

The short answer

The deepest hole people have ever made is the Kola Superdeep Borehole, in the far north-west of Russia, near the border with Norway. Soviet scientists started drilling in 1970. By 1989, they had reached 12,262 m, about 12 km straight down. That's deeper than Mount Everest is tall, by more than 3 km.

But it's not a hole you could climb into. At the bottom, it's only about 23 cm wide, narrower than a dinner plate. The drilling stopped because the rock got too hot. Today the top is sealed with a rusty metal cap.

Surprising but true

Even the deepest hole ever drilled reaches only about 0.2% of the way to Earth's centre.

Why drill so deep?

The Kola project wasn't about oil or gold. It was pure science. During the Cold War, the Soviet Union and the USA competed in space and also in reaching deep inside Earth. American scientists had tried a deep-sea drilling project called Project Mohole in the 1960s, but it was cancelled.

The Soviets wanted to drill through the crust, the Earth's outer skin, and learn what it was really made of. Nobody had ever seen rock from so far down. Geologists had theories from studying earthquake waves, but they wanted real samples.

Drilling that deep was slow, hard work. Instead of spinning the whole 12 km string of pipe, the team used a turbine at the bottom, driven by mud pumped down the pipe, to turn the drill bit. Every time a bit wore out, the entire pipe had to be hauled up and taken apart, section by section, which could take many hours.

What they found down there

The deep rock held surprises. Scientists had expected a change from granite to basalt rock at about 7 km. It wasn't there. Instead, the rock had been cracked and changed by heat and pressure, and water was trapped in it, squeezed out of minerals.

They found tiny fossils of plankton more than 6 km down, preserved in rocks around 2 billion years old. The mud from the hole was said to bubble with hydrogen gas. And the temperature rose faster than predicted. At 12 km, it was about 180 °C, nearly double what models suggested.

Deeper than Kola?

Some oil wells are longer than Kola. In 2011, the Z-44 Chayvo well near Sakhalin Island in Russia reached about 12,345 m. But oil wells often curve sideways through the rock to reach oil, so they're long without going as deep.

In 2025, China finished Shenditake 1, a vertical well in the Taklamakan Desert. It reached 10,910 m, the deepest in Asia, though still short of Kola.

For deep places people can actually visit, the record holders are mines. The Mponeng gold mine in South Africa goes about 4 km down, and miners need powerful cooling to cope with rock hot enough to burn skin.

Just a scratch

Earth's centre is about 6,371 km below your feet. So the Kola hole is just 0.2% of the way there. If Earth were an apple, the hole wouldn't even get through the skin.

The crust under continents is usually 30 to 50 km thick, so Kola didn't come close to the mantle underneath. Scientists still learn about deep Earth mainly by studying how earthquake waves bend and bounce. Under Indian cities, many borewells go down a few hundred metres to find water, which is nothing compared with Kola, but enough to drain old aquifers. Physicists have even worked out what would happen if a hole went all the way, and the answer, in a hole through the Earth, involves a fall lasting roughly 40 minutes.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Scientists drilling in the Kola hole recorded the screams of people in hell.

Truth: This was a hoax that spread through radio shows and newsletters around 1989 and 1990, and later online. The "recording" was taken from a horror film soundtrack. The Kola scientists never reported any such thing.

Try this

Peel a hard-boiled egg and cut it in half with an adult's help. The thin shell is like Earth's crust, the white is like the mantle and the yolk is like the core. Now picture the deepest hole as a scratch far thinner than the shell itself.
