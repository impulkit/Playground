How did Dubai rise from the desert?

Less than 70 years ago, Dubai was a small town of pearl divers and traders beside a creek. Now it has the tallest building ever built.

The short answer

Dubai grew from a small trading town into a giant modern city in just a few decades. It began with a natural harbour, the Dubai Creek, and a ruling family that welcomed traders. Oil, found in 1966, paid for roads, ports and airports. But Dubai's oil was never huge, so its rulers used the money to turn the city into a hub for trade, flights, banking and tourism.

In 2025 Dubai passed 4 million people. Around nine in ten of them come from other countries, and the largest group is from India.

Surprising but true

Dubai is surrounded by desert, yet it has shipped in sand from elsewhere for building. Desert sand grains are too smooth and round to make strong concrete.

Pearls and the creek

In 1833, members of the Bani Yas tribe, led by the Al Maktoum family, settled by the creek. The Al Maktoums still rule Dubai today. Fishing and pearl diving were the main work. Divers held their breath for a minute or more as they dropped to the sea floor to collect oysters, coming up again and again through long hot days.

The pearl trade collapsed in the 1930s, when Japan began farming cheaper cultured pearls. Dubai then made its money from trade, with dhows, wooden sailing boats, carrying goods to and from India, Iran and East Africa. Old houses in the Al Fahidi area still have wind towers, tall chimneys that catch breezes and send them down into the rooms to cool them.

The rupee in the Gulf

For many years, Dubai and its neighbours used Indian money. From 1959 to 1966, the Reserve Bank of India even printed a special Gulf rupee for use in the region. Ties with India are still strong. Indian schools, temples and restaurants are found across the city, and flights connect Dubai to dozens of Indian cities.

Oil, then a big plan

In 1966, oil was found off Dubai's coast, and exports began in 1969. In 1971, Dubai joined six other emirates to form the United Arab Emirates. Its rulers knew the oil would not last forever. They dredged the creek, built Jebel Ali port, one of the largest human-made harbours in the world, and set up zones where foreign companies could do business easily.

Today oil makes up only a tiny share of Dubai's economy. Dubai International Airport is one of the world's busiest for international passengers.

Up into the sky

Dubai's skyline rose fast. The Burj Khalifa, opened in 2010, is about 828 m tall, more than twice the height of the Empire State Building. Its Y-shaped base and stepped shape help it stand up to desert winds, and its elevators are some of the fastest in the world. On a clear day you can see the top from about 95 km away.

Islands made of sea sand

Dubai also built islands. Palm Jumeirah, shaped like a palm tree, was made by spraying millions of cubic metres of sand dredged from the sea floor. You might wonder why they didn't use desert sand. Its grains have been rolled by wind until they are too smooth and round to pack firmly or make good concrete.

Living with the heat

Summer temperatures in Dubai often pass 40 °C, and sometimes 45 °C. Almost everything is air-conditioned, from malls to bus stops. Rain is rare, so drinking water comes from desalination plants that remove salt from seawater. These use lots of energy, and the city is building large solar farms in the desert. Out in the dunes, people still race camels, now often guided by small robot jockeys.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Dubai is the capital of the United Arab Emirates, or a country of its own.

Truth: Dubai is one of seven emirates that together form the United Arab Emirates. The capital of the UAE is Abu Dhabi, a separate emirate and city.

Try this

Test sand shapes. Fill one cup with round things such as dried peas, and another with broken pieces of dry pasta. Tip each cup upside down onto a plate and lift it. Which pile holds its shape better? Rough, angled grains lock together. Round ones roll apart.
