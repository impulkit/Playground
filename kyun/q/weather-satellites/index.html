How do weather satellites watch storms?

In 2013, satellites tracked Cyclone Phailin for days as it crossed the Bay of Bengal, and close to a million people were moved to safety before it hit.

The short answer

Weather satellites are cameras in space pointed at Earth. They take pictures of clouds, measure temperatures and sense water in the air, then beam the data to weather offices on the ground. Forecasters use the pictures to spot storms early and follow where they go.

There are two main kinds. Some sit very high up, about 35,800 kilometres above the equator, and watch the same half of the globe all the time. Others fly much lower, about 800 kilometres up, passing over the poles and scanning the whole planet in strips. Together they let forecasters see cyclones forming over empty ocean long before they reach land.

Surprising but true

A "geostationary" weather satellite is not standing still. It races around Earth at about 3 km every second, exactly fast enough to keep pace with the planet's spin.

Two ways to watch

A geostationary satellite circles Earth once a day, the same time Earth takes to spin. So it seems to hover over one place, like a lamp hanging over a table. It can photograph its half of the planet every few minutes, which makes it perfect for watching storms grow and move.

A polar-orbiting satellite is closer, so it sees finer detail. It sweeps from pole to pole while Earth turns beneath it, covering each place about twice a day. Its instruments can measure temperature and moisture at different heights in the air, which helps the computers that make weather forecasts.

Seeing in the dark

Clouds are easy to photograph in daylight, but storms don't stop at night. So weather satellites also see in infrared, a kind of light our eyes can't detect but which we feel as heat. Cold, high cloud tops show up clearly, and the coldest tops usually mean the strongest storms.

Another channel picks up water vapour, the invisible water in the air, showing rivers of moist air flowing across the oceans. Some low satellites use microwaves, which pass through cloud, to measure rain falling inside a storm or the speed of winds over the sea.

Satellites keep working after a storm has passed. Their pictures show flooded fields, swollen rivers and damaged forests, which helps rescue teams decide where to go first. Over months and years, the same measurements build a record of how sea temperatures, ice and clouds are changing.

The first pictures

On 1 April 1960, the USA launched TIROS-1, the first successful weather satellite. Its first fuzzy television pictures showed cloud patterns over North America, and forecasters were thrilled. It worked for only 78 days, but it proved the idea.

Within a few years, satellites were spotting hurricanes out in the Atlantic that ships and planes had missed. Today the USA's GOES satellites, Europe's Meteosat, Japan's Himawari and others share their pictures, so nearly the whole planet is watched all the time.

India's eyes on the monsoon

India's weather satellites watch the Indian Ocean, where the monsoon and cyclones are born. In 2002, ISRO launched its first satellite built only for weather. After the space shuttle Columbia disaster in 2003, it was renamed Kalpana-1 in honour of the astronaut Kalpana Chawla.

Later came the INSAT-3D series. INSAT-3DS, launched in February 2024, sends images and readings to the India Meteorological Department. Like other Indian satellites, it is built and run by ISRO.

A storm seen coming

In October 1999, a super cyclone struck Odisha. Warnings did not reach enough people in time, and about 10,000 people died. Fourteen years later, in October 2013, Cyclone Phailin headed for the same coast with similar strength.

This time, satellites tracked Phailin for days as it grew over the Bay of Bengal. Forecasters predicted where and when it would land, and officials moved close to a million people to shelters. Phailin damaged many homes and crops, but the death toll was far lower. Forecasters still launch weather balloons and use radar, but the view from space now gives coastal towns days, not hours, to prepare.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Geostationary satellites sit still in space.

Truth: They are moving fast, at about 11,000 km per hour. They only seem to stay still because they go round Earth in exactly the time Earth takes to spin once.

Try this

Pick a window with a view of the sky. Every hour for one afternoon, sketch the clouds and note the time. Line your sketches up in order and flip through them. Weather forecasters do the same thing with satellite pictures to see where storms are heading.
