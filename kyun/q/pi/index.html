What is pi?

Take any circle, from a one-rupee coin to a Ferris wheel, and its distance around is always a little more than three times its width.

The short answer

Pi is the number you get when you divide the distance around a circle, its circumference, by the distance across it, its diameter. It does not matter how big the circle is. The answer is always the same: about 3.14159. Mathematicians write it with the Greek letter π.

Pi's decimals go on forever and never fall into a repeating pattern. Computers have worked out more than a hundred trillion digits, and no pattern has ever appeared. Pi turns up all over maths and science, wherever circles, spheres, waves or spinning things are involved.

Surprising but true

NASA uses only about 15 decimal places of pi to steer spacecraft across the solar system, because more would make almost no difference.

Why it is always the same

All circles are the same shape. A small circle is just a big circle shrunk down, so the ratio between around and across never changes. Double the width of a circle and you double the distance around it too.

People noticed long ago that the distance around was a bit more than three times the width. The Bible describes a round tank about three times as far around as across. Ancient Egyptian and Babylonian scribes used values close to 3.16 and 3.125. Close enough for building, but not quite right.

Trapping pi

Around 250 BCE, the Greek mathematician Archimedes, who lived in Syracuse on the island of Sicily, found a clever way to pin pi down. He drew shapes with straight sides inside and outside a circle. The circle had to be bigger than the inner shape and smaller than the outer one. Using shapes with 96 sides, he showed that pi lies between 3 10/71 and 3 1/7, or about 3.1408 and 3.1429. Much of the maths of ancient Greece worked in this careful way.

Pi in India

In 499 CE, the Indian astronomer Aryabhata gave a value for pi that works out to 3.1416. He also described it as approximate, which some historians take as a hint that he knew it could not be written exactly.

Around 1400, Madhava of Sangamagrama, a mathematician in Kerala, found a way to calculate pi using an endless sum of fractions. Mathematicians in Europe found similar formulas about 250 years later. Much later, in the early 1900s, Srinivasa Ramanujan discovered formulas that produce many correct digits of pi very quickly. Versions of them are still used in record-breaking computer calculations.

A number that never ends

In 1761, the Swiss mathematician Johann Lambert proved that pi is irrational. That means it can never be written exactly as one whole number divided by another. Its decimals go on forever with no repeating pattern. In 1882 it was shown to be even stranger. No simple equation with whole numbers can produce it. That means you cannot construct a square with exactly the same area as a circle using only a ruler and compass.

The symbol π was first used for this number by the Welsh mathematician William Jones in 1706. It became popular after the great Swiss mathematician Leonhard Euler used it.

Pi Day and silly laws

In the United States, people write dates month first, so 14 March is 3/14. Many schools celebrate it as Pi Day, often by eating pie. It is also Albert Einstein's birthday.

In 1897 a bill in Indiana tried to set a wrong value for pi by law, after a local doctor claimed he had solved an ancient circle puzzle. It passed one part of the state government before a visiting mathematics professor helped stop it. The value of pi, like all of maths, cannot be decided by voting.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pi is exactly 22/7.

Truth: 22/7 is 3.142857..., a handy approximation that is correct to two decimal places. Pi is 3.14159... and cannot be written exactly as any fraction. Archimedes showed over 2,000 years ago that 22/7 is slightly too big.

Try this

Find five round things, like a plate, a bangle, a jar lid and a bucket. Wrap a piece of string around each one, then measure the string with a ruler. Measure the width across the middle too. Divide the distance around by the width, using a calculator if you like. Your answers should all be close to 3.1.
