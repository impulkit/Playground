Why doesn't food stick to non-stick pans?

The coating on a non-stick pan was discovered by accident in 1938, when a chemist opened a gas cylinder and found it full of slippery white powder instead.

The short answer

A non-stick pan is coated with a plastic called PTFE, short for polytetrafluoroethylene, and often sold under the name Teflon. PTFE is one of the most slippery materials ever made. Food has almost nothing to grab onto, so an egg that would glue itself to a steel pan slides right off.

The secret lies in its molecules. PTFE is a polymer, a long chain of carbon atoms. Each carbon is surrounded by fluorine atoms, which form a tight, smooth shield. Fluorine holds on to its own electrons very strongly, so other substances, including food, water and oil, have very little to stick to.

Surprising but true

Non-stick coating is so slippery that it is hard to make it stick to the pan itself. Makers roughen the metal first so the coating can grip into tiny pits.

An accident in a laboratory

On 6 April 1938, a young chemist named Roy Plunkett was working for the DuPont company in New Jersey. He was trying to make a new chemical for refrigerators. He had stored a gas in metal cylinders, but when he opened a valve, nothing came out, even though the cylinder still weighed as much as before.

Curious, Plunkett and his assistant cut the cylinder open. Inside was a waxy white powder. The gas had joined up into long chains, forming a brand-new substance that was slippery, did not melt easily and resisted almost every chemical they tried. It became one of the best-known accidental inventions.

From fishing line to frying pan

At first, PTFE was used in factories and scientific equipment, where its resistance to chemicals was useful. Then, in France in the early 1950s, an engineer named Marc Grégoire coated his fishing tackle with it to stop lines from tangling. His wife, Colette, suggested he try it on her cooking pans.

It worked. In 1956, Grégoire started a company called Tefal to sell non-stick pans. By the early 1960s, they had reached the USA. Today they are in kitchens everywhere, including Indian ones where non-stick tawas turn out dosas and rotis with very little oil.

Making slippery stick

Here is the puzzle. If PTFE sticks to almost nothing, how does it stay on the pan? Makers roughen the pan's metal surface first, by blasting it with tiny particles or etching it. Then they spray on a primer and several layers of coating and bake the pan at high temperature.

The coating melts into the tiny pits and ridges and grips them as it hardens. It is held on mostly by shape, not glue. That is also why scratching it with metal spoons can make it peel.

Using it safely

Non-stick pans are safe for normal cooking. But PTFE starts to break down if it gets very hot, above about 260 °C. An empty pan left on a high flame can reach that in a few minutes. The fumes can give people flu-like symptoms for a short time, and they can be deadly to pet birds, whose lungs are very sensitive.

So cooks are advised to use low or medium heat, never heat a non-stick pan empty, and use wooden or silicone spoons. A chemical once used in making PTFE, called PFOA, raised health concerns and was phased out by major US makers by 2015.

The old way

Long before PTFE, cooks made their own non-stick surfaces. A cast-iron tawa rubbed with oil and heated again and again builds up a dark, hard layer. The oil turns into a kind of natural plastic that food slides off. That is why many grandmothers in South India still swear by an old, well-used iron dosa tawa, and why American cooks talk about "seasoning" their cast-iron skillets. Both are examples of the science of cooking at work.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Non-stick coating was invented for the space programme.

Truth: PTFE was discovered in 1938, almost twenty years before the first satellite. Space missions did use it later, which may be why the story stuck.

Try this

With an adult, put a drop of honey on three surfaces, such as a steel plate, a piece of baking paper and a sheet of plastic food wrap. Tilt each one slowly and watch which drop slides first. Smooth, slippery surfaces with less grip let the honey move easily, just as food slides on a non-stick pan.
