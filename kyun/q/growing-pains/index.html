What are growing pains?

Growing pains have had that name for about 200 years. Doctors are now fairly sure they have little to do with growing.

The short answer

Growing pains are aches in the legs that many children get, usually between about 3 and 12 years old. They tend to come in the late afternoon, evening or night, often in both legs, in the calves, the fronts of the thighs or behind the knees. Some nights the ache is strong enough to wake a child up. By morning it's gone.

Despite the name, doctors don't think growing causes them. Nobody knows the exact cause. The good news is that growing pains are harmless, leave no lasting damage and fade as children get older.

Surprising but true

Growing pains usually strike in the evening or at night, while children actually grow fastest in babyhood and the teenage growth spurt, not in the years when these pains are most common.

A name from 1823

In 1823, the French doctor Marcel Duchamp wrote about children with aches he called "douleurs de croissance", or pains of growth. The name was catchy and spread around the world.

But it doesn't hold up well. Bones grow slowly and steadily through childhood, with the fastest growth in babyhood and during the teenage growth spurt. Growing pains are most common in the years between, and nobody has found that children with them grow faster. The aches are also felt in muscles, not at the ends of bones where growth happens.

What might cause them

Doctors have several ideas, and the answer might be a mix.

One idea is simple tiredness. Growing pains often come after a day full of running, jumping and climbing, a bit like the ache adults feel after unusual exercise. Another is that some children are more sensitive to pain in general. Growing pains also tend to run in families. A few studies have looked at whether low vitamin D or less-dense bones play a part, but the results aren't clear yet.

Researchers in Australia found in 2004 that over a third of children aged 4 to 6 in their study had pains matching the description. Other studies give lower figures, partly because doctors don't all define growing pains the same way.

What helps

Growing pains usually go away by themselves, and there are simple things that can make a bad night easier. Many children find that gentle massage of the sore legs by a parent helps. Warmth, like a warm wheat bag or a warm bath, can relax the muscles.

Stretching the calves and thighs, especially before bed, helps some children. Athletes use similar stretches, and sports scientists study how stretching and rest help muscles recover. If the pain is bad, a parent can ask a doctor or pharmacist what else might help.

When to see a doctor

Growing pains have a typical pattern, and doctors check for anything that doesn't fit it. Tell a parent or trusted adult if the pain is in only one leg or one spot, or in a joint like the knee or ankle. Swelling, redness or limping are other reasons to speak up. So is pain during the day that stops you playing, a fever, or feeling unwell in other ways.

These signs don't usually mean anything serious, but they do mean a doctor should take a look. Growing pains are what doctors call a diagnosis of exclusion: they decide it's growing pains once they've ruled other causes out.

Growing out of them

Most children stop getting growing pains by their early teenage years, often well before. Anyone woken up by aching legs can take comfort that they leave no lasting harm. Plenty of adults remember them, and plenty of children who had them go on to run marathons, dance and play every sport there is.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Growing pains are caused by bones growing faster than the body can handle.

Truth: Bones grow slowly and steadily, and growing pains don't match times of fast growth. They also happen in muscles, not in bones or joints. The name has stuck even though the explanation hasn't.

Try this

Learn a simple calf stretch. Stand facing a wall with your hands on it, one foot forward and one back. Keep your back heel on the floor and lean forward gently until you feel a stretch in the back leg. Hold for 15 seconds, then swap legs. Some children find stretching before bed helps.
