Why do bruises change colour?

A bruise is a slow colour show under your skin, from red to purple to green to yellow, as your body cleans up spilled blood.

The short answer

A bruise forms when a bump breaks tiny blood vessels called capillaries under your skin, but the skin itself stays unbroken. Blood leaks out and pools there. You see it through the skin as a dark patch.

The colours change because your body slowly breaks that trapped blood down and carries it away. Red blood gets its colour from haemoglobin. As the body takes haemoglobin apart, it turns into new substances one after another, and each has a different colour: blue-purple, then green, then yellow and brown. Then it fades.

Surprising but true

The green in a bruise comes from biliverdin, the same substance that turns the blood of some lizards in New Guinea bright green.

Why fresh bruises look blue

Spilled blood is dark red. But the skin lets through some colours of light better than others. Red light passes into skin and is scattered, while blue light bounces back from closer to the surface. So a patch of dark blood under the skin often looks blue or purple, even though it is red.

Deep bruises can take a day or more to show, because the blood takes time to spread nearer the surface.

The clean-up crew

Soon after the bump, your immune system sends clean-up cells called macrophages to the spot. Their name means "big eaters". They swallow the spilled red blood cells and break up the haemoglobin.

First they split off the iron, which the body keeps and reuses. What is left is turned into biliverdin, a green substance. Biliverdin is then turned into bilirubin, which is yellow. Bilirubin travels in the blood to your liver, which gets rid of it in bile.

Brown edges and iron

Some of the iron is stored in a substance called haemosiderin, which is golden-brown. This can make an old bruise look brownish for a while, especially on the legs. It is a little like the orange-brown of rust, since both come from iron.

The edges of a bruise often turn yellow before the middle, because the clean-up works from the outside in.

Why some people bruise easily

Children often have bruises on their shins and knees. Those parts hit furniture, football boots and playground slides, and there is little padding between skin and bone.

Older people bruise more easily because their skin gets thinner and their blood vessels more fragile. Some medicines that thin the blood also make bruises more likely. If someone gets lots of bruises without knowing why, it is worth telling a doctor.

Deep bruises and different skin

Not all bruises are just under the skin. A hard knock can bruise a muscle, making it stiff and sore, and a heavy blow can even bruise a bone. These deep bruises may never show on the surface at all, but doctors can see them on scans.

Bruises also look different on different skin. On darker skin, a fresh bruise may look dark purple, brown or almost black rather than blue, and the green and yellow stages can be harder to spot. Doctors are taught to look for swelling and tenderness as well as colour.

Black eyes and ice packs

A black eye is simply a bruise around the eye. The skin there is thin and loose, so blood spreads easily and the colours show clearly.

A cold pack wrapped in a cloth, held on a fresh bruise for a few minutes, can narrow the blood vessels and limit the swelling. After that, there's not much to do but wait. In about two weeks, most bruises have vanished, and the iron from that blood may already be inside new red blood cells.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You can tell exactly how old a bruise is from its colour.

Truth: Studies have found that even doctors can't reliably date a bruise by its colour. Bruises change at different speeds depending on where they are, how deep they are, and the person's age and skin.

Try this

When you get an ordinary bruise, draw it in a notebook every day with coloured pencils. Note the date and the colours you see. Over a week or two you will make your own record of the colour changes, and see how the edges fade first.
