Why is shooting an Olympic sport?

In 10-metre air rifle, the perfect "10" is a dot half a millimetre wide, about the size of a full stop.

The short answer

Shooting is an Olympic sport because it tests skills that are very hard to master: stillness, precision and calm under pressure. It has been part of the modern Olympics since the first Games in Athens in 1896. Its roots are in hunting and the military, but as a sport it is about hitting paper or electronic targets and clay discs, never living things.

Olympic shooting has three main kinds: rifle, pistol and shotgun. Athletes compete at set distances, such as 10, 25 and 50 metres. Ranges have strict safety rules, and coaches, judges and range officers watch every shot.

Surprising but true

Pierre de Coubertin, the Frenchman who started the modern Olympics, was himself a keen pistol shooter, and shooting was on the programme at the very first modern Games in 1896.

The size of a full stop

In the 10-metre air rifle event, the target's centre "10" ring is a dot just 0.5 millimetres across. Electronic targets measure where each pellet lands, even scoring tenths of a point, like 10.6 or 10.9.

At 10 metres, that is like hitting a grain of sand at the far end of a classroom. To do it, shooters wear stiff jackets and special shoes to stay steady. They aim through sights, breathe slowly and press the trigger so gently that the rifle barely moves.

Heartbeats and breath

When you are nervous, your heart beats faster and your hands shake a little. In shooting, that tiny shake can ruin a score. So shooters train their minds as much as their bodies.

Many use breathing exercises, meditation and visualisation, picturing a perfect shot before making it. Top shooters often fire in the brief pause after breathing out. Some even learn to feel their pulse and shoot between beats, which is a kind of extreme mindfulness.

Abhinav Bindra's gold

On 11 August 2008, in Beijing, Abhinav Bindra won the 10-metre air rifle. It was India's first ever individual Olympic gold medal. He was so focused that he barely smiled at first.

Bindra had trained for years, even practising on a range his family built at home. Four years earlier, in Athens, the army officer Rajyavardhan Singh Rathore had won silver in double trap, a shotgun event. These two medals helped make shooting one of India's most successful Olympic sports. Bindra later wrote that after winning he felt strangely empty, because the goal he had chased for years was suddenly gone. Many athletes say the same.

The Paris medals

In 2012 Gagan Narang won bronze and Vijay Kumar silver in London. Then came a long gap. In Paris in 2024 India's shooters came back strongly.

Manu Bhaker, from Haryana, won bronze in the 10-metre air pistol and another bronze with Sarabjot Singh in the mixed team event. She became the first Indian since independence to win two medals at the same Olympics. Swapnil Kusale added a third bronze, in the 50-metre rifle three positions event, where shooters fire kneeling, lying down and standing.

Shotguns and clay discs

In shotgun events, machines fling orange clay discs through the air at high speed. Shooters must swing, aim and fire in less than a second. A shotgun fires a spread of tiny pellets, which helps hit a fast-moving disc.

Shooting has been popular in the USA for a long time. American shooters such as Kim Rhode have won Olympic medals at six Games in a row. As with archery, the target is always the challenge, and the best shooters say the hardest opponent is their own nerves.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Shooting sport is just about having good eyesight.

Truth: Good vision helps, but the hardest part is staying still and calm under pressure. Shooters train their muscles, breathing and concentration for years, and many top shooters wear glasses.

Try this

Fill a cup almost to the brim with water and hold it at arm's length. Count how long you can keep it perfectly still before the surface ripples. Try again after five slow breaths. Shooters train to stay this still for many seconds.
