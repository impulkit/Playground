What is a jury?

In 1670, a London judge locked up a jury without food because they refused to find William Penn guilty. The jurors won, and juries have been free to decide ever since.

The short answer

A jury is a group of ordinary citizens chosen to listen to the evidence in a court case and decide the facts. In a criminal trial, they decide whether the accused person is guilty. In some civil cases, they decide who was in the wrong and how much money should be paid.

Juries are used in the USA, the UK, Canada, Australia and some other countries. American criminal juries usually have 12 members. The judge runs the trial and explains the law, but the jury reaches the verdict. The idea is that the community itself, not just officials, should decide whether a person loses their freedom.

Surprising but true

India once used juries in some criminal trials, but phased them out. The famous Nanavati murder trial of 1959 is often linked to their end.

Judgment by your peers

The roots go back to medieval England. In 1215, the Magna Carta promised that free men would not be punished except by the "lawful judgment of their peers", meaning equals, or by the law of the land. Over the following centuries, juries of local people became part of English justice.

When the American colonies broke away, they wrote jury trials into the US Constitution. The Sixth Amendment, from 1791, promises a speedy public trial by an impartial jury in criminal cases. The Seventh protects juries in many civil cases.

The jury that wouldn't give in

In 1670, the Quaker preacher William Penn was put on trial in London for preaching in the street. The jury refused to find him guilty of unlawful assembly. The furious judge locked them up without food, water or heat and ordered them to change their minds. They would not.

The jurors were fined and jailed. One of them, Edward Bushel, challenged this in a higher court and won. The ruling, known as Bushel's Case, established that jurors cannot be punished for their verdict. Penn later founded the American colony of Pennsylvania.

How a jury works

Jurors are usually picked at random from lists of voters or residents. Being called is known as jury duty, and most adults are expected to serve if asked. Lawyers for both sides may question possible jurors and remove some who seem biased.

During the trial, jurors listen to witnesses and look at evidence. They are told not to read news about the case or discuss it with anyone. At the end, they go to a private room to talk it over. In a criminal case, they must be sure of guilt "beyond reasonable doubt". If they cannot agree, the trial may be held again. The 1957 film 12 Angry Men shows how this can play out: one juror doubts the evidence and, over one hot afternoon, slowly persuades the other eleven.

Why India stopped using juries

Under British rule, some criminal trials in India's presidency towns, such as Bombay, used juries. In 1959, a naval officer, K. M. Nanavati, was tried in Bombay for shooting a man. Newspapers backed him loudly, and the jury found him not guilty. The judge sent the case to the High Court, which overturned the verdict.

Many people argued the jury had been swayed by publicity. The case is often said to have hastened the end of jury trials. India's 1973 criminal procedure code did not provide for them, and today trained judges decide cases. People still argue about which system is fairer. Supporters of juries say serving on one is among the most important duties of a citizen. Supporters of judges point to their training and experience.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A jury decides what the law is.

Truth: The judge explains the law. The jury decides the facts, such as what happened and whether the evidence proves guilt beyond reasonable doubt.

Try this

Watch or read a simple mystery story with friends, then hold a mock jury. Each person argues whether a character is guilty, using only the evidence in the story. To decide, everyone must agree. Notice how hard it is to persuade the last person.
