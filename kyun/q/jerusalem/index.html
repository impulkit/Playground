Why is Jerusalem important to so many people?

Inside one walled old city, smaller than a square kilometre, stand some of the holiest places of Judaism, Christianity and Islam.

The short answer

Jerusalem is an ancient city in the hills between the Mediterranean Sea and the Dead Sea. Its walled Old City is smaller than a square kilometre, yet it holds sacred places for three of the world's great religions. For Jews, it is the site of the ancient Temple and the holiest city on Earth. For Christians, it is where Jesus taught, died and, they believe, rose from the dead. For Muslims, it is the third holiest city after Mecca and Medina.

Because so many people care so deeply about it, Jerusalem has been fought over many times. Today, both Israelis and Palestinians see it as their capital, and its future is one of the hardest questions in world politics.

Surprising but true

For centuries, the key to the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, one of the holiest places in Christianity, has been looked after by Muslim families.

The Western Wall

Jewish tradition says King David made Jerusalem his capital about 3,000 years ago, and his son Solomon built the first Temple. A second Temple later stood on a vast stone platform. In 70 CE, the armies of ancient Rome destroyed it.

A section of the huge wall that held up the platform survives. It is called the Western Wall, or the Kotel. Jews from all over the world come to pray there. Many write prayers on small slips of paper and tuck them into the cracks between the giant stones. Twice a year the notes are gathered and buried respectfully on the Mount of Olives nearby.

The Dome of the Rock and Al-Aqsa

On top of that same platform, which Muslims call the Noble Sanctuary and Jews call the Temple Mount, stand two important Islamic buildings. The Dome of the Rock, finished around 691 CE, covers a rock that is sacred in both Islam and Judaism. Its golden dome is the most famous sight on the city skyline.

Next to it is the Al-Aqsa Mosque. Muslims believe the Prophet Muhammad was carried here in one night on his Night Journey, before rising to heaven. In the earliest years of Islam, Muslims faced Jerusalem when they prayed, before the direction changed to Mecca.

The church and its key

A short walk away is the Church of the Holy Sepulchre. Many Christians believe it was built over the place where Jesus was crucified and buried. Several Christian groups share the building, and they have strict rules about who looks after which corner, which lamps and even which steps.

To keep the peace between them, the church door has for centuries been opened each morning by members of Muslim families. One family keeps the large iron key, another opens the door. It is a small daily ritual in a city where neighbours of different faiths have often found ways to share space.

Walls, stone and a disputed future

The walls around the Old City were built in the 1530s by the Ottoman sultan Suleiman the Magnificent. Inside are four quarters, Jewish, Christian, Muslim and Armenian, packed with narrow lanes, markets and homes. Under a rule dating from British times, new buildings are faced with pale local limestone, so the whole city glows gold at sunset.

Israel controls the whole city and calls it its capital. Palestinians want East Jerusalem, which includes the Old City, as the capital of a future Palestinian state. Most countries keep their embassies in Tel Aviv while the question is unresolved, though the USA moved its embassy to Jerusalem in 2018.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Western Wall is a wall of the ancient Jewish Temple itself.

Truth: The Temple was destroyed by the Romans in 70 CE. The Western Wall is part of the huge retaining wall built around the platform the Temple stood on.
