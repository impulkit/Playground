Why does a roti puff up?

A perfect roti blows up like a balloon in a few seconds, and the gas inside is the same thing that rattles a pressure cooker.

The short answer

A roti puffs because of steam. Roti dough is mostly wheat flour and water. When the rolled disc is cooked on a hot tawa, both faces set into thin, tough skins. Then it meets a direct flame, and the water trapped between the skins suddenly turns to steam.

Steam takes up well over a thousand times more room than the water it came from. The skins can't let it out fast enough, so the roti swells into a ball. Wheat's stretchy gluten holds it together. Press it and the steam puffs out with a soft hiss.

Surprising but true

When water turns into steam, it takes up well over a thousand times more space, which is why a few drops in the dough can inflate a whole roti.

Flat bread, no yeast

Roti, also called chapati, is an unleavened bread. That means it has no yeast or baking soda to make gas. All its lift comes from water and heat, which is why it can be made in minutes.

The word chapati is often linked to the Hindi word chapat, a slap, from the way dough was once flattened between the palms. The puffed version is called phulka, from phoolna, to swell. Roti is eaten across north and central India, and in many homes it is still made fresh for every meal.

The science of the puff

Making a good puffing roti is quiet physics. The dough must be soft, with enough water, and rested so the gluten can relax. It must be rolled to an even thickness, because a thin spot will tear and leak steam.

On the tawa, the first side is cooked lightly and the second side a little more, so both have a skin. On the flame, the heat is so strong that water near the middle boils almost at once. The same thing happens in a pressure cooker, where trapped steam builds force until the whistle blows.

What happens when water boils?The big bubbles in a boiling pot aren't air. They are pockets of water that has turned into gas.

A bread that travelled

Indian workers who went abroad in the 1800s and 1900s took roti with them. Many were indentured labourers, workers bound to a contract, sent to sugar estates in the Caribbean, Fiji and East Africa. In Trinidad and Tobago, "roti" now often means a flatbread wrapped around curry and sold as street food. In Kenya and Uganda, chapati is an everyday food.

In the USA, rotis are sold frozen in Indian grocery stores from New Jersey to California. There are even countertop machines that knead, roll and cook a roti on their own.

The biggest roti kitchen

At the Golden Temple in Amritsar, the community kitchen, called a langar, serves free meals to everyone who comes, whatever their religion. On busy days it feeds many tens of thousands of people. Volunteers roll rotis by hand and machines help to keep up.

The langar tradition began with Guru Nanak, the first Sikh Guru, more than 500 years ago, as a way to show that everyone sits as equals. A plate of dal and hot rotis, eaten sitting side by side on the floor, is still at the centre of it.

Why rotis go stiff

A fresh roti is soft, but leave it out for an hour and it turns leathery. As it cools, its starch slowly hardens and the leftover water escapes. Wrapping rotis in a cloth or keeping them in a closed casserole traps the steam and keeps them soft until the thali reaches the table.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A roti puffs because air gets trapped in it while the dough is kneaded.

Truth: Kneading adds very little air. The puff is steam, made in seconds when water in the dough meets intense heat. Kneading helps by building stretchy gluten that holds the steam in.
