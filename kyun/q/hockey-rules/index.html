How is hockey played?

In field hockey you may only hit the ball with the flat side of your stick, and a goal counts only if the shot comes from inside a marked semicircle.

The short answer

Field hockey is played by two teams of 11, each with a goalkeeper, on a rectangular pitch about the size of a football field. Players use a curved stick to dribble, pass and hit a hard ball into the other team's goal. A match has four quarters of 15 minutes, and the team with more goals wins.

What makes hockey different from football is the stick. You may only touch the ball with the flat face of the stick, not the rounded back, and outfield players can't kick it. Goals count only if an attacker touches the ball inside the shooting circle, a semicircle in front of each goal.

Surprising but true

India's men won eight Olympic gold medals in hockey between 1928 and 1980, more than any other country, including six in a row from 1928 to 1956.

The stick and the ball

A hockey stick is flat on one side and rounded on the other, with a hooked end. Players hold it with the flat side facing the ball. Using the rounded side is a foul. To move the ball to their left, skilled players flip the stick over with a quick twist of the wrists.

The ball is solid plastic, about the size of a cricket ball but harder and smoother. On modern artificial pitches, which are watered before games to make the surface fast, it can be hit at well over 100 km/h. Players wear shin guards and mouth guards. Goalkeepers wear helmets and big padded kickers, and they are the only players allowed to use their feet or body in their own circle.

Fouls and cards

Players can't hit another player's stick, obstruct an opponent with their body or lift the ball dangerously. When a foul happens outside the circle, the other team gets a free hit.

Umpires show cards for bad fouls. A green card sends a player off for two minutes. A yellow card means at least five minutes off. A red card means the player is out for the rest of the match. Teams play a player short while someone is off, much like in ice hockey.

Penalty corners

The most exciting set piece is the penalty corner. It is given when defenders foul inside their own circle, or sometimes just outside it. An attacker pushes the ball from the back line to teammates waiting at the top of the circle.

Up to five defenders, including the goalkeeper, rush out from the goal to block it. The attackers stop the ball and then shoot, or "drag-flick" it, slinging it towards goal in one smooth movement. Top drag-flickers like India's Harmanpreet Singh score many of their team's goals this way. A penalty stroke, a one-on-one shot from a spot 6.4 metres out, is given for fouls that stop a likely goal.

India's golden era

Modern field hockey rules were drawn up in England in the 1800s, and the game spread through the British Empire. India took to it brilliantly. Between 1928 and 1956, India's men won six Olympic gold medals in a row, led for many years by the great Dhyan Chand. They won two more, in 1964 and 1980.

When the game moved from grass to artificial turf in the 1970s, faster, more physical styles took over, and India struggled for decades. In Tokyo in 2021, the men won bronze, their first Olympic medal in 41 years. They won bronze again in Paris in 2024. The women's team reached the semi-finals in Tokyo, finishing fourth.

Hockey on ice

In the United States and Canada, "hockey" usually means ice hockey. It is played on skates, with six players a side, a flat rubber disc called a puck and sticks with a flat blade. Players can bump into each other, and the game is very fast.

Field hockey in the US is mostly played by women and girls in schools and colleges, though the national teams include both men and women. Both games share a stick, a goal and a love of speed. Like the rules of cricket, the rules of each have been tweaked over the years to keep matches fair and exciting, and the umpires have plenty to watch.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Hockey is officially India's national game.

Truth: Many textbooks used to say so, and hockey has a special place in Indian sporting history. But in 2012 the Government of India said, in reply to a Right to Information request, that it has never officially declared any sport the national game.

Try this

Mark a semicircle in chalk on a playground, about a big step or two from a "goal" made of two shoes. Using a broom handle or rolled-up newspaper, try pushing a soft ball only with one flat side. Play a round where goals only count from inside the semicircle, and see how it changes the game.
