How does a fridge keep things cold?

A fridge doesn't make cold. It picks up heat from your food and dumps it out of the back.

The short answer

A fridge is a heat mover. Inside its walls, a special liquid called a refrigerant flows round and round through thin pipes. When the refrigerant evaporates, turning from liquid to gas in the coils inside the fridge, it soaks up heat from the food and air around it. That's why the inside gets cold.

The gas then flows to a pump called a compressor, usually the humming box at the bottom of the fridge. The compressor squeezes the gas hard, which makes it hot. The hot gas flows through coils at the back or sides of the fridge, where it gives its heat to the kitchen air and turns back into a liquid. Then it squeezes through a very narrow valve, cools sharply, and goes back inside to soak up more heat. Round and round, all day and night.

Surprising but true

In 1833, a ship sailed from Boston, USA, to Calcutta carrying about 180 tonnes of ice cut from frozen ponds. Around 100 tonnes survived the four-month voyage, and it sold.

Cooling by drying

You already know the trick behind a fridge. When you climb out of a swimming pool on a windy day, you shiver, even in summer. As water evaporates from your skin, it carries heat away with it. Your body uses the same idea when it sweats.

In India, a clay matka or surahi keeps drinking water cool for the same reason. Water seeps slowly through the tiny holes in the clay and evaporates from the outside of the pot, taking heat with it. A fridge does the same thing, but in a sealed loop, using a liquid that evaporates far more easily than water.

Ice that crossed the world

Before fridges, the only way to keep things really cold was ice. In winter, workers in the north-eastern USA sawed blocks of ice from frozen lakes and stored them under sawdust. A businessman from Boston named Frederic Tudor began shipping this ice to hot places. In 1833, one of his ships, the Tuscany, reached Calcutta, now Kolkata, after about four months at sea. Nearly half the ice had melted on the way, but the rest sold quickly to people desperate for a cold drink.

Ice became a luxury in British India. In Madras, now Chennai, a round building on the beach was built in 1842 to store Tudor's ice. Later it became famous as the place where Swami Vivekananda stayed in 1897, and today it is called Vivekanandar Illam.

Machines take over

During the 1800s, inventors in the USA and Europe built machines that made cold using evaporating liquids and compressors. At first they were huge and used for breweries, meat packers and ships. By the 1920s and 1930s, electric fridges small enough for a kitchen were spreading in American homes. In India, fridges became common in middle-class homes much later, in the decades after the 1980s.

Fridges changed what people eat. Milk, meat, vegetables and leftovers could last for days instead of hours. Cooking and shopping changed too, since families no longer needed to buy fresh food every day.

A problem in the sky

For decades, many fridges used gases called CFCs. They were safe and did not burn, but when old fridges leaked or were thrown away, the gases drifted high into the sky. There they damaged the ozone layer, which shields Earth from harmful sunlight.

In 1987, countries signed the Montreal Protocol, agreeing to phase out these gases. Modern fridges use different refrigerants, and the ozone layer is slowly healing. Some of the newer gases still trap heat and warm the planet, so engineers keep looking for better ones, including gases as simple as the propane-like isobutane found in many new fridges.

Myth or fact?

Myth: An open fridge or freezer door can cool down a hot room.

Truth: A fridge only moves heat from one place to another, and its motor adds heat as it works. With the door open, the room gets slightly warmer. That's why air conditioners have a part outside the window.

Try this

Feel evaporation cooling. Wet one finger with water and leave another dry, then blow gently on both. The wet one feels much colder, because the evaporating water carries heat away from your skin. Then, with an adult, carefully touch the side or back of a running fridge and notice that it feels warm.
