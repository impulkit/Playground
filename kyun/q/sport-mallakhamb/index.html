What is mallakhamb?

In mallakhamb, gymnasts climb a greased wooden pole and then balance upside down on top of it, using it like a partner in a wrestling match.

The short answer

Mallakhamb is a sport from India in which athletes do gymnastics and yoga-like poses on a wooden pole or a hanging rope. They climb, twist, hang upside down, lock their legs around the pole and hold impossible-looking shapes, all in a few breathless minutes.

The pole is about 2.6 m tall, smooth and polished, and thicker at the bottom than the top. Athletes rub castor oil and dust their hands so they can grip and slide. The sport is strongest in Maharashtra and Madhya Pradesh, and in 2013 Madhya Pradesh made it its official state sport. Strength, balance and courage come together in it, much like gymnastics without the springboard.

Surprising but true

The name means "wrestler's pole". Wrestlers once practised their holds on a wooden post because it never got tired.

A wrestler's training partner

The name says it all. Malla means wrestler and khamb means pole. Wrestlers once practised grips and throws on a wooden post, which never got tired and never complained. Over time, the training became a sport of its own.

It is mentioned in the Manasollasa, a Sanskrit book about royal life written in 1135 CE by King Somesvara III of the Chalukya dynasty. The modern form is credited to Balambhatt Dadadeodhar, who was a fitness teacher for the court of the Peshwa Bajirao II in Pune in the early 1800s. Even today, it shares a home with Indian wrestling in many akharas.

Pole, rope and hanging

There are three main kinds. Pole mallakhamb uses the fixed wooden pole. Rope mallakhamb uses a thick cotton rope hanging from above. Athletes wrap it around their arms and legs and climb into poses, without using knots. Rope mallakhamb became especially popular with girls.

Hanging mallakhamb is a shorter pole that hangs from hooks and chains, so it swings while the athlete moves. That makes balance even harder.

Many poses come from yoga and are given names such as the peacock or the bow. The mix of strength and stretch is close to what researchers study in yoga science.

Shown at the Berlin Olympics

In 1936, a club from Amravati, Maharashtra, travelled to the Berlin Olympics and showed Indian sports, including mallakhamb and kho-kho. European spectators had never seen anything like it.

For decades after, mallakhamb remained mostly a school and club sport in India. National championships began in 1962, and the Mallakhamb Federation of India organises competitions today.

Going viral

Mallakhamb has had a big comeback. Teams performed on TV talent shows, and short videos of children on the rope spread on social media. In 2019, Mumbai hosted the first Mallakhamb World Championship, with athletes from countries including the United States, Germany, Japan and Malaysia.

Some players never thought of themselves as athletes. In Bastar, Chhattisgarh, a group of village children trained in mallakhamb became famous for their performances and won national medals.

Physics on a pole

Watch closely and you can see clever physics. Athletes keep their weight close to the pole, so they need less strength. When they stick their legs out sideways, the pole works like a lever, and the farther out the legs go, the harder the muscles must work.

Coaches train beginners, children and adults alike, to build core strength first, because the belly and back muscles hold every pose together. It is also why many Indian martial arts, like kalaripayattu, start with similar body training.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Mallakhamb is a new sport invented for TV talent shows.

Truth: Mallakhamb appeared on TV talent shows such as India's Got Talent, which made it famous, but it is centuries old. It is described in a 12th-century text and has been practised in Maharashtra for more than 200 years.

Try this

Try a "pole hold" with a friend spotting you on a soft floor. Hold a sturdy table leg or bed post with both hands, lie on your side with legs straight, and see how long you can keep your body off the ground. You'll feel how much core strength mallakhamb needs. Stop if it hurts.
