What is a particle accelerator?

Under the border of France and Switzerland, scientists race tiny particles around a 27 km ring at nearly light speed and smash them together.

The short answer

A particle accelerator is a machine that speeds up tiny particles, such as electrons, protons or the centres of atoms, using electric and magnetic forces. It gives them a lot of energy and then aims them at a target or at each other.

Scientists use the biggest accelerators to smash particles together and study what comes flying out. That tells them what matter is made of at the smallest scale. But accelerators also have everyday jobs. Hospitals use small ones to treat cancer, and factories use them to make computer chips. If you're old enough to remember a fat, old box TV, that was a tiny accelerator too. It fired a beam of electrons at the screen to make the picture.

Surprising but true

Most particle accelerators in the world aren't in physics labs. They're in hospitals, where they help treat cancer, and in factories.

How it works

Particles like protons and electrons carry an electric charge. That means electric fields can push them, the same way a charged balloon can pull your hair. Each push adds a bit more speed.

A linear accelerator lines up many pushing sections in a straight tube, and particles get faster down its length. A circular one sends them around a ring, giving them a push each lap. Powerful magnets bend their path so they stay on the circle. Inside the tubes, the air is pumped out so the particles don't crash into air molecules.

The Large Hadron Collider

The biggest accelerator in the world is the Large Hadron Collider, or LHC, run by CERN near Geneva. Its ring is about 27 km around and sits about 100 m underground, crossing the border between Switzerland and France.

Two beams of protons travel in opposite directions, each going at more than 99.9999 per cent of the speed of light. They loop around the ring about 11,000 times a second. At four points, the beams cross and protons collide. Huge detectors, some as tall as an eight-storey building, record the spray of new particles.

The magnets that steer the beams are cooled with liquid helium to about minus 271 °C, colder than outer space. At that temperature, their wires carry electricity with no resistance.

Finding the Higgs

On 4 July 2012, scientists at CERN announced that they had found a new particle, the Higgs boson. Physicists had predicted it nearly 50 years earlier. It is linked to a field that gives other basic particles their mass.

Finding it took thousands of scientists from dozens of countries, including India. Indian physicists have helped build and run two of the LHC's big detectors, and India became an associate member of CERN in 2017.

Accelerators in India and the US

India has accelerators of its own. At Indore, the Raja Ramanna Centre for Advanced Technology runs two rings called Indus-1 and Indus-2. As electrons whizz around them, they give off very bright light, including X-rays, which scientists use to study materials, medicines and even ancient objects.

The United States has big accelerator labs too. Fermilab, near Chicago, ran the Tevatron, the world's most powerful collider until the LHC took over. At Stanford in California, SLAC has a straight accelerator about 3 km long.

Small machines, big jobs

Most accelerators are small. In hospitals, machines called linacs, short for linear accelerators, fire electron beams that make X-rays to destroy cancer cells. They are carefully aimed so healthy tissue is spared as much as possible.

Factories use accelerators to plant tiny amounts of other elements into silicon when making chips. Others kill germs on medical tools and some foods. Ernest Lawrence's first cyclotron, a circular accelerator built in California around 1930 to 1931, was small enough to hold in your hand. The LHC is the same idea, made wide enough to circle a city.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Large Hadron Collider could make a black hole that swallows the Earth.

Truth: Scientists checked this carefully before it was switched on. Cosmic rays from space hit Earth and the Moon with even more energy all the time, and they have never made a dangerous black hole. The LHC has run for years without any problem of this kind.

Try this

Rub a balloon on your hair or a woolly jumper, then hold it close to a thin stream of water from a tap. The stream bends towards the balloon. The balloon's electric charge is pulling on the water, which is the same kind of force accelerators use to push particles faster and faster.
