Who was Confucius?

A teacher who lived in China about 2,500 years ago has a family tree recorded for more than 80 generations, with around two million registered descendants.

The short answer

Confucius was a teacher and thinker who lived in ancient China about 2,500 years ago. In Chinese he is called Kongzi or Kong Fuzi, meaning Master Kong. The name Confucius comes from the Latin version made by European visitors to China in the 1600s.

He taught that people should be kind, honest and respectful, that families should care for one another, and that rulers should govern by good example rather than by force. His students wrote down his sayings in a book called the Analects. Over the centuries, his ideas shaped how people in China, Korea, Japan and Vietnam thought about family, education and government.

Surprising but true

For about 1,300 years, until 1905, people who wanted top government jobs in China had to pass exams based on the books linked to Confucius.

A poor boy who loved learning

Confucius was born, by tradition in 551 BCE, in the small state of Lu, in what is now Shandong province. His father died when he was very young, and his family was poor. He worked at ordinary jobs, such as looking after a nobleman's grain stores and animals, while teaching himself history, poetry, music and ceremonies.

China at the time was split into rival states that often fought each other. Confucius believed the troubles came from people forgetting how to behave well. He hoped to become an adviser to a ruler and fix things from the top.

Teaching anyone who wanted to learn

Confucius never got the high government job he wanted, at least not for long. He travelled from state to state for years, looking for a ruler who would follow his advice, and found none.

But he became famous as a teacher. Unusually for his time, he said he would teach anyone who brought even a small gift, whether rich or poor. Tradition says he had thousands of students. His teaching was mostly conversation, and the Analects is full of short exchanges in which a student asks a question and the Master answers.

What he taught

One of his central ideas is ren, often translated as kindness or humaneness. When a student asked for one word to guide a whole life, Confucius is recorded as answering: do not do to others what you would not want done to yourself. Many cultures have a similar rule, sometimes called the Golden Rule.

He also cared deeply about respect within families, especially children's respect for parents and grandparents. He believed that good manners and ceremonies, done sincerely, helped people live together in peace. And he thought a ruler should be like the wind, with ordinary people like grass that bends whichever way the wind blows, so a good ruler makes a good country.

Exams, schools and an empire

After his death in 479 BCE, his followers kept teaching his ideas. A few centuries later, emperors of the Han dynasty made Confucian teaching the official philosophy of the state.

From around 605 CE, China chose many government officials through written exams based on the Confucian classics. Candidates studied for years and sat in tiny exam cells for days. The system lasted until 1905. It meant that, in theory, anyone who studied hard could rise to power, though in practice wealthy families had a big advantage. Many historians see it as an early model for the exams that schools and governments use today.

Praised and criticised

Not everyone admired Confucius. In the early 1900s, young Chinese reformers blamed his ideas for keeping women, young people and the poor in their place. During the Cultural Revolution of the 1960s and 1970s, Confucian temples were damaged and his followers were attacked.

In recent decades, China has honoured him again. His hometown, Qufu, has a vast temple, his family mansion and a cemetery where his descendants are buried, all now a UNESCO World Heritage Site. In Taiwan, Teachers' Day is celebrated on 28 September, his traditional birthday.

Great teachers of one age

Confucius lived at about the same time as the Buddha in India, and shortly before Socrates in Greece. None of them wrote the books that made them famous. Their students did, and the questions they asked about how to live are still being asked in classrooms today.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Funny sayings that begin "Confucius say..." are real quotes from Confucius.

Truth: Those jokes were made up, mostly in the USA in the 20th century, and have nothing to do with him. His real sayings, recorded in the Analects, are about learning, kindness, family and good government.
