How do lizards climb walls?

A gecko doesn't use glue or suction to cling to your ceiling. It sticks using forces so tiny they act between single molecules.

The short answer

Most lizards can't walk up a wall. The famous wall-climbers are geckos, including the small, pale house lizard that chirps "tik-tik-tik" near tube lights across India.

A gecko's toes look smooth, but under a microscope they are covered in ridges carrying hundreds of thousands of tiny hairs called setae. Each hair splits at the end into hundreds of even smaller flat tips, far thinner than a human hair.

Those tips get so close to the wall that a very weak attraction between molecules, called the van der Waals force, kicks in. One tip on its own holds almost nothing. Billions of them together can hold the gecko's whole weight, even upside down.

Surprising but true

A gecko's toes are covered in hundreds of thousands of microscopic hairs, and each hair splits into hundreds of even tinier tips.

Sticking without glue

For a long time, people guessed that geckos used suction cups or some kind of glue. Neither is true. Their feet are dry and leave no sticky trace.

In 2000, a team led by the American biologist Kellar Autumn measured the pull of a single gecko hair for the first time. The result was tiny, but when they multiplied it by all the hairs on a foot, the numbers were huge. A gecko could, in theory, hang from a single toe.

The trick works on almost anything smooth: glass, painted walls, polished stone. It even works in a vacuum. It fails on surfaces that attract nothing, like a non-stick pan, and it weakens when the feet are wet.

Letting go fast

Sticking is only half the problem. A gecko racing after a moth has to unstick each foot in a split second, many times a second.

It does this by peeling, not pulling. Geckos curl their toes up from the tip, like peeling sticky tape off a parcel. Changing the angle of the hairs breaks their grip almost instantly. Then the toe uncurls and slaps down again.

Geckos also keep their feet clean without washing. Dirt particles stick to the wall more strongly than to the tiny hairs, so a few steps rub them off.

Not every lizard climbs

There are more than 7,000 kinds of lizard, and they live in very different ways. Monitor lizards climb trees with sharp claws. The garden lizard you see bobbing its head on Indian walls, with a red throat in the breeding season, uses claws too. Many desert lizards never climb at all.

Even among geckos, not all have sticky pads. The leopard gecko, a popular pet in the USA, has small claws instead. Anoles, the little green lizards of Florida and the Caribbean, grew pads of their own separately.

Living with house geckos

The Indian house gecko spends its nights near lights, waiting for insects. A single lizard can eat a lot of mosquitoes, moths and flies. Many families are glad to have one, even if they jump when it falls off the ceiling. House geckos are most active at night, and their big eyes see well in dim light. During the day they hide in cracks, behind cupboards and under roof tiles.

If you grab a house gecko, it may drop its tail. The tail keeps wriggling to distract a predator while the lizard escapes. A new, slightly shorter tail grows back over a few weeks.

Gecko science at work

Engineers have copied gecko feet to make dry tapes that grip and release cleanly. Stanford University built a climbing robot called Stickybot, and NASA engineers have tested gecko-style grippers for catching objects in space, where ordinary suction doesn't work. It's a lesson in friction and stickiness written into the toes of an animal that also has to hide from hungry birds.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The common house lizard is poisonous, and food it falls into becomes deadly.

Truth: House geckos are not venomous. Like many animals, they can carry germs such as Salmonella, so it's sensible to throw away food they've touched and wash hands, but the lizard itself is not poison.

Try this

Press a dry sheet of cling film flat onto a clean window and smooth it with your hand. It stays put with no glue, because thin, flexible things can get very close to a surface. A gecko's toe hairs use the same idea at a far smaller scale.
