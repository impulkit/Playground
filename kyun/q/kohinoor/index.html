What is the story of the Kohinoor diamond?

The Kohinoor was taken by a Persian invader, a Sikh maharaja and a British queen, and in 1852 it was cut down to little more than half its size.

The short answer

The Kohinoor is a diamond of 105.6 carats, about the weight of seven one-rupee coins. It was probably mined in the Golconda region of south India, centuries ago. It passed between Mughal emperors, a Persian conqueror, Afghan rulers and Maharaja Ranjit Singh of Punjab. In 1849, after the British East India Company defeated the Sikh kingdom, it was taken and sent to Queen Victoria.

Today it sits in a crown in the Tower of London, where millions of visitors see it each year. India, Pakistan, Afghanistan and Iran have all claimed it at different times. The British government's position is that it was handed over legally under a treaty. Many people in South Asia see it as a symbol of colonial loot.

Surprising but true

When the Kohinoor was recut in London in 1852, it lost about 43% of its weight, and many people thought it looked less impressive afterwards.

A diamond with a misty past

Diamonds from Golconda, near today's Hyderabad, were famous across the world for centuries. These mines produced many legendary stones, and the Kohinoor was very likely one of them.

Its early story is hazy. The Mughal emperor Babur wrote about a huge diamond in 1526, and some historians think it was the Kohinoor. Others disagree. Legends say it was worn by gods or ancient kings, but there is no evidence for those stories. The first clear sighting comes from 1739, in Delhi.

Mountain of light

In 1739, the Persian ruler Nader Shah invaded Delhi and carried off the Mughals' treasures, including the jewelled Peacock Throne. Some accounts say the diamond was set into the throne itself. A popular story says that when Nader Shah first saw the stone, he cried "Koh-i-noor!", meaning "Mountain of light". The story may not be true, but the name stuck.

After Nader Shah was killed in 1747, the diamond passed to Ahmad Shah Durrani, whose empire was the forerunner of modern Afghanistan. His family kept it for decades. In 1813, the exiled Afghan ruler Shah Shuja handed it to Maharaja Ranjit Singh in Lahore, who wore it on his arm.

What made Golconda fort special?Clap your hands under the main gate of Golconda fort in Hyderabad, and the sound can be heard at the hilltop pavilion about a kilometre away.

To the queen

Ranjit Singh died in 1839, and his kingdom fell into turmoil. After two wars with the East India Company, Punjab was taken over in 1849. A treaty forced the ten-year-old Maharaja Duleep Singh to give up the Kohinoor to the Queen of England.

The stone was shipped to Britain in 1850 and shown at the Great Exhibition in London in 1851. Crowds lined up, but many were let down. Uncut, it looked more like a lump of glass than a blaze of fire.

Cut down to sparkle

In 1852, Prince Albert, Queen Victoria's husband, had it recut to make it shine. The work took 38 days. The diamond shrank from about 186 carats to 105.6 carats, losing about 43% of its weight. It sparkled more, but some experts thought the cut was poor.

Since then it has been worn only by queens. It was set into the crown made for Queen Elizabeth, the Queen Mother, in 1937. At the coronation of King Charles III in 2023, Queen Camilla wore a different crown, a choice many people saw as a way to avoid controversy.

Who owns it

Since India's independence in 1947, Indian politicians and campaigners have asked for the diamond back several times. Pakistan, Afghanistan and Iran have also claimed it. Britain has always said no.

Both sides have serious arguments. Some point to the treaty. Others say a treaty forced on a defeated ten-year-old can't be fair. The question is part of a much bigger debate about objects taken in colonial times and kept in museums. For now, the mountain of light stays in the Jewel House, among the other crown jewels, behind thick glass, glowing for tourists.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Kohinoor has always been the biggest diamond in the world.

Truth: It isn't even close today. The Cullinan diamond, found in South Africa in 1905, weighed about 3,106 carats before cutting. Its largest piece, about 530 carats, is five times heavier than the Kohinoor and sits in the British royal sceptre.

Try this

See how cutting changes sparkle. Shine a torch onto a smooth glass marble in a dark room, then onto a cut-glass object like a bowl or a glass with a patterned base. Notice how many more flashes of light the cut glass throws on the wall. Diamond cutters shape stones the same way, adding flat faces to bounce light around.
