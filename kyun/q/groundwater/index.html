What is groundwater?

Beneath your feet, rainwater is soaking slowly through the rock. India pumps up more of it than any other country, about a quarter of all the groundwater used on Earth.

The short answer

When rain falls on soil, some of it runs off into streams and some evaporates. The rest soaks down. It trickles between grains of soil and sand and into cracks in rock until it reaches a layer where every gap is already full of water. That hidden water is groundwater.

The top of the soaked layer is called the water table. A layer of rock or sand that holds a lot of usable water is called an aquifer. When you dig a well or drill a borewell deep enough, you reach it, and water seeps in. Groundwater is a quiet part of the water cycle: it can stay underground for days or for thousands of years before it returns to the surface.

Surprising but true

Some of the water pumped from deep under deserts fell as rain thousands of years ago, when the Sahara was green.

A sponge, not a lake

People often picture underground lakes, but rock holds water more like a kitchen sponge. Sandstone, gravel and cracked limestone have lots of tiny spaces, so they make good aquifers. Clay and solid granite let very little water through.

Groundwater moves, but very slowly, from higher ground to lower ground. Where the water table meets the surface, it comes out as a spring, or it feeds a river. Many rivers keep flowing in the dry season because groundwater is quietly topping them up.

How long water stays underground varies hugely. Water in shallow sand may reach a stream within weeks. Water in deep rock can sit for thousands of years. Under parts of the Sahara, wells pump up water that fell as rain when the desert was green, many thousands of years ago, and it is not being replaced.

India's hidden reservoir

India uses more groundwater than any other country. Most of it goes to farms. During the Green Revolution, electric pumps and cheap electricity let farmers draw water up from deep below, and harvests soared.

Today groundwater supplies most of India's irrigation and most drinking water in villages. Millions of borewells pump it up. In parts of Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan, the water table has dropped so far that wells have to be drilled deeper every few years. NASA's twin GRACE satellites, which measured tiny changes in Earth's gravity, even detected the water disappearing from under northern India from space.

How did stepwells store water?Chand Baori in Rajasthan has about 3,500 narrow steps zigzagging down 13 levels, so that people could reach the water however low it fell.

Filling the ground back up

Aquifers refill when rain soaks in, which is called recharge. Concrete, roads and roofs stop that from happening, so cities often drain their own groundwater. Chennai faced a severe shortage in 2019 when its reservoirs ran dry and many wells failed.

People across India are trying to put water back. Rainwater harvesting sends rain from roofs into pits and old wells instead of drains. Tamil Nadu made it compulsory for buildings in 2003. Farmers in parts of Rajasthan and Gujarat have rebuilt small earth dams and village ponds that hold water long enough for it to soak in. India's old stepwells were built for exactly this, storing the monsoon underground.

Problems below the surface

Groundwater can carry things we cannot see. In parts of West Bengal, Bihar and Bangladesh, some wells contain arsenic that dissolves naturally from rocks. In other areas there is too much fluoride. Testing well water is the only way to know. In Bangladesh, millions of tube wells have been tested, and their spouts painted red if the water is unsafe or green if it is fine to drink.

Pumping too much can make land sink, as it has in Mexico City and parts of California. In the United States, the huge Ogallala Aquifer under the Great Plains waters much of the country's wheat and corn, and in places it is falling faster than rain can refill it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Groundwater flows in big underground rivers and lakes.

Truth: A few caves do have underground streams, but nearly all groundwater sits in tiny spaces between grains of sand and in cracks in rock, like water in a sponge. It moves very slowly, often only a few centimetres a day.

Try this

Fill a clear glass with dry gravel or small pebbles, then slowly pour in water and watch it fill the gaps. The water level you see is like a water table, and the pebbles are like an aquifer. Now try the same with fine sand and see which one soaks up water faster.
