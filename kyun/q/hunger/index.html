How does your body know it's hungry?

In 1912 a medical student swallowed a balloon on a tube so scientists could watch his stomach squeeze whenever he felt hungry.

The short answer

Hunger is a message from your body to your brain saying "time to refuel". Several signals work together. Your stomach releases a hormone called ghrelin when it has been empty for a while, and ghrelin travels in the blood to the brain. Your blood sugar levels, your fat stores and even your daily routine add their own signals.

All this information arrives at the hypothalamus, a small area deep in the brain. It weighs the "eat now" signals against "I'm full" signals and creates the feeling we call hunger. Once you eat, the stomach stretches and the gut releases other hormones that tell your brain to stop.

Surprising but true

Your stomach rumbles not because it's empty, but because its muscles keep squeezing to sweep it clean. With no food inside to muffle the noise, you hear the gurgles.

The balloon experiment

In the early 1900s, the American physiologist Walter Cannon wanted to know whether hunger came from the stomach. His student Arthur Washburn trained himself to swallow a small balloon attached to a tube. Once it was in his stomach, the balloon was blown up a little, and any squeeze of the stomach pushed air up the tube to a recording device.

Washburn pressed a key each time he felt a hunger pang. The pangs matched the stomach's squeezes. In 1912, they published their results. Later scientists found that people whose stomachs had been removed still felt hungry, so the stomach can't be the whole story.

Hormones of hunger and fullness

Two discoveries in the 1990s changed the picture. In 1994, Jeffrey Friedman's team in New York found leptin, a hormone made by fat cells. The more fat the body stores, the more leptin it releases, telling the brain there is energy to spare.

In 1999, Japanese scientists Masayasu Kojima and Kenji Kangawa discovered ghrelin. It rises when the stomach is empty and before regular mealtimes, and falls after eating. It's the main hormone that says "eat".

When you start eating, the stretching stomach and intestines release fullness hormones. These signals take a little time to build, which is one reason why eating slowly can help you notice when you've had enough.

Why your stomach rumbles

Every couple of hours on an empty stomach, waves of muscle squeezes sweep through your stomach and intestines, pushing leftover bits along. Gas and liquid gurgle as they're squeezed. With food inside, the noise is muffled. With no food, it echoes, and everyone in the classroom hears it.

Rumbling doesn't always mean you're hungry, and you can be hungry without rumbling. The sound is a clue, not a reliable signal.

Hunger versus appetite

Hunger is your body needing food. Appetite is wanting food, and it can be switched on by smell, sight and habit. The smell of fresh jalebis or popcorn can make you want to eat even straight after dinner. Around 1900, the Russian scientist Ivan Pavlov showed that dogs started to drool at signals that meant food was coming.

Foods packed with sugar, salt and fat, like many types of junk food, can be easy to keep eating even when you're full. Lack of sleep can also push up hunger hormones.

When hunger is serious

Feeling hungry before a meal is normal. Going hungry for long periods is not. Children who don't get enough to eat find it harder to concentrate and grow. That is why programmes such as India's mid-day meal scheme and school lunch programmes in the USA provide food for millions of children every school day.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Your stomach shrinks if you eat less, so you'll stop feeling hungry.

Truth: An adult stomach doesn't permanently shrink from eating less. Feelings of hunger come mostly from hormones and the brain, not just from how big or empty the stomach is.

Try this

Keep a hunger diary for a day. Every hour, rate how hungry you feel from 0 (not at all) to 5 (starving) and note what you last ate and when. At the end, draw a graph. Look for patterns, like hunger rising near mealtimes or after running around.
