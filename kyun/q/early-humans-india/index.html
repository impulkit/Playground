When did the first people reach India?

Stone hand axes found near Chennai are about 1.5 million years old, made by a kind of human that lived long before our own species existed.

The short answer

The story has two parts. The first people of any kind in India were not our own species. Early humans, probably related to a species called Homo erectus, were making stone tools in South India by about 1.5 million years ago.

Our own species, Homo sapiens, evolved in Africa and spread out much later. Most genetic studies suggest modern humans reached India roughly 50,000 to 65,000 years ago, travelling along the coasts of Arabia and Iran. Some archaeologists think they may have arrived earlier, perhaps before 74,000 years ago. This is still debated. Either way, people lived in India for tens of thousands of years before the first villages and cities appeared.

Surprising but true

About 74,000 years ago, a giant volcano called Toba erupted in Sumatra, and its ash fell across India. Archaeologists use that ash layer like a bookmark in the soil to date the stone tools above and below it.

Hand axes near Chennai

At Attirampakkam, about 60 km from Chennai, archaeologists led by Shanti Pappu dug through layers of river clay and gravel. They found teardrop-shaped stone hand axes and cleavers. In 2011, they reported that the oldest tools there were about 1.5 million years old.

That makes them some of the oldest hand axes found outside Africa. Whoever made them had walked a very long way. No bones of the toolmakers have been found at the site, so we don't know exactly which human species they were.

In 2018, the same team reported smaller, more carefully shaped tools from the site, about 385,000 years old. They suggested that new ways of making tools appeared in India earlier than many had believed.

A skull by the river

In 1982, a geologist named Arun Sonakia found part of a fossil skull in the banks of the Narmada at Hathnora, in Madhya Pradesh. It's still the oldest human fossil found in India.

Experts have argued about it ever since. Some thought it belonged to Homo erectus. Others see features closer to later humans. Estimates of its age range widely, from around 250,000 years to much younger. It's a reminder that India's early human story has big gaps that future finds may fill.

The volcano bookmark

About 74,000 years ago, the Toba volcano in Sumatra erupted with enormous force. A layer of its ash settled over much of India and can still be found in the ground today.

At Jwalapuram, in Andhra Pradesh, archaeologists found stone tools both below and above this ash. Some argued that the tools below it were made by modern humans, which would mean our species arrived before 74,000 years ago. Others think those tools were made by earlier human species, which would fit with genetic studies pointing to a later arrival.

Painting on rock walls

At Bhimbetka, in the forests of Madhya Pradesh, hundreds of rock shelters were lived in for tens of thousands of years. Their walls carry paintings of hunters, dancers, deer, bison and elephants in red and white. Some are thought to be around 10,000 years old or older, though dating rock art is difficult. Bhimbetka became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2003, and it holds some of the oldest known cave paintings in India.

Their descendants today

DNA studies show that almost everyone in India today carries ancestry from those first modern humans. Later, other groups arrived too, including early farmers from the region of Iran and herders from Central Asia, and their ancestries mixed over thousands of years.

So India's population is a blend of very old and newer roots. Scientists study this mixing by comparing DNA from living people with DNA from ancient skeletons, including one woman buried at the Indus city of Rakhigarhi about 4,500 years ago. Long before the Indus cities were built, hunters and gatherers had already learned India's rivers, forests and seasons.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The first people in India were the builders of the Indus cities.

Truth: The Indus cities are about 4,500 years old. Modern humans had already lived in India for tens of thousands of years, and earlier human species had been making tools there for more than a million years.

Fill a clear jar with layers of different things, like sand, then a thin layer of tea leaves, then soil, then more sand. Push a few beads into different layers. Now imagine you're an archaeologist. Which beads went in first? The tea-leaf layer works like the Toba ash, a marker you can use to say "older" or "younger".
