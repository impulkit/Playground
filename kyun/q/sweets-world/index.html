What sweets do children eat around the world?

Candy floss was made popular by a dentist. William Morrison and a sweet-maker sold it as "Fairy Floss" at the 1904 World's Fair in St. Louis.

The short answer

Children everywhere love sweets, but what counts as a treat changes from place to place. In India it might be a gulab jamun or a piece of barfi. In Japan, a chewy mochi. In Brazil, a chocolate ball called a brigadeiro. In the United States, a bag of Halloween candy.

Many of these sweets are tied to festivals, family recipes and local farms. They use whatever grows nearby, such as rice in Japan, nuts in Turkey, milk in India and cocoa in the Americas. Together they tell a story about history, trade and celebration.

Surprising but true

The English words "sugar" and "candy" both trace back to Sanskrit, from sharkara and khanda, because India was among the first places to make crystal sugar.

Where sugar began

Sugarcane was first grown in New Guinea thousands of years ago. But turning its juice into dry crystals was worked out in India, more than 1,500 years ago. The Sanskrit word for the grains, sharkara, travelled through Persian and Arabic and became "sugar" in English. A lump of it, khanda, gave us "candy".

Traders carried sugar along routes to the Middle East and Europe, where it was so costly it was called "white gold". Today it's cheap, which is why sweets are everywhere.

India's milky treats

Indian sweets often start with milk, boiled down for hours into a thick paste called khoa. That becomes barfi, peda and the dough for gulab jamun, which are fried and soaked in syrup. Bengal is famous for spongy rasgullas made from chhena, a fresh cheese.

Both West Bengal and Odisha say the rasgulla was born on their land. In 2017 West Bengal received a GI tag, an official label linking a product to a place, for its "Banglar Rasogolla". In 2019 Odisha got one for "Odisha Rasagola". Both sides are proud, and both sweets are delicious.

What is daily life like in Japan?In most Japanese schools there are no cleaners. After lunch, the students sweep, mop and scrub their own classrooms, corridors and even toilets.

Rice, nuts and pastry

In Japan, families pound steamed sticky rice into soft mochi, especially at New Year. Some are filled with sweet red bean paste. Children also love dango, small rice balls on a stick, often glazed with a sweet soy sauce that tastes salty and sugary at once.

In Turkey and the Middle East, baklava layers paper-thin pastry with chopped nuts and honey or syrup. Spanish and Mexican children dip churros, long ridged doughnuts, into thick hot chocolate. In Argentina, dulce de leche, slowly cooked sweet milk, goes into almost everything.

A Brazilian party sweet

Brigadeiros are small balls of condensed milk, cocoa and butter, rolled in chocolate sprinkles. A popular story says they were created in the 1940s and named after Brigadier Eduardo Gomes, who was running for president of Brazil. Today no Brazilian children's birthday party is complete without them. Children often help roll the sticky mixture into balls with buttered hands, which means a lot of licking of fingers.

In Australia, lamingtons, squares of sponge cake dipped in chocolate and rolled in coconut, are a school bake-sale favourite.

Candy floss and Halloween

In 1897 a dentist named William Morrison and a sweet-maker named John C. Wharton came up with an electric machine that spun melted sugar into fine threads. They sold it as "Fairy Floss" at the 1904 World's Fair in St. Louis. Americans now call it cotton candy, and Indians often call it buddhi ke baal, or old lady's hair.

In the United States, Halloween is a giant sweets festival. Children dress up and go door to door collecting chocolate bars and chewy candy. Indian children have their own sweet-filled festivals, from Diwali boxes of mithai to tilgul for Makar Sankranti.

Most sweets are mainly sugar, which feeds the germs that cause tooth decay. Dentists suggest eating them with a meal rather than nibbling all day, and brushing well afterwards.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Sugar makes children hyperactive.

Truth: Many studies, including ones where nobody knew who had eaten sugar, found no effect on behaviour. But too much sugar is still bad for teeth and health, so sweets are best as an occasional treat.
