How do planes land on ships?

A fighter jet landing on an aircraft carrier goes from about 240 km/h to a complete stop in roughly two seconds, caught by a steel cable across the deck.

The short answer

An aircraft carrier's runway is far too short for a normal landing, so carrier planes are built to stop in a hurry. Each one has a strong steel hook, called a tailhook, hanging under its tail. Stretched across the back of the deck are three or four thick steel cables, called arresting wires.

The pilot flies down at a steep, steady angle and slams the plane onto the deck instead of floating down gently. The hook snags one of the wires. Machinery below deck lets the wire pay out under huge resistance, and the plane goes from about 240 km/h to a stop in roughly 100 metres. Pilots call it a "controlled crash".

Surprising but true

The moment a jet's wheels hit the carrier deck, the pilot pushes the engines to full power. If the hook misses the wire, the jet must be able to take off again straight away.

The very first landing

On 18 January 1911, an American pilot named Eugene Ely flew a flimsy biplane towards the warship USS Pennsylvania in San Francisco Bay. A wooden platform had been built over the ship's back deck. Ropes stretched across it were tied to bags of sand on each side.

Hooks on Ely's plane caught the ropes, and the sandbags dragged him to a stop. It was the first time a plane had landed on a ship. The idea was simple, and modern carriers still use the same basic trick, with steel cables and hydraulic machinery in place of rope and sand.

A runway that moves

Landing on a carrier is one of the hardest things a pilot can do. The deck is small, it moves forward as the ship sails, and in rough seas it rises and falls. At night, it is a dim rectangle in a black ocean.

To help, carriers have a landing deck angled to one side. If a plane misses the wires, it can fly off the side without hitting parked aircraft at the front. A row of special lights shows the pilot whether the plane is too high or too low. An officer on deck, watching every landing, talks to the pilot by radio and can order them to go round again.

Getting back into the air

Taking off is the opposite problem: getting a heavy jet up to flying speed on a short deck. Big American carriers use catapults, which fling the plane forward, from standstill to over 200 km/h in about two seconds. Older ones use steam, while the newest, USS Gerald R. Ford, uses powerful electric motors.

India's carriers use a ski-jump instead. The front of the deck curves upward, so planes leave the ship angled into the sky. That gives them a few extra seconds to build speed before they need to fly. It helps to know how planes fly: a wing needs fast-moving air over it to make enough lift.

India's carriers

India has had aircraft carriers since 1961. The first INS Vikrant, bought from Britain, played a part in the 1971 war in the Bay of Bengal. Today the Indian Navy operates INS Vikramaditya, a converted Russian ship, and a new INS Vikrant.

The new Vikrant was designed in India and built at Cochin Shipyard in Kerala. It was commissioned on 2 September 2022. It is about 262 metres long, roughly the length of two and a half football fields, and its pilots are naval aviators, not members of the Indian Air Force.

Cities at sea

The largest American carriers are over 330 metres long and carry more than 5,000 people, with kitchens, hospitals, gyms and a post office on board. They weigh about 100,000 tonnes and still float, because their huge hulls push aside a weight of water greater than their own.

Not every aircraft needs the wires. Helicopters can land almost anywhere on the deck, and some jets, like the F-35B, can slow down and drop vertically. For most carrier jets, though, every landing still comes down to a hook, a wire and the pilot's nerve.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Planes land on aircraft carriers by braking very hard with their wheels.

Truth: Wheel brakes alone would need a runway far longer than any ship. Most carrier jets stop by catching a steel cable with a hook under the tail. The cable is linked to machinery below deck that pays it out and slows the jet smoothly.

Try this

Make a mini arresting wire. Stretch a rubber band between two heavy books on a smooth table. Bend a paperclip into a hook and tape it under a toy car. Push the car towards the band and see if the hook catches. Try different speeds and see how far the band stretches before the car stops.
