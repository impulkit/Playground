What was the Cold War?

For more than forty years, the USA and the Soviet Union raced, spied and built enough bombs to end the world, yet never fought each other directly.

The short answer

The Cold War was a long rivalry, from about 1947 to 1991, between the United States and the Soviet Union, a communist country centred on Russia. Both had been allies in the Second World War, but they had very different ideas about how countries should be run.

The USA believed in elections and private businesses. The Soviet Union was ruled by a single Communist Party, which controlled the economy. Each side feared the other would spread its system across the world. They never fought each other directly, because both had nuclear weapons and a war could have destroyed them both. Instead they competed in almost everything else.

Surprising but true

The famous hotline between Washington and Moscow, set up in 1963, was never a red telephone. It began as a pair of teleprinters that typed out messages.

Two camps

After 1945, Soviet troops stayed in the countries of eastern Europe they had freed from Germany, and communist governments took charge there. In 1946, Britain's Winston Churchill said an "iron curtain" had come down across Europe.

The USA and western European countries formed a military alliance, NATO, in 1949. The Soviet Union and its allies formed the Warsaw Pact in 1955. Germany itself was split in two, and later its old capital was divided by the Berlin Wall.

The bomb

The USA dropped the first atomic bombs in 1945. The Soviet Union tested its own in 1949. Both sides then built thousands of ever more powerful weapons, a contest called the arms race.

The same science powered new nuclear power stations, but the weapons made people everywhere nervous. Some American families built fallout shelters in their gardens, stocked with tinned food and water. Both sides also tested huge bombs in deserts, on remote islands and underground.

Thirteen days

The scariest moment came in October 1962. The USA discovered that the Soviet Union was secretly placing nuclear missiles in Cuba, only about 150 km from Florida. US President John F. Kennedy surrounded Cuba with navy ships. For thirteen days, the world waited.

Finally, Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev agreed to remove the missiles. Secretly, the USA agreed to remove its own missiles from Turkey. Afterwards, the two sides set up a direct hotline so their leaders could talk quickly in a crisis.

Racing for space

Rockets that could carry bombs could also carry satellites. In 1957 the Soviet Union launched Sputnik, the first satellite. Americans were shocked, and the space race began. It ended with Americans walking on the Moon in 1969.

What was Sputnik?In October 1957, a metal ball the size of a beach ball began beeping from space, and the whole world stopped to listen.

Wars by other names

Although the superpowers avoided fighting each other, they backed opposite sides in wars elsewhere, including Korea from 1950 to 1953, Vietnam and Afghanistan. Millions of people died in these conflicts.

India's path

India did not want to join either camp. Nehru helped found the Non-Aligned Movement in 1961, with leaders from Egypt, Yugoslavia, Indonesia and Ghana. India took help from both sides: American wheat and Soviet steel plants. In 1971, India signed a friendship treaty with the Soviet Union, and the two countries stayed close for years.

How it ended

In the 1980s, the Soviet economy was struggling. A new leader, Mikhail Gorbachev, allowed more freedom of speech and tried to reform the system. Across eastern Europe, people protested peacefully against their governments. On 9 November 1989, the Berlin Wall opened.

In 1991, the Soviet Union split into 15 separate countries, the largest being Russia. The Cold War was over. The two sides still had thousands of nuclear weapons, but the fear of a sudden world-ending war began to fade.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Cold War was called "cold" because it was fought in snowy places.

Truth: It was "cold" because the two superpowers never fought each other directly in a "hot" war. Instead they competed through spying, weapons, space and wars fought by other countries.
