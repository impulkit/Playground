Why do some stories get told for thousands of years?

A clever monkey, a crocodile and a heart left in a tree. You may know this story. It's about 2,000 years old.

The short answer

Stories that last often have clever heroes, tricky problems and a lesson tucked inside.

They change as they travel. Each storyteller adds a little, so one tale can have hundreds of versions.

Telling stories might be one of the oldest things humans do, maybe as old as language itself. Long before writing, people remembered stories by telling them again and again. The ones that were easy to remember, useful to know and fun to hear were the ones that survived.

Surprising but true

Some Panchatantra tales reached Europe over a thousand years ago and turned up in other famous fable collections.

The monkey and the crocodile

The Panchatantra is a collection of animal fables from ancient India. Tradition says a wise teacher named Vishnu Sharma told them to three lazy princes, to teach them how to live wisely. The stories sit inside one another, like boxes within boxes. A character starts telling a tale, and inside that tale another character tells another.

In one famous story, a crocodile befriends a monkey who gives him sweet fruit. The crocodile's wife wants to eat the monkey's heart, so the crocodile carries the monkey across the river on his back. Halfway, he admits the plan. The quick-thinking monkey says he left his heart in the tree, and must go back to fetch it. He escapes, and the friendship ends.

Stories that crossed the world

Around 570 CE, a Persian doctor named Burzoe translated the Panchatantra into Middle Persian. About two centuries later, a writer named Ibn al-Muqaffa turned it into Arabic as Kalila wa Dimna. From there it was translated into Hebrew, Latin, Spanish, German and many more languages.

In 1678, the French writer Jean de La Fontaine mentioned an Indian sage, "Pilpay", as a source for some of his fables. The scholar Franklin Edgerton counted over 200 versions of the Panchatantra in more than 50 languages.

Remembering without books

India's great epics, the Ramayana and the Mahabharata, were sung and told aloud for generations before being written down. The Vedas were memorised so carefully, with special chanting patterns, that they were passed on almost word for word for thousands of years. UNESCO recognises this Vedic chanting as part of the world's cultural heritage.

Repetition, rhythm and patterns helped. Many stories use things in threes, like three wishes or three brothers. Animals stand for human types: the clever fox, the boastful lion, the crafty crow. These patterns make stories easier to remember and retell.

How old can a story be?

The Epic of Gilgamesh, from Mesopotamia, was written on clay tablets about 4,000 years ago. But some stories may be older than writing itself. In 2016, two researchers compared folk tales across many languages, using methods biologists use to build family trees. They suggested that a tale called "The Smith and the Devil" might be around 6,000 years old. Not everyone agrees, but it is a clever way to look into the past.

Cinderella-style stories turn up all over the world too. A Greek writer told one about a girl named Rhodopis over 2,000 years ago, and a Chinese version, about a girl named Ye Xian, was written down in the 800s CE.

Old stories, new forms

Old stories keep finding new homes. In 1967, Anant Pai started Amar Chitra Katha to tell Indian history and legends as comics. In 1986, R.K. Narayan's stories about Malgudi, a small South Indian town, became a much-loved TV show. Today, films, games and apps retell the same tales, and a child in Chennai and a child in Chicago may both know the monkey who left his heart in the tree.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Every folk tale has one true, original version.

Truth: Most folk tales have hundreds of versions, told differently in different places and times. Scholars can sometimes trace a tale's family tree, but there is rarely a single "correct" original.

Try this

Play "story telephone". Sit in a circle with friends or family, and whisper a short story to the first person, who retells it to the next, and so on. Ask the last person to tell it aloud. Notice what stayed the same, what changed and what was added. Old stories changed in the same way over centuries.
