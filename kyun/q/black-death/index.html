What was the Black Death?

Between 1347 and 1351, a disease spread across Europe so fast that it killed perhaps a third or more of all the people living there.

The short answer

The Black Death was a massive outbreak of plague that swept across Asia, the Middle East, North Africa and Europe in the mid-1300s. In Europe, the worst years were 1347 to 1351. Historians estimate it killed between a third and half of Europe's people, perhaps 25 million or more.

Plague is caused by a bacterium called Yersinia pestis. It usually lives in rodents such as rats and is spread by their fleas. When infected fleas bite people, they can pass on the disease. In some cases it also spreads directly from person to person through coughing. Without medicine, most people who caught it died within days.

Surprising but true

The bird-beaked masks we link with plague doctors were not worn during the Black Death. They were first described about 300 years later, in the 1600s.

Where it came from

The plague probably began in Central Asia. In 2022, scientists studied skeletons from two old cemeteries in the Chüy Valley of Kyrgyzstan, not far from Lake Issyk-Kul, where gravestones from 1338 and 1339 mention a deadly illness. They found plague DNA in the teeth, matching the strain that later spread west.

The disease travelled along trade routes, including the Silk Road, carried by merchants, soldiers and their animals. By 1346 it had reached the Black Sea. In October 1347, ships from there arrived in Messina, in Sicily. Within months it was in Italy, France and Spain, and by 1349 it had reached England and Norway.

What it did to people

Victims usually developed fever and chills, then painful swellings called buboes in the neck, armpits or groin. This form is called bubonic plague. A more dangerous form infected the lungs and spread through coughing.

Nobody at the time knew about germs. Some people blamed bad air, the position of the planets or divine punishment. Doctors tried bloodletting, herbs and sweet-smelling flowers. In some towns, Jewish communities were falsely blamed for spreading the disease and were attacked, one of the darkest parts of the story.

Learning to isolate

People did notice that the disease moved with travellers. In 1377, the port of Ragusa, now Dubrovnik in Croatia, ordered ships and travellers from plague areas to wait 30 days before entering the city. Venice and other cities later made it 40 days, quaranta giorni in Italian. That is where the word "quarantine" comes from.

These were some of the first public health rules of their kind. Cities also appointed health officers and set up special hospitals. Quarantine is still used today, for example during the COVID-19 pandemic.

A changed world

With so many people dead, there were not enough workers to farm the land. In parts of Europe, surviving peasants could demand higher wages and more freedom. When rulers tried to stop this, anger boiled over in revolts such as the Peasants' Revolt in England in 1381.

Plague kept returning to Europe every few years for centuries. It also struck the Middle East, North Africa and parts of Asia, though records from those places are less complete.

Plague today

Plague has not disappeared. It still lives in wild rodents in parts of Africa, Asia and the Americas. Madagascar and the Democratic Republic of the Congo report the most cases. In the western United States, a handful of people catch it each year, usually from wild rodents or their fleas. India had an outbreak in Surat in 1994.

The difference is that plague can now be cured. Antibiotics, discovered in the 20th century, kill the bacterium if treatment starts early. A disease that once emptied whole villages can now be treated in a hospital in about a week or two.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Plague doctors in beaked masks treated victims of the Black Death.

Truth: The beaked mask and long coat were first described in the 1600s, around 300 years after the Black Death. During the Black Death, doctors wore ordinary clothes.
