How do clownfish live in anemones?

Clownfish live among stinging tentacles that can kill other small fish. And if the female in charge dies, her male partner turns into a female.

The short answer

A sea anemone looks like a flower but is an animal, a relative of jellyfish and corals. Its waving tentacles are armed with thousands of tiny stinging cells that fire when a small fish touches them. Most small fish are stung, stunned and eaten.

Clownfish, also called anemonefish, are different. A thick coat of slime on their skin stops the stinging cells from firing, or at least stops them doing harm. So clownfish live inside the anemone, sleeping and sheltering among its tentacles, where few predators dare to follow. In return, they keep the anemone clean, chase away fish that nibble it and feed it with their waste. Both animals do better together than apart.

Surprising but true

All clownfish are born male. The biggest fish in each group becomes the only female, and if she dies, the largest male changes sex to take her place.

Life among stingers

Each stinging cell holds a coiled thread, like a tiny harpoon. When something touches it, the thread shoots out in a fraction of a second and injects venom.

Scientists think clownfish mucus avoids setting off these harpoons. Some studies suggest it lacks certain chemicals, such as particular sugars, that normally trigger a sting. Many clownfish also go through a careful routine when they meet a new anemone. They touch its tentacles briefly with their fins and belly, again and again, over minutes or hours. During this time, they may pick up some of the anemone's own mucus. After that, the anemone seems to treat them as part of itself.

Only about 10 of the more than 1,000 kinds of sea anemones host clownfish, and each kind of clownfish has favourite partners.

A good deal on both sides

The clownfish gets a safe home, a nest site and scraps from the anemone's meals. The anemone benefits too. Clownfish chase away butterflyfish, which bite anemone tentacles. Their wriggling moves water through the tentacles, bringing fresh oxygen. Their droppings provide nutrients for the anemone and for the tiny algae that live inside its body.

This kind of partnership, where both sides gain, is called mutualism. Coral reefs are full of them.

The biggest fish is the boss

A single anemone may house a little group of clownfish. The largest is always the female, and she's in charge. The second largest is the breeding male. The rest are smaller males that don't breed, waiting their turn.

All clownfish start life as males. If the female dies, the breeding male grows larger and changes into a female, and the biggest of the young males becomes the new breeding male. Scientists call this kind of switch protandry.

The female lays hundreds of eggs on a rock right next to the anemone. The male guards them, fanning them with his fins and removing any that go bad, until they hatch about a week later. The tiny babies drift away in the open sea before settling on a reef and searching for an anemone of their own.

Nemo's fame

In 2003, the Pixar film Finding Nemo made clownfish famous worldwide. Demand for them as pets shot up, and many were caught from reefs. Today, many aquarium clownfish are bred in tanks instead. In Kerala, researchers at the Central Marine Fisheries Research Institute breed them so coastal families can earn money without taking fish from the wild.

Clownfish in India

Clownfish live on reefs around the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, Lakshadweep and the Gulf of Mannar. Divers there often find them by first spotting an anemone.

Warm seas are a growing danger. When the water gets too hot, anemones, like corals, can lose the algae that live inside them and turn white. This coral bleaching weakens the anemone, and a clownfish without a healthy host has nowhere safe to hide.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Clownfish are simply born immune to every sea anemone.

Truth: Clownfish live safely only with certain kinds of anemone, and many seem to get used to a new host slowly, brushing against its tentacles bit by bit. Their protection depends on a special mucus coat, not a total immunity.

Try this

Mix a spoonful of cornflour with a little water to make a slippery paste. Rub a thin layer on the back of one hand, and gently press a piece of sticky tape onto each hand. Notice how much less the tape grips the coated hand. A clownfish's slime works in a related way, stopping the anemone's stinging cells from firing properly.
