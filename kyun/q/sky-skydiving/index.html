How do skydivers fall safely?

A skydiver who jumps from 4,000 m falls for about a minute at around 200 km/h, faster than a Vande Bharat train at full speed, then lands gently enough to walk away.

The short answer

Skydivers fall safely because of air, careful training and backup equipment. When you jump from a plane, gravity pulls you faster and faster, but the air pushes back harder as your speed grows. After about 10 to 12 seconds, the two forces balance and you stop speeding up. This top speed is called terminal velocity, about 200 km/h for someone falling flat.

At a set height, usually around 1,000 to 1,500 m, the skydiver opens a parachute. Its big fabric wing catches the air and slows the fall to about the speed of a gentle jog. The skydiver steers to a landing field and touches down on their feet. Every jumper carries a spare parachute and a small computer that can open it automatically.

Surprising but true

In 2012, Felix Baumgartner jumped from a balloon about 39 km up and fell faster than the speed of sound, reaching about 1,358 km/h without any vehicle around him.

Falling, fast but not faster

Gravity tries to make everything fall faster every second. Without air, a skydiver would keep speeding up all the way to the ground.

But air pushes on anything moving through it, a kind of friction called drag, like the wind on your hand out of a car window. The faster you go, the harder it pushes. Lying flat on their belly, arms and legs spread, a skydiver acts like a sail. The air soon pushes up as hard as gravity pulls down.

By changing shape, skydivers can change their speed. Arms in and head down, they fall faster. Spread out wide, they fall slower. They also learn to turn, move forward and even link hands with others in the sky.

What's in the backpack

A skydiver's container holds two parachutes. The main one is folded carefully by the jumper or a trained packer. The reserve parachute must be packed and checked by a certified rigger at regular intervals.

Most jumpers also carry an automatic activation device, or AAD. It is a tiny computer that measures height and speed. If a skydiver is still falling fast near the ground, perhaps because they passed out, it cuts a loop and opens the reserve. AADs have saved many lives.

Skydivers wear an altimeter on the wrist or a beeper in the helmet, warning them when it is time to open.

The parachute ride

Modern sport parachutes are not round. They are rectangular wings, like a paraglider's, made of cells that fill with air. When the canopy opens, the skydiver goes from about 200 km/h to around 15 to 20 km/h in a few seconds.

They pull on steering lines to turn left or right. Just before landing, they pull both lines down together. This "flare" slows them almost to a stop, so they can step onto the ground.

Starting safely

Beginners start with tandem jumps. A student is clipped to the front of an experienced instructor, who handles everything. Students who want to jump alone take courses and practise on the ground for hours first.

Indoor skydiving is another way to learn. A vertical wind tunnel blows air upward at about 200 km/h, so people can float in free fall without ever leaving the ground.

Accidents do happen, but trained skydivers using modern gear have a strong safety record. In the USA, skydivers make millions of jumps each year, and the number of deaths is very small compared with that.

Jumps from the edge of space

In 2012, the Austrian Felix Baumgartner rose under a giant helium balloon, a cousin of the hot-air balloon, to about 39 km, then stepped off. The air up there is so thin that he quickly broke the speed of sound, reaching about 1,358 km/h. He wore a pressure suit, like an astronaut's.

In 2014, the American engineer Alan Eustace went even higher, about 41 km, and set a new record. When anything travels faster than sound, it can make a loud bang, and the science behind it is the sonic boom.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In free fall you can't breathe, because the air rushes past too fast.

Truth: Skydivers breathe normally. It can feel windy and loud, but there is plenty of air. Only very high jumps, above about 4,500 m, need extra oxygen, and record jumps from the edge of space need a pressure suit.

Try this

Test air resistance. Drop a flat sheet of paper and a scrunched-up ball of the same paper from the same height at the same time. Watch which lands first. Then fold the sheet into a little cone and try again. Skydivers change their body shape in the same way to fall faster or slower.
