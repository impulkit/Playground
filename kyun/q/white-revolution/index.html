How did India become the world's top milk producer?

India makes about a quarter of the world's milk, and most of it comes from small farmers with just two or three animals each.

The short answer

India produces more milk than any other country, roughly a quarter of the world's total. It wasn't always so. In the 1950s and 1960s, Indian cities often faced milk shortages, and the country imported milk powder.

The change is called the White Revolution. It grew from a farmers' cooperative in Anand, Gujarat, which became the famous brand Amul. A young engineer, Verghese Kurien, helped build it. From 1970, a national programme called Operation Flood copied the Anand model across India, linking villages to cities. In 1998, India overtook the USA as the world's largest milk producer.

Surprising but true

Verghese Kurien, the engineer known as the Milkman of India, said he did not like drinking milk himself.

Farmers versus middlemen

In the 1940s, dairy farmers around Kaira district in Gujarat sold their milk to a private dairy that supplied Bombay. The farmers felt they were paid too little. Their leaders went to Sardar Patel, who advised them to form their own cooperative.

A cooperative is a business owned by its members, who share the profits. In 1946, after a milk strike, the Kaira District Cooperative Milk Producers' Union was registered in Anand. Local leader Tribhuvandas Patel led it. It was later named Amul, from a Sanskrit word, amulya, meaning priceless.

Since 1966, Amul has been advertised by a cartoon girl in a polka-dot dress. Her billboards make jokes about the news of the week, and they have run for so long that grandparents and grandchildren both know her.

The engineer who stayed

Verghese Kurien was born in Kerala in 1921 and trained as an engineer. In 1949 the government sent him to Anand to work at a small creamery. He planned to leave as soon as he could.

Instead he stayed for decades. He fixed the cooperative's machines, then helped grow it into one of India's biggest food businesses. Each village had its own society, where farmers brought milk twice a day. Their milk was tested for fat and they were paid according to quality, often on the spot.

Operation Flood

In 1964, Prime Minister Lal Bahadur Shastri visited Anand and was impressed. He asked Kurien to repeat the model nationwide. The National Dairy Development Board was set up in 1965 with Kurien as chairman.

Operation Flood began in 1970. Dairy products donated by Europe were sold in India, and the money paid for village cooperatives, chilling centres and dairies. Milk tankers and trains carried milk from rural areas to cities. Over about 26 years, the programme reached tens of thousands of villages.

A film paid for by farmers

In 1976, director Shyam Benegal made a film, Manthan, about a dairy cooperative. About five lakh Gujarat farmers each gave ₹2 to fund it, making them its producers. When it was released, farmers came by the truckload to watch "their" film. A restored version was shown at the Cannes Film Festival in 2024.

Why it worked, and what's next

The cooperative model let small farmers, including many women and landless families, earn a steady income from just a couple of animals. Payment came regularly, which helped families plan.

There are challenges. Many Indian cows and buffaloes give less milk than those on big Western farms, and feeding and caring for animals in heat waves is getting harder. Some people also raise questions about animal welfare and the environment.

Still, think of a glass of milk, a bowl of curd or a block of paneer in an Indian home. It very likely started with a small farmer carrying a steel can to the village society.

Myth or fact?

Myth: India's milk comes mostly from big industrial dairy farms.

Truth: Most of India's milk comes from millions of small farmers, many with only a few cows or buffaloes. Village cooperatives collect it twice a day and send it to big dairies for processing.

Try this

With an adult, warm a cup of milk, stir in a spoonful of lemon juice, and watch it split into curds and whey. Strain the curds through a clean cloth and press them. You've made a simple paneer, one of many products India's dairies sell.
