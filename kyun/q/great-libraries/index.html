What happened to the Library of Alexandria?

The ancient world's most famous library did not vanish in a single dramatic fire. It faded away over hundreds of years.

The short answer

The Library of Alexandria in Egypt was the most famous library of the ancient world. It was founded around 300 BCE by the Greek kings who ruled Egypt after Alexander the Great. Its aim was bold: to collect every book in the world.

There was no single moment of destruction. The Library suffered damage in wars, including a fire during Julius Caesar's fighting in the city in 48 BCE. Over the following centuries it lost royal support, scholars left, and its buildings were damaged in more conflicts. By around 400 CE, there was little left. The popular image of one huge blaze is mostly a myth.

Surprising but true

Ships arriving in Alexandria were reportedly searched for books. Scribes copied them, and the Library sometimes kept the originals and gave the owners the copies.

A library for the whole world

Alexandria was a great port city on the Mediterranean. The Ptolemy kings built the Mouseion, a "house of the Muses", where scholars lived, studied and were paid by the king. The Library was part of it.

The kings were hungry for books. Books then were scrolls, long rolls of papyrus, made from a reed that grew along the Nile. Agents bought scrolls across the Greek world. One ancient writer says ships in the harbour were searched, and any books found were copied.

Nobody knows exactly how many scrolls it held. Ancient writers gave numbers from about 40,000 to 4 lakh, and some scrolls held only part of a book.

Who invented paper?The first people to make paper weren't humans. Wasps have been chewing wood into paper nests for millions of years.

Who studied there

Scholars at Alexandria edited the poems of Homer and catalogued Greek literature. The mathematician Euclid worked in the city. Eratosthenes, one of the chief librarians, calculated the size of Earth with shadows and geometry.

The Library helped make Alexandria a centre of learning for hundreds of years.

How it faded

In 48 BCE, Julius Caesar was trapped in Alexandria during a civil war. He set fire to ships in the harbour, and the fire spread. Some ancient writers say books were burned, possibly in warehouses near the docks. But scholars kept working in Alexandria afterwards.

Over time, the rulers who had funded it lost interest or money. Scholars were driven out during power struggles. Fighting in the 270s CE damaged the palace district. In 391 CE, the Serapeum, a temple that held a smaller "daughter" library, was destroyed.

A later story says an Arab general burned the books in 642 CE. Most historians now think this story was invented centuries later.

India's great library

India had its own great centre of learning at Nalanda, in Bihar, a Buddhist university that drew students from China, Korea and Central Asia. Its library was said to have three large buildings, one of them nine storeys tall.

Nalanda was attacked around the end of the 12th century, and tradition says its library burned for months. Historians debate some details, but the university never recovered. Its ruins are now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Libraries that survived

Some libraries have come back from disaster. The Library of Congress in Washington, D.C. was burned by British troops in 1814. Former President Thomas Jefferson sold it his own collection of 6,487 books to start again. Today it is the largest library in the world, with more than 17 crore items.

In 2012, when fighters took over Timbuktu in Mali, librarians secretly carried hundreds of thousands of old manuscripts out of the city in boxes and sacks to keep them safe. In 2002, Egypt opened a new library in Alexandria, the Bibliotheca Alexandrina, with a huge tilted roof shaped like a rising sun.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Library of Alexandria was destroyed in one great fire, and all its knowledge was lost in a day.

Truth: Julius Caesar's fire in 48 BCE may have burned some books, but the Library continued afterwards. It declined over centuries through lost funding, wars and neglect. Many works also survived as copies elsewhere.
