What is Cherrapunji like in the monsoon?

Between August 1860 and July 1861, Cherrapunji got about 26 metres of rain, enough to drown an eight-storey building. That is still a world record.

The short answer

In the monsoon, Cherrapunji is wrapped in cloud, roaring with waterfalls and drenched by rain that can fall for days on end. This small town in the Khasi Hills of Meghalaya, known locally as Sohra, is one of the wettest places on Earth.

It holds two world records from long ago. One is the most rain in a year, about 26,461 mm from August 1860 to July 1861. The other is the most in one calendar month, 9,300 mm in July 1861. In an ordinary year it still gets around 11,000 to 12,000 mm. Delhi gets about 800 mm a year. Mumbai, famous for its monsoon, gets about 2,200.

Nearby Mawsynram, about 15 km to the west, now usually gets slightly more rain on average, and is often called the wettest place on Earth.

Surprising but true

One of the wettest places on Earth often runs short of drinking water in winter, because the rain rushes straight off its bare hills.

Why the rain piles up here

The answer is geography. From June to September, the monsoon winds blow north from the Bay of Bengal, heavy with water. They cross the flat, low plains of Bangladesh, with nothing to stop them.

Then they hit a wall. The Khasi Hills rise steeply, almost like cliffs, to more than 1,400 metres. The wet air is forced upward. As it rises, it cools, and cool air cannot hold as much water vapour. The vapour turns into droplets, the droplets join, and rain pours down on the hilltops.

Cherrapunji sits right at the edge of those cliffs, where the clouds first crash into the hills.

Life under a hundred days of rain

In the monsoon, people in Sohra live with damp clothes, mould on walls and roads that turn into streams. Many still use a traditional rain shield called a knup. Made of split bamboo woven into a curved frame and covered with leaves, it sits on the back and head like a turtle's shell. Your hands stay free for farming or carrying loads.

Rain often falls hardest at night. Mornings can be misty, with clouds drifting through houses and over the road. Some days you cannot see more than a few metres.

Waterfalls and root bridges

The rain feeds some of India's most spectacular waterfalls. Nohkalikai Falls, near the town, drops about 340 metres in a single plunge, one of the highest in India. That is taller than the Eiffel Tower. Seven Sisters Falls spreads across a cliff in several streams during the monsoon.

The rain also explains the living root bridges. The Khasi people learned to guide the roots of rubber fig trees across streams, letting them grow into strong bridges over decades. Wooden bridges would rot in the endless wet, but living root bridges grow stronger with age. Near the village of Nongriat, one is even a double-decker, with one bridge above another.

The wettest town that runs dry

Here is the strangest part of Cherrapunji. After the monsoon, from about November to March, it barely rains at all. The hills around the town have lost much of their forest over the centuries, and the thin soil cannot hold the water. So the rain rushes off the cliffs and down into Bangladesh.

For years, families in Sohra have had to buy water from tankers or walk to springs in winter. Today, people collect rainwater on rooftops, plant trees and protect old forests to help the ground hold more.

So the town that once got 26 metres of rain in a year is also a lesson about water. How much the land can keep matters as much as how much falls. The places that stay greenest in Meghalaya are often the sacred forests, which Khasi communities have protected for generations, and where the soil can soak up the monsoon like a sponge.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Places with a lot of rain always have plenty of water.

Truth: Cherrapunji gets enormous rain in a few months, but much of it runs straight off the steep, bare hills into Bangladesh. In the dry winter, many families there have had to buy or carry water.

Try this

Make a rain gauge. Cut the top off a clear plastic bottle (ask an adult), flip it upside down into the base as a funnel, and mark centimetres on the side with a ruler. Put it outside and check it every day of the monsoon. Then compare your total with Cherrapunji's 26 metres.
