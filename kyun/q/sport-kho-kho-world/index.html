How is kho-kho going global?

In January 2025, teams from 23 countries, from Nepal to Brazil to England, came to New Delhi to play a game Indian kids play in school playgrounds.

The short answer

Kho-kho is a chasing game from Maharashtra, played by millions of Indian children. Until recently, few people outside South Asia had heard of it. That has changed fast.

In January 2025, New Delhi hosted the first Kho Kho World Cup. Men's and women's teams came from 23 countries, including Nepal, Iran, Kenya, South Africa, Brazil, the United States, England and Australia. India won both titles, beating Nepal in both finals. Meanwhile a professional league, matches on TV and coaching for foreign players are pushing the game onto the world map, the same way the kabaddi league did for kabaddi.

Surprising but true

Kho-kho was shown to the world at the 1936 Berlin Olympics, as a demonstration by an Indian sports club, nearly 90 years before its first World Cup.

How the game works

A kho-kho court has a central lane with eight players crouching in a row, facing alternate directions. One more chaser is active. The defending team sends runners in, usually three at a time, who dodge and weave around the crouching line.

The active chaser can't turn back or cut across the lane. To change direction, they tap a crouching teammate and shout "kho". That player springs up and becomes the new chaser. A good team passes the chase back and forth like a relay, closing in from both sides, which is why kho-kho coaches drill passing as much as running.

Runners score for their team by lasting longer. Chasers score by tagging them out. It is all about short bursts of speed, sudden stops and reading the other player, the kind of split-second thinking studied in reaction time.

From Pune to Berlin

Versions of chasing games are very old in India. In the early 1900s, the Deccan Gymkhana club in Pune wrote down the first set of kho-kho rules, around 1914, and the court took its modern shape over the next few decades.

In 1936, an Indian sports club from Amravati travelled to the Berlin Olympics and gave demonstrations of Indian sports, including kho-kho and mallakhamb. It wasn't an official event, but European crowds saw kho-kho for the first time.

What traditional games do Indian children play?Ludo, snakes and ladders and chess all began as Indian games, long before they came back to India in cardboard boxes.

Why now

For decades, kho-kho stayed a school sport in India, with national championships but little money. Then, in 2022, a league called Ultimate Kho Kho started, with city teams, TV coverage and faster rules made for television. Matches got shorter, and the action became easier to follow on screen.

The World Cup was the next step. Some countries put teams together from Indian families living abroad, but many players were new to the sport. Nepal's teams were strong enough to reach both finals. Coaches in places like Kenya and Brazil liked that kho-kho needs no expensive kit, just a flat ground, two poles and some lines.

The road ahead

The big dream for kho-kho fans is to see it at the Asian Games and, some day, the Olympics. For that, a sport has to be played widely across many continents, with strong national federations. Kho-kho isn't there yet, but the 2025 World Cup was a big jump.

There is also a simple reason it travels well. Every child in every country already knows how to play tag, whether they call it tag, tig, chor-police or something else. Kho-kho just adds a team, a line and one shouted word. That idea, that an old village game can turn into a global sport, is exactly what happened to the pole sport of mallakhamb too.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Kho-kho is just a playground game with no proper rules.

Truth: It has a standard court, timed turns, referees and a points system. Rules were first written down in Pune in the early 1900s, and international matches now follow a rulebook used in dozens of countries.

Try this

Play a mini kho-kho with six friends. Three sit in a row facing alternate directions, one chaser stands at the end, and two runners dodge. The chaser can only change direction at the ends of the line and must tap a sitter and shout "kho" to switch. Time how long each runner lasts.
