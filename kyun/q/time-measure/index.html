Why are there 60 minutes in an hour?

We split an hour into 60 minutes because of astronomers in ancient Babylon who counted in sixties, more than 4,000 years ago.

The short answer

The 60 comes from Babylon, in what is now Iraq. Babylonian astronomers did their maths in base 60, the way we do ours in base 10. When they divided a circle or an hour into smaller parts, they used sixtieths.

Later Greek astronomers borrowed this. They split each degree of a circle into 60 small parts, and each of those into 60 smaller parts again. In Latin these were the pars minuta prima, "first small part", and pars minuta secunda, "second small part". That is where the words minute and second come from.

The 24 hours in a day came from ancient Egypt, which split daytime and night-time into 12 parts each.

Surprising but true

For less than two years in the 1790s, France officially used decimal time, with 10 hours in a day and 100 minutes in each hour.

The power of sixty

Nobody knows for sure why the Babylonians chose 60. One good guess is that it divides so neatly. Sixty can be split into halves, thirds, quarters, fifths, sixths, tenths and twelfths, all without fractions. For people doing sums by hand, that was a big help.

Some historians think it may have come from counting on your fingers. Using your thumb to count the three sections of each finger on one hand gives 12. Counting each 12 on the five fingers of the other hand gives 60.

Hours that stretched and shrank

Egyptians tracked the night by watching stars rise, and the day with shadow clocks. They divided each into 12 hours. But because days are longer in summer than in winter, their hours changed length through the year. A summer daytime hour was much longer than a winter one.

Equal hours only became normal after mechanical clocks spread across Europe in the 1300s. A gear-driven clock can't easily change speed with the seasons, so hours became fixed.

How does a clock keep time?Every clock, from a sundial to an atomic clock, has two parts: something that repeats steadily, and something that counts.

India's own clock

Ancient India had its own way of dividing the day. A day and night together contained 60 ghatis, so each ghati was 24 of our minutes. A ghati was split into 60 palas, each 24 seconds long. Some astrologers and almanacs in India still use ghatis. A muhurta, used to choose a good time for weddings and ceremonies, is two ghatis, or 48 minutes.

In the 1720s and 1730s, Maharaja Sawai Jai Singh II built giant stone observatories called Jantar Mantar in Jaipur, Delhi and other cities. The huge sundial in Jaipur, called the Samrat Yantra, casts a shadow that moves several centimetres a minute, so the time can be read to within a few seconds. The Jaipur observatory is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

The day France tried tens

After the French Revolution, the new government wanted everything in tens. In 1793, it declared that a day would have 10 hours, each hour 100 minutes, and each minute 100 seconds. Clockmakers made special clock faces.

People hated it. They had to replace all their clocks and learn a new way of reading them. In 1795 the rule was dropped. The metric system for length and weight survived, but time stayed in sixties.

Seconds measured by atoms

For centuries, the second was simply 1/86,400 of a day. But the Earth's spin is not perfectly steady. So in 1967, scientists redefined the second using atoms of caesium, which give off waves at a very exact rate. One second is now 9,192,631,770 of those waves.

Atomic clocks are so precise they would lose less than a second in millions of years. They keep the internet, mobile phones and satellite navigation running in step.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A day is always exactly 24 hours long.

Truth: Twenty-four hours is the average length of a day measured by the Sun. The Earth's spin wobbles very slightly and is gradually slowing over millions of years, so scientists sometimes add a "leap second" to keep clocks in step.

Try this

On a sunny day, push a stick upright into a pot of soil or sand, outdoors where you are allowed to be. Every hour, mark the tip of its shadow with a pebble. By the end of the day you have made a simple sundial, the kind of clock people used for thousands of years.
