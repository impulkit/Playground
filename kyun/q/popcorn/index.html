Why does popcorn pop?

Every popcorn kernel is a tiny pressure cooker that explodes when the steam inside gets strong enough to burst its shell.

The short answer

Each popcorn kernel holds a tiny drop of water inside a hard, waterproof shell called the hull. When you heat the kernel, that water turns into steam. The steam can't escape, so the pressure inside keeps rising.

At about 180°C, the hull can't take it any more and bursts open. In a fraction of a second, the soft starch inside, heated and squeezed by the steam, puffs out into a white foam. It cools and sets almost instantly into the fluffy shape you eat. The kernel turns inside out and grows many times bigger.

Surprising but true

In 2015, French scientists filmed popcorn with super-fast cameras and found that the "pop" sound comes from steam escaping, not from the shell cracking.

A pressure cooker in a seed

A popcorn kernel works much like a pressure cooker. The water inside it gets hotter than the usual boiling point of 100°C, because the hull keeps it under pressure. Just before it bursts, the pressure inside is roughly nine times that of the air around you.

When the hull breaks, the pressure drops suddenly. The super-hot water flashes into steam all at once, blowing up the starch like a balloon. That's why the kernel pops instead of slowly cooking.

Why some kernels don't pop

Every bowl has a few hard kernels left at the bottom. Some didn't have enough water inside them, perhaps because they dried out in storage. Others had a tiny crack in their hull, so steam leaked out slowly and pressure never built up.

Popcorn makers store kernels carefully so they keep just the right amount of moisture, about 14%. Too dry, and they won't pop. Too wet, and they pop into chewy, small pieces. Popped kernels come in two main shapes. "Butterfly" popcorn has wings that catch salt and butter, while round "mushroom" popcorn is tougher, so it is used for caramel coatings.

Slow-motion popcorn

In 2015, two French scientists filmed popcorn at thousands of frames a second. They saw that the starch bursting out pushes against the pan like a little leg, making the kernel jump into the air.

They also found that the pop doesn't come from the hull breaking. It happens when steam suddenly rushes out of the kernel, a bit like the pop of a cork. Each kernel pops at a slightly different moment, which is why you hear a crackle.

Ancient popcorn

Maize was first farmed in Mexico about 9,000 years ago. Archaeologists found corn cobs in Peru, about 6,700 years old, that show signs of being popped. People of the Americas have eaten popcorn for thousands of years.

India has popping foods too. Jowar can be popped, and puffed rice, called murmura, is made by heating rice grains until their moisture puffs them up. Phool makhana, the seeds of a water lily grown in Bihar, are roasted until they pop.

Popcorn goes to the movies

In early American cinemas, many owners didn't want popcorn, because it was messy. But during the Great Depression of the 1930s, popcorn was one of the few treats most people could afford, and street sellers set up outside cinemas. Soon cinemas sold it themselves, and it helped many stay in business.

In the 1940s, the American engineer Percy Spencer, testing machines that made microwaves, tried popping corn with them. It worked. Microwave popcorn bags became popular in the 1980s, and today popcorn is sold in cinemas from Hollywood to Hyderabad.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Any kind of corn can be popped.

Truth: Only a few varieties pop well. They have hard starch and a thick, airtight hull that traps steam. Sweet corn and most field corn don't pop properly.

Try this

Ask an adult to pop a bag of microwave popcorn or a pan of kernels. When it's done, count how many kernels didn't pop and how many did. Compare with a bag that has been left open for a week, which may have dried out and pop less well.
