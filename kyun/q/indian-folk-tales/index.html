What are some favourite Indian folk tales?

A monkey who tricks a crocodile by claiming he left his heart up in a tree is a story told in India for well over 2,000 years, and versions turn up from Persia to France.

The short answer

India has an enormous treasure of folk tales, told in hundreds of languages. Some of the best known are the animal fables of the Panchatantra and the Jataka tales of the Buddha's past lives. Others are the ghost story-riddles of Vikram and Betal, and the funny tales of clever courtiers like Birbal and Tenali Raman.

Most of these stories were passed on by voice long before anyone wrote them down. Grandparents told them at bedtime, travelling storytellers performed them at fairs, and puppeteers and singers acted them out. Each teller changed them a little, so the same story can have dozens of versions.

Surprising but true

The Panchatantra was translated into Persian about 1,450 years ago, then into Arabic, and its animal stories eventually reached Europe. The French writer La Fontaine named an Indian sage as one of his sources.

Animals who talk sense

The Panchatantra, meaning "five books", is a set of animal fables said to have been written by a wise teacher, Vishnu Sharma, to teach three lazy princes how to rule. Lions, jackals, crows, tortoises and monkeys argue, plot and make friends. A tortoise who can't keep his mouth shut falls from the sky. A clever rabbit tricks a lion into jumping into a well at his own reflection.

The stories are nested like boxes. One character starts telling a tale, and inside it another character tells another, so a single book holds dozens of stories within stories.

The Jataka tales tell similar stories, with the future Buddha as a wise animal or person. The monkey and crocodile story appears in both collections.

Stories that travelled

Around 570 CE, a Persian physician is said to have brought the Panchatantra from India to the court of the Persian king Khusrau. It was translated into the language of Persia, then into Arabic, where it became Kalila wa Dimna, named after two jackals. From there it spread into Hebrew, Latin, Spanish and many more languages.

When the French poet Jean de La Fontaine published more fables in 1678, he mentioned an Indian sage, "Pilpay", as a source. Some of the fables we think of as Western, alongside those of Aesop, have Indian cousins.

Clever wits at royal courts

Many favourite Indian tales star a clever courtier who outwits proud nobles and teaches the king a lesson. Akbar and Birbal are set at the Mughal court. Birbal was a real minister, though most of the stories were made up much later.

In the south, Tenali Raman is linked to King Krishnadevaraya of Vijayanagara in the 1500s. In Bengal, Gopal Bhar plays tricks at the court of a Nadia king. Sheikh Chilli, a lovable daydreamer whose plans always collapse, is known across north India and Pakistan.

Grandmothers, ghosts and giants

Every region has its own stories. In Bengal, Thakurmar Jhuli, "Grandmother's Bag", printed in 1907, collected tales of princes, demons and talking birds, and children still read it. In Rajasthan, the writer Vijaydan Detha spent decades collecting folk tales from villagers. One became the Hindi film Paheli.

The 11th-century Kashmiri writer Somadeva put together the Kathasaritsagara, "Ocean of the Streams of Story", one of the largest story collections ever made. It includes the Vikram and Betal tales. In each, a ghost tells the king a story ending in a riddle. If the king knows the answer and stays silent, his head will burst into pieces.

Old tales, new forms

In 1991, the poet and scholar A. K. Ramanujan published Folktales from India, with stories gathered from 22 languages. Since 1967, the comic series Amar Chitra Katha has retold folk tales for millions of children, and cartoons and films keep borrowing them. Like all folk tales, they keep changing, and that is how they survive. A story told in a Tamil village today may differ from a Punjabi version in its heroes, its jokes and its ending, and both count as the real thing.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Folk tales are just simple stories for small children.

Truth: Many Indian folk tales were made for adults too. They carry lessons about power, politics, friendship and trickery, and some, like the riddles of Vikram and Betal, are puzzles that have kept grown-ups arguing for centuries.

Try this

Ask the oldest person in your family to tell you a story they heard as a child. Listen without writing, then retell it the next day to someone else. Compare your version with theirs. You'll notice you changed small things without meaning to, which is exactly how folk tales grow and shift.
