What does the AQI number mean?

On a smoggy day, the news says the AQI is 400. That one number squeezes a whole cocktail of invisible pollution into something you can read at a glance.

The short answer

AQI stands for Air Quality Index. It turns the amounts of several kinds of pollution in the air into one number, usually from 0 to 500.

The higher the number, the dirtier the air. In India, 0 to 50 is "good" and anything above 400 is "severe". Each band has a colour too, from green for clean air to deep red or maroon for the worst.

Surprising but true

The most harmful specks in smog, called PM2.5, are about 30 times thinner than a single hair from your head, small enough to slip deep into your lungs.

Six bands

India's scale has six bands. From 0 to 50 is Good, 51 to 100 Satisfactory, and 101 to 200 Moderate. From 201 to 300 is Poor, 301 to 400 Very poor, and 401 to 500 Severe.

In the good band, everyone can play outside. In the poor band, people with asthma or heart trouble may feel unwell. In the severe band, even healthy people can find breathing uncomfortable.

How the number is made

Monitoring stations suck in air and measure up to eight pollutants. The main ones are PM2.5 and PM10, tiny specks of dust, soot and smoke. The others are gases such as ozone, nitrogen dioxide, sulphur dioxide and carbon monoxide.

Each pollutant gets its own score on the 0 to 500 scale. The AQI is simply the worst of those scores, so one bad pollutant is enough to push the number up. Most days in Indian cities, the worst is PM2.5, made by combustion in engines, stoves, fireworks and burning crop stubble.

Why it rises in winter

In cities like Delhi, the AQI often climbs in November. Cold, still air settles near the ground under a layer of warmer air, like a lid, and the pollution cannot escape. This is part of what turns fog into smog. Slow winter winds cannot push it away.

PM2.5 specks are so small they slip past the nose and throat and go deep into the lungs. That is why doctors advise children to play indoors on days when the AQI is very poor or severe.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If the air looks clear, it must be clean.

Truth: The most harmful specks, PM2.5, are far too small to see one by one. Air can look fairly clear and still have a high AQI, and some gases, like ozone, are invisible.
