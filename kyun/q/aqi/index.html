What does the AQI number mean?

On a smoggy day, the news says the AQI is 400. That one number squeezes a whole cocktail of invisible pollution into something you can read at a glance.

The short answer

AQI stands for Air Quality Index. It turns the amounts of several kinds of pollution in the air into one number, usually from 0 to 500.

The higher the number, the dirtier the air. In India, 0 to 50 is "good" and anything above 400 is "severe". Each band has a colour too, from green for clean air to deep red or maroon for the worst.

Surprising but true

The most harmful specks in smog, called PM2.5, are about 30 times thinner than a single hair from your head, small enough to slip deep into your lungs.

How it works

Think of a school report card where your final grade is your worst subject, not your average. That is how the AQI works.

Monitoring stations suck in air through a pipe and measure up to eight pollutants. The main ones are PM2.5 and PM10, tiny specks of dust, soot and smoke. "PM" means particulate matter, and the number is the size of the speck in micrometres. The others are gases: ozone, nitrogen dioxide, sulphur dioxide, carbon monoxide and ammonia, plus lead.

Step one: the station measures how much of each pollutant is in the air, usually averaged over the past 24 hours. Step two: each amount is turned into its own score on the 0 to 500 scale, using a table set by scientists. Step three: the AQI is simply the highest of those scores. One bad pollutant is enough to push the number up.

India's scale has six bands. From 0 to 50 is Good, 51 to 100 Satisfactory, and 101 to 200 Moderate. From 201 to 300 is Poor, 301 to 400 Very poor, and 401 to 500 Severe. An AQI of 100 matches India's safe limit for a day's breathing.

Most days in Indian cities, the worst pollutant is PM2.5. It is made by combustion, the burning that happens in engines, stoves, power plants, fireworks and fields of crop stubble.

Where it came from

Big cities have long had smoky air. In the 1970s, the USA began giving people one simple number for it. Its Pollutant Standards Index started in 1976 and was renamed the Air Quality Index in 1999. China, the UK, Singapore and many other countries now have their own scales, and they do not all match.

India launched its National Air Quality Index in October 2014, in New Delhi. Before that, readings were published as long tables of chemicals that few people could understand. The new index gave every city the same six bands and colours.

India's story

India's AQI is run by the Central Pollution Control Board, along with state pollution boards. Hundreds of stations now feed live readings to a public website and to weather apps.

In northern cities like Delhi, the AQI often climbs in October and November. Cold, still air settles at the bottom of the atmosphere, under a layer of warmer air, like a lid on a pot. Pollution cannot escape. This is part of what turns fog into smog. Slow winter winds cannot push it away, and smoke from burning crop stubble in Punjab and Haryana drifts in. Firecrackers on Diwali night add a sudden burst.

Delhi and its nearby cities follow an emergency plan called GRAP, which the Supreme Court approved in 2016. It has four stages. Stage one starts at AQI 201. Stage four starts above 450 and can stop building work, keep older diesel trucks out and move some school classes online.

Cities in the south and on the coast usually have cleaner air. Sea breezes and the monsoon rains wash a lot of the dust away.

The numbers that matter

30 times thinner than a hair. That is how small a PM2.5 speck is. It slips past the nose and throat and reaches the tiny air sacs in your lungs, called alveoli.

60 and 15. India says breathing up to 60 micrograms of PM2.5 per cubic metre, averaged over a day, is acceptable. The World Health Organization's 2021 advice is much stricter: 15. So a "satisfactory" day in India can still be above WHO's safe level.

84. That was Delhi's yearly average for PM2.5 in 2020, in micrograms per cubic metre, according to the air-monitoring company IQAir. WHO's yearly limit is 5, more than 15 times lower.

Nearly half. About 46% of Indians live in places where PM2.5 is above India's own yearly limit, according to the University of Chicago's Air Quality Life Index (2025 report).

About 20 lakh. The Health Effects Institute linked about 2 million deaths in India in 2023 to air pollution, from heart and lung illnesses built up over many years.

What people argue about

People agree the air is often bad. They disagree about what matters most in fixing it.

Some experts say crop stubble burning is the biggest cause in November, so farmers need help with machines and other ways to clear their fields. Others point out that cars, trucks, road dust, factories and power plants pollute all year round, and stubble burning lasts only a few weeks. Farmers' groups say they are blamed unfairly for a city problem.

There is also a debate about the scale itself. Some scientists want India's safe limits brought closer to WHO's, so the bands warn people sooner. Others say India should first meet its own limits, which many cities still miss, before making them stricter.

Myths and mix-ups

"500 is the most polluted air can get." India's scale stops at 500, but the air can be even dirtier. A reading of 500 can hide pollution well beyond it.

"Every app shows the same AQI." Some apps use the US scale, which is stricter for PM2.5. The same air can show a higher number on a US-scale app than on India's official one.

"A mask of any kind keeps out PM2.5." Cloth masks and handkerchiefs stop big dust but let most tiny specks through. Only well-fitting masks marked N95 or similar filter out most PM2.5.

How do your lungs breathe?Your lungs have no muscles to pull air in. A dome of muscle underneath them does almost all the work, about 20,000 times a day.

What it means for you

The AQI can help your family plan the day, a bit like a weather forecast. On a Good or Satisfactory day, play outside as much as you like. On a Poor day, children with asthma or allergies should take it easy. On Very poor or Severe days, keep running and games indoors, keep windows shut during the worst hours and, if you can, use an air purifier in one room.

Check the reading for your own area, not just your city. A busy road and a leafy park a kilometre apart can show very different numbers. Morning and late evening are often the worst times in winter, when the air near the ground is coldest and stillest.

Small habits add up across a city: walking or cycling short trips, sharing rides, not burning leaves or rubbish, and choosing greener ways to celebrate. Cleaner electric cars and buses help too. The AQI lets everyone see, day by day, whether the air is getting better.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If the air looks clear, it must be clean.

Truth: The most harmful specks, PM2.5, are far too small to see one by one. Air can look fairly clear and still have a high AQI, and some gases, like ozone, are invisible.

Try this

Look up your city's AQI on a weather app or the CPCB website. Check it every day for a week at the same time and draw a bar chart. Which day was cleanest, and was it windy or rainy that day?
