What are dwarf planets?

Ceres was called a planet for about 50 years, then an asteroid for 150, and since 2006 it has been a dwarf planet.

The short answer

A dwarf planet is a world that orbits the Sun and is big enough for its own gravity to pull it into a round shape. But it hasn't cleared other objects out of its path. That last rule is what separates it from the eight planets.

The International Astronomical Union (IAU), which names objects in space, created this category in 2006. Five dwarf planets are officially recognised: Ceres, Pluto, Eris, Haumea and Makemake. Ceres lives in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter. The other four orbit beyond Neptune, among the icy bodies of the Kuiper Belt and further out.

Astronomers think there could be many more, perhaps over a hundred, still waiting to be studied or officially counted.

Surprising but true

The dwarf planet Haumea spins so fast, once every four hours or so, that it has been stretched into the shape of a rugby ball.

Ceres, the planet that kept changing

On 1 January 1801, the Italian astronomer Giuseppe Piazzi found a small object orbiting between Mars and Jupiter. It was named Ceres, after the Roman goddess of farming, and was called a planet. Then astronomers found more and more objects in the same region. By the 1850s, they had a new name for them all: asteroids.

In 2006, Ceres was promoted to dwarf planet, because it is round. It is about 940 km across, a little less than the road trip from Delhi to Ahmedabad. NASA's Dawn spacecraft orbited it from 2015 to 2018 and found bright white spots inside a crater. They turned out to be salts left behind by salty water that seeped up from below.

The world that started the argument

In 2005, a team led by the American astronomer Mike Brown found a distant world that seemed bigger than Pluto. It was named Eris, after the Greek goddess of strife and discord, a fitting name from the Greek myths given the fight that followed.

If Pluto was a planet, Eris had to be one too, and there were probably dozens more like them. Astronomers had to choose between a solar system with dozens of planets and a new definition. At a meeting in Prague in 2006, IAU members voted for the new rules, and Pluto became a dwarf planet.

Later measurements showed Pluto is slightly wider than Eris, but Eris is about a quarter heavier.

Strange little worlds

Haumea spins so fast, once every four hours or so, that it has stretched into an egg or rugby-ball shape. In 2017, astronomers found it has a thin ring. It is named after Haumea, a goddess of birth in Hawaiian tradition, since telescopes on Hawaii helped study it and its two small moons.

Makemake is named after the creator god of the people of Rapa Nui, also called Easter Island. It was found shortly after Easter in 2005. Its surface is coated with frozen methane.

Other big icy bodies, such as Gonggong, Quaoar, Sedna and Orcus, may also be round enough to qualify. Some astronomers already call them dwarf planets, while the IAU has not formally added them.

Still arguing

Many planetary scientists dislike the 2006 definition. Only a few hundred astronomers voted, out of thousands of IAU members, because the vote was held on the last day of the meeting. Critics also say that "clearing the orbit" is vague, since even Earth shares its path with some asteroids.

Alan Stern, who leads NASA's New Horizons mission to Pluto, argues that anything round enough should count as a planet. That would make the solar system home to well over a hundred planets. Others say a clear dividing line is useful, even if it isn't perfect. Students in India and the USA learn the same answer for now: eight planets and a growing family of dwarfs.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pluto was demoted because scientists found out it was smaller than they thought.

Truth: Pluto's small size had been known for decades. It was reclassified because astronomers found Eris and other similar worlds, and had to decide whether to call them all planets or create a new category.

Try this

Use a kitchen scale to weigh a few round fruits and vegetables, like a grape, a cherry tomato, an orange and a melon. Decide on your own rule for which are "planets" and which are "dwarfs". Then ask a friend to make a different rule, and see how hard it is to agree.
