Who was Mary Anning, the fossil hunter of the cliffs?

A poor girl from an English seaside town dug up sea monsters that famous scientists built their careers on.

The short answer

Mary Anning was one of the most important fossil hunters who ever lived. She grew up poor in Lyme Regis, a small town on the south coast of England, and spent her life searching the crumbling cliffs for fossils to sell.

She found the first ichthyosaur skeleton to be recognised by scientists, the first complete plesiosaur, and Britain's first pterosaur. Her finds helped convince scientists that whole kinds of animals had lived long ago and died out. Because she was a woman and had little money, she was rarely given credit in her lifetime.

Surprising but true

Mary Anning was not allowed to join Britain's leading geology society because she was a woman, even though its members bought and studied her fossils.

A family of fossil sellers

Mary was born in 1799. Her father, Richard, was a carpenter who also collected fossils from the beach and sold them to visitors. He took Mary and her brother Joseph along with him. A famous story comes from even earlier. In 1800, when Mary was a baby, lightning struck a tree in Lyme Regis where people were sheltering. The woman holding her was killed, but Mary survived. Local people called the fossils "curiosities", and spiral ammonites were known as "snakestones".

Her father died in 1810, leaving the family in debt. Selling fossils became a way to put food on the table. Mary learned to read, and taught herself geology and anatomy from books and papers, sometimes copying them out by hand.

The sea dragon

Around 1811, Joseph found a strange long-snouted skull in the cliffs. Over the next year, Mary, then about 12, dug out the rest of the skeleton. It was an ichthyosaur, a dolphin-shaped reptile that swam in the seas about 200 million years ago. At the time, many people called it a crocodile or a "sea dragon".

In 1823, Mary found a nearly complete plesiosaur, with a tiny head, a very long neck and four flippers. One leading French scientist at first suspected it was a fake. After studying it, he changed his mind.

A sharp eye

Mary did more than dig. She cleaned and studied her fossils carefully, and visiting scientists often asked her opinion. She noticed that strange stones often found inside ichthyosaur skeletons were full of fish scales and small bones. They were fossil poo. The geologist William Buckland published the idea, and the stones were named coprolites.

Her work was dangerous. The cliffs at Lyme Regis are soft and slip after storms, which is exactly when new fossils appear. In 1833, a landslide killed her dog, Tray, at her side.

Left out, and remembered

Scientists in London bought her fossils, wrote papers about them and named the animals. Mary was usually not mentioned. The Geological Society of London did not admit women, so she could not attend its meetings. She did receive some support near the end of her life, and after her death in 1847, the Society honoured her. In 2010, the Royal Society, Britain's oldest science academy, named her among the ten British women who most influenced the history of science.

Today, her ichthyosaurs and plesiosaurs are displayed at the Natural History Museum in London. The coast she searched is part of the Jurassic Coast, a World Heritage Site. In 2022, a statue of Mary was unveiled in Lyme Regis, after a campaign started by a girl named Evie Swire when she was 11. It shows Mary with her hammer and her dog, looking down at the beach.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The tongue twister "She sells seashells by the seashore" was written about Mary Anning.

Truth: This is often said, but there is no clear evidence for it. The rhyme became famous as a song in 1908, long after she died, and the link to her was suggested later.

Press a shell, leaf or small toy into a flat piece of clay or play dough, then lift it out carefully. You've made a mould, like the hollow a buried shell leaves in rock. Fill it with a little more clay to make a cast, the way many fossils form.
