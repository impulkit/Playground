Why are there so many cooking oils?

Coconut oil turns solid on a cold Delhi morning but stays liquid all year in Kerala, and that one fact explains a lot about cooking oils.

The short answer

Almost any seed, nut or oily fruit can be squeezed for oil, and people around the world used whatever grew near them. Coconut palms line the coast of Kerala, so Kerala cooks with coconut oil. Mustard grows in the winter fields of the north and east, so Bengal, Bihar, Punjab and Assam use mustard oil. Groundnuts thrive in Gujarat, and sesame in Tamil Nadu.

Over centuries, each region's food grew around the taste of its oil. A fish curry from Kolkata wouldn't taste right without mustard oil's sting, and a Kerala avial would seem odd without a final spoonful of coconut oil poured over the top. On top of that, oils behave differently in the pan, so cooks pick different ones for frying, for tempering spices and for salads.

Surprising but true

India buys about 60% of its cooking oil from other countries, mostly palm oil from Indonesia and Malaysia (2024–25).

What makes oils different

All cooking oils are fats, built from smaller pieces called fatty acids. Some fatty acids are "saturated", with straight shapes that pack together tightly. Fats with lots of them, like coconut oil, ghee and butter, turn solid when it is cool. Coconut oil hardens below about 24 °C, which is why a bottle goes white and cloudy in a north Indian winter. Oils with mostly bendy, "unsaturated" fatty acids, like sunflower and groundnut oil, stay liquid even in the fridge.

Oils also differ in their smoke point, which matters to every cook who has filled a kitchen with smoke. This is the temperature at which they start to break down and smoke. Refined oils, which have been filtered and cleaned, can usually get hotter than unrefined ones, so they suit deep frying. Unrefined, cold-pressed oils keep more flavour and smell.

From seed to bottle

For centuries in India, oil came from a ghani, also called a kolhu: a big wooden or stone mortar with a heavy pestle turned by a walking bullock. Seeds were slowly crushed, and oil trickled out. Mustard oil made this way is sold as kachi ghani. It is stronger in smell and taste.

Big factories today crush seeds with steel screw presses. To get every last drop, many also use a solvent, a liquid that dissolves the oil out of the crushed seeds and is then removed. The oil is then usually refined to make it clear, pale and long-lasting.

A world of oils, and a lot of palm

Worldwide, the oil produced in the largest amount is palm oil, from the fruit of the oil palm tree. Indonesia and Malaysia grow most of it. It is cheap and solid enough to use in biscuits, instant noodles and soap. But clearing rainforest for palm plantations has destroyed habitat for orangutans and many other animals, so some companies now promise to buy only from farms that don't cut new forest.

India eats far more oil than its farmers grow. In 2024–25, it bought about 60% of its cooking oil from abroad, more than 16 million tonnes, much of it palm oil. In the USA, the most-used oil is soybean oil. Canola oil is also popular there. Canadian scientists bred it in the 1970s from rapeseed, a cousin of mustard, and named it after Canada.

Oil and health

Bodies need some fat. It helps you absorb vitamins A, D, E and K, and builds the walls of every cell. Problems come from eating a lot of it, especially saturated fat and a type called trans fat. Trans fat forms when oil is partly hardened in factories, and small amounts form when oil is reused again and again for frying.

Since 2022, India's food regulator has limited trans fat in oils and fats to 2%. Doctors often suggest using a mix of oils rather than just one, using less of all of them, and not reheating the same frying oil many times. Many Indian kitchens keep two or three bottles on the shelf anyway: a neutral one for frying, and one that tastes of home.

Myth or fact?

Myth: All oil is the same; only the brand and the price change.

Truth: Oils differ in the fats they contain, how hot they can get before smoking, and how they taste. Mustard oil is sharp, sesame is nutty, and refined sunflower oil has almost no flavour. That is why recipes often name a particular oil.

Try this

With a grown-up, put a spoonful each of two or three oils from your kitchen into small steel bowls, and place them in the fridge for an hour. Which ones turn cloudy or solid? Oils with more saturated fat harden first. Then smell each one at room temperature and describe the difference.
