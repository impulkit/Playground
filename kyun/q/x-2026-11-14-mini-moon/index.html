Does Earth really have a second moon right now?

Since April, a space rock about as long as a grown-up is tall has been quietly looping around our planet, and almost nobody noticed.

The short answer

Yes, for a little while. A small space rock called 2026 QQ8 has been caught by Earth's gravity since around April 2026. It is about 1 to 2 metres wide, roughly the size of a person.

It is a temporary "mini-moon", not a real second Moon. Our Moon is 3,474 km across. This one could fit inside a classroom, and it will leave in a few weeks.

Surprising but true

When an astronomer first reported 2026 QQ8, experts thought it might be a piece of old rocket. Only careful tracking proved it was a natural space rock.

Why it's in the news

Hungarian astronomer Krisztián Sárneczky first spotted the rock on 20 August 2026, using a telescope at Piszkéstető Observatory. It was so small that experts first wondered if it was a piece of an old rocket. Follow-up observations, including from telescopes in Arizona and Hawaii, showed it was natural.

On 29 October 2026, the Minor Planet Center, which keeps track of every known asteroid, said 2026 QQ8 was temporarily bound to Earth. Space.com and BBC Sky at Night Magazine reported it in early November, and on 13 November Live Science said it should stay into the holiday season.

How does a planet catch a moon?

Most small asteroids go round the Sun in their own orbit. A few have orbits very like Earth's, so they drift alongside us slowly, like two cars on a motorway driving at almost the same speed.

When one of these slow rocks passes close, Earth's gravity can bend its path into big, wobbly loops around our planet. 2026 QQ8 loops far beyond our Moon, which is about 384,000 km away. At its closest, it comes within roughly 570,000 km of Earth. The Sun's pull never stops tugging, though, so after some months the rock slips away again.

Some mini-moons may even be chips of our own Moon, knocked off long ago by crashes.

Mini-moons before

Astronomers have found only a handful. In 2006, a rock called 2006 RH120 orbited Earth for about a year. Another, 2020 CD3, stayed for over a year until 2020. In late 2024, a roughly 10-metre rock called 2024 PT5 visited for about two months.

What happens next

2026 QQ8 is expected to drift free around December or January and go back to circling the Sun. Calculations suggest it will come close to Earth again in 2058. Bigger telescopes, like the new Rubin Observatory in Chile, should find many more mini-moons in coming years.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Earth has only ever had one moon.

Truth: Earth has one permanent Moon, but small space rocks are caught for a few months or years quite often. Scientists think there may be a tiny mini-moon around Earth much of the time. Most are just too small and faint to spot.

Try this

Roll a marble slowly past a bowl so it just catches the rim. Sometimes it circles once or twice inside before rolling out again. That wobbly, short visit is a bit like how Earth catches a mini-moon and then lets it go.
