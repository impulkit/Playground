How are cartoons made?

Every blink of a cartoon character is drawn or moved by someone, one frame at a time.

The short answer

A cartoon is a series of still pictures shown so quickly that your brain sees them move. In cinema that means 24 pictures, called frames, every second. Hand-drawn cartoons often use 12 to 24 drawings for each second, with each drawing slightly different from the one before.

That adds up fast. A short cartoon can need thousands of drawings and many artists, and a feature film far more. Today many cartoons are made on computers, but the idea is the same: someone has to plan every movement, one frame at a time.

India has a growing animation industry, making cartoons for TV channels and films around the world, with large studios in cities such as Mumbai, Hyderabad and Bengaluru.

Surprising but true

A 90-minute hand-drawn film can need over 1 lakh drawings.

From story to key poses

Every cartoon starts with a story. Artists sketch a storyboard, a comic strip of the whole film, to plan each scene and camera angle. Then voice actors usually record the lines, so animators can match mouths to words.

Lead animators draw the key poses, the most important moments of a movement: a cat crouching, then a cat in mid-leap. Assistants add the in-between drawings that fill the gaps. In the old days, the finished drawings were traced onto clear plastic sheets called cels, painted by hand and photographed one by one over painted backgrounds.

Tricks that bring drawings to life

Disney animators Frank Thomas and Ollie Johnston wrote down twelve principles of animation in their 1981 book. One is "squash and stretch": a bouncing ball flattens when it hits the ground and stretches as it flies. Real balls barely do this, but the exaggeration makes the movement feel alive.

Another is timing. More drawings close together make a movement slow and smooth. Fewer, spaced far apart, make it snappy. A character who blinks in two frames looks alert, while one who takes eight looks sleepy. Good animators study real people and animals for hours, because movement on film looks natural only when it is cleverly faked.

Computers join in

In 1995, Toy Story became the first feature film made entirely with computer animation. Instead of drawing, artists build each character as a 3D model, a digital puppet with hundreds of controls for its arms, face and eyes. Animators pose the puppet at key moments, and the computer works out the movement in between.

Then comes rendering, when computers calculate light, shadow and colour for every frame. Each picture is stored as millions of pixels, and a single frame of a big film can take hours to render. Studios use rooms full of computers, called render farms, working day and night.

Clay, puppets and more

Some films use stop-motion. Animators move clay figures or puppets a tiny bit, take a photo, move them again and take another photo. A good day's work might produce only a few seconds of film.

Other studios use motion capture. Actors wear suits covered in markers, and cameras record their movements so they can be copied onto digital characters.

Animation in India

Indian animation has a long history. In 1974, the government's Films Division made Ek Anek Aur Ekta, a short cartoon about unity that many Indians remember from TV. Today, Indian studios make series watched in many countries.

Chhota Bheem, made by Green Gold Animation in Hyderabad, began on TV in 2008 and became one of the most popular children's cartoons in India. Indian artists also work on Hollywood films, doing everything from lighting to perspective drawing for backgrounds. A cartoon you watch might have been coloured in Mumbai and animated in California.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In computer animation, the computer does all the work.

Truth: Animators still decide every important pose, expression and timing, frame by frame. Computers help fill in the in-between movement and turn the scene into finished pictures, but a person has to tell them what to do.

Try this

Make a flipbook from a small notepad. On the last page, draw a ball near the top. On each page before it, draw the ball a little lower, then squashed flat when it hits the bottom, then rising again. Flip the pages fast with your thumb and watch it bounce.
