What was Babylon?

An hour has 60 minutes and a circle has 360 degrees partly because of people who lived in Babylon, in what is now Iraq, nearly 4,000 years ago.

The short answer

Babylon was one of the great cities of the ancient world. It stood on the banks of the Euphrates river in Mesopotamia, the land between the Tigris and Euphrates, in today's Iraq. People lived there for well over 2,000 years.

It rose to power twice. Around 1750 BCE, King Hammurabi made it the capital of a large kingdom and set down his famous laws. More than a thousand years later, King Nebuchadnezzar II rebuilt it into a city of thick walls, glazed brick gates and a giant stepped temple tower. Its ruins lie about 85 kilometres south of Baghdad.

Surprising but true

The Ishtar Gate, covered in blue glazed bricks and golden dragons, was dug up in pieces and rebuilt inside a museum in Berlin.

Hammurabi's capital

Before Hammurabi, Babylon was one small city-state among many. He spent decades fighting and making deals with his neighbours until he controlled most of southern Mesopotamia. Scribes wrote on clay tablets in wedge-shaped cuneiform script, and thousands of their letters, contracts and school exercises survive.

Babylonian scholars were very good at maths. They used a system based on 60 and solved problems we would now call algebra. One famous tablet, known as Plimpton 322, lists sets of numbers that fit the rule about right-angled triangles now named after Pythagoras. It was written more than a thousand years before he was born. Their counting in sixties is still on every clock.

Nebuchadnezzar's city of blue

After centuries of being ruled by others, Babylon rose again in the 600s BCE. Nebuchadnezzar II, who ruled from 605 to 562 BCE, filled it with building projects. The most famous was the Ishtar Gate, faced with bricks glazed a deep blue. On them marched rows of bulls and dragons in yellow and white. A long Processional Way, lined with glazed lions, led through it.

Archaeologists from Germany dug up the gate between 1899 and 1917 and shipped many of the broken bricks to Berlin. There, the gate was pieced back together in a museum, where it towers over visitors today.

Life in the city

Most Babylonians were not kings or priests. They were farmers, brickmakers, weavers, boatmen and traders. Fields around the city were watered by canals dug from the Euphrates, and barley and dates were the main crops. Barley was so important that wages and loans were often counted in it.

Houses were built of mud brick around a small open courtyard, which kept rooms cool in the fierce summer heat. Children who trained as scribes went to a "tablet house", where they copied signs over and over on soft clay. Some of their practice tablets survive, mistakes and all. A few have the teacher's neat model on one side and the pupil's wobbly copy on the other.

A tower to the heavens

In the middle of the city stood a ziggurat, a temple built like a stack of giant steps, called Etemenanki. It may have reached about 90 metres, as tall as a 30-storey building. Many historians think stories of it helped inspire the Bible's Tower of Babel.

Babylonian priests watched the sky every night and kept records for hundreds of years. Their star patterns fed into the zodiac and the constellations we still use.

Gardens, conquerors and ruins

Greek writers listed the Hanging Gardens of Babylon among the Seven Wonders of the ancient world. They described terraces of trees watered by machines lifting river water. Yet no clay tablet from Babylon mentions them, which is why experts still argue.

In 539 BCE, Cyrus the Great of Persia captured the city, mostly without a fight. Alexander the Great later planned to make it his capital and died in Nebuchadnezzar's palace in 323 BCE. After that the city slowly emptied. Its mud bricks crumbled back into mounds, and in 2019 the site was named a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Hanging Gardens of Babylon definitely existed and their ruins have been found.

Truth: No ruins have been identified for certain, and no Babylonian text describes them. Greek and Roman writers did, centuries later. One researcher, Stephanie Dalley, argues they were really in Nineveh, another Mesopotamian city.
