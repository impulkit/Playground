How do river deltas form?

The world's biggest delta, where the Ganga and Brahmaputra meet the sea, is home to well over 100 million people and the tigers of the Sundarbans.

The short answer

A delta forms where a river flows into a sea or lake and slows down. A fast river can carry a lot of mud and sand. When it meets still water, it loses speed, and it can't carry its load any more. The sediment drops to the bottom and slowly builds new land.

As the land builds up, the river splits into many smaller channels, called distributaries, which fan out across the new ground. Seen from above, many deltas look like a triangle or a fan, which is how they got their name.

Surprising but true

The word "delta" comes from a Greek letter, Δ. About 2,500 years ago, the Greek writer Herodotus noticed that the mouth of the Nile had the same triangle shape.

Slowing down

Think of a river as a conveyor belt for sand and mud. The faster the water flows, the more it can carry. The Ganga and the Brahmaputra pick up enormous amounts of sediment from the young, crumbling mountains of the Himalaya.

When a river reaches the sea, its water spreads out and slows down. The biggest, heaviest grains drop first, close to the shore. Finer mud is carried farther out before it settles. Over time, these deposits build up into sandbanks, then islands, then solid land.

Channels that split and shift

As sediment piles up in front of the river mouth, the river finds the main path blocked. It breaks through its banks and finds a new, lower route to the sea. Then that path fills up, and it shifts again.

So a delta is never finished. Its channels branch, wander and sometimes swap places over years and centuries. In big floods, rivers can jump to a whole new course. People living on deltas have always had to live with water that moves.

The biggest delta of all

The Ganga and the Brahmaputra meet in Bangladesh and flow into the Bay of Bengal through the largest delta on Earth. It covers most of Bangladesh and part of West Bengal in India. Its rich, fertile soil feeds well over 100 million people, who grow rice, jute and vegetables and catch fish in its waterways.

At its southern edge, where river meets sea, lies the Sundarbans, the largest mangrove forest in the world. Its trees grow in salty mud, and it is home to the famous Bengal tigers that swim between islands. Both the Indian and Bangladeshi parts are UNESCO World Heritage Sites.

The Nile and the Mississippi

The Nile delta in northern Egypt is the classic triangle that gave deltas their name. For thousands of years, the Nile's floods spread fresh mud across it. That made it some of the best farmland in the ancient world, as you can read in the Nile and ancient Egypt.

The Mississippi delta, in Louisiana, has a different shape. Its channels stretch far into the Gulf of Mexico like the toes of a bird's foot. Scientists call it a bird's-foot delta.

Disappearing land

Deltas need a steady supply of sediment to keep building. Dams trap mud upstream. Walls built along rivers to prevent floods also stop the mud from spreading over the land. Meanwhile, the soft delta ground slowly sinks under its own weight, and the sea is rising.

Louisiana has lost about 4,800 square kilometres of land since 1932, according to the US Geological Survey. In recent decades, it has lost on average about a football field of land every 100 minutes. In Egypt, the Aswan High Dam has cut off most of the Nile's mud from its delta.

A delta in the desert

Not every delta reaches the sea. In Botswana, the Okavango River flows into the Kalahari Desert and spreads out into a huge inland delta of channels, lagoons and islands. The water never reaches an ocean. It soaks into the sand or evaporates. Every year the flood brings water to the desert, and elephants, hippos and countless birds follow it. In 2014, it became a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Every river makes a delta where it meets the sea.

Truth: A delta only forms where a river drops sand and mud faster than waves, tides and currents can carry it away. Some big rivers, like the Amazon, have strong tides and currents at their mouths and don't build a classic delta.

Try this

Make a delta in a tray. Prop up one end of a baking tray on a book and spread a layer of damp sand at the top end. Slowly pour water from a jug onto the sand. Watch the water carve a little channel, then fan out and drop sand where it reaches the flat end. Keep pouring and the channel splits into branches.
