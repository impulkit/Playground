Why do people love riddles?

In one Greek myth, a monster ate anyone who couldn't answer its riddle. In the Mahabharata, a lake spirit asked a prince more than a hundred.

The short answer

A riddle is a puzzle made of words. It describes something in a tricky or surprising way, and you have to work out what it is. Riddles are found in almost every culture, from ancient Greek myths to Hindi paheliyan told on long train journeys.

People love them because of the moment when the answer clicks. Psychologists call it "insight", the sudden "aha!" when a puzzle snaps into place. Riddles also let you test your wits against someone else's, and a clever one is fun to share with friends.

Surprising but true

Riddles are among the oldest kinds of literature. A collection of about 90 Old English riddles, copied around 1,000 years ago, survives in a single book at Exeter Cathedral in England.

Riddles of life and death

In Greek myth, the Sphinx was a monster with a lion's body, a woman's head and an eagle's wings. She sat outside the city of Thebes and asked travellers a riddle. Anyone who failed was killed. The hero Oedipus solved it, and the Sphinx was defeated.

In the Mahabharata, the Pandava brothers find a lake during their exile. A spirit called a yaksha guards it, and four brothers drink without answering his questions and fall dead. The eldest, Yudhishthira, answers patiently. "What is faster than the wind?" The mind. "What is the greatest wonder?" That every day people see others die, yet live as if they never will. Pleased, the yaksha brings his brothers back to life.

A puzzle made of words

Riddles work by describing an ordinary thing in an unusual way. A riddle might call an egg "a little white house with a yellow treasure and no door". Your brain has to let go of the obvious meaning and find a new one.

Some riddles use metaphor, describing one thing as another. Some use puns, where a word has two meanings. "What has keys but can't open locks?" A piano. The answer seems obvious once you know it, which is part of the fun. In logic puzzles, you reason step by step. In a riddle, you often need a sideways leap.

The oldest riddles

Riddles appear in some of the world's oldest writings. The Rigveda contains riddle hymns, and in Vedic times priests held riddle contests as part of rituals. Ancient Babylonian school tablets include riddles too.

In England, a book copied around 970 CE, now called the Exeter Book, holds about 90 riddles in Old English. One describes a bookworm that "ate words" without becoming any wiser. Many have no answers written down, and scholars still argue about some of them.

In the 1200s and 1300s, the poet Amir Khusrau, who lived in Delhi, wrote playful paheliyan and other word puzzles in Hindavi, an early form of Hindi and Urdu. Some are still recited today.

Riddles in stories

Stories love riddle contests, because they combine danger with brainpower. In J.R.R. Tolkien's The Hobbit (1937), Bilbo Baggins plays a riddle game in the dark with the creature Gollum. He wins by asking, "What have I got in my pocket?" Readers still argue about whether that was fair.

In Indian folk tales, clever characters like Tenali Raman and Birbal often face riddles from kings. The riddles test whether wisdom can beat power.

Why your brain enjoys them

Studies suggest that solving a puzzle in a sudden flash switches on reward areas in your brain, a little like winning a game. Scientists who study "aha" moments have found that sudden solutions feel especially satisfying, and people tend to remember them well.

Riddles also teach flexible thinking. To solve "What gets wetter the more it dries?" you have to flip the usual meaning of drying. The answer is a towel. Children who enjoy riddles are practising a skill that helps in maths, science and jokes alike: looking at the same thing from a new side.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Great Sphinx of Giza in Egypt asked travellers riddles.

Truth: The riddling Sphinx is from Greek myth, a winged monster near the city of Thebes in Greece. The Great Sphinx in Egypt is a much older statue and has no riddle story attached to it.

Try this

Write a riddle about an everyday object without naming it. Describe it in a strange way, as if it were alive. A pencil, for example, could be "I have a black tongue and grow shorter every time I speak." Try it on your family and see how long they take.
