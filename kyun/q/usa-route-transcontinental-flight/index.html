Who first flew across America?

In 1911 a pilot crossed the USA in a plane named after a grape soda, and crashed so often it was almost rebuilt on the way.

The short answer

The first person to fly across the United States was Calbraith Perry Rodgers, a tall, cigar-chewing pilot known as Cal. He left Sheepshead Bay in Brooklyn, New York, on 17 September 1911. He reached Pasadena, California, on 5 November, 49 days later.

He did not fly straight. He hopped from field to field, landing about 70 times, and crashed so often that mechanics rebuilt much of the plane on the way. His time actually in the air was only about 82 hours. Today a passenger jet does the same trip in about six hours, while you watch a film and eat peanuts.

Surprising but true

When the Vin Fiz finally reached California, almost nothing of the original plane was left except a rudder and a drip pan.

A prize, a plane and a grape soda

In 1910 the newspaper owner William Randolph Hearst offered $50,000 to the first pilot to cross the country in 30 days or less. Rodgers had learned to fly only that summer, at the school run by the Wright brothers. He bought one of their light Model EX planes.

Planes were costly, so he found a sponsor. A company selling a new grape soda called Vin Fiz paid him for every mile flown, as long as the drink's name was painted on the wings. The plane became a flying advertisement, years before most people had ever seen a plane at all.

A train full of spare parts

Rodgers had a secret helper below him. A special train followed his route, carrying his wife Mabel, his mechanics and a whole hangar's worth of spare parts. Pilots of the time often steered by following railway lines, so the railroad across America doubled as his map.

He needed that train. He crashed on take-off and on landing, again and again. He was hurt more than once and at one point carried his crutches strapped to the plane. By the time he reached California, nearly every part had been replaced. Only the vertical rudder and a drip pan from the original plane are said to have made it the whole way.

Too slow for the prize

The 30 days ran out while Rodgers was still in Texas, so he never won Hearst's money. He kept going anyway, because being first still mattered to him. His winding route through Chicago and the southern deserts covered about 6,800 km, far longer than a straight line.

After Pasadena he wanted to touch the ocean too. A crash delayed him for weeks, but on 10 December 1911 he rolled his wheels into the Pacific surf at Long Beach. That same year, on the other side of the world, a small plane carried the first airmail in India, across the Yamuna at Allahabad.

Faster and faster

Records fell quickly after that. In 1921 the US Post Office flew mail coast to coast in relays, partly at night, in about 33 hours. In May 1923 two army pilots, Oakley Kelly and John Macready, flew a big Fokker T-2 from New York to San Diego without landing once. It took them about 27 hours.

The most startling record came in 1990. A Lockheed SR-71 Blackbird spy plane crossed from Los Angeles to Washington, D.C. in a little over an hour. That is less time than many school buses take to finish their morning round.

Where the Vin Fiz is now

The patched-up Vin Fiz survived. It hangs today in the Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum in Washington. Next to the sleek jets around it, it looks like a kite made of wood, wire and cloth.

Rodgers himself died in a crash at Long Beach in April 1912, only months after his big flight. His trip proved something people doubted in 1911: a plane could link one coast to the other. The question of how a machine of wood and cloth stays up at all is answered by the same physics that lifts every airliner today.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The first flight across America was a single, non-stop dash.

Truth: The first crossing in 1911 took 49 days and about 70 stops. Nobody crossed without landing until 1923, when two army pilots flew a Fokker T-2 from New York to San Diego.
