What is chewing gum made of?

Scientists read the complete DNA of a girl who lived 5,700 years ago, from a lump of gum she spat out.

The short answer

Chewing gum has two main parts. The first is the gum base, the rubbery part that never dissolves no matter how long you chew. The second is everything that does dissolve: sweeteners, flavours, softeners and colour.

Long ago, gum bases came from nature, such as the sticky sap of trees. Today, most gum base is made from synthetic polymers, long chains of molecules similar to those in some plastics and rubbers, approved as safe to chew. Gum makers rarely share their exact recipes. The flavour fades as your saliva washes the sweeteners and flavourings away, until only the tasteless base is left.

Surprising but true

Old-fashioned chewing gum came from the sap of the sapodilla tree, the same tree that gives India its chikoo fruit.

Stone Age gum

People have chewed sticky stuff for thousands of years. In Scandinavia, archaeologists have found lumps of birch-bark tar, a black, gooey substance made by heating birch bark, with human tooth marks in them. Some are around 10,000 years old. People probably chewed the tar to soften it for use as glue for Stone Age tools.

In 2019, scientists in Denmark studied a 5,700-year-old lump of chewed birch tar found at a site called Syltholm. From the saliva still trapped in it, they read the girl's complete DNA. They worked out she probably had dark skin, dark hair and blue eyes, and they even found traces of a meal of duck and hazelnuts.

The sapodilla tree

In Central America, the Maya and Aztecs chewed chicle, the milky sap of the sapodilla tree. Workers called chicleros climbed the trees and cut zigzag slashes into the bark, then collected the sap that oozed out and boiled it into blocks.

The sapodilla is the same tree that gives India its chikoo fruit. It was brought to India from the Americas and now grows widely in Gujarat, Maharashtra and Karnataka. Ancient Greeks had their own gum too: mastic, a resin from a tree that grows on the island of Chios.

How gum became big business

In the 1860s, a New York inventor named Thomas Adams got a supply of chicle and tried to turn it into rubber. It did not work. Instead, he rolled it into small pieces and sold it as chewing gum, and in 1871 he patented a gum-making machine.

A salesman named William Wrigley Jr. made gum famous in the 1890s with bold advertising. Spearmint and Juicy Fruit gums, launched in 1893, are still sold. In 1928, Walter Diemer, an accountant at a Philadelphia company, found a recipe stretchy enough to blow bubbles. During the Second World War, American soldiers carried gum in their rations and helped spread it around the world.

From trees to factories

Chicle was slow and costly to collect, and demand was huge. By the middle of the 20th century, chemists had made synthetic replacements from petroleum. Most modern gum is made from these. A few companies still sell gum made from natural chicle.

Because gum base does not break down easily, spat-out gum is a big cleaning problem. Grey blobs on pavements can stay for years, and removing them takes steam machines and scrapers.

Gum and the law

In 1992, Singapore banned the sale of chewing gum, partly because of gum stuck on pavements and in the doors of its new metro trains. Since 2004, gum with health benefits, such as nicotine gum or some dental gums, can be sold in pharmacies. Visitors are still often surprised by the rule.

Sugar-free gum can actually help teeth. Chewing makes more saliva, which washes away food and acid. Dentists say it is no replacement for brushing, though.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Swallowed chewing gum stays in your stomach for seven years.

Truth: Your body cannot digest the gum base, but it does not get stuck. It moves through your gut and leaves your body in a few days, like other things you cannot digest. Swallowing large amounts often is still a bad idea, especially for small children.

Try this

Hold a stopwatch and chew a piece of sugar-free gum for one minute, then five, then ten, tasting it each time. Write down when the flavour fades. The sweeteners and flavour dissolve in your saliva and are swallowed, but the gum base, which does not dissolve, stays behind in your mouth.
