What is the Voynich manuscript?

A 600-year-old book is full of plants nobody can name, written in a script nobody can read. Codebreakers have tried for over a century.

The short answer

The Voynich manuscript is a small, thick book about the size of a school notebook. Its pages are packed with flowing handwriting in an alphabet found nowhere else on Earth. Around the words are drawings of plants that don't quite match any real plant, circles of stars, and tiny people bathing in green pools connected by strange tubes.

Scientists have dated its pages to the early 1400s. That is roughly when the Vijayanagara empire ruled much of south India. Since 1969 it has lived at Yale University in the USA, catalogued as MS 408. Anyone can look at every page online. Nobody can read a single word.

Surprising but true

Some of the codebreakers who cracked secret messages in the Second World War tried to read the Voynich manuscript, and failed.

A book with a strange family tree

The book is named after Wilfrid Voynich, a Polish-born bookseller who bought it in Italy in 1912. Tucked inside was a letter from 1665 or 1666. It claimed that Emperor Rudolf II of Bohemia, a ruler who loved oddities, had paid 600 gold coins for it. If that is true, it was a fortune for a book nobody could understand.

Before that, the trail goes cold. We don't know who wrote it, where, or why.

What's inside

Experts sort the pages into sections by their pictures. There are about 240 pages left, and some fold out to several times the normal size.

  • - Plants: over 100 drawings, most of them unidentified.
  • - Stars and zodiac: circular charts with suns, moons and star signs.
  • - Bathing: small figures in pools and pipes.
  • - Medicine: jars, roots and leaves, a bit like a pharmacy guide.
  • - Text only: short paragraphs marked with little stars.

The writing runs smoothly, with no crossings-out. Whoever wrote it seems to have known exactly what they were putting down.

Codebreakers versus the book

After the Second World War, William Friedman, one of America's greatest code experts, set up a team to study it. These were people who had cracked enemy ciphers. They got nowhere.

Here is the odd part. The text behaves a lot like real language. Some words appear very often and most appear rarely, which is how English, Hindi and every other language work. But some words repeat two or three times in a row, which real languages almost never do. And a few "words" appear only in certain sections, as if each part has its own vocabulary.

Computers have since counted every character. That gives people a lot to argue about, but no answers.

The three big guesses

It's a real language in an invented script. Someone may have written a known language, such as a dialect of German or Italian, in a home-made alphabet. Then the challenge is matching the shapes to sounds.

It's a code. The words might be scrambled using a system, like the ones in cryptography. If so, it is a very unusual one.

It's a hoax. In 2004 a British computer scientist, Gordon Rugg, showed that a simple card with holes could churn out text with some Voynich-like patterns. Others reply that a fake from the 1400s would have taken months of careful work, all for a joke.

Why it still matters

Every claim to have solved it fails the same test. A real solution should let you read a page you haven't seen yet. So far, none does.

The Voynich manuscript is not the only unreadable writing in the world. The Indus script, carved on seals more than 4,000 years ago, also waits for its key. The difference is that the Indus people left only short lines. The Voynich author left an entire book, and still told us nothing.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Someone cracked the Voynich manuscript a few years ago.

Truth: Every few years a person announces they have solved it, and news stories spread. So far, no solution has convinced the experts who study it, because none can be used to read new pages correctly.
