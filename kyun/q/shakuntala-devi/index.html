Who was Shakuntala Devi, the "human computer"?

In 1980 a woman from Bengaluru multiplied two 13-digit numbers in her head in 28 seconds, and the answer went into the Guinness Book of Records.

The short answer

Shakuntala Devi was an Indian mathematician and performer who could do enormous calculations in her head faster than most people could type them into a machine. Newspapers called her the "human computer".

She toured the world from the 1950s onwards, solving problems shouted out by audiences at universities, schools and theatres. In 1980 she set a world record by multiplying two 13-digit numbers in 28 seconds. She also beat a computer in a contest in Dallas in 1977, finding the 23rd root of a 201-digit number. Later in life she wrote books to help children enjoy maths, arguing that numbers should be fun rather than frightening.

Surprising but true

Shakuntala Devi never went to school regularly. Her father, who worked in a circus, noticed her talent when she beat him at card tricks at about the age of three.

A circus childhood

Shakuntala Devi was born in Bangalore, now Bengaluru, on 4 November 1929. Her father performed in a circus as a trapeze artist and showman. When she was about three, he was teaching her card tricks and realised she was remembering every card and beating him.

The family was poor, and she never attended school regularly. Instead, her father took her on the road to show her skills. By the age of six she was performing calculations at the University of Mysore, and she was soon giving shows across India.

The record in London

On 18 June 1980, at Imperial College London, a computer picked two random 13-digit numbers: 7,686,369,774,870 and 2,465,099,745,779. Shakuntala Devi was asked to multiply them.

She gave the full 26-digit answer, 18,947,668,177,995,426,462,773,730, in 28 seconds. The time included saying all those digits out loud. The feat was listed in the 1982 Guinness Book of Records. To get a sense of the size, that answer is far bigger than the number of grains of sand on a beach, a scale explored in big numbers.

How did she do it?

She never fully explained her methods. What is clear is that she practised with numbers every day from early childhood. In 1988, the American psychologist Arthur Jensen tested her at the University of California, Berkeley. He found she could find cube roots and other answers faster than he could copy down the question.

Scientists who study mental calculation think experts like her use a huge store of number facts in memory, so they don't have to work out small steps again. They also break big problems into chunks in clever ways. Mental calculators of this kind are rare, but their skills seem to be built from ordinary brain abilities pushed to an extreme.

Teacher and writer

Shakuntala Devi wanted other people, especially children, to stop fearing maths. She wrote books such as Figuring: The Joy of Numbers and Puzzles to Puzzle You, full of tricks, riddles and shortcuts. She also wrote on astrology and cookery.

In 1977 she published a book about gay people, one of the first in India to discuss them with sympathy. It was unusual and brave for its time. In 1980 she even stood for election to the Lok Sabha, India's lower house of Parliament, though she did not win.

Remembered today

Shakuntala Devi died in Bengaluru on 21 April 2013, aged 83. In 2020, a Hindi film about her life, starring Vidya Balan, brought her story to a new generation. Teachers still use her puzzle books, and her record from 1980 is still talked about whenever people compare human brains with machines.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Shakuntala Devi could calculate faster than any computer.

Truth: She sometimes beat computers in public contests, but those races usually counted the time needed to type in a program. For pure arithmetic, even computers of the 1970s were far faster. What made her special was doing it with a human brain.

Practise one of Shakuntala Devi's favourite kinds of trick. To multiply any two-digit number by 11, add its two digits and put the total in the middle. So 43 × 11 is 4, then 4 + 3 = 7, then 3, which gives 473. Work out what to do when the digits add up to more than 9.
