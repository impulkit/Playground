How do companies sell all over the world?

McDonald's opened in India in 1996 without a single beef burger on the menu, and the potato McAloo Tikki became one of its best sellers.

The short answer

Companies sell around the world by setting up shops, factories or partners in other countries, and by changing their products to suit local people. A company that does this is called a multinational.

Some open their own branches. Others let local business owners run shops under their name, which is called franchising. Many make things in one country, sell them in another and buy parts from a dozen more. Ships, planes, the internet and trade agreements tie it all together. The hard part is not getting the product there. It is persuading people in a new place, with different tastes, laws and languages, to want it.

Surprising but true

Jaguar and Land Rover, two famous British car brands, have been owned by India's Tata Motors since 2008.

Changing the recipe

When McDonald's opened its first Indian restaurant in Delhi in 1996, it knew a beef burger would not work. It invented new items: the McAloo Tikki, a spiced potato patty, and a chicken version of the Big Mac called the Maharaja Mac. Its kitchens keep vegetarian and non-vegetarian food apart.

Other companies do the same. KitKat in Japan comes in dozens of flavours, including green tea. Pizza chains in India add paneer and tandoori toppings. The idea is called localisation. A global brand keeps its name and logo but bends almost everything else. It's part of the long story of fast food spreading across the world.

Even names and adverts change. Some products are renamed so they are easy to say, or so they don't sound silly in the local language. Adverts swap faces, music and festivals. In India, many global brands launch their biggest campaigns around Diwali, while in the USA the busiest shopping season begins just after Thanksgiving.

One product, many countries

A modern phone shows how spread out making things has become. Its design might come from California or South Korea. Its chips may be made in Taiwan, its screen in Korea or China, its rare metals mined in Africa or South America. It is then put together in a factory in China, Vietnam or, more and more, India.

Many of the smartphones sold worldwide are now assembled in India, some for export. Parts and finished goods travel in steel containers on giant ships, through busy sea ports such as Mumbai's Nhava Sheva or Los Angeles.

How do giant ships use ports?The biggest container ships are about 400 m long, with room for over 20,000 of the smaller steel boxes, yet a port can empty and refill one in a couple of days.

Going the other way

Global companies are not all American or European. Tata, an Indian group, bought the British tea brand Tetley in 2000 and the car makers Jaguar and Land Rover in 2008. Indian software companies such as Infosys and TCS run computer systems for banks and shops across the world.

Smaller firms go global too. A craft business in Jaipur can sell block-printed cloth to buyers in Texas through a website. Selling abroad once needed offices in each country. Today, for some products, it needs a good website and a reliable courier. Some companies end up with more customers abroad than at home. Netflix, an American company, and Spotify, a Swedish one, both have most of their users outside their home countries.

The tricky parts

Selling abroad brings headaches. Each country has its own taxes, safety rules and labelling laws. Money changes value, so a price that made sense last year might not this year. Advertising has to work in new languages, and a joke or colour that feels friendly in one place can feel rude in another.

Big global companies also face criticism. Some are accused of pushing out small local shops, paying low wages in factories or avoiding taxes. Governments try to set rules so that their own businesses can compete fairly. Others argue that global companies bring jobs, cheaper goods and new ideas. Both views shape the laws countries write about trade.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Chevy Nova car flopped in Spanish-speaking countries because "no va" means "it doesn't go".

Truth: This story is told in business classes everywhere, but it isn't true. The Nova sold reasonably well in places like Mexico and Venezuela, and Spanish speakers read "nova" as one word, not "no va".
