How does a magnifying glass make things bigger?

A magnifying glass doesn't make anything bigger. It bends light so cleverly that your brain is fooled into seeing a larger thing that isn't there.

The short answer

A magnifying glass is a lens, a piece of clear glass or plastic that is thicker in the middle than at the edges. This shape is called convex.

When light passes from air into glass and out again, it bends. This bending is called refraction. A convex lens bends light rays inwards, towards each other. When you hold the lens close to something small, like an ant, the light rays from the ant are bent before they reach your eye.

Your brain doesn't know the light was bent. It assumes light travels in straight lines, so it traces the rays backwards and pictures a much bigger ant, further away. That bigger ant is an image that exists only in your head.

Surprising but true

A single round drop of water can work as a magnifying glass, and in the 1670s a Dutch cloth merchant used tiny glass beads to see bacteria for the first time.

Why the brain is fooled

Your brain has spent your whole life learning that light travels straight. When rays from an object reach your eyes, the brain works out the object's size from how widely the rays are spread.

A convex lens spreads the rays from a small object so they reach your eye as if they came from something large. The brain trusts its rule and shows you a giant. Scientists call this a virtual image, because no light actually comes from where you see it.

The trick only works when the object is fairly close to the lens. Each lens has a distance called its focal point. Inside it, you get a big upright image. Beyond it, the picture blurs, then flips upside down and shrinks.

Reading stones and spectacles

People have known for a very long time that curved glass and water make things look bigger. Around 1011 to 1021, the Arab scientist Ibn al-Haytham, working in Cairo, wrote a huge Book of Optics explaining how light and vision work. It was later translated into Latin and studied across Europe.

By the 1200s, monks in Europe were using polished "reading stones", rounded pieces of crystal laid on top of a page to enlarge the letters. Not long after, craftsmen in Italy began fitting two small lenses into a frame. Those were the first spectacles.

How did microscopes reveal a hidden world?In the 1670s, a Dutch cloth seller peered through a lens smaller than a pea and saw tiny creatures swimming in a drop of pond water. Nobody had ever seen them before.

Seeing tiny life

In the 1670s, a Dutch cloth merchant named Antonie van Leeuwenhoek wanted to look closely at the threads in fabric. He learned to grind tiny, very curved lenses, some smaller than a peppercorn, and mounted them in metal holders. His best lenses magnified things around 270 times.

Looking at pond water and scrapings from teeth, he saw tiny moving creatures nobody had seen before. He called them "animalcules". We now know they included bacteria. His single-lens instruments were really very strong magnifying glasses, and they helped begin the science of microscopes.

Bigger, but not sharper forever

A stronger magnifying glass has a more curved surface. It bends light more and magnifies more, but you have to hold it very close and you see a smaller area. Ordinary hand lenses rarely go beyond about ten times. Stronger single lenses have to be tiny and held almost against the eye, as van Leeuwenhoek's were, and they tend to blur colours and edges.

That's why scientists combine lenses into microscopes and telescopes. Jewellers, stamp collectors and doctors examining skin still use simple hand lenses every day. A magnifying glass can also focus sunlight into a hot, bright spot. That's why lenses should never be left in sunny windows, and nobody should ever look at the Sun through one.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Using a magnifying glass or reading glasses weakens your eyes.

Truth: Eye doctors say there's no evidence for this. Lenses only change the light reaching your eyes, and your eyes go back to normal when you take them away. Many adults need reading glasses as they age because the lens inside the eye stiffens, not because of the glasses.

Try this

Lay a clear plastic sheet over a newspaper and put one drop of water on it with your finger. Look at the letters through the drop. They look bigger. Try a small drop and a bigger one. Which magnifies more? Never look at the Sun through any lens.
