Why do we need fractions?

An American burger chain once lost customers because people thought a third of a pound of meat was smaller than a quarter.

The short answer

Whole numbers are good for counting things that come in ones, like apples or children. But the world is full of parts. You might eat half a roti, wait a quarter of an hour, or share a pizza among three friends. Fractions are how we describe and calculate with those parts.

A fraction has two numbers. The bottom one, the denominator, says how many equal pieces the whole is cut into. The top one, the numerator, says how many of those pieces you have. So 3/4 means three pieces of something cut into four. Without fractions, cooking, building, music, money and sharing would be much harder.

Surprising but true

Hindi has everyday words for fractions that English lacks, like sawa for one and a quarter, dedh for one and a half and dhai for two and a half.

Pharaohs and parts

The ancient Egyptians used fractions over 3,500 years ago. A scroll called the Rhind papyrus, now in the British Museum, is full of maths problems, like how to share loaves among workers. Egyptian scribes mostly used unit fractions, which have 1 on top, like 1/2 or 1/5. To write 3/4, they wrote 1/2 + 1/4.

This sounds clumsy, but it was practical for dividing bread. Share 5 loaves among 8 people by giving each person 1/2 + 1/8 of a loaf. Records from ancient Egypt show workers on great building projects were paid partly in bread and beer, so fair shares really mattered. Getting a fraction wrong could mean a hungry worker, or an angry crew.

A number on top of a number

Indian mathematicians, from at least the time of Brahmagupta in the 600s, wrote fractions as one number above another, with no line between. Arab mathematicians adopted the idea and, by around the 1100s, added the bar between the two. Europeans learned it from them. The way you write fractions at school has a long journey behind it.

Fractions also live in everyday language. In Hindi and many other Indian languages, there are single words for amounts like 1¼ (sawa), 1½ (dedh), 2½ (dhai) and ¾ (paune). Shopkeepers and grandparents use them without thinking. Some Indian children even learned "times tables" for these, like the dhai ka pahada, the table of two and a half.

Half notes and half time

Music depends on fractions. In Western music, a whole note can be split into two half notes, four quarter notes or eight eighth notes. Musicians in the United States still use those names. Each music note tells a player how long to hold a sound, as a fraction of a beat.

Sport and time use them too. A football match has two halves. Basketball games in the NBA have four quarters. "Quarter past nine" means 15 minutes, a quarter of an hour, after nine.

Money in pieces

For centuries, Indian money was based on sixteenths. Sixteen annas made one rupee, and four paise made one anna. In 1957, India switched to a decimal system, with 100 naye paise in a rupee. The old word stays alive in phrases like "solah aane sach", sixteen annas true, meaning completely true.

American stock prices were quoted in fractions of a dollar, like 25 3/8, until about 2001, when markets switched to decimals. That habit went back to old Spanish silver dollars, which were often cut into eight pieces. Today, decimals and percentages are other ways of writing parts of a whole, and every one of them can be turned back into a fraction.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A fraction with a bigger number on the bottom is bigger, so 1/8 is more than 1/4.

Truth: The bottom number tells you how many pieces the whole is cut into. More pieces means each piece is smaller. So 1/8 of a roti is half the size of 1/4 of the same roti.

Try this
