How did theatre begin?

In ancient Greece, an audience of thousands could hear an actor's voice clearly from the back row of an open-air theatre, with no microphones at all.

The short answer

Theatre, acting out stories in front of an audience, began in many places. Two of the oldest traditions we know well are from ancient Greece and ancient India.

In Athens, more than 2,500 years ago, plays grew out of religious festivals for the god Dionysus, with songs, dance and masked actors. In India, Sanskrit drama was described in a great guide called the Natyashastra, roughly 2,000 years old, which explains acting, music, dance, costume and stage design. Theatre also grew separately in China, Japan, Southeast Asia and elsewhere, often from festivals, storytelling and dance. Nobody can point to a single first play.

Surprising but true

Kutiyattam, a form of Sanskrit theatre from Kerala, is one of the oldest living theatre traditions in the world, and a single performance can be spread over many nights.

Festivals in Athens

Every spring, Athens held a big festival for Dionysus, god of wine and celebration. At first, a chorus of men sang and danced stories together. Greek tradition says that in the 500s BCE a performer named Thespis stepped out from the chorus and spoke as a character. That's why actors are sometimes called thespians.

Soon writers like Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides were writing tragedies about gods, kings and terrible choices. Others wrote comedies that made fun of politicians. Plays were performed as a competition, and ancient Greek citizens took them very seriously.

The theatre that carries a whisper

Greek theatres were built into hillsides, with stone seats rising in a half circle around a round floor where the chorus danced. The Theatre of Epidaurus, built in the 300s BCE, seats about 13,000 people.

Its sound is famous. A coin dropped or a match struck on the stage can be heard high up in the back rows. Scientists think the rows of stone seats help, reflecting the voices back towards the audience while soaking up some background noise.

Bharata and the Natyashastra

The Natyashastra is said to have been written by a sage named Bharata. Scholars date it anywhere from about 200 BCE to 200 CE. It is a huge handbook covering how to move the eyes, hands and feet, how to build a stage and how to stir the audience's feelings.

Its most famous idea is rasa, the emotional flavour a performance creates, such as love, courage, sorrow or wonder. A good play, it says, leaves the audience tasting these feelings, the way a good meal leaves many flavours. Indian classical dances, including Kathak and Bharatanatyam, still use ideas from it.

Kalidasa and Sanskrit plays

The poet Kalidasa, who probably lived around the 4th or 5th century CE, wrote some of the most loved Sanskrit plays. The best known, Abhijnanashakuntalam, tells of the king Dushyanta and Shakuntala, who forget and find each other again. When it was translated into English in 1789, it caused a stir among European writers.

In Kerala, Kutiyattam keeps a form of Sanskrit theatre alive. Actors use detailed eye and hand movements, and one act can be stretched across many nights.

Theatre for everyone

Not all theatre happened in grand buildings. Across India, folk forms like Yakshagana in Karnataka, Jatra in Bengal, Tamasha in Maharashtra and Nautanki in north India travelled from village to village. Every year, Ramlila performances act out the Ramayana in towns across north India, sometimes for weeks.

In London, Shakespeare's plays drew huge crowds to the Globe Theatre, built in 1599, where poorer people paid a penny to stand. In Japan, Noh theatre began in the 1300s and Kabuki in the 1600s. Today Broadway in New York, London's West End and the theatres of Mumbai and Kolkata carry on the same old idea: people gathered in the dark, watching a story come alive.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Theatre began in one place and spread from there to the rest of the world.

Truth: Theatre grew up in many places, including Greece, India, China and Japan, often from religious festivals, storytelling and dance. These traditions developed in different ways, mostly on their own.
