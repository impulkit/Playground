What can dominoes teach us about maths?

A falling domino can knock over another about one and a half times its size. Start with one just 5 millimetres tall, and the fifteenth domino in a growing line could be as tall as a ten-year-old.

The short answer

Dominoes look like a simple game, but they are packed with maths. Counting the tiles in a set leads to triangular numbers. Covering a board with dominoes leads to the Fibonacci pattern and to puzzles that seem impossible until you spot the trick. And a falling line of dominoes is a lesson in energy, the same idea mathematicians use to prove things go on forever.

All you need to explore it is a box of tiles, or some rectangles cut from card.

Surprising but true

A standard double-six domino set has exactly 28 tiles, which is 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6 + 7. The pattern is no accident.

Counting the set

A standard set goes from blank-blank up to six-six. Each tile is a pair of numbers from 0 to 6, and no pair repeats. How many tiles is that?

Start with the tiles that include a six. There are seven: six with 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6. Then the tiles with a five but no six: six of them. Keep going and you add 7 + 6 + 5 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 28. Numbers made this way are called triangular numbers, because you can arrange that many dots into a triangle. A double-nine set follows the same rule and has 55 tiles.

Dominoes and Fibonacci

Take a strip of squares two high and a few long, and cover it with dominoes, each covering two squares. A strip one long can be covered in only one way. Two long, in two ways. Three long, three ways. Four long, five ways. Five long, eight ways.

Each answer is the sum of the two before it. That is the Fibonacci sequence, which also turns up in sunflower seeds and pine cones. The reason is neat. The right-hand end of the strip is either one upright domino or two lying flat, so every tiling grows out of the two shorter strips.

The impossible chessboard

Here's a famous puzzle. A chessboard has 64 squares, and 32 dominoes cover it perfectly. Now cut off two opposite corners, leaving 62 squares. Can 31 dominoes cover what's left?

People try for ages. The answer is no, and colour shows why. Every domino covers one black and one white square. But the two cut corners are the same colour, so the board now has 32 squares of one colour and 30 of the other. No arrangement can ever work. A clever idea replaced hours of trying.

The chain reaction

Stand a domino upright and it stores a little energy, because its centre is raised. Tip it, and gravity turns that into movement, enough to push over the next one. In 1983 the physicist Lorne Whitehead showed a domino can topple one about 1.5 times bigger.

That multiplies quickly. Fifteen dominoes, each 1.5 times taller than the last and starting at 5 millimetres, would end with one almost 1.5 metres high. Twenty-nine would end with a domino more than 400 metres tall, in theory. Physicists have built real chains like this, and the tiny first push is barely a nudge with a fingertip.

Proofs that fall forever

Mathematicians use dominoes to explain a way of proving things called induction. Show that the first domino falls. Then show that whenever one falls, it knocks the next. Together, those two facts prove every domino in an endless line falls, without checking them one by one.

Dominoes themselves came from ancient China, where they were recorded about 800 years ago and often had pips for dice rolls. They reached Europe in the 1700s. Their cousins, shapes made of four squares, became famous in 1984 when a Soviet computer scientist used them to make Tetris, a game about filling gaps, which is a cousin of tessellations.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A falling domino can only knock over one the same size or smaller.

Truth: Experiments show one domino can topple another about one and a half times its size. A growing chain multiplies that, so small pushes can end up toppling big blocks.

Try this

Draw a strip of squares on paper, two squares tall and four long. Use small rectangles cut from card, each covering two squares, and find every way to cover the strip. You should find five. Then try a strip five long and see if the Fibonacci pattern holds.
