Who was Mansa Musa?

In 1324 a king from West Africa crossed the Sahara with so much gold that he made its price fall in Cairo for years.

The short answer

Mansa Musa was a king of the Mali Empire, which stretched across West Africa in the 1300s. "Mansa" means king or emperor. He ruled from about 1312 to 1337, when Mali controlled some of the richest gold fields in the world.

He is famous above all for one journey. In 1324 he set off on the Hajj, the Muslim pilgrimage to Mecca, crossing the Sahara with a vast caravan. Writers in Cairo were still talking about it years later. He spent and gave away so much gold that its value dropped there. He came home with scholars and builders and turned the city of Timbuktu into a centre of learning.

Surprising but true

A Spanish map made in 1375, the Catalan Atlas, shows Mansa Musa on a throne in the middle of Africa, holding a large gold nugget.

Gold and salt

Mali grew rich by standing between two things people wanted. To the south lay gold fields, where miners dug and panned for gold. To the north, deep in the Sahara, lay salt mines at places like Taghaza, where people cut salt into heavy slabs.

People in hot lands lose salt as they sweat, so they need it badly. Traders carried salt south on camels and took gold north to the Mediterranean. Mali's kings taxed this trade. Much of the gold used in Europe and North Africa at the time came from West Africa.

The empire itself had been founded more than 70 years before Musa by Sundiata Keita, around the 1230s. His story is still sung by griots, the storytellers and musicians of West Africa.

The great caravan

Accounts of Musa's caravan describe thousands of people, from soldiers and officials to servants. Camels carried loads of gold. Some numbers in old books, like 60,000 people, are probably exaggerated, but everyone agrees it was huge.

In Cairo, the writer al-Umari spoke to people who had met Musa. They said he handed out gold so freely that the metal lost value in the city's markets for about 12 years. By the end of his journey, Musa had run short and borrowed money to get home. The story spread across the Mediterranean.

Timbuktu and its books

Musa came back with scholars, judges and an architect, often named as Abu Ishaq al-Sahili. In Timbuktu he ordered the Djinguereber Mosque, built of mud brick and wood. A version of it still stands, and it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Over the next two centuries, Timbuktu became famous for learning. Students came to study religion, law, astronomy and mathematics. Families collected handwritten books, and hundreds of thousands of old manuscripts survive in the region. In 2012 and 2013, during fighting in northern Mali, librarians quietly smuggled many of them to safety.

A traveller's report

About 15 years after Musa died, the Moroccan traveller Ibn Battuta visited Mali during the reign of Musa's brother, Mansa Sulayman. He praised how safe the roads were and wrote that travellers did not fear thieves. He also grumbled about the gifts he received, which he thought were too small.

Remembered on a map

Mali's fame reached Europe. On the Catalan Atlas, made in Majorca in 1375, Mansa Musa sits on a throne wearing a gold crown. He holds a gold nugget up to a rider approaching on a camel. For European mapmakers who had never been to Africa, Mali meant one thing: gold.

The empire weakened in the 1400s, and the Songhai Empire took over much of its land. Today, Mansa Musa often appears on lists of the richest people in history. Those lists use guesswork, and the comparisons don't really work. What we do know is that a West African king once caught the attention of half the known world.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Mansa Musa was the richest person who ever lived, worth hundreds of billions of dollars.

Truth: He was certainly enormously rich, but there is no fair way to compare his wealth with modern billionaires. The big dollar numbers you see online are modern estimates without real evidence behind them.

Try this

On a world map or a globe, find Timbuktu in Mali, then Cairo in Egypt, then Mecca. Lay a piece of string along the route and use the map's scale to measure it. In a straight line it is roughly 5,000 km, and Mansa Musa's caravan walked farther than that, much of it through desert.
