How do artists make a flat picture look deep?

A drawing is flat. So why does a road in a painting seem to go on forever?

The short answer

A picture is flat, but your brain is easy to fool. Artists use the same clues your eyes rely on in real life. Faraway things look smaller. Closer things hide things behind them. Distant hills look paler and bluer.

The most powerful trick is called linear perspective. Parallel lines, like the two rails of a railway track, seem to meet at a single spot on the horizon, called the vanishing point. Draw your lines heading to that point, and a flat page suddenly seems to open up like a window.

Around 1415 in Florence, Italy, the architect Filippo Brunelleschi showed how to do this with mathematical precision.

Surprising but true

Faraway hills look blue for the same reason the sky does.

Brunelleschi's peephole

Brunelleschi painted a small picture of a building in Florence, the Baptistery, using his new rules. Then he drilled a hole through the painting. A viewer stood in front of the real Baptistery, looked through the hole from the back, and held up a mirror. In the mirror they saw the painting, lined up with the real building behind it. The painting is now lost, but his biographer wrote that it was hard to tell picture from reality.

In 1435, Leon Battista Alberti wrote the rules down in a book called On Painting. Within a generation, artists across Italy were using vanishing points, and the Renaissance had one of its favourite tools.

Big near, small far

Linear perspective has a rule about size too. A tree twice as far away should be drawn about half as tall. That's why a row of electricity poles beside a highway shrinks steadily into the distance, and why a fielder at the boundary looks tiny on TV compared with the batter.

Overlapping helps as well. If one hill covers part of another, your brain decides it must be in front. Even young children use this clue when they draw. Shadows give another hint. A ball with a shadow right beneath it seems to sit on the ground, but move the shadow away and the ball seems to float.

Blue, hazy hills

Look at a range of hills on a clear day and the farthest ones look paler and bluer than the nearest. That's because there is more air between you and them. Air scatters blue light more than red, the same reason the sky is blue. The farther away something is, the more of that blue haze sits in front of it.

Painters call this aerial or atmospheric perspective. Leonardo da Vinci wrote about it in his notebooks and used it in the misty, blue-green landscape behind the Mona Lisa. You can see the same effect from a hill station in the Western Ghats, where ridge after ridge fades into blue.

Other ways of seeing

Linear perspective is one way to show space, not the only one. Mughal miniature painters often showed several viewpoints in a single picture. A palace courtyard might be seen from above, while the people in it are shown from the side, and a garden pool tilts up so you can see the fish. This let a painter show far more of a scene than one fixed eye could.

Many Chinese scroll painters also avoided a single vanishing point, so a viewer could wander through a landscape as the scroll unrolled. In the 20th century, Pablo Picasso broke the rules on purpose, painting a face from the front and the side at once. Each of these is a choice about what a picture should do.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Before the Renaissance, artists drew "wrong" because they didn't understand depth.

Truth: Many artists chose other ways of showing space on purpose. Roman wall painters could suggest depth, and Mughal, Chinese and Egyptian artists followed their own rules to show what mattered most.

Try this

Stand at a window with a view of a road, a row of buildings or a corridor. Close one eye and keep your head still. With a washable marker, trace the main lines you see straight onto the glass. Then look at your tracing. The lines going away from you should tilt towards one spot, the vanishing point. Wipe the glass clean afterwards.
