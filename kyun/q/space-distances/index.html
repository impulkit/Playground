How far is far in space?

In November 2026, NASA's Voyager 1 is so far away that a radio message from Earth takes a whole day to reach it.

The short answer

Space is so big that the numbers stop making sense. The Moon is about 384,400 kilometres away. The Sun is about 150 million kilometres away, nearly 400 times farther. And the nearest other star is about 40 trillion kilometres away.

To cope, astronomers measure using light. Light is the fastest thing there is, about 300,000 kilometres every second. It crosses from the Moon to Earth in just over one second, but takes about 8 minutes 20 seconds to arrive from the Sun. Light from the nearest star takes more than four years, so we say it is about 4.2 light-years away.

Surprising but true

If the Sun were the size of a football, the nearest other star would be another football more than 6,000 km away, about as far as Delhi is from London.

A drive to the Moon

Try this to get a feel for the distances. Picture a car driving at 100 kilometres an hour without ever stopping. To reach the Moon, it would need about 160 days, roughly half a school year.

To reach the Sun, the same car would need about 170 years. Driving to Proxima Centauri, the nearest star after the Sun, would take around 45 million years. This kind of sum is simple: distance divided by speed. The hard part is believing the answers.

Shrinking the solar system

Another trick is to make a scale model, like a map. Shrink the Sun to the size of a football, about 22 centimetres across. At that scale, Earth is a peppercorn about 24 metres away, the length of a cricket pitch plus a bit. The Moon is a speck of dust about 6 centimetres from the peppercorn.

Jupiter would be a small marble about 120 metres away, and Neptune would be a grain of rice about 700 metres away. Then comes the shock. The nearest star would be another football more than 6,000 kilometres away, about as far as Delhi is from London. Everything in between is almost empty.

Rulers for space

Kilometres soon get clumsy, so astronomers use bigger rulers. Inside the solar system, they use the astronomical unit, or AU, which is the average distance from Earth to the Sun: about 150 million kilometres. Jupiter is about 5 AU from the Sun, and Neptune about 30.

For stars, they use the light-year, the distance light travels in a year, about 9.5 trillion kilometres. Professional astronomers often use another unit called the parsec, which is about 3.26 light-years. Using these rulers is a bit like measuring a road trip in hours rather than centimetres. The numbers become small enough to compare in your head.

Voyager's long trip

In 1977, NASA launched two spacecraft called Voyager. Voyager 1 has been flying away from us ever since, at about 17 kilometres every second. That is fast enough to cross India from Kashmir to Kanyakumari in under four minutes.

In mid-November 2026, Voyager 1 reaches about one light-day from Earth, roughly 25.9 billion kilometres away. A radio command sent from NASA then takes about 24 hours to reach it, and the reply takes another 24 hours to come back. Even at its speed, Voyager would need more than 70,000 years to travel as far as the nearest star.

Looking back in time

Because light takes time to travel, looking into space means looking into the past. You see the Moon as it was a little over a second ago, and the Sun as it was about 8 minutes ago.

On a dark night, you can spot the Andromeda Galaxy as a faint smudge. It is about 2.5 million light-years away, so the light reaching your eyes left it before modern humans existed. Our own Milky Way is about 100,000 light-years across, and it is only one of billions of galaxies. Astronomers have seen light that has been travelling for more than 13 billion years.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The stars in a constellation are close together in space.

Truth: They only look close from Earth. One star in a pattern might be ten times farther away than its neighbour. Seen from another star system, the same stars would make a completely different shape.

Try this

Make a pocket model. Put a football at one end of a long corridor or playground, then walk 24 big steps and put down a peppercorn or a tiny bead for Earth. Try to work out where Neptune would go at the same scale. The answer is about 700 metres away.
