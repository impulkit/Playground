How is food frozen?

Modern frozen food began when an American naturalist watched Inuit fishers in icy Labrador pull fish from the water and saw them freeze solid almost at once.

The short answer

Food is frozen by pulling heat out of it until the water inside turns to ice. At −18 °C, the usual temperature of a home freezer, bacteria and moulds can't grow, and the chemical changes that make food go bad slow almost to a stop.

The real trick is speed. Freeze food slowly and it can turn mushy when it thaws. Freeze it very fast, in minutes rather than hours, and it comes out tasting much closer to fresh. Factories use blasts of freezing air, ice-cold metal plates or even liquid nitrogen to freeze food as quickly as possible.

Surprising but true

Frozen peas are often frozen within a few hours of being picked, so they can keep more of some vitamins than "fresh" peas that spent days in a lorry and a shop.

The fishermen of Labrador

Between about 1912 and 1915, an American naturalist named Clarence Birdseye lived in Labrador, in the far northeast of Canada. In winter the air there could drop to −40 °C. He watched Inuit fishers catch fish through holes in the ice. The fish froze almost instantly in the bitter wind.

Months later, when the fish were cooked, they tasted fresh. Birdseye wondered why, since frozen food sold in American cities at that time was usually soggy and bland. He decided the answer was the speed of freezing, and set out to copy the Arctic with machines.

Ice crystals and cells

Food is made of tiny cells, each a bag of watery juice. When water freezes slowly, it forms large, sharp ice crystals. They grow and pierce the cell walls. When the food thaws, the juice leaks out and the food becomes limp.

When water freezes fast, it has no time to build big crystals. Instead it forms millions of tiny ones, which leave most of the cell walls whole. Birdseye built a machine that pressed packed food between metal plates chilled to very low temperatures. In 1930, the first quick-frozen foods under his name went on sale in Springfield, Massachusetts.

How factories do it

Today, peas, corn and chopped vegetables are usually washed and dipped briefly in boiling water first. This step, called blanching, stops natural enzymes that would otherwise slowly change their colour and taste even in the freezer. Then they travel on a conveyor through a tunnel of air at around −30 °C or colder, which freezes each piece separately.

Some foods, such as prawns or berries, are frozen in liquid nitrogen at about −196 °C. That is so cold that it freezes them almost instantly. Ice cream is frozen while being stirred, so that the crystals stay tiny and the result is smooth, which is the secret of good ice cream.

The cold chain

Frozen food has to stay frozen all the way from the factory to your kitchen. Refrigerated lorries, cold stores and shop freezers make up a "cold chain". If any link breaks and the food thaws and refreezes, its quality drops.

India grows huge amounts of fruit, vegetables and fish, but much of it spoils before it is eaten because there are not enough cold stores and refrigerated trucks, especially near farms. Better cold chains could cut food waste a lot. In the USA, most families have a large freezer at home, and frozen pizza, vegetables and waffles are everyday food.

Is frozen food good for you?

Freezing does not add anything to food. It simply pauses it. A bag of plain frozen peas or spinach can be just as nutritious as fresh. Ready-made frozen meals are a different story, because some contain a lot of salt, sugar or fat, so it is worth reading the label. In Indian supermarkets you can now find frozen parathas, momos and sweetcorn, which shows how far Birdseye's idea from a frozen Canadian lake has travelled.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Frozen vegetables are always less healthy than fresh ones.

Truth: Vegetables frozen soon after harvest keep most of their goodness. Fresh vegetables are great too, but they slowly lose some vitamins while they travel and wait in shops.

Try this

Ask an adult to help you put two small cups of fruit juice in the freezer, one in a thin metal cup and one in a thick plastic cup with a cloth wrapped around it. Check every 30 minutes. The metal cup freezes faster. When both are solid, compare how smooth or crunchy each one feels on your tongue.
