What if all the ice on Earth melted?

The sea would rise by about 70 metres, higher than a 20-storey building. Mumbai, Kolkata, Chennai, New York and Miami would all be under water.

The short answer

If every glacier and ice sheet on Earth melted, the sea would rise by about 65 to 70 metres. That's higher than a 20-storey building. Huge stretches of coastline would vanish, including most of Bangladesh, the Netherlands and Florida, and cities from Mumbai to New York.

It wouldn't happen overnight. Scientists think melting all the ice would take thousands of years, even with fast warming. But some of it is already melting, and the sea has risen about 10 cm since 1993.

Surprising but true

Melting sea ice at the North Pole barely raises sea level at all, because floating ice already pushes aside its own weight in water.

Where the ice is

Almost all of Earth's ice sits on land in two places. Antarctica holds about nine-tenths of it, in a sheet up to about 4.8 km thick. That alone could raise the seas by about 58 metres.

Greenland holds most of the rest, enough for about 7 metres. Mountain glaciers, like those in the Himalaya, the Alps and Alaska, add less than half a metre between them. But they matter in other ways, because hundreds of millions of people in India and nearby countries depend on rivers fed by Himalayan snow and ice.

The new map

With a 70-metre rise, India's coasts would change shape. Mumbai, Kolkata, Chennai and much of Gujarat's coast would be underwater. The sea would push far inland up the Ganga plain and cover most of Bangladesh.

Delhi, at about 200 metres above sea level, would stay dry, and Bengaluru, at over 900 metres, would be safe. In the USA, Florida would almost disappear. New York, Washington DC and Houston would flood. London, Shanghai and Venice would be lost.

Why is the sea level rising?The sea has risen by more than 20 cm since 1880, and it is rising faster now than at any time in thousands of years.

Floating ice doesn't count

When ice floats, it pushes aside its own weight of water. So when floating ice melts, the water level barely changes. That's why melting Arctic sea ice adds almost nothing to sea level. You can test this with an ice cube in a full glass.

Ice on land is different. When it melts and runs into the ocean, it adds new water. That is why scientists watch Greenland and Antarctica so closely. And as seawater warms, it expands a little, which also raises the sea.

How fast is the sea rising now?

NASA measures sea level using satellites. In 2024, it rose by about 0.59 cm, faster than scientists had expected. The rate has more than doubled since 1993.

That doesn't sound like much, but a few centimetres can make storm floods reach much further inland. In the Sundarbans, where India and Bangladesh share the world's largest mangrove forest, some islands have already been swallowed by the sea and many families have had to move.

The land would rise too

Ice is heavy. The Antarctic ice sheet presses the rock beneath it down into the Earth by hundreds of metres. When ice melts, the land slowly bounces back up.

This is happening today in places like Scandinavia and Canada, which were covered by thick ice in the last ice age. Parts of them are still rising by about 1 cm a year, more than 10,000 years after the ice left.

It has happened before

Sea levels have gone up and down many times. About 20,000 years ago, during the last ice age, so much water was locked in ice that the sea was about 120 metres lower than today. People could walk from Asia to North America, and from England to France.

About 3 million years ago, when Earth was a little warmer than today, the seas were probably more than 10 metres higher. That's why scientists say the choices people make about warming the planet now will shape the coastlines of the far future. Meanwhile, the water is already creeping up a few millimetres every year, and rising seas are changing coasts right now.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The melting of Arctic sea ice is what makes the sea rise.

Truth: Arctic sea ice is already floating, so melting it adds almost nothing. Rising seas come mainly from land ice melting into the ocean, and from seawater expanding as it warms.

Try this

Put ice cubes into a glass and fill it with water right to the brim. Watch as the ice melts. The glass won't overflow. Now try piling ice on a plate tipped into a tray of water, as if it were sitting on land. As it melts, the water level in the tray goes up.
