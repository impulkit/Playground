How does a smoke alarm work?

Many smoke alarms contain a speck of radioactive metal smaller than a grain of rice. It's what lets them sniff out smoke.

The short answer

A smoke alarm has a small sensor that notices tiny particles of smoke in the air. When enough smoke drifts inside, the sensor triggers a loud alarm, loud enough to wake most adults in the middle of the night.

There are two main kinds. Ionisation alarms use a tiny amount of radioactive material to make the air inside a little chamber carry a weak electric current. Smoke gets in the way of that current, and the alarm goes off. Photoelectric alarms use light instead. A small light shines inside a dark chamber, and when smoke floats in, it scatters the light onto a sensor. Many alarms today combine both.

Surprising but true

An early smoke detector was invented by accident in the 1930s. A Swiss physicist, Walter Jaeger, was trying to build a detector for poison gas and noticed his machine reacted to cigarette smoke instead.

The ionisation chamber

Inside an ionisation alarm are two small metal plates, close together, with a battery connected across them. Next to them sits a speck of americium-241, a radioactive metal. It gives off tiny particles that knock electrons off the air molecules between the plates. The air becomes charged, so a weak electric current flows across the gap.

When smoke gets in, the smoke particles grab those charged bits of air. The current drops. A small circuit notices the drop and sets off the alarm. Ionisation alarms are very quick to sense fast, flaming fires, which make lots of tiny smoke particles.

The americium decays slowly, losing half its radioactivity in about 430 years, so it lasts far longer than the alarm itself.

The light beam

A photoelectric alarm has a little light and a sensor, set at an angle so the light doesn't normally hit the sensor. The chamber is dark inside, with a maze of walls to keep outside light out but let air in.

When smoke drifts in, the particles scatter light in all directions, like dust in a sunbeam. Some of it hits the sensor, and the alarm sounds. Photoelectric alarms are better at catching smouldering fires, such as a cigarette burning slowly into a sofa, which make bigger, thicker smoke.

A happy accident

In the 1930s, the Swiss physicist Walter Jaeger was trying to invent a device to detect poison gas. His design used ionised air, but the gas had no effect. According to the story often told, he lit a cigarette in frustration and the meter jumped. He had found a smoke detector instead.

Home smoke alarms became cheap and common in the United States in the 1970s, once small battery-powered models were sold. In the US, about three in five home fire deaths happen in homes without working smoke alarms, according to the National Fire Protection Association.

Beeps and chirps

A short chirp every minute or so usually means the battery is running low, not that there's a fire. That chirp is designed to be annoying, so someone fixes it. People sometimes take the battery out to stop beeping when they cook, then forget to put it back. Firefighters say this is one of the most common reasons alarms fail.

Alarms don't last forever. Most need replacing after about ten years, and the date is often printed on the back.

Alarms in India

In India, offices, malls, hotels, hospitals and tall apartment blocks are required by building and fire safety rules to have smoke detection systems. In ordinary homes they are much less common than in the US or UK.

Kitchens cause many home fires, but an alarm right next to a stove will go off every time someone fries puris. That's why experts suggest putting alarms near bedrooms and in hallways, not right above the cooker. Studies have also found that many children sleep through smoke alarms, so families should have a fire plan that includes waking them. Firefighters often visit schools to teach children what to do when they hear one.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The radioactive material in smoke alarms makes them dangerous to have at home.

Truth: The amount is tiny and sealed inside a metal case. The radiation it gives off outside the alarm is far less than what you get naturally from rocks, food and the sky. The only rule is not to open or break the alarm.

Try this

With an adult, press the test button on a smoke alarm at home, at school or in a building you visit, and listen for the beep. Then draw a plan of your home and mark two ways out of every room. Agree on a meeting spot outside, and practise once as a family.
