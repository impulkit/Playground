Why does India have so many kinds of mango?

Plant the stone of your favourite Alphonso and the tree that grows will almost certainly give a different mango. That simple fact explains why India has over a thousand kinds.

The short answer

India has more than a thousand kinds of mango for two reasons. People have grown mangoes here for thousands of years, across very different climates. And a mango stone almost never grows into a copy of its parent.

Each seed carries a new mix of genes from two parent trees, the way brothers and sisters differ. So every seedling tree is a little experiment. When one gave delicious fruit, farmers kept it going by grafting, joining its twigs onto young roots. Over centuries, in thousands of villages, that produced Alphonso, Kesar, Dasheri, Langra, Banganapalli, Himsagar, Totapuri and hundreds more, each with its own colour, shape, smell and taste.

Surprising but true

The emperor Akbar is said to have planted an orchard of about one lakh mango trees near Darbhanga in Bihar, still remembered as the Lakhi Bagh.

Home of the mango

The wild mango comes from the region around northeast India, Bangladesh and Myanmar. Mangoes appear in old Sanskrit and Tamil writing and in Buddhist stories, and the mango tree is sacred in many Hindu traditions. It is India's national fruit.

India is by far the world's largest grower. In the early 2020s it produced roughly two-fifths of all the mangoes grown on Earth, though most are eaten inside India rather than exported.

Kings and their orchards

Mughal emperors loved mangoes. Akbar is said to have planted an orchard of about one lakh trees near Darbhanga in Bihar, and his court kept careful notes on the best kinds. Mughal writers described mangoes with great admiration. Nawabs, rajas and wealthy landlords competed to own rare varieties, and gardeners became skilled at grafting.

Many famous mangoes carry stories in their names. The Dasheri is named after a village near Lucknow where, local people say, the first tree still stands, about 200 years old. The Langra, from Varanasi, means "lame", and is said to be named after a farmer who walked with a limp.

The Portuguese touch

The Alphonso, called Hapus in Marathi, is named after Afonso de Albuquerque, a Portuguese commander in Goa in the early 1500s. The Portuguese are often credited with spreading grafting along India's west coast. Alphonsos from Ratnagiri and Devgad in Maharashtra are now protected with a GI tag, a label that says a product really comes from a particular place.

Scientists also create new kinds on purpose. In the 1970s, researchers in New Delhi crossed the Dasheri with the Neelam to produce the Amrapali, a small tree that suits home gardens and gives fruit every year.

A mango for every month

Because India is so big, mango season moves across the map. The first fruit often arrive from the south as early as March. Alphonsos peak in April and May, Kesar and Dasheri in early summer, and Chausa and Fazli can last into July and August.

Each region swears its own is best. Gujarat has Kesar from the slopes near Gir, Andhra Pradesh has Banganapalli and Bengal has Himsagar. The stringy Totapuri, shaped like a parrot's beak, goes into much of the mango pulp sold in cartons.

Mangoes are eaten unripe too. Raw green mango goes into pickles, chutneys, cooling aam panna and the sour powder called amchur.

Mangoes in America

Most mangoes in American shops are grown in Mexico and Central and South America. A popular kind, the Tommy Atkins, has a tough skin that travels well. Its family tree goes back to Florida in the early 1900s, and to an Indian variety brought there as a young grafted tree.

Indian mangoes were barred from the United States for years because of worries about insect pests. In 2007 the ban was lifted for fruit treated to kill any insects, and Alphonso and Kesar boxes began arriving in Indian grocery shops across the country.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If you plant the stone of a great mango, you'll get a tree with the same great mangoes.

Truth: Most Indian mango varieties don't grow true from seed, because each stone carries a new mix of genes from two parent trees. The fruit can be very different. That's why farmers graft to copy good trees, and also why new varieties keep appearing.

Try this

Hold a mango tasting at home in the season. Get two or three different kinds, cut them, and note the colour of the flesh, how stringy it is, how sweet or sour it tastes and what the smell reminds you of. Give each a score out of ten and see if your family agrees.
