How are ice sculptures made?

Every winter in Harbin, China, workers saw blocks of ice from a frozen river and build a glowing city of castles, slides and towers that melts away by spring.

The short answer

Ice sculptors start with big, clear blocks of ice, often weighing well over 100 kg each. They draw a design on the block or press a paper pattern against it. Then they cut away the rough shape with chainsaws, carve details with chisels and power tools, and smooth the surface with a warm iron or even a heat gun.

Big sculptures are built from many blocks stacked like bricks. A little water brushed between them freezes and welds them together. It all has to be done fast, in freezing air or a cold room, because the artwork starts melting the moment it gets warm.

Surprising but true

Ice from your freezer is cloudy because air gets trapped as it freezes. Sculptors use machines that freeze water slowly from the bottom up, pushing the air out, so their blocks are as clear as glass.

Why clear ice matters

Ordinary ice from a tray is cloudy. As water freezes from the outside in, air and minerals dissolved in it are pushed towards the middle and trapped as tiny bubbles. Cloudy ice looks white and cracks more easily.

Sculptors use special machines that freeze water slowly from the bottom up, while a pump keeps the water moving. Bubbles drift up and away. After a few days, the result is a block as clear as window glass. Light passes straight through, and lit from behind, a finished sculpture seems to glow.

A city made of ice

Harbin, in north-east China, holds the world's biggest ice festival every winter. Temperatures there often drop below minus 20°C. In December, workers cut huge blocks from the frozen Songhua River and haul them to the festival grounds.

Thousands of workers and artists then build castles, temples, slides and towers, some as tall as a ten-storey building. At night, coloured lights inside the ice turn the whole site into a glowing city. Visitors come from all over China and the world, wrapped in thick layers, and phone batteries often give up in the cold long before the visitors do. Japan has a famous winter festival too, in Sapporo, where giant sculptures are carved from packed snow. By spring, it melts away into the river it came from.

Sleeping in ice

In Jukkasjärvi, a village in northern Sweden, the ICEHOTEL is built fresh every winter from ice taken from the Torne River. Artists design new rooms each year, with ice beds covered in reindeer skins. The temperature inside stays at about minus 5°C. Guests sleep in thermal sleeping bags. Each spring, the rooms melt back into the river.

In the USA, the World Ice Art Championships are held in Fairbanks, Alaska, every winter. The local ice is so clear that sculptors call it "Arctic diamond".

Ice in India

Many Indian weddings and hotel buffets feature ice sculptures of swans, elephants or names. In India's heat, they last only a few hours, so carvers often make them in cold rooms on the day and deliver them on trays that catch the drips.

In winter, Kashmir gets real snow and ice art. Gulmarg has drawn crowds to an igloo café built from packed snow, with tables and seats made of snow inside.

The science of a cold art

Water is strange. Most things shrink when they freeze, but water expands by about 9%. That is why a full glass bottle in the freezer can crack, and why ice floats on water. Sculptors must keep this in mind, because ice can crack if it warms or cools too quickly.

Carvers also learn to read a block. Ice is made of crystals, and a sharp knock in the wrong place can split a block along a hidden weak line, a bit like wood splitting along its grain. The same six-sided crystal pattern that runs through a carved swan also shapes every snowflake, which is why snowflakes almost always have six arms.

Myth or fact?

Myth: To get crystal-clear ice, you just need to boil the water first.

Truth: Boiling removes some air, but the ice usually still turns cloudy. The real trick is freezing slowly in one direction, so bubbles and impurities are pushed out of the way instead of trapped.

Try this

Ask an adult to help you fill a small insulated lunch box with water and leave its lid off in the freezer. Fill a plain plastic cup too. After a day, take both out. The lunch box ice freezes mostly from the top down, so its top part is often much clearer than the ice in the cup.
