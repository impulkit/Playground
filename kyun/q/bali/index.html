What is Bali like?

On one day each year, the whole island of Bali goes silent, and even its international airport shuts for 24 hours.

The short answer

Bali is a tropical island in Indonesia, the world's largest island country, just east of Java. It is small, about one and a half times the size of Goa, but packs in volcanoes, rice terraces, beaches and thousands of temples. Millions of tourists visit each year.

What makes Bali different from the rest of Indonesia is its religion. Most Indonesians are Muslim, but most Balinese follow their own form of Hinduism. It came from India more than a thousand years ago and blended with local beliefs about spirits, ancestors and nature. Many visitors from India are surprised to hear stories from the Ramayana and the Mahabharata on the island, though in very Balinese forms.

Surprising but true

A deep channel of sea between Bali and the next island, Lombok, marks a boundary between Asian and Australian wildlife, so the animals change sharply across a strait less than 40 km wide.

Offerings at every doorway

Each morning, Balinese women place small square trays of woven palm leaf on doorsteps, shrines and even car dashboards. These offerings, called canang sari, hold flower petals, a little rice and perhaps an incense stick. They are gifts to gods and spirits, made fresh every day.

Most family compounds have their own temple, and every village has several. That is why Bali is sometimes called the Island of the Gods. Temple festivals happen so often that on many days, somewhere on the island, people in white and gold clothes are carrying tall towers of fruit on their heads.

Water temples and rice terraces

Green rice terraces climb Bali's hillsides like giant steps. They are watered by subak, a system of canals and tunnels that shares water from springs and rivers among farmers. It goes back to at least the 9th century.

Subak is run by farmers' groups together with water temples. Priests and farmers agree when each area will plant and flood its rice fields, so that everyone gets a fair share and pests have less chance to spread. UNESCO made it a World Heritage Site in 2012.

What is the Ramayana?A prince, a princess, a demon king and an army of monkeys. The Ramayana has been retold for more than 2,000 years, from Indian villages to Thai palaces.

Dance, gongs and shadows

Balinese dance is precise and dramatic, with flashing eyes and fingers bent back. In the Kecak dance, a circle of men chant "chak-a-chak" while dancers act out scenes from the Ramayana. Music comes from the gamelan, an orchestra of bronze gongs and metal xylophones that shimmer when played together.

At night, in wayang kulit, a single storyteller works flat leather puppets behind a cloth screen lit by an oil lamp, voicing all the characters and leading the musicians.

The silent day

Nyepi, the Balinese New Year, usually falls in March. The night before, villages parade ogoh-ogoh, giant monster figures made of bamboo and paper, through the streets with drums and torches, then often burn them. The next morning, everything stops. No work, no travel, no lights, no noise. Even the airport closes for a day, and tourists are asked to stay in their hotels.

A line in the sea

In the 1850s, the British naturalist Alfred Russel Wallace noticed something odd. On Bali, the birds and animals were mostly like those of Asia. On Lombok, just across a narrow strait, many were like Australia's, with cockatoos and honeyeaters.

The channel between them is deep. Even in ice ages, when sea levels fell, it never dried up, so animals could not walk across. Scientists still call the boundary the Wallace Line. Bali also sits on a chain of volcanoes: Mount Agung erupted in 1963 and again in 2017, closing the airport.

subak, a UNESCO World Heritage Site

Myth or fact?

Myth: Bali is a country.

Truth: Bali is one of the provinces of Indonesia, a nation of thousands of islands. It is famous enough that many visitors don't realise they are in Indonesia.

Make a stepped hillside from a pile of books covered with a plastic bag. Use small rolls of clay or play dough to build little walls along each step, then pour a cup of water gently at the top. Watch how terraces slow the water and share it from one level to the next, as Bali's rice terraces do.
