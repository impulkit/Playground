Why are flowers colourful?

Many flowers that look plain yellow to you have bold patterns that only bees can see, like landing lights on a runway.

The short answer

Flowers are colourful because they are advertising. Most plants cannot move, so they need animals to carry pollen from one flower to another. Bright petals tell bees, butterflies, birds and even bats: food here. The reward is usually sweet nectar, or extra pollen to eat.

Each colour comes from chemicals in the petals called pigments. Different animals see colours differently, so flowers have evolved colours that suit their favourite visitors. Grass and many trees, which use the wind to carry pollen, do not bother with bright flowers at all.

Surprising but true

Bees cannot see red well, but they can see ultraviolet light, so a flower that looks all yellow to you may look like a target with a dark centre to a bee.

Pigments in the petals

Reds, purples and many blues come from pigments called anthocyanins. The same family of chemicals colours red cabbage, jamun and blueberries. Yellows and oranges usually come from carotenoids, also found in carrots and marigolds. White petals often have almost no pigment. They look white because air pockets inside scatter light, the way foam on a wave looks white.

Some flowers change colour with conditions. Hydrangeas grown in acidic soil tend to be blue. In less acidic soil, the same plant often turns pink. Gardeners sometimes add things to the soil to shift the shade.

What a bee sees

Bees see colour, but not the way your eyes do. Human eyes have three kinds of colour sensors, tuned to red, green and blue light. A honeybee also has three, but they are tuned to green, blue and ultraviolet. So bees are poor at seeing red, which to them looks dark, but they see ultraviolet light, which humans cannot. Under a special camera, many flowers show ultraviolet patterns: dark centres, stripes or rings that point towards the nectar. Scientists call them nectar guides.

A sunflower or a marigold looks evenly coloured to us. To a bee, it can look like a bullseye. The pattern says land here, and saves the bee time, which is good for the bee and good for the plant.

Red for birds

Many red flowers are pollinated by birds. In the Americas, hummingbirds hover at red flowers with long tubes, like trumpet vines. In India, sunbirds visit red and orange flowers such as the flame of the forest, or palash, whose blossoms turn whole hillsides orange in spring.

Birds see red well and have little sense of smell. So bird flowers tend to be bright, tube-shaped and scentless. Bee flowers are more often blue, purple or yellow and sweet-smelling. Moths, which fly at night, prefer white or pale flowers with strong scents, like jasmine and raat ki rani, the night-blooming jessamine.

Smells and tricks

Not every flower plays fair. Some orchids look a bit like female bees or wasps, and males try to visit them. Rafflesia, a giant flower from Southeast Asian forests, is reddish brown and smells of rotting meat. It attracts flies that normally lay eggs on dead animals.

Colour can also change after pollination. Some lantana flowers start yellow and shift to orange or red once they have been visited. Insects learn that the newer colour means no more nectar, and skip those blooms.

A blue hillside every 12 years

In the Western Ghats, a shrub called neelakurinji covers the hills near Munnar in Kerala with blue-purple flowers only once every 12 years. The last mass flowering in Munnar was in 2018, and the next is expected around 2030. Tourists travel from all over India to see it. For the bees, it is a once-in-a-dozen-years feast. After flowering, the shrubs set seed and die, and the 12-year countdown starts again.

Flowering plants first appeared during the age of dinosaurs, more than 100 million years ago. Their bright colours spread alongside the insects that visited them, one of the longest partnerships in the living world.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Flowers are colourful to make the world look pretty for people.

Truth: Flower colours evolved long before humans existed. They are signals to animals, mostly insects and birds, that carry pollen from flower to flower. People simply enjoy the result.

Try this

Spend fifteen minutes watching one flowering plant in a garden, park or balcony pot. Note the flower colour and every visitor you see, from bees and butterflies to ants or birds. Then watch a flower of a different colour. Do the visitors change?
