How fast could dinosaurs run?

Footprints left in mud 120 million years ago can still be timed, a bit like a race on a stopwatch.

The short answer

Most dinosaurs were probably no faster than today's big animals, and the giants were fairly slow. Small two-legged dinosaurs were the speed champions. Some footprint trails show them moving at around 40 km/h, as fast as a top human sprinter.

Scientists work out speeds in two ways. Fossil footprints show how a real animal moved on a real day. Computer models of skeletons and muscles test how fast a body could go. The two often disagree for big, famous dinosaurs like T. rex, which is why the question is still argued about.

Surprising but true

Most dinosaur footprints show animals strolling at walking pace, slower than you walk to school.

Timing a footprint

In 1976, a British zoologist called Robert McNeill Alexander found a simple rule. He studied living animals, from people to elephants. When any animal speeds up, its stride gets longer compared to its leg length. So if you know the length of a dinosaur's leg and the gap between its footprints, you can estimate its speed.

Leg length can be worked out from the size of the footprint itself. Using this rule, scientists have measured thousands of trackways around the world.

The rule has limits. Very soft mud can stretch or smear prints, and an animal that slipped or turned changed its stride. So scientists look for long, straight trails with clear prints. Even then, they give a range of speeds rather than one exact number, because a leg length worked out from a footprint is itself an estimate.

Mostly strolling

The surprise is how slowly most dinosaurs moved. The great majority of trackways show walking speeds of about 3–8 km/h, the pace of a person out for a walk. That makes sense. Animals spend most of their time walking, and only sprint for a few seconds at a time.

At Dinosaur Valley State Park in Glen Rose, Texas, you can see tracks left by long-necked giants and large meat-eaters along the Paluxy River. They walked across soft mud by an ancient shoreline, slowly and steadily.

The sprinters

Some trackways do show running. In 2021, scientists in La Rioja, Spain, described tracks left by two-legged meat-eaters with very long strides. One worked out at about 45 km/h, nearly as fast as the Olympic sprinter Usain Bolt at full speed.

Ornithomimids, often called "ostrich mimic" dinosaurs, had long slim legs, light bodies and big thigh muscles. Many scientists think they were among the fastest dinosaurs. The ostrich itself, a living dinosaur, can reach about 70 km/h.

The trouble with T. rex

For T. rex, there are no running tracks to measure, so scientists build computer models. In 2002, John Hutchinson and Mariano Garcia worked out how much leg muscle T. rex would need to run fast. A sprinting T. rex would have needed far more than its legs could hold.

Later models gave a range of answers. Some suggest a top speed of about 25–40 km/h. One study in 2017 argued its bones would have broken at any pace faster than a brisk walk of about 20 km/h. Big animals face a real limit. Weight grows faster than the strength of bones and muscles, which is why elephants never gallop.

Young T. rex were slimmer, with longer legs for their size. They may have been the quick ones, chasing fast prey that their heavy parents could not catch. Growing up, a T. rex may have swapped speed for size and strength.

Myth or fact?

Myth: T. rex could chase down a speeding car.

Truth: Studies of its leg muscles and bones suggest an adult T. rex could not sprint. Most estimates are below 40 km/h, and some are as low as a brisk walk or slow jog.

Try this

In a playground, walk ten steps and measure the distance between your footprints or chalk marks. Then jog ten steps and measure again. Your steps get longer as you go faster. That's the same clue scientists use with dinosaur tracks.
