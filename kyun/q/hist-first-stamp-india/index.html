What was India's first postage stamp?

India's first stamp, from 1852, was a little red circle that could crack like a biscuit, which is why so few survive today.

The short answer

India's first postage stamp was the Scinde Dawk, issued on 1 July 1852 in the province of Sindh, now in Pakistan. It was a small round stamp, worth half an anna, with the emblem of the East India Company pressed into it. "Dawk" was the British spelling of the Hindi word dak, meaning post.

It was the first postage stamp in Asia. Only about 12 years earlier, in 1840, Britain had issued the world's first, the Penny Black. Two years after the Scinde Dawk, in 1854, stamps were issued for the whole of British India.

Surprising but true

The Scinde Dawk was the first postage stamp in all of Asia, issued just 12 years after the world's first stamp in Britain.

Why stamps were needed

Before stamps, letters in India were usually paid for by the person receiving them. The price depended on distance and weight, and post office clerks had to work it out by hand. It was slow, confusing and easy to cheat.

Sir Bartle Frere, the British official in charge of Sindh, wanted something simpler. He had seen how Britain's new stamps worked. If senders paid in advance with a small sticker, the post could move faster and more reliably. So in 1852 he brought out the Scinde Dawk.

Frere also reformed the old system of runners and camel riders that carried mail across the deserts of Sindh. He set up a regular postal service, with fixed routes and timetables, and the new stamp was part of making it work.

A stamp that cracked

The first Scinde Dawks were pressed into red sealing wax on paper. Wax was a terrible idea in Sindh's heat. It cracked, crumbled and flaked off letters. Very few of these red wax stamps survive, and those that do are among the rarest stamps in the world.

Later versions were pressed into white and blue paper instead. They didn't carry any picture of a king or queen, only the Company's mark, a heart shape divided into sections with the letters "EIC". In 1854 the Scinde Dawk was withdrawn when stamps for all of India took over.

How did kings send messages across India?In the 1330s, a sultan in Delhi got news from Sindh in about five days, a trip that took travellers fifty, thanks to runners with bells.

Stamps for all of India

On 1 October 1854, the post office of British India issued its first stamps for the whole country. They showed Queen Victoria and came in values of half an anna, one anna, two annas and four annas. A letter could now travel anywhere in British India for one flat price, depending only on weight.

The four anna stamp was printed in two colours, red and blue, in two separate runs. On a few sheets, the queen's head was printed upside down. These "inverted head" stamps are now worth a fortune to collectors, because so few were made.

Stamps of a free India

After independence, India needed new stamps. On 21 November 1947, the first stamp of independent India came out. It showed the new national flag, with the words "Jai Hind". It cost three and a half annas.

Later Indian stamps have shown Mahatma Gandhi, tigers, satellites, cricketers, temples, festivals and famous scientists. Some celebrated India's space missions, others its national symbols. India Post has issued thousands of different designs since 1947. In 1948, India issued four stamps in memory of Gandhi, a year after independence. Collectors prize them today, especially a rare version printed only for use by the Governor-General's office.

A hobby called philately

Collecting stamps is called philately. Stamp collectors study paper, ink, perforations (the little holes between stamps) and postmarks. A single tiny error can make a stamp very valuable.

Even in the age of email, stamps are still printed and collected. Special postmarks, like the boat design used at the floating post office on Dal Lake, turn an ordinary envelope into something people want to keep.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Old stamps are always worth a lot of money.

Truth: Most old stamps were printed in huge numbers and are worth very little. Value comes from rarity and condition, which is why the Scinde Dawk and a few misprinted stamps are so precious while millions of others cost almost nothing.
