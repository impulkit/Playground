How were elephants used in war?

Around 300 BC, an Indian emperor traded 500 elephants for a huge slice of land, and those elephants went on to change a war in Turkey.

The short answer

Elephants were the tanks of the ancient world. Armies in India were probably the first to train them for war, more than 2,500 years ago. A war elephant carried a driver, called a mahout, and often soldiers with bows and spears in a box on its back. In battle, elephants charged lines of soldiers, smashed through gates and terrified horses that weren't used to their smell.

From India the idea spread to Persia, Greece, Egypt and North Africa. But elephants were also hard to control, and in the end gunpowder ended their careers in battle.

Surprising but true

Some ancient writers say Roman-era armies scared off war elephants with squealing pigs.

Kings counted their elephants

Ancient Indian kings measured their strength partly in elephants. The Arthashastra, a guide to running a kingdom linked to Chanakya, describes a whole government office for elephants. It covers how to catch them, feed them, train them and look after their health. It even protects elephant forests.

Alexander the Great met war elephants in India in 326 BC, at the river Hydaspes, now the Jhelum. King Porus had perhaps 85 to 200 of them, depending on which ancient writer you trust. Alexander won, but his horses were spooked and his soldiers shaken. Soon after, his army refused to go further into India.

The great elephant trade

Around 303 BC, Chandragupta Maurya, founder of the Mauryan Empire, made a deal with the Greek king Seleucus. Seleucus gave up land in what is now Afghanistan and Pakistan. In return he got about 500 elephants.

Two years later, at the Battle of Ipsus in Turkey, those elephants helped Seleucus win. Indian elephants helped decide a war far from India.

Elephants also fought elephants. At the Battle of Raphia in 217 BC, between two Greek kings, African forest elephants faced Indian elephants. According to the historian Polybius, the African ones, which were smaller, panicked and fled. The forest elephants of North Africa used in ancient wars are now extinct.

Who are mahouts?Some mahouts spend 40 years with the same elephant, an animal that can weigh as much as four cars and still answers to one quiet voice.

Hannibal crosses the Alps

The most famous war elephants marched with Hannibal, a general from Carthage in North Africa. In 218 BC, he led an army, with about 37 elephants, from Spain across the snowy Alps to attack Rome. The mountain passes were narrow and icy. Almost all the elephants died within a year or so, but the sight of them became legend.

The Romans had first met elephants years earlier, in 280 BC, against King Pyrrhus. Soon they learned tricks. Soldiers stepped aside to let elephants rush through gaps, then attacked from behind. Some writers say armies used squealing pigs to frighten elephants, since the animals hated the noise.

Elephants in Indian battles

In India, elephants stayed in armies for nearly 2,000 more years. Rajput, Mughal and Deccan rulers kept thousands. Emperors rode them into battle so their soldiers could see them, though this made the king an easy target too. Elephants also served as moving command posts. A commander high on an elephant's back could see across the whole battlefield, and his soldiers could see him. If the elephant turned and fled, though, the army often thought the battle was lost.

At the First Battle of Panipat in 1526, Babur's guns and cannons frightened Ibrahim Lodi's elephants, and some turned and ran through Lodi's own army. As gunpowder spread, elephants became too easy to hit.

Workers, not warriors

Elephants still worked for armies long after they stopped charging into battle. They hauled heavy cannons in British India and carried supplies through thick jungle in Burma during World War II, where trucks couldn't go.

Elephants are intelligent and remember people for years. Today, India's elephants are protected, and the skill of working with them lives on with mahouts who care for temple and forest elephants. These animals talk to each other with rumbles so deep humans can't hear them, which is its own story in how elephants talk.

Myth or fact?

Myth: War elephants always won battles because nothing could stop them.

Truth: Elephants terrified enemies who had never seen them, but they could panic when hurt or frightened. A stampeding elephant sometimes trampled its own side, so generals learned to fight back with noise, fire, spears and later guns.
