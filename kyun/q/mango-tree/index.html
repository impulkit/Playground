Why is the mango called the king of fruits?

One mango tree near Lucknow bears about 300 different kinds of mango, all grafted onto the same trunk.

The short answer

People have called the mango the king of fruits for centuries, especially in India, and it is easy to see why. A ripe mango is sweet, juicy and fragrant, and there are hundreds of varieties, each with its own taste. Mango trees grow huge, live for a century or more, and give shade as well as fruit.

The mango is also deeply woven into Indian life. It has been grown in India for over 4,000 years. It appears in poems, paintings and festivals, and mango leaves are strung over doorways on happy occasions.

Today India grows more mangoes than any other country, roughly two in every five mangoes on Earth, and the mango is India's national fruit.

Surprising but true

Mangoes are cousins of cashews and pistachios, and also of poison ivy. That is why mango sap can give some people an itchy rash.

A tree from India's northeast

Wild mangoes grow in the forests around northeast India, Bangladesh and Myanmar. The wild fruit is small and stringy. Over thousands of years, people chose and planted the trees with the best fruit, slowly turning it into the mango we know.

A mango tree can grow over 30 m tall, about as high as a ten-storey building, with a wide, dark-green crown. Some trees in India are well over a hundred years old and still bear fruit. The Buddha is said to have rested in mango groves, and one grove was given to him and his followers as a gift.

A thousand varieties

India has over a thousand named mango varieties. Alphonso, from Ratnagiri and Devgad in Maharashtra, is famous for its saffron flesh. Dasheri comes from Malihabad near Lucknow. Langra, Chausa, Kesar from Gujarat, Banganapalli from Andhra Pradesh and Himsagar from Bengal all have loyal fans.

Most varieties are kept going by grafting: joining a cutting from a favourite tree onto young roots. Portuguese settlers in Goa helped spread grafting in India from the 1500s, and the Alphonso is widely said to be named after the Portuguese general Afonso de Albuquerque.

In Malihabad, a farmer named Haji Kalimullah Khan has grafted around 300 varieties onto a single old tree. He was awarded the Padma Shri in 2008.

Why is Akbar called 'the Great'?The emperor who never learned to read built a huge library, and invited Hindus, Muslims, Jains, Zoroastrians and Christians to argue in front of him.

Emperors and poets

The Mughal emperors loved mangoes. Emperor Akbar is said to have planted an orchard of a lakh mango trees near Darbhanga in Bihar, known as the Lakhi Bagh. Mughal gardens across north India had mango groves for shade and fruit.

Poets praised the mango too. In Sanskrit poetry, including the works of Kalidasa, mango blossoms announce the arrival of spring and are linked with Kama, the god of love. The curved paisley shape found on many Indian fabrics is often called ambi, meaning "little mango".

Mangoes go to America

Mangoes reached the Americas with Portuguese and Spanish traders, and today they grow in Mexico, Brazil and Florida. For years, the USA banned Indian mangoes because of worries about pests. In 2007, after a trade agreement, the first Alphonsos were shipped to America, treated to make sure no insects came along.

Mangoes also play a part in friendship between countries. Leaders in South Asia sometimes send boxes of their best mangoes to one another as gifts, a habit people jokingly call mango diplomacy. It is one small part of world trade.

Handle the skin with care

The mango belongs to the same plant family as cashews, pistachios and poison ivy. Its sap and skin contain a substance that can cause an itchy rash in some people, especially around the mouth. If that happens to you, it's best to let an adult peel and slice the fruit.

In the hot weeks of summer, mangoes are everywhere in India: in aam panna, aamras with puris, pickles and lassi. The season is short, but people wait all year for it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A mango's colour tells you whether it is ripe.

Truth: Many varieties stay green or only partly yellow when ripe. A gentle squeeze and a sweet smell near the stalk are better clues than colour.

Try this

Collect a few different mangoes in season, or look carefully at pictures if you can't. Compare their shape, skin colour, smell near the stalk, and how soft they feel when gently pressed. With an adult, cut them open and taste. Make a table and give each one a score.
