How do translators work?

In German, someone who doesn't notice the obvious "has tomatoes on their eyes". Translating that word for word would leave English readers baffled.

The short answer

A translator turns writing in one language into another. An interpreter does the same for speech or sign language, often live. Both need to understand two languages and two cultures very well.

The job isn't swapping words. Translators read a whole sentence or paragraph, work out what it means and how it feels, and then write it again as a native speaker would. A joke must stay funny, a sad poem sad and a legal contract exact. That takes judgement, research and many small decisions.

Surprising but true

Live interpreting through headphones, now used at the United Nations, was first done on a big scale at the Nuremberg trials after the Second World War, in 1945 and 1946.

Words that don't line up

Languages don't match word for word. Hindi has separate words for your father's brother (chacha) and your mother's brother (mama), while English has only "uncle". Some languages, like Turkish, can build what English says in a whole sentence into one long word.

Idioms are the biggest trap. If a Hindi speaker says "naach na jaane, aangan tedha", the words mean "she can't dance and says the courtyard is crooked". The meaning is "blaming your tools for your own mistakes". A translator into English might use "a bad workman blames his tools".

Tone matters too. Hindi has three ways to say "you" (tu, tum and aap), from very close to very polite. English has only one. A translator must find other ways to show how polite or rude a character is being.

Stones and stories

In 1799, French soldiers in Egypt found a stone slab carved in 196 BCE. It had the same decree written three ways: in hieroglyphs, in another Egyptian script called Demotic, and in ancient Greek. Scholars could read the Greek. Using it as a key, Jean-François Champollion announced in 1822 that he had cracked hieroglyphs. The Rosetta Stone is now in the British Museum in London.

Translation also carried stories around the world. India's Panchatantra was translated into Middle Persian in the 500s CE, then into Arabic as Kalila wa Dimna, then into Hebrew, Latin and many European languages. Along the way the animals' names and some stories changed. Many European fables can be traced back to it.

Interpreting in real time

Interpreters work with speech, so they can't stop to look things up. In "consecutive" interpreting, the speaker pauses every few sentences. In "simultaneous" interpreting, the interpreter speaks almost at the same time as the speaker, usually from a soundproof booth, a few words behind.

Simultaneous interpreting was first used on a large scale at the Nuremberg trials in 1945 and 1946, when Nazi leaders were tried after the war. Courtroom speeches were interpreted live into English, French, Russian and German through headphones. Today the United Nations works in six official languages: Arabic, Chinese, English, French, Russian and Spanish.

Sign language interpreters do the same job between spoken and signed languages, which have their own grammar.

Machines that translate

In 1954, an IBM computer in New York translated about 60 Russian sentences into English. People predicted machines would replace translators within a few years. It took much longer.

Modern translation apps use artificial intelligence trained on huge numbers of texts that already exist in two languages. They are now very useful for menus, signs and quick messages. But they can still miss jokes, tone, culture and context. For books, films, medicine and law, human translators usually check and correct machine translations, or do the work themselves.

In a country like India, with 22 languages listed in the Constitution, translators help people read each other's literature and understand each other's laws. Every film that is dubbed or subtitled, every Hindi film shown in Tamil, has a translator behind it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A good translation swaps each word for the matching word in the other language.

Truth: Languages put words together differently and use different idioms. Good translators carry over meaning, tone and feeling, which often means changing the words quite a lot.
