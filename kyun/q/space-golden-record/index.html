What is the Voyager Golden Record?

Two spacecraft leaving our Solar System each carry a gold-covered record of Earth's sounds and music, including a raga sung by an Indian singer.

The short answer

The Voyager Golden Record is a message in a bottle for the stars. In 1977, NASA launched two spacecraft, Voyager 1 and Voyager 2, to fly past Jupiter, Saturn and beyond. Bolted to the side of each is a 30 cm record made of copper and covered in gold.

The record holds greetings in 55 languages, music from around the world, sounds of Earth and 115 pictures. It also carries instructions, cut into its cover, showing how to play it. Both Voyagers are now outside the bubble of the Sun's wind, travelling between the stars. If anyone ever finds one, the record is our hello.

Surprising but true

Voyager 1 reaches one light-day from Earth in November 2026, so a radio message sent to it takes a whole day to arrive.

Who chose what to send

NASA gave the job to a small committee led by the astronomer Carl Sagan. They had about six weeks to decide what should represent all of humanity. They argued over every choice.

They picked pictures of DNA, a mother feeding a baby, a supermarket, a page from a book and the Taj Mahal. They chose sounds like rain, wind, footsteps, a kiss, a laughing child, a train, a dog barking and a heartbeat. The spoken greetings range from ancient Sumerian to modern languages. Hindi, Bengali, Gujarati, Marathi, Telugu, Kannada, Punjabi and Urdu are all there.

A raga in deep space

The 27 music pieces include Bach and Beethoven, a Peruvian wedding song, Navajo night chants, a song from Azerbaijan and Chuck Berry's rock and roll hit "Johnny B. Goode". One track comes from India: "Jaat Kahan Ho", sung by the Hindustani classical singer Kesarbai Kerkar in a raga called Bhairavi.

So a three-and-a-half-minute recording of an Indian singer is now among the farthest human-made sounds from Earth. Kesarbai, as it happens, was famous for rarely letting her concerts be recorded at all.

How to play a record with no record player

The aliens would need to build their own player. The record came with a needle and cartridge, and its cover shows, in drawings and binary code, how to place the needle and how fast to spin it. It even shows how to turn the signals into pictures.

To say where we live, the cover has a map of 14 pulsars, stars that flash like lighthouses at precise speeds. From the pattern, someone clever could work out the position of our Sun. There is also a tiny dab of uranium on the cover. It decays at a known rate, so it acts like a clock showing how long ago the record left.

A message that will outlast us

The record is made to last. Out between the stars there is no air, no rust and very little dust, so it could survive for hundreds of millions of years, maybe a billion. The Earth's continents will have shifted shape long before it wears out.

In November 2026, Voyager 1 is set to reach one light-day from Earth, about 25.9 billion km. Signals from NASA, travelling at the speed of light, will take about a full day to reach it. Even so, it will not pass close to another star for about 40,000 years, and space is so empty that nobody may ever find the record. Some scientists say that is fine. Making it forced humans to ask what we would want strangers to know about us.

Voyager 1 is still sending faint signals home with a radio of about 22 watts, roughly as much power as a fridge light bulb. How a nearly 50-year-old machine manages that, and what it has discovered, is the story of the Voyager probes. How far 40,000 years of travel really is becomes clearer once you understand light-years. And whether anyone is out there to listen is one of the biggest questions in science, explored in alien life.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Golden Record was sent to a specific alien planet.

Truth: The Voyagers are not aimed at any planet. They will drift between the stars for millions of years, and the record will only be found if someone happens to come across it.
