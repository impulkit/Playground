How did the harmonium become India's favourite instrument?

It arrived from Europe in the 1800s, was banned from All India Radio for about 30 years, and still turned up at almost every bhajan, qawwali and music class.

The short answer

The harmonium is a small box with a keyboard on top and bellows at the back. You pump the bellows with one hand and play the keys with the other. It came to India from Europe in the 1800s, during British rule, and Indian musicians quickly made it their own.

It caught on for practical reasons. It is easy to carry, it stays in tune for years, and a beginner can play a melody within days. Singers love it because it gives them a steady note to sing against.

Surprising but true

All India Radio kept the harmonium off its programmes from 1940 until 1971, because critics said it could not play the delicate slides between notes that Indian music needs.

A reed, not a string

Inside a harmonium are rows of thin brass tongues called reeds, each fixed over a slot. When you press a key, a hole opens. Air from the bellows rushes through and makes that reed wobble, producing one note. Short reeds give high notes and long reeds give low ones.

This kind of reed is called a free reed, and it is very old. China's sheng, a mouth organ of bamboo pipes, has used free reeds for thousands of years. European inventors borrowed the idea in the 1700s and 1800s. In 1840, Alexandre Debain in Paris patented an instrument he called the harmonium.

Many harmoniums have knobs called stops. Pulling one lets air reach an extra set of reeds, which makes the sound fuller. Some Indian models also have a scale changer: the whole keyboard slides sideways, so a singer can move every note higher or lower to suit their voice without learning new fingering.

Made in Kolkata

European harmoniums were big, with foot pedals for the bellows. They suited a church or a parlour, but Indian musicians usually sit on the floor. In 1875, Dwarkanath Ghose of the firm Dwarkin and Son in Kolkata is credited with building a smaller version pumped by hand.

That change made all the difference. A singer could now sit cross-legged, play with the right hand and pump with the left. Harmoniums spread from Kolkata to temples, theatres and homes across the country. Before long they were a fixture of qawwali singing at Sufi shrines.

The great harmonium ban

Not everyone was pleased. Rabindranath Tagore disliked it, and so did John Foulds, a British composer who worked at All India Radio in the 1930s. Their main complaint was about tuning. Indian music often glides smoothly from one note to another, a slide called meend, and it uses tiny shades of pitch. A harmonium can only jump from key to key, with each note fixed.

In 1940, All India Radio stopped using the harmonium on air. The ban lasted until 1971. During those years, musicians kept using it everywhere else, on stage, in films and in homes.

Everywhere today

Today you can hear a harmonium in a village bhajan, a Bollywood song or a classical concert. Sikh ragis play it while singing kirtan at the Golden Temple in Amritsar. Masters like Pandit Tulsidas Borkar and Pandit Appasaheb Jalgaonkar showed that, in skilled hands, it can be a solo instrument too.

The old argument has not completely gone away. Some musicians still prefer the sarangi, a bowed string instrument that can slide between notes. But the harmonium is cheap, sturdy and forgiving. It also travelled with Indian families abroad, so you can hear it in gurdwaras and temples in Canada, Britain and the United States. For millions of children, its keys are where learning music begins.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The harmonium is an ancient Indian instrument.

Truth: It was invented in Europe in the 19th century and reached India during British rule. Indian makers then changed it so much, with hand bellows and a small floor-sitting body, that it now feels completely Indian.

Try this

Hold a blade of grass or a thin strip of paper tightly between your two thumbs and blow hard across the gap. The buzzing you hear is a reed vibrating in moving air, the same idea that makes a harmonium sing.
