Why did people first keep goats and sheep?

The first sheep that people kept weren't woolly at all. They had short, hairy coats, and fluffy wool came thousands of years later.

The short answer

Goats and sheep were among the first animals ever kept on farms, about 10,000–11,000 years ago in West Asia. People kept them because they were so practical. They could live on rough grass and shrubs that people couldn't eat, and turn them into milk, meat, skins and later wool. They were small enough to handle, lived in herds that followed a leader, and had young quickly.

For early farmers, a flock was like a walking store cupboard. Instead of hunting wild animals that might run away, families could keep food alive and close by, all year round.

Surprising but true

A goat's pupils are sideways rectangles, which give it an extremely wide view of the world around it, so it can watch for danger while its head is down eating.

From wild herds to flocks

Wild goats called bezoar ibex lived in the Zagros Mountains, along today's border of Iran and Iraq. At a site called Ganj Dareh, archaeologists found goat bones about 10,000 years old. The bones showed that people were killing mostly young males and keeping females to breed, a sign of herding, not hunting.

Sheep came from a wild sheep called the Asiatic mouflon, found in the hills of today's Turkey, Iraq and Iran. In South Asia, at Mehrgarh in Balochistan, people were keeping goats and sheep by about 7000 BCE, alongside early wheat and barley farming.

Hair first, wool later

Early sheep had a coat of coarse hair, with only a little soft wool underneath, like their wild cousins. Over thousands of years, herders chose to breed the sheep with the softest, longest fleeces. Woolly sheep spread across West Asia several thousand years after sheep were first tamed.

Wool changed history. It could be spun into thread, woven into warm cloth and traded. In medieval England, wool was so important to the country's wealth that the Lord Speaker in Parliament still sits on a large cushion stuffed with wool, called the Woolsack. Unlike wild animals, modern woolly sheep don't shed their coat by themselves, so they must be sheared every year. The fleece then becomes wool for blankets, shawls and sweaters.

How did farming begin?The ancestor of corn was a skinny wild grass with a few rock-hard seeds. It took thousands of years of farmers' choices to turn it into a cob.

Goats: tough, clever climbers

Goats are browsers. Instead of just grazing on grass like sheep, they nibble leaves, twigs and bushes, often standing on their back legs to reach. In Morocco, goats even climb argan trees to eat the fruit. They can survive in dry, rocky places where cows would starve.

Their eyes are built for watching out. The rectangular pupils give a very wide view, so a goat can see predators coming while its head is down. Goats are also curious and quick to learn, and studies have shown they can remember how to solve a food puzzle for months.

Goats and sheep in India

India has huge flocks. The 2019 livestock census counted about 14.9 crore goats and 7.4 crore sheep. For many families with little land, goats are a kind of savings account: they breed quickly and can be sold when money is needed. Mahatma Gandhi famously drank goat's milk for much of his life.

In the high, cold desert of Ladakh, Changthangi goats survive winters of minus 30 °C or colder. Their fine, soft undercoat is combed out each spring to make pashmina, one of the most prized wools in the world. Sheep herders like the Gaddis of Himachal Pradesh and the Rabaris of Gujarat and Rajasthan still walk their flocks between summer and winter pastures, as their ancestors did for centuries.

Famous sheep

In 1996, scientists in Scotland created Dolly, the first mammal cloned from an adult cell. She was a sheep, and she became one of the most famous animals in science. She was named after the singer Dolly Parton, and she had six lambs of her own. Today, sheep and goats live almost everywhere people do, from Welsh hillsides and the ranches of the American West to village lanes in Rajasthan.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Goats will eat anything, even tin cans.

Truth: Goats are actually fussy eaters. They nibble and test things with their lips, which is why they seem to chew paper labels or clothes. They prefer leaves, twigs and shrubs, and they don't eat metal.

Try this

Look at photos of a goat, a sheep, a cow and a horse, and compare their eyes. Notice the sideways pupils. Then hold a cardboard tube up to one eye and look around. A narrow view like that makes it harder to see someone sneaking up, which is why grazing animals evolved such wide views.
