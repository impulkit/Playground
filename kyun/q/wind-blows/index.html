Why does the wind blow?

Every breeze you feel began with the Sun heating some places more than others.

The short answer

Wind is air on the move. It blows because the Sun heats Earth unevenly. Where the ground or sea gets warm, the air above it warms, spreads out and rises, leaving lower air pressure near the surface. Where it is cooler, air sinks and the pressure is higher.

Air always flows from high pressure towards low pressure, like water running downhill. That flow is the wind. The bigger the difference in pressure, the stronger it blows.

Surprising but true

The strongest gust of wind ever measured (outside a tornado) was 408 km/h, recorded on Barrow Island, Australia, during Cyclone Olivia in 1996.

Pushed by pressure

Air has weight. The whole column of air above you presses down on everything, though you don't notice it. That push is called air pressure.

When air is heated, its molecules move faster and spread out. Warm air is lighter than the same amount of cool air, so it rises, a bit like a hot-air balloon. Cooler, heavier air flows in underneath to replace it. A weather map with high and low pressure areas is really a map of where the wind will go.

Sea breezes and the monsoon

Land heats up faster than water. On a sunny day at a beach in Goa or California, the sand gets hot by afternoon while the sea stays cooler. Air over the land rises, and cool air flows in from the sea. That is a sea breeze. At night the land cools faster, and a gentle breeze often blows the other way.

The monsoon works on a much bigger scale. In summer, the land of South Asia heats up much more than the Indian Ocean. Moist ocean air flows towards the land, bringing months of rain. In winter, the pattern reverses.

Why winds curve

If Earth stood still, wind would blow straight from high to low pressure. But Earth spins. As air moves over the spinning planet, its path seems to curve: to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern. This is called the Coriolis effect.

It is why big storms spin. Cyclones over the Bay of Bengal and hurricanes over the Atlantic turn anticlockwise, while those south of the equator turn clockwise. It also shapes the steady trade winds that sailing ships once relied on to cross oceans.

Winds high and fast

High above the ground, about 9 to 12 km up, narrow bands of very fast wind called jet streams race around the planet from west to east. Pilots use them. A flight from the United States to Europe often takes less time than the return trip, because the plane rides the jet stream on the way.

Near the ground, the fastest winds come from tornadoes, but they are hard to measure. The strongest gust recorded by an instrument, outside a tornado, was 408 km/h during Cyclone Olivia in Australia in 1996. For decades before that, the record belonged to Mount Washington in New Hampshire, USA, with 372 km/h in 1934.

Measuring the wind

In 1805, the British naval officer Francis Beaufort devised a scale for sailors to describe wind by what it did to the sea and to a ship's sails. Later it was adapted for land: at force 3, leaves and twigs move; at force 6, umbrellas are hard to use; at force 10, trees are uprooted.

Today weather stations measure wind with spinning cups called anemometers. India's coasts and hills are also dotted with tall wind turbines that turn moving air into electricity. India is one of the world's biggest producers of wind power, and Tamil Nadu and Gujarat have some of its windiest sites.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Trees make the wind by waving their branches.

Truth: Many young children think this, and the scientist Jean Piaget recorded it in the 1920s. It is the other way round. Wind is moving air, pushed by differences in air pressure, and it makes the trees sway.

Try this

Watch air move from warm to cool. On a cold day, open a door between a warm room and a cold one just a crack. Hold a strip of tissue paper near the top of the gap and then near the bottom. You'll often see the tissue blow one way at the top and the other way at the bottom, as warm air flows out above and cold air slides in below.
