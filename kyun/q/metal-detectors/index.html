How does a metal detector find coins?

A metal detector never touches the coin. It makes the coin send back a tiny magnetic echo, and listens for it.

The short answer

A metal detector has a flat loop of wire, called a coil, in the round plate at the bottom. Electricity pulses through the coil and makes a magnetic field that reaches down into the ground.

When that field passes through a coin, it makes tiny swirls of electric current flow inside the metal. Those swirls make a weak magnetic field of their own. A second coil, or the same one between pulses, picks up this faint echo. The electronics turn it into a beep.

So the detector never sees the coin. It feels the coin answering back.

Surprising but true

In 1881, Alexander Graham Bell, the telephone pioneer, built an early metal detector to find a bullet inside a wounded US president.

A trick discovered in 1831

The idea goes back to Michael Faraday, who found that a changing magnetic field makes electricity flow in nearby metal. Scientists call this electromagnetic induction. It is the same trick that makes power stations work, and the same one used by induction cooktops to heat a steel pan.

A metal detector uses it twice. Its coil makes a changing field, the field makes currents in the coin, and those currents make a changing field that the detector can catch. Plastic, glass, stone and dry soil don't carry electricity, so they stay silent.

Good detectors can even guess what kind of metal they've found. Iron, gold and copper answer back in slightly different ways, and the machine shows a number or plays a different tone.

The president and the bullet

In July 1881, US President James Garfield was shot. Doctors could not find the bullet. Alexander Graham Bell, already famous for the telephone, rushed to build a machine that would hum in an earpiece when it passed over metal.

He tested it at the president's bedside, but the results were confusing. One likely reason was the metal springs in the mattress. Garfield died that September. Bell's idea, though, was sound, and it pointed the way to the detectors we use now.

Treasure under a farmer's field

In 2009, a man with a hobby metal detector was walking across a farm field in Staffordshire, England. His machine kept beeping. Over the next weeks, experts dug up thousands of pieces of gold and silver, mostly fittings from swords and helmets, about 1,300 years old.

It became known as the Staffordshire Hoard, the largest hoard of Anglo-Saxon gold ever found. Finds like this are why archaeologists and detector users now often work together, and why many countries have rules about reporting what you dig up. Gold barely rusts or tarnishes, so it can come out of the ground looking almost new.

Detectors you walk through

You have probably walked through a metal detector already. The door-shaped frames at airports, at Delhi Metro stations and at American sports stadiums work the same way, with coils hidden in the sides. Security staff use hand-held wands that are tiny versions of the same idea.

Detectors also clear old landmines from former battlefields, find buried pipes before builders dig, and check packets of food in factories for stray bits of metal. A chocolate bar or a biscuit packet may have passed through one before it reached the shop.

How deep can it see?

A typical hobby detector finds a coin about 20–30 cm down, roughly the length of a school ruler. Bigger objects can be found deeper, because they send back a stronger echo. Wet, salty or iron-rich soil makes the job harder, since the ground itself starts to answer back, a bit like trying to hear a whisper in a noisy classroom.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Metal detectors only find iron and other magnetic metals.

Truth: They find almost any metal that conducts electricity, including gold, silver, copper and aluminium. That's why a gold ring or a foil wrapper can set one off at an airport.

Try this

Hide a few coins, a steel spoon and some plastic bottle caps under a towel. Hold a fridge magnet on a string just above the towel and sweep it slowly. Notice which hidden things it finds and which it misses, then think about why a real detector would find all the metal ones.
