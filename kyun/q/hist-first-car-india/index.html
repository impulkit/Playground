When did the first car arrive in India?

The first motor cars rattled into Calcutta and Bombay in the late 1890s, so rare that people stopped in the street to stare at a carriage with no horse.

The short answer

The first motor car in India is usually said to have arrived in Calcutta in 1897, owned by a British resident. A year later, in 1898, four cars appeared on the streets of Bombay. One belonged to the industrialist Jamsetji Tata.

These early cars were imported from Europe, noisy and slow, with top speeds around 30 km/h, a bit faster than a good cyclist. Roads were made for bullock carts and horse carriages, so a car journey often involved punctures, breakdowns and a crowd of curious onlookers.

Surprising but true

One of the first four cars in Bombay, in 1898, belonged to Jamsetji Tata, whose company would build India's own cars about a century later.

Horseless carriages

The world's first practical petrol car, built by Karl Benz in Germany, appeared in 1886. Just over ten years later, rich Indians and British officials were importing cars by ship.

They looked like carriages with the horse removed. Drivers sat high up, steered with a lever or wheel, and wore goggles against the dust. There were no petrol pumps. Fuel came in tins, bought from chemists or hardware shops. A horse could take fright at the clatter, so some towns made rules about speed and noise.

Cars also needed numbers. Bombay and Madras began registering motor vehicles in the early 1900s, and each car got a plate with letters for its city. Madras cars carried "MC" numbers, and the first registered car in the south, MC-1, belonged to a railway official named Francis Spring. Driving licences followed, though early tests were simple. Most owners hired a trained driver, called a chauffeur, who also cleaned and repaired the car.

In Madras, a director of Parry and Company drove his car daily from Adyar to his office from 1901. In 1903, the engineer Samuel John Green built India's first steam car in the same city.

Maharajas and their machines

The keenest early buyers were India's princes. Maharajas ordered Rolls-Royces, Daimlers and Bentleys, sometimes dozens at a time, often with custom bodies covered in silver, ivory or brocade. Some were built as hunting cars, with seats for spotting tigers.

By the 1930s, Indian princes were among the biggest customers of Rolls-Royce anywhere outside Britain. Some of those cars still survive and appear at vintage car shows in Mumbai, Delhi and Gujarat, polished and still running.

One of the best known is a 1920s Rolls-Royce built for a maharaja's hunting trips, with a searchlight for spotting animals at night. Collectors in India now restore such cars piece by piece, sometimes hunting for years for one missing part.

Ambassadors and Padminis

After independence in 1947, India wanted to build its own cars. Hindustan Motors, near Kolkata, began making the Ambassador in 1958, based on a British design. Premier made the Padmini in Bombay, based on an Italian Fiat.

For decades, these two were almost the only choices. Families waited months or even years to buy one. The rounded white Ambassador became the official car of ministers, and the black-and-yellow Padmini became Mumbai's famous taxi, until it was retired in 2023.

The little car that changed everything

On 14 December 1983, Prime Minister Indira Gandhi handed the keys of the first Maruti 800 to Harpal Singh, a Delhi airline employee. The small, fuel-saving car, made with the Japanese company Suzuki, cost about Rs 50,000. For millions of middle-class families, it was their first car. The Maruti 800 stayed on sale for about 30 years, until 2014, and close to 29 lakh were sold. Many are still chugging along on Indian roads.

Today India is the world's third-largest market for new cars, behind China and the USA, selling about 43 lakh passenger vehicles in 2024–25. And the next big change has already started, as more Indian buyers switch to electric cars. Surprisingly, electric cars are older than petrol ones. Some of the first motor vehicles of the 1880s ran on batteries.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Ambassador was an Indian design.

Truth: It was based on the British Morris Oxford of the 1950s. Hindustan Motors built it in India with Indian parts for 56 years, and it became so common that many people thought of it as India's own.
