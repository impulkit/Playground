What was life like in a castle?

Medieval castles were cold, smoky and crowded, and the toilets often emptied straight down the outside wall.

The short answer

In medieval Europe, roughly 1000 to 1500 CE, castles were the fortified homes of kings and lords. They were part fortress and part house, and a lot of people lived inside.

Life in a castle was busy, noisy and often uncomfortable. The lord's family, knights, servants, cooks, grooms and soldiers all shared the space. Most people ate and some slept in a big room called the great hall, warmed by a fire whose smoke stung the eyes. Floors were covered with rushes, a kind of dried reed. Windows were small, to keep out arrows and cold. The toilets dropped waste down the walls. Castles also ran farms and courts, and they were where the local lord kept order.

Surprising but true

A knight in full plate armour could climb onto his horse without help. The idea that he needed a crane comes from later plays and films.

From mud to stone

The first castles were quick to build. Soldiers piled earth into a mound, called a motte, and put a wooden tower on top. A fenced yard, the bailey, sat below.

After William of Normandy conquered England in 1066, the Normans built hundreds of these. Wood burns, so lords soon switched to stone. The White Tower at the Tower of London, begun around 1078, has walls up to about 4.5 metres thick at the base. Later castles added rings of walls, towers and gatehouses with iron gates called portcullises.

A day in the great hall

The great hall was the centre of castle life. The lord and his family sat at a raised table at one end, while everyone else ate at long trestle tables.

Meals could be big: bread, stews, roast meat on feast days, and fish on the many days when the Church forbade meat. People often used thick slices of stale bread, called trenchers, as plates. Musicians and jugglers entertained guests. At night, servants might sleep on the hall floor near the fire.

Becoming a knight

Knights were trained fighters on horseback who served a lord in return for land, part of a system historians call feudalism. A boy from a noble family might start as a page at about seven, serving at table and learning manners.

At around fourteen he became a squire, caring for a knight's horse and armour and following him to battle. If he did well, he could be knighted around 21, in a ceremony called dubbing. Knights were expected to follow a code of honour called chivalry, though many did not.

Armour and attack

Early knights wore chain mail, a shirt of thousands of linked iron rings. By the 1400s, rich knights wore full plate armour weighing roughly 15 to 25 kg. Because the weight was spread out, they could move quite freely.

To attack castles, armies used battering rams, siege towers and the trebuchet, a giant throwing machine. It used a heavy counterweight on a long arm, working like a lever, to hurl stones at walls. Often, though, attackers simply surrounded a castle and waited for its food to run out.

Why castles ended

In the 1400s, cannons became powerful enough to smash high stone walls. Kings grew stronger, and lords no longer needed private fortresses.

Castles turned into palaces, prisons, ruins or tourist sites. India has its own great forts, such as Mehrangarh in Jodhpur, begun in 1459 on a rocky hill about 120 metres above the city, and the Maratha hill forts of Shivaji. Japan built tall white castles for its samurai lords. Around the world, people facing similar dangers came up with surprisingly similar answers.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Knights' armour was so heavy that they had to be lifted onto their horses with cranes.

Truth: Full plate armour weighed about as much as a soldier's pack today, spread over the whole body. Knights trained to run, climb and mount horses in it.
