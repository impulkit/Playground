Could life exist on Earth without the Moon?

About 400 million years ago, a year on Earth had around 400 days. Corals recorded it in their skeletons, and the Moon is the reason it changed.

The short answer

Most scientists think life could exist on Earth without the Moon. Living things probably appeared in the oceans, and the Sun alone would still give warmth, light and small tides. But the Moon has shaped our planet in deep ways. It makes strong tides, it has slowed Earth's spin, and it may help keep Earth's tilt, and so its seasons, steady.

Without it, life would almost certainly have developed differently. How differently is something scientists still argue about.

Surprising but true

The Moon is drifting away from Earth by about 3.8 cm a year, roughly as fast as your fingernails grow. Astronauts left mirrors on the Moon that let scientists measure this with lasers.

Tides and the edge of the sea

The Moon's gravity pulls on the oceans, raising two bulges of water on opposite sides of Earth. As Earth turns, coasts pass through them, giving most beaches two high and two low tides a day. The Sun does the same, but more weakly, so the Moon causes about two-thirds of the tidal effect. The shape of the coast matters too. In Canada's Bay of Fundy, the sea can rise and fall by more than 15 metres. In India, the Gulf of Khambhat in Gujarat has some of the country's biggest tides, around 10 metres at their highest.

Tides create a strip of shore that is sometimes underwater and sometimes dry. Creatures in this zone, such as crabs, barnacles and the life of tide pools, have to cope with both. Some scientists have suggested that this repeated wetting and drying helped early life, or helped sea creatures adapt to land. These ideas are interesting, but they are hard to prove.

What lives in tide pools?Twice a day the sea goes out and leaves behind tiny worlds in the rocks, full of creatures waiting for the water to return.

A steadier tilt

Earth is tilted on its axis by about 23.4°. That tilt is why we have seasons. The planets, especially Jupiter, tug on Earth and try to change its tilt. The Moon's pull on Earth's bulging middle helps resist this, so the tilt only wobbles between about 22° and 24.5° over tens of thousands of years.

In the 1990s, French scientists calculated that without the Moon, Earth's tilt could swing hugely and unpredictably, perhaps flipping between almost upright and lying on its side. That would make climates change wildly. But in 2012, American researchers ran new models. They found that a moonless Earth's tilt would still change, but mostly within a narrower range and over very long times. So the Moon helps, but it may not be essential.

Shorter days long ago

Tides also act like a brake. The moving bulges of water rub against the seabed and slowly slow Earth's spin. About 1.4 billion years ago, a day may have lasted only about 18 hours. Soon after the Moon formed, probably in a giant crash about 4.5 billion years ago, days were shorter still. As Earth slows, the Moon gains energy and drifts outward, about 3.8 cm a year.

In 1963, the scientist John Wells counted tiny daily growth lines on fossil corals about 400 million years old. They suggested there were around 400 days in a year back then, because each day was only about 22 hours long. The year was the same length. The days were shorter.

Living by the Moon

Many animals time their lives by the Moon. On Australia's Great Barrier Reef, many corals release their eggs and sperm together a few nights after a full moon in late spring, in one of the biggest spawning events on Earth. In California, small fish called grunion wriggle onto beaches to lay eggs just after the highest tides, which follow full and new moons.

Many night animals use moonlight to find food or avoid predators. A world without the Moon would have darker nights, smaller tides, faster days and perhaps a less steady climate. Life would probably still be here, but the rhythms of the shore, the reef and the night would be very different.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Moon's pull is strong enough to raise tides in a cup of tea or in our bodies.

Truth: Tides need a huge body of water stretching across thousands of kilometres. The difference in the Moon's pull from one side of a cup, a lake or a person to the other is far too tiny to notice.

Try this

Use a tide table for a beach near you, or for Mumbai or Chennai, printed in a newspaper or found online with a grown-up. Note the high tide times for a week. Then check a calendar for the Moon's phase. You should see high tides get later by roughly 50 minutes each day, and the biggest tides near full and new moon.
