Where do superstitions come from?

In 1948, a scientist made hungry pigeons superstitious in just a few days, spinning and bobbing because they seemed to think it made food appear.

The short answer

Superstitions come from the way the human brain hunts for patterns. If something good or bad happens right after you do something, your brain tends to link the two, even if it was pure chance. Once people share that belief, it gets passed on through families, stories and cultures.

Many superstitions also have old roots. Some come from ancient religious ideas, some from practical warnings, and some from old explanations of the world before science. Others started as jokes and stuck. People keep them because they are comforting. Doing a small ritual gives a feeling of control when life feels uncertain, like before an exam, a match or a journey.

Surprising but true

A black cat crossing your path is bad luck in many parts of India and the USA, but in parts of Britain and Japan it has long been seen as good luck.

Pigeons with lucky dances

In 1948, the American psychologist B. F. Skinner put hungry pigeons, birds better known for finding their way home, in boxes. A machine dropped food in every 15 seconds, no matter what the birds did.

After a few days, most of the pigeons had invented strange rituals. One turned in circles before each feed. Another bobbed its head up and down. Each was repeating whatever it had been doing when the food happened to arrive. Skinner called this "superstition". Humans do something similar when they wear the same socks for every match because they once won wearing them.

A black cat and a broken mirror

The black cat superstition is very old. In medieval Europe, black cats were linked with witches, and some people feared them. In India, many people believe a black cat crossing your path means bad luck, and some wait for someone else to cross first.

Yet in parts of Britain and in Japan, black cats have been seen as lucky. Sailors' families in England once kept them to bring their men home safely.

The broken mirror idea is often traced back to the ancient Romans, who thought a mirror held a piece of your soul. Some say the seven years of bad luck came from a belief that life renewed itself every seven years.

Lemons, chillies and the evil eye

Walk past shops in many Indian towns and you will see a lemon and seven green chillies hung on a string over the door. This is nimbu-mirchi, meant to protect against nazar, the evil eye. One popular explanation is that the sour and spicy smell keeps away bad luck, or the goddess of misfortune, Alakshmi.

Belief in the evil eye is found from India to Turkey, Greece and Mexico. In Türkiye, people hang blue glass eyes called nazar boncuğu. In India, parents may put a small black dot of kajal on a baby's cheek.

Lucky rituals in sport

Sports stars are famous for superstitions. Some cricketers always put on their left pad first, and the list of strange sporting habits is long. Others carry a red handkerchief or refuse to change their bat during a winning streak. Tennis players bounce the ball a set number of times before serving.

Psychologists think such rituals can help a little. They don't change luck, but they can calm nerves and help players focus. A 2010 study in Germany found that people who were told their golf ball was "lucky" sank more putts. Whether that result holds up is still debated, as other teams could not always repeat it.

Testing superstitions

The best way to test a superstition is to keep careful records, an idea from probability. If Friday the 13th is unlucky, there should be more accidents on those days than on other Fridays. When researchers check, they usually find no real difference.

But knowing that doesn't always stop people. Plenty of tall buildings still skip floor 13, just as hotels in China skip floor 4. Numbers have their own long list of lucky and unlucky meanings, and many of them started in the same place: a brain trying to find order in a world full of chance.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Only people who don't know science are superstitious.

Truth: Superstitions are common among all kinds of people, including scientists, doctors, pilots and sports stars. They come from the way every human brain looks for patterns and tries to feel in control, especially when stakes are high.

Run a fair test of a lucky charm. Pick something you think is lucky, like a pen. Do 20 short spelling or maths quizzes, using the lucky pen for 10 and an ordinary pen for 10, chosen by tossing a coin. Score each quiz and compare the averages. Did the charm make any difference?
