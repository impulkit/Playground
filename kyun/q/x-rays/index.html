How do X-rays see inside you?

The first X-ray picture of a person showed a woman's hand: her bones, and her wedding ring.

The short answer

X-rays are a kind of light with far more energy than the light we see. They can pass through soft parts of the body, like skin, fat and muscle, much as sunlight passes through a window.

Dense things are harder to get through. Bones are packed with calcium, so they stop many X-rays. When a doctor takes an X-ray, a machine sends a short burst of rays through you towards a detector on the other side. Where the rays get through, the picture turns dark. Where bone blocks them, it stays pale. The result is a shadow picture of the inside of your body, with your bones shining white.

Surprising but true

X-rays were discovered by accident in 1895, and within a few months doctors were already using them to find bullets and broken bones.

A glow in a dark lab

On 8 November 1895, the German physicist Wilhelm Röntgen was testing a glass tube with electricity running through it in Würzburg. He had covered the tube in black card, yet a screen across the room began to glow. Something invisible was escaping the tube and passing through the card.

He called it "X" radiation, because X stands for something unknown. A few weeks later, he made a picture of his wife Anna Bertha's hand, with the bones and her ring clearly visible. Newspapers printed it and the news spread around the world within weeks. Röntgen refused to patent his discovery, so anyone could build an X-ray machine. In 1901, Röntgen received the first ever Nobel Prize in Physics.

From the lab to the battlefield

Doctors grabbed the new rays almost at once to find broken bones and swallowed coins. During World War One, Marie Curie helped fit cars with X-ray machines so surgeons near the front could locate bullets and shrapnel. The cars became known as "little Curies".

Early users didn't know that strong X-rays could harm living cells, and some suffered burns from long exposures. Today the doses are tiny, the beam is aimed only where needed, and staff stand behind shields. A heavy apron lined with lead may cover parts of you that don't need to be pictured. If you are ever unsure about a scan, it is fine to ask the doctor or radiographer what it is for and how it works.

Seeing in slices

An ordinary X-ray flattens everything into one shadow, so organs overlap. In the 1970s, the British engineer Godfrey Hounsfield and the American physicist Allan Cormack worked out how to take X-rays from many angles and let a computer build slices through the body. This CT scan earned them a Nobel Prize in 1979.

X-rays are also used outside hospitals. Airport scanners look through suitcases. Dentists check for holes inside teeth. Museums X-ray old paintings and find earlier pictures hidden beneath the paint. Factories X-ray metal parts of aircraft to look for cracks too small to see from outside, and archaeologists use X-rays to peer inside ancient mummies without unwrapping them.

X-rays from space

Hot, violent places in the universe, such as gas swirling around black holes, give off X-rays. Earth's air blocks almost all of them, which is lucky for us but hard for astronomers. So NASA launched the Chandra X-ray Observatory in 1999 to look from above the atmosphere. India's AstroSat, launched by ISRO in 2015, carries X-ray instruments as well. These telescopes have to be built very differently from ordinary ones, because X-rays go straight through a normal mirror. Instead, the rays are made to skim off curved metal surfaces at a very shallow angle, like a stone skipping across a pond.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Having an X-ray makes your body radioactive.

Truth: X-rays pass through you and are gone the instant the machine switches off. Nothing stays behind. The dose from an ordinary X-ray is small, and doctors still use it only when the picture is useful.

Try this

In a dark room, press a bright torch against the back of your fingers or the edge of your hand. The thin parts glow red, because some light passes through skin and blood. You can't see bones this way, because ordinary light gets scattered. X-rays have much more energy, so they pass through muscle and stop mainly at bone.
