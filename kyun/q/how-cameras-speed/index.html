How do slow-motion cameras work?

In 2011, scientists at MIT built a camera system fast enough to film a pulse of light travelling through a plastic bottle.

The short answer

A video is really a stack of still pictures, called frames, shown quickly one after another. Normal video records about 24 to 30 frames every second. Your eyes and brain blend them together into smooth movement, the trick explained in how movies move.

A slow-motion camera takes many more frames every second, such as 240, 1,000 or even millions. Then it plays them back at normal speed. If a camera records 240 frames in one second and plays them at 30 a second, that single second stretches out to 8 seconds on screen. Nothing is slowed down. The camera has simply captured far more moments.

Surprising but true

In 1878, Eadweard Muybridge used 12 cameras and tripwires to prove that a galloping horse lifts all four hooves off the ground at once, which painters had been getting wrong for centuries.

The bet about a horse

For centuries, painters showed galloping horses with their legs stretched out front and back, like a rocking horse. Nobody could see what horses' legs really did, because they moved too fast.

In 1878, in California, the photographer Eadweard Muybridge set up 12 cameras along a racetrack, each triggered by a thread the horse broke as it ran. The photos showed that at one moment, all four hooves are in the air at once, but tucked underneath the body, not stretched out. A story says the railway businessman Leland Stanford had a bet riding on the answer, though historians aren't sure that's true.

How was photography invented?The oldest surviving photograph from a camera needed hours, perhaps days, of sunlight to appear. It shows a blurry view of rooftops from a window in France.

Freezing a splash

In the 1930s, the American engineer Harold Edgerton at MIT used flashes of light lasting a tiny fraction of a second to freeze movement. His photographs showed a bullet bursting through an apple and, in 1957, a drop of milk splashing into a perfect crown of tiny droplets.

That "Milk Drop Coronet" photo is now in museums. It showed people a hidden world that had always been there, just too quick to see. Edgerton's flash inventions later helped photograph nuclear tests and the deep sea.

Why it needs so much light

Every frame needs light to make a picture. At 30 frames a second, each frame can collect light for up to a thirtieth of a second. At 1,000 frames a second, each gets a thousandth or less. Less light means darker, grainier pictures.

That is why slow motion looks best outdoors in sunshine or under bright studio lamps. It also explains a strange effect: many lights, including LED bulbs, flicker on and off very fast. Your eyes can't notice, but a slow-motion camera often can, and the room seems to pulse.

Cameras that see the invisible

Scientists use high-speed cameras to study things too fast for eyes. They film hummingbird wings beating about 50 times a second, insects taking off, car crash tests and the moment a lightning bolt branches across the sky. Engineers check how engines spin and how phones survive being dropped.

Some research cameras reach millions of frames a second. In 2011, a team at MIT led by Ramesh Raskar, an engineer who grew up in Nashik, went much further. Using a laser and a special technique that combines many repeated shots, they captured light itself moving through a plastic bottle, at a rate equivalent to about a trillion frames a second.

Slow motion in your pocket

High-speed cameras once cost as much as a car. Now many phones film at 240 frames a second, and some have tried 960 for very short clips. Cricket broadcasters use slow-motion replays to check whether the ball hit the bat, and football referees use them to judge offside.

Sports science teams film athletes to study every step of a sprint or a bowling action. And the smartphone camera in your hand is the result of decades of shrinking lenses and sensors, the story of phone cameras.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Slow-motion video is made by slowing down a normal video.

Truth: Slowing a normal video makes it jerky, because there aren't enough pictures. Real slow motion needs a camera that takes many more pictures per second, so there are enough frames to fill the stretched-out time smoothly.

Try this

If your family has a phone with a slow-motion mode, film the same thing at normal speed and in slow motion, such as a water balloon bursting or a coin spinning on a table. Count how many seconds each clip lasts. Then try filming a ceiling light in slow motion. You may see it flicker, which your eyes normally can't notice.
