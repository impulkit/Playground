Who was Nelson Mandela?

He spent 27 years in prison for fighting unfair laws, walked out at 71, and four years later became his country's president.

The short answer

Nelson Mandela was a South African lawyer and leader who fought against apartheid, a system of laws that kept people apart by race and gave white South Africans power over everyone else.

For this, he spent 27 years in prison. When he was released in 1990, he worked with the government to end apartheid peacefully. In 1994, South Africa held its first election in which people of all races could vote, and Mandela became its first Black president. Instead of seeking revenge, he tried to bring the country together. People around the world still call him by his clan name, Madiba.

Surprising but true

Mandela's birth name was Rolihlahla, which in the Xhosa language is often translated as "pulling the branch of a tree", or more loosely, "troublemaker". A teacher gave him the name Nelson on his first day at school.

A boy from the hills

Mandela was born in 1918 in the village of Mvezo, in the Eastern Cape. His family belonged to the Thembu royal house of the Xhosa people. As a boy he herded cattle and listened to elders tell stories of his people's history.

He studied at mission schools, then at university, and moved to Johannesburg. In 1952 he and his friend Oliver Tambo opened South Africa's first Black-run law firm. Queues of people waited outside for help with the unfair laws.

Apartheid

From 1948, South Africa's government passed laws enforcing apartheid, meaning "apartness" in Afrikaans. People were sorted by race. Black South Africans, most of the population, could not vote, had to carry passes, and were forced to live in separate areas. Schools, hospitals, beaches and even park benches were divided.

Mandela joined the African National Congress (ANC), which campaigned against these laws. At first it used peaceful protest, strikes and boycotts.

In 1960, police shot dead 69 protesters at Sharpeville, and the ANC was banned. Mandela decided peaceful methods alone could not win and helped start an armed group that sabotaged government targets. Some people criticised this then and still do. The US government kept him on a terrorism watch list until 2008.

Robben Island

Mandela was arrested and, in 1964, sentenced to life in prison. At his trial he said he was prepared to die for the ideal of a free society where everyone lived together in harmony.

He spent 18 years on Robben Island, off Cape Town. Prisoners broke rocks in a limestone quarry, where the glare damaged Mandela's eyes. He slept on a mat in a tiny cell. Even so, prisoners taught each other, and the island became known as "the university".

Around the world, people campaigned for his release. Musicians sang about him, and countries refused to trade or play sport with South Africa. The island is now a museum and a World Heritage Site.

Freedom and forgiveness

On 11 February 1990, Mandela walked out of prison, holding his wife Winnie's hand, as crowds cheered. He and President F.W. de Klerk negotiated an end to apartheid, and both won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1993.

On 27 April 1994, millions of South Africans queued for hours to vote, many for the first time. Mandela became president. He set up a Truth and Reconciliation Commission, where people could tell what had happened under apartheid. He served one term, then stepped down.

The India connection

Mandela often spoke of Gandhi, who had spent 21 years in South Africa as a young lawyer and first developed his methods of protest there. India was among the first countries to cut trade with apartheid South Africa, in 1946.

India gave Mandela the Bharat Ratna in 1990, soon after his release. Since 2010, 18 July, his birthday, has been marked worldwide as Nelson Mandela International Day. People are asked to spend 67 minutes helping others, one minute for every year he spent working for change.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Mandela was always committed to completely peaceful protest, just like Gandhi.

Truth: Mandela began with peaceful protest, but after police killed protesters at Sharpeville in 1960, he helped start an armed wing of the ANC. He explained this choice at his trial. In later life he worked hard for peace and reconciliation.
