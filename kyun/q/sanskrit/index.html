What is Sanskrit?

About 2,400 years ago, a scholar named Panini described the whole of Sanskrit grammar in about 4,000 short rules. Linguists still study his work.

The short answer

Sanskrit is an ancient language of India. Its name means "refined" or "perfected". It is the language of the Vedas, among the oldest religious texts in the world, and of the two great epics, the Mahabharata and the Ramayana. For more than 2,000 years it was the main language of learning across South Asia, used for poetry, medicine, mathematics, law and philosophy.

Few people speak Sanskrit at home today. But it lives on in prayers and rituals, in classrooms and in the names of people and places. It also lives inside many Indian languages. Hindi, Marathi and Bengali grew from its family tree, and Tamil, Telugu and Kannada borrowed thousands of its words.

Surprising but true

The Sanskrit word for three, trayah, is a distant cousin of the English "three" and the Latin "tres".

The oldest voice

The oldest form, Vedic Sanskrit, is found in the Rigveda, a collection of more than 1,000 hymns composed over 3,000 years ago. For centuries these hymns were not written down at all. They were memorised and recited, passed from teacher to student with astonishing care.

Students learned to chant them in several patterns, word by word and in interlocking orders that repeat and reverse pairs of words. With so many cross-checks, a single changed syllable would be noticed. The method kept the hymns almost unchanged for thousands of years. UNESCO recognises the tradition of Vedic chanting as part of the world's intangible cultural heritage.

Panini's rule book

Around the 4th century BCE, perhaps earlier, a scholar from the north-west of the subcontinent named Panini wrote the Ashtadhyayi, meaning "eight chapters". In about 4,000 short rules, called sutras, he described how Sanskrit words and sentences are formed. The rules are so compact they read almost like formulas, with special symbols and abbreviations.

Modern linguists admire Panini as one of the greatest grammarians who ever lived. His grammar fixed Sanskrit's form so firmly that later writers, such as the poet Kalidasa, followed it closely, which is where the "refined" language gets its name. Mathematicians such as Aryabhata wrote in Sanskrit verse too, packing numbers and methods into short poems that were easy to memorise.

Cousins across the world

In 1786, a British judge in Kolkata named William Jones gave a famous lecture. He pointed out that Sanskrit, Greek and Latin were so alike that they must have come from a common ancestor, which perhaps no longer existed. He was right. Linguists now call that family Indo-European. It also includes Hindi, Bengali, Persian, Russian, German and English.

You can hear the family likeness. Sanskrit matr (mother) and pitr (father) sound like Latin mater and pater. Sanskrit nava (new) sounds like Latin novus and English "new". The languages drifted apart over thousands of years, but traces of their shared ancestor still show through.

Sanskrit today

In the 2011 census, only about 25,000 people in India named Sanskrit as their mother tongue. Yet it is one of the 22 languages listed in the Constitution, and many schools teach it. A few villages, such as Mattur in Karnataka, are known for families who make a point of speaking it every day.

You may have heard that NASA called Sanskrit the perfect language for computers. That story grew from a 1985 article by a NASA researcher, Rick Briggs, who argued that Sanskrit grammar could help computers represent knowledge, an early idea in artificial intelligence. NASA never adopted Sanskrit. Computers today run on programming languages built for machines, though Panini's precise rules do still interest computer scientists.

Myth or fact?

Myth: NASA has declared Sanskrit the best language for computer programming.

Truth: In 1985, a researcher at NASA wrote an article suggesting Sanskrit grammar could help computers represent knowledge. NASA never adopted Sanskrit, and computers are programmed in languages designed for the job.
