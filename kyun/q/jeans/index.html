How did jeans become popular?

Jeans began as tough work trousers for miners and labourers in the American West. The tiny pocket above the right front pocket was made for a pocket watch.

The short answer

Jeans began in 1873 as hard-wearing work trousers in the American West. A tailor named Jacob Davis put small copper rivets at the corners of the pockets so they would not tear. He teamed up with Levi Strauss, a cloth seller in San Francisco, and the two got a patent.

Miners, farmers, cowboys and railway workers wore them for decades. Then, in the 1950s, film stars and teenagers made jeans a symbol of youth and rebellion. From there they spread around the world, and today they are worn by almost everyone, from schoolchildren to grandparents.

Surprising but true

The blue dye in jeans, indigo, was for centuries grown in India. In 1917, Gandhi's first big campaign in India, at Champaran in Bihar, was about the hardships of indigo farmers.

A tailor, a merchant and some rivets

Jacob Davis was a tailor in Reno, Nevada. Around 1870, a customer asked for trousers that would not fall apart. Davis hammered copper rivets at the weak points, where pockets usually ripped. The trousers sold well, but Davis could not afford a patent.

He wrote to Levi Strauss, who supplied his cloth. Strauss paid, and on 20 May 1873 the two received a US patent for riveted work trousers. Davis moved to San Francisco to run the factory. Their trousers were first called "waist overalls", not jeans.

Denim, Nîmes and Genoa

The fabric is denim, a tough cotton cloth with a diagonal weave. The name probably comes from "serge de Nîmes", a fabric from the town of Nîmes in France. The word "jeans" is linked to Genoa in Italy, where sailors wore trousers of a similar cloth.

Denim is made from cotton. Blue threads run along the length and white threads run across. The weave pushes the blue threads to the outside, which is why jeans are pale inside.

How is cotton turned into cloth?Your T-shirt began as fluffy fibres wrapped around seeds on a plant, and people in India were spinning them into cloth around 7,000 years ago.

Why jeans are blue

The blue comes from indigo, one of the oldest dyes in the world. For centuries much of it came from the indigo plant grown in India, and the word itself comes from the Greek for "Indian". Under British rule, many farmers in Bengal and Bihar were forced to grow indigo instead of food.

In 1917, Mahatma Gandhi went to Champaran in Bihar to investigate the indigo farmers' complaints. It was his first major campaign in India. By then, German chemists had found a way to make indigo in factories, and from 1897 synthetic indigo began replacing the plant.

Indigo does not soak right into cotton. It clings to the outside of each thread. That's why jeans fade with washing and wear, showing white at the knees and pockets.

From work clothes to fashion

In the 1930s, holidaymakers from American cities visited ranches in the West and brought jeans home. During the Second World War, American soldiers wore them off duty and carried them abroad.

Then came the 1950s. Young film stars wore jeans in films about rebellious teenagers, and some schools in the USA banned them. That made them even more popular with the young. By the 1970s, jeans were sold everywhere, in every shape: flared, straight, tight and baggy.

Jeans and the planet

Making one pair of jeans uses a lot of water, mostly to grow the cotton. Estimates run into thousands of litres for a single pair, depending on where the cotton is grown and how it is watered. Dyeing and washing add more.

Some companies now recycle old jeans, use less water when finishing them and grow cotton more carefully. Wearing jeans longer, washing them less often and passing them on all help too. Many people already do this without thinking, because old jeans are usually the most comfortable kind.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Levi Strauss invented jeans on his own.

Truth: The idea of strengthening trousers with metal rivets came from Jacob Davis, a tailor in Reno, Nevada. Levi Strauss, a cloth merchant in San Francisco, supplied the fabric and paid for the patent, which they shared.

Try this

Look closely at the inside and outside of an old pair of jeans, using a magnifying glass if you have one. Find the diagonal lines of the twill weave. Then look at the worn parts, such as the knees, and notice the white core showing through the faded blue threads.
