How do kangaroos hop so far?

A red kangaroo can cover about 8 m in one bound, the length of a school bus, and hopping faster barely makes it more tired.

The short answer

Kangaroos hop so far because their back legs work like giant pogo sticks. Long, stretchy tendons run down the back of each leg. When a kangaroo lands, those tendons stretch and store energy, like a spring being squashed. On the next jump the energy is released and flings the animal forward again.

A big red kangaroo can cover about 8 m in one bound and race at over 50 km/h for short bursts. Its thick tail acts as a balance beam behind it. And once it gets going, hopping faster costs it hardly any extra energy, which no running human can claim.

Surprising but true

A newborn kangaroo is about the size of a jellybean, yet it crawls up its mother's fur and into the pouch on its own within minutes of birth.

Built-in springs

The secret is in the tendons, the tough cords joining muscle to bone. A kangaroo's leg tendons are long and elastic. At each landing the animal's weight stretches them. Then they snap back, much as a stretched rubber band does.

That means the muscles don't have to start every jump from scratch. A large share of each hop is powered by energy recycled from the hop before. It is the same principle as the metal springs in a trampoline or a mattress.

Faster without getting tired

In the 1970s, two scientists put kangaroos on a treadmill and measured how much oxygen they used. The result surprised everyone. Between gentle hopping and quick bounding, the energy cost barely changed. The kangaroos simply took longer hops instead of hopping more often.

Breathing helps too. As a kangaroo lands, its guts slide forward and push air out of its lungs. As it takes off, they slide back and pull air in. So its breathing rides along with its hops for free.

Five legs at slow speed

Hopping is great for speed but clumsy for shuffling around while grazing. So at slow speeds, kangaroos do something strange. They put their front paws and tail on the ground, then swing both back legs forward together.

A 2014 study found the tail pushes as hard as the front and back legs put together during this slow walk. Scientists now describe the tail as working like a fifth leg. Kangaroos also find it very hard to walk backwards, partly because of that big tail and their long feet. Put a kangaroo in water, though, and it changes style completely. It swims well, kicking its back legs one at a time.

A jellybean in a pouch

Kangaroos are marsupials, mammals whose babies finish growing in a pouch. A red kangaroo joey is born after only about 33 days. It is about 2 cm long and weighs less than a gram, smaller than a jellybean.

The tiny, blind newborn crawls through its mother's fur to the pouch, using its front legs. Inside, it latches on to a teat and stays for many months. Mothers can even pause the growth of a second, tiny embryo until the pouch is free again. Australia has plenty of other odd mammals, from the egg-laying platypus to the koala.

More kangaroos than people

Kangaroos are common across much of Australia. In 2022 surveys, about 36 million were counted in the areas where governments track them. Australia's human population is about 27 million. Numbers rise after rainy years and crash in droughts, sometimes by half.

Big males stand about as tall as a grown man, and they fight by leaning back on their tails and kicking with both feet. Still, the hop is the real superpower. A champion human high jumper clears about 2.4 m, and the science of jumping humans shows how hard we work for every centimetre that a kangaroo gets for free.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The word kangaroo comes from an Aboriginal phrase meaning "I don't understand you".

Truth: That story is false. Captain James Cook's crew recorded the word in 1770 from the Guugu Yimithirr people of north Queensland, whose word gangurru names a large grey kangaroo.

Try this

Mark a starting line, then see how far you can jump with feet together from standing. Measure it with a tape or by counting your shoe lengths. A big red kangaroo's 8 m bound is probably around five times your best jump.
