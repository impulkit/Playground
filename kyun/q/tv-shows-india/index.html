What shows did India watch together on Doordarshan?

On Sunday mornings in 1987, streets across India went quiet and families crowded around one neighbour's TV to watch the Ramayan.

The short answer

For most of the 1980s, India had one TV channel: Doordarshan, run by the government. Because there was nothing else on, the whole country watched the same shows at the same time. Families, neighbours and sometimes whole villages gathered around a single set.

The biggest of all were two serials based on the great epics. Ramanand Sagar's Ramayan (1987–88) and B. R. Chopra's Mahabharat (1988–90) emptied streets every Sunday morning. Around them were dozens of other favourites, from Malgudi Days to the weekly film song show Chitrahaar.

Surprising but true

When Doordarshan re-ran the Ramayan during the 2020 lockdown, Prasar Bharati said about 7.7 crore people watched one episode in a single day.

One channel, one country

In the early 1980s, a TV set was expensive, and many homes did not have one. Those who did often found their living room full of guests at showtime. Children sat on the floor, and neighbours brought snacks.

The 1982 Asian Games in Delhi brought colour TV to India, and Doordarshan quickly built more transmitters. By the end of the decade, most Indians lived within reach of a signal. Satellites, as India's satellites explains, carried the pictures from Delhi to the rest of the country.

Soap operas with a message

Hum Log (1984) followed a middle-class family in Delhi and their daily troubles. It was India's first soap opera, and it was made to raise questions about family size, women's rights and education. At its peak, it is said to have drawn tens of millions of viewers and huge piles of letters.

Buniyaad (1986) told the story of a family through the Partition of 1947. Older viewers who had lived through it wrote in with their own memories. TV was becoming a place where the country talked to itself.

Sunday mornings with the epics

When Ramayan began in January 1987, nobody expected what followed. On Sunday mornings, shops shut, weddings were timed around it, and roads emptied. Some viewers garlanded their TV sets and lit incense in front of them. The actors who played Rama and Sita were greeted like gods in public.

Mahabharat followed in 1988, with 94 episodes. Its theme song and the narrator's line "Main samay hoon" (I am Time) are still recognised by millions. Both stories come from ancient texts, the Ramayana and the Mahabharata.

Malgudi, Mowgli and science

Not everything was serious. Malgudi Days (1986) brought R. K. Narayan's stories of a small South Indian town to the screen, with sketches by his brother, the cartoonist R. K. Laxman. Its whistled theme tune is famous. Children sang along to the Hindi version of the Japanese cartoon The Jungle Book, with its song "Jungle jungle baat chali hai".

Turning Point explained science in Hindi and English, and Surabhi showed Indian culture and crafts, with viewers sending in millions of postcards for its quiz. Krishi Darshan, a programme for farmers, has run since 1967.

When the choice arrived

In the early 1990s, satellite dishes and cable wires brought private channels into Indian homes. Suddenly there were dozens of channels, then hundreds. Families no longer watched the same thing at the same time.

Then, in March 2020, offices and schools closed during the COVID-19 lockdown. Doordarshan brought back Ramayan and Mahabharat, and grandparents, parents and children watched together once more. For a few weeks, it felt a little like Sunday mornings in 1987.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In the Doordarshan years, Indian TV was only news and lectures.

Truth: Doordarshan did show plenty of news and educational programmes, but it also had comedies, cartoons, science shows, detective serials and the Sunday film. Many of those shows are still remembered fondly.

Try this
