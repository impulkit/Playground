Who was Madhava of Kerala?

Around 1400, in a village in Kerala, a mathematician found a way to work out pi using a sum that never ends. Europe found the same idea some 250 years later.

The short answer

Madhava of Sangamagrama was a mathematician and astronomer who lived in Kerala from about 1340 to about 1425. He founded what historians call the Kerala school, a chain of teachers and students that lasted about 200 years.

His big discovery was the infinite series: a sum with no last step, where each new piece is smaller than the one before. Madhava found series that give exact values for pi and for the sine and cosine used in measuring triangles. These ideas are central to calculus, the maths of change, which Europe developed in the 1600s.

Surprising but true

Madhava's own writings on series are lost. We know his discoveries because later students in Kerala copied them into books, often as verses to be memorised.

A village by the river

Madhava lived in a village called Sangamagrama. Many historians think it was near Irinjalakuda, in today's Thrissur district of Kerala, though nobody knows for certain. He was a Brahmin astronomer whose main job was making accurate calendars and predicting the positions of planets and eclipses.

That practical work needed very exact values for pi and for sines. India had a long tradition of such calculation, stretching back to Aryabhata in 499 CE. Madhava pushed it much further. His series for sine and cosine let astronomers calculate them to many decimal places without long tables. In Europe, Isaac Newton found the same series in the 1660s.

The endless sum for pi

Madhava's best-known result says that pi divided by 4 equals 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + 1/9, and so on, forever. In Europe, James Gregory and Gottfried Leibniz found it in the 1670s, and it was long called the Leibniz series. Many books now call it the Madhava–Leibniz series.

There is a catch. The series gets close to pi painfully slowly. Even after 100 terms, the answer for pi is still off by about 0.01. Madhava found a smart fix: a correction term added at the end that makes a short sum far more accurate. Using methods like this, he calculated pi as 3.14159265359, correct to 11 decimal places.

How we know about him

Almost none of Madhava's own writing about series survives. A few astronomy works with his name exist, but his mathematics reaches us through his students' students.

Around 1500, the astronomer Nilakantha Somayaji wrote the Tantrasangraha, which records Madhava's results. Soon after, Jyeshthadeva wrote the Yuktibhasha, in Malayalam rather than Sanskrit. It explains how the series were worked out, not just what they were, so it is sometimes called one of the earliest calculus textbooks. Many results were also written in verse, so students could memorise them.

Did Europe learn from Kerala?

In the 1500s and 1600s, Portuguese traders and Jesuit missionaries were active on the Kerala coast, and some studied local astronomy. A few historians suggest that Kerala's ideas could have travelled to Europe this way.

There is no clear evidence, such as a translated manuscript, that this happened. Most historians think Europe probably found the series on its own. Either way, the Kerala school reached these results first. Isaac Newton and Leibniz went on to build calculus into the general tool that physics and engineering now depend on.

A forgotten school

For centuries, the outside world knew little of Madhava. In 1835, a British official named Charles Whish wrote a paper describing the Kerala series, but it was mostly ignored. Only in the 20th century did historians of mathematics, many of them Indian, study the manuscripts closely and give Madhava his due.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Calculus and infinite series were invented entirely in Europe by Newton and Leibniz.

Truth: Newton and Leibniz built calculus into a complete system in the late 1600s. But Madhava and his followers in Kerala had already found infinite series for pi, sine and cosine, ideas at the heart of calculus, about 250 years earlier.
