Why does February have 28 days?

February is short because of ancient Romans who thought even numbers were unlucky, and an extra month that got squeezed in at the end of their year.

The short answer

February has 28 days because of the ancient Roman calendar. The Romans added it late, made it the last month of their year, and gave it the leftover days. Later rulers fixed the calendar to match the Sun, but they kept February short and simply added a spare day to it every few years.

So the odd month you live with today is a patch on top of a patch, more than 2,000 years old.

Surprising but true

In September 1752, people in Britain and its colonies, including India and America, went to bed on the 2nd and woke up on the 14th. Eleven days were skipped to fix the calendar.

A year with a hole in it

Roman writers said their earliest calendar had just ten months, starting in March and ending in December. That is why September, October, November and December are named after the Latin for seven, eight, nine and ten. Winter, when there was little farm work, was simply not counted.

According to tradition, King Numa Pompilius added January and February around 700 BCE. Romans considered even numbers unlucky, so most months got 29 or 31 days. But twelve odd numbers can't add up to an odd total like 355. One month had to be even, and February, a month for remembering the dead, got 28.

Caesar fixes the Sun

The trouble is that Earth takes about 365.2422 days to orbit the Sun. A 355-day year drifted badly, so priests kept adding extra months, sometimes for political reasons. By the time of Julius Caesar, festivals were landing in the wrong seasons.

In 46 BCE Caesar brought in a 365-day year, worked out with the help of the astronomer Sosigenes of Alexandria. Every fourth year got one extra day, and it went into February. That gave us the leap year.

Eleven missing days

Caesar's year averaged 365.25 days. That is about 11 minutes too long. Eleven minutes sounds like nothing, but over centuries it added up. By the 1500s the calendar was about ten days out of step with the Sun.

In 1582 Pope Gregory XIII made a new rule. Century years, like 1700 or 1900, would skip their leap day unless they divide by 400. Catholic countries jumped ahead ten days at once. Britain waited until 1752, by which time it needed to skip 11 days. Its colonies, including the thirteen American colonies and the British-ruled parts of India, changed with it. That is why George Washington's birthday moved from 11 February to 22 February.

Rhymes and knuckles

Because the month lengths follow no neat pattern, people invented tricks to remember them. English-speaking children learn an old rhyme that begins "Thirty days hath September, April, June and November". It goes back at least to the 1500s in England.

Another trick uses your knuckles. Make a fist and count the months across the bumps and the dips. Every month that lands on a bump has 31 days. July and August both land on bumps, side by side, because two 31-day months sit next to each other in the middle of the year. February sits in a dip, as it has for more than 2,000 years.

How close is close enough

The Gregorian year averages 365.2425 days. The real year is 365.2422. The gap is so small that the calendar will only be about one day off after roughly 3,000 years.

Many people use more than one calendar. India's national calendar, adopted in 1957, is based on the Saka era, and its months start in March. Most traditional Indian calendars, like the Jewish and Chinese ones, follow both the Moon and the Sun. The Islamic calendar follows the Moon alone. That is why Diwali moves around a little each year, while Eid slides about 11 days earlier every year and travels right through the seasons.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Emperor Augustus stole a day from February so his month, August, would be as long as July.

Truth: There is no Roman evidence for this. The story was told by a writer in the Middle Ages, more than a thousand years later. August already had 31 days before Augustus renamed it.

Try this

Make a fist and count the months across your knuckles and the dips between them, starting with January on the first knuckle. Months on knuckles have 31 days and months in the dips have fewer. When you reach July on the last knuckle, start again on the first knuckle with August.
