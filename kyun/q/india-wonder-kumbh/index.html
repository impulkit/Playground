How is the Kumbh Mela organised?

For 45 days in 2025, a temporary city bigger than many real ones rose on the riverbanks of Prayagraj, then was packed away again.

The short answer

The Kumbh Mela is a Hindu pilgrimage where people come to bathe in sacred rivers, believing a dip at the right time washes away sins. It rotates between four cities: Prayagraj, Haridwar, Nashik and Ujjain. The biggest, the Maha Kumbh at Prayagraj, drew enormous crowds from 13 January to 26 February 2025.

Organising it is like building a city in a few months. The Ganga and Yamuna meet at Prayagraj. On their sandy banks, the Uttar Pradesh government and many agencies laid out about 4,000 hectares in 25 sectors, with roads, bridges, power lines, water pipes, hospitals, police stations and tents. When the mela ended, almost all of it came down again.

Surprising but true

The Kumbh Mela tent city is built on riverbed sand that lies underwater during the monsoon, so it has to be rebuilt from scratch every time.

A city on a riverbed

The site at Prayagraj is called the Sangam, meaning confluence. During the monsoon, much of it lies underwater. Only when the rivers shrink in winter does the land appear, so building starts each autumn.

Engineers float pontoon bridges across the river, lay steel plates over sand for roads and set up electricity for lights and loudspeakers. In 2025 the authorities said they put up about 1.5 lakh toilets. Water was piped to taps across the site, and tens of thousands of police and volunteers managed the crowds. It is urban planning on fast-forward.

Counting the uncountable

How do you count tens of crores of people? Organisers used cameras, drones, train and bus records and even software that estimates crowd size from images. The official total for 2025 was over 66 crore visits, around half of India's population.

Not everyone accepts that number. Some journalists and researchers argue that the methods count the same people many times, or overestimate crowds. What is clear is that on the biggest bathing days, crowds ran into crores, among the largest human gatherings ever recorded.

Why these four places?

Hindu tradition tells of gods and demons churning the ocean to find amrit, the nectar of immortality. As it was carried away in a pot, or kumbh, four drops fell to Earth. They landed at the four Kumbh sites.

The date of each mela depends on astrology, the positions of Jupiter, the Sun and the Moon. That is why the gaps between gatherings are not always exactly equal. The next full Kumbh is the Simhastha at Nashik and Trimbakeshwar, expected in 2027, followed by Ujjain.

Holy men and lost children

Some of the most famous sights are the processions of akharas, orders of sadhus (holy men), many of them ash-covered, who lead the first baths on the most important days. People also come to camp for a whole month, living simply, praying and listening to teachers.

With so many people, getting lost is easy. "Lost and found" centres, called khoya-paya kendras, have been part of the mela for decades. In 2025 there were ten digital centres that registered missing people and announced names on loudspeakers across the site.

When it goes wrong

Huge crowds can be dangerous. On 29 January 2025, one of the busiest bathing days, a crowd crush near the Sangam killed at least 30 people according to official figures. Journalists later reported that the true number was higher. Each such tragedy leads to new rules about how crowds should be guided and spread out.

Seen from space

Satellites have photographed the Kumbh, showing the tent city as a vast grid on the riverbank. Other pilgrimages, such as the Hajj in Mecca, face many of the same puzzles of water, shade and crowd flow. And the river at the centre of it all has a journey of its own, from a Himalayan glacier to the Bay of Bengal, traced in the journey of the Ganga.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Kumbh Mela happens in the same place every year.

Truth: It moves between four cities. Each site hosts a full Kumbh roughly every 12 years, with smaller gatherings in between, and the dates are set by the positions of Jupiter, the Sun and the Moon.
