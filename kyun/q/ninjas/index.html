Were ninjas real?

Real ninjas almost never wore black masks or threw star-shaped blades. Most of them dressed like farmers, monks or travelling salesmen.

The short answer

Yes, ninjas were real. In Japan they were usually called shinobi, which means someone who sneaks in. Between about 1450 and 1650, Japan was split into warring territories. Lords hired shinobi to spy on enemies, sneak into castles, set fires, spread false rumours and gather information.

But the ninja of films, all in black, running across rooftops and throwing star-shaped blades, is mostly made up. Real shinobi were closer to spies and special soldiers than superheroes. Their best tool was blending in.

Surprising but true

The black ninja costume probably comes from Japanese theatre, where stagehands dressed all in black so audiences would pretend they were invisible.

A time of war

From the late 1400s, Japan fell into more than a century of fighting known as the Sengoku period, the "Age of Warring States". Rival lords, called daimyo, fought for land and power. They needed to know what their enemies were planning.

Two mountain areas in central Japan, Iga and Koka, became famous for shinobi. These areas were run by groups of local families rather than one big lord. They were good at guerrilla fighting and spying, and other lords hired them. In 1581, the warlord Oda Nobunaga invaded Iga with a huge army and crushed it.

How a real ninja worked

Old Japanese records describe shinobi slipping into enemy castles at night, sometimes during a siege. They might start fires to cause confusion, or open a gate. More often, they simply watched and listened. Their kit was simple and practical: a rope with a hook, a folding saw, a small cloth for filtering water and a tiny bag of medicine. Many tools doubled as farm tools, so a ninja searched at a checkpoint looked like any other villager. Even the famous sickle-and-chain weapon began as a farmer's sickle.

Disguise was everything. A ninja might travel as a wandering monk, an entertainer, a merchant or a farmer. He would learn the local way of speaking and the prices in the market. A manual called the Bansenshukai, written in 1676, gives advice on disguises, signals, climbing walls and making fire, and even notes on reading the weather and the stars.

Where the black suit came from

In Japanese kabuki and puppet theatre, stagehands wear black from head to toe. They move props on stage, and the audience agrees to ignore them, as if they were invisible. When playwrights wanted a ninja to appear suddenly and kill someone, they had him dressed like a stagehand. The audience got the joke.

Later novels, comics and films took the costume and ran with it. By the 1960s, ninja films were popular in Japan, and by the 1980s ninjas were everywhere in America, from movies to the Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles.

Spies in India too

Japan was not alone. More than 2,000 years ago, the Arthashastra, linked to Chanakya, described a whole system of royal spies. They disguised themselves as students, monks, traders and holy men. They passed messages in code and reported back to the king.

Spies in India kept working through many kingdoms. The Mughals ran a network of news-writers across their empire. Every powerful state, it seems, invents its own ninjas.

Ninja studies today

In 2017, Mie University, in the old Iga region, opened the International Ninja Research Center. Scholars there study real old ninja manuals, letters and family records. In 2018, the first student finished a master's degree there with ninja history as a focus.

In the city of Iga, there's a ninja museum with trick houses: secret doors, hidden rooms and floorboards that squeak on purpose to warn of intruders. The real ninja skill wasn't magic. It was knowing how people notice things, and how to slip past without being seen at all. Many animals do it better than any human, using camouflage that took millions of years to perfect.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ninjas wore black and used throwing stars to fight their enemies.

Truth: Throwing blades called shuriken did exist, but they were small and rarely deadly, and most ninja work was spying, not fighting. The black outfit comes from theatre and later films.
