How do swimmers go so fast?

Water is about 800 times denser than air, so the fastest swimmers win by fighting drag as much as by pulling hard.

The short answer

Swimmers go fast by pushing a lot of water backwards while letting as little water as possible push back against them. That push-back is called drag. Water is about 800 times denser than air, so drag matters far more in a pool than on a running track.

Top swimmers keep their bodies long, straight and flat, like an arrow. They use their arms and legs as paddles, kick powerfully underwater after every start and turn, and wear tight caps and suits. A world-class swimmer can reach about 8 km an hour, roughly a brisk jog.

Surprising but true

In 2008 and 2009, swimmers broke over 100 world records wearing new full-body suits. The suits were banned from 2010.

The enemy called drag

Drag comes in a few forms. Some comes from the shape of the body pushing water aside, which is why swimmers stretch out with arms over their heads. Some comes from water rubbing along the skin, a kind of friction. Some comes from waves the swimmer makes at the surface.

Good technique cuts all three. Swimmers rotate their bodies to slice through the water and keep their heads low. Some shave body hair before big races. Studies suggest this reduces drag slightly and makes the water feel smoother.

Four strokes

Front crawl, often called freestyle, is the fastest stroke. The arms pull in turn, so there is almost always one arm driving the swimmer forward, while the legs flutter kick.

Butterfly is the second fastest. Both arms swing forward together while the legs make a dolphin-like kick. Backstroke is like front crawl on the back. Breaststroke is the slowest, because the arms and legs recover forward under the water, creating extra drag. Its frog-like kick is still the best stroke for staying afloat and looking around.

Pools built for speed

A fast pool is part of the secret. Olympic pools are 50 metres long and deep, which keeps waves from bouncing off the bottom. Lane ropes have spinning discs that break up waves, and gutters at the edges soak up the splashes.

Swimmers save time with tumble turns and long underwater kicks. Because underwater swimming was becoming so fast, rules now limit it to 15 metres after each start or turn.

Suits that broke the records

Around 2008, new swimsuits made of polyurethane trapped air and squeezed swimmers into a smooth shape. Some designs were inspired by shark skin, which has tiny ridges. In 2008 and 2009 over 100 world records fell. Many said the suits, not the swimmers, were winning races.

From 2010, full-body suits were banned. Men's suits may now reach only from waist to knee, and women's from shoulder to knee.

Built to swim

Michael Phelps of the USA won eight gold medals at the 2008 Beijing Olympics and 23 Olympic golds in total. His arm span, about 2.01 metres, is longer than his height of about 1.93 metres. Long arms and large feet work like big oars and flippers.

Indians have made swimming history too. In 1958 Mihir Sen became the first Asian to swim the English Channel between England and France. In 1959 Arati Saha became the first Asian woman to do it. In 2021 Sajan Prakash and Srihari Nataraj became the first Indian swimmers to reach the strict "A" qualifying time for the Olympics. Coaches now film every stroke with underwater cameras, and sports scientists study the footage frame by frame.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You must wait an hour after eating before swimming, or you will get a cramp and drown.

Truth: Reviews by safety experts, including the American Red Cross, found no evidence for this. A huge meal might feel uncomfortable, but a normal snack is fine. Always swim where an adult or lifeguard is watching.

Try this

In a bath or bucket, push your flat hand through the water, palm first. Then turn it sideways, like a knife edge, and push again. Feel how much easier the second way is. That is the same streamlining swimmers use.
