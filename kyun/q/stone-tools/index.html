How did early people make stone tools?

In 1863, a geologist picked up a stone hand axe near Chennai. It showed that people had lived in India in the very distant past.

The short answer

Early people made stone tools by hitting one stone with another. A hard, rounded hammerstone was struck against the edge of a stone such as flint, quartzite or obsidian. A thin, sharp flake broke off. That flake could cut meat, scrape hides or whittle wood.

This way of shaping stone is called knapping. The oldest widely accepted tools, from Ethiopia, are about 2.6 million years old. They were probably made by early members of our human family, long before our own species appeared. Over time, toolmakers learned to shape stones into neat, balanced hand axes, then tiny blades that could be fixed onto spears and arrows.

Surprising but true

A freshly broken edge of obsidian, a volcanic glass, can be thinner and sharper than a steel surgical blade.

Choosing the right rock

Not every stone will do. The best ones are hard and glassy inside, with no grain. When struck, they break in a smooth curve, like a chip off a thick glass bottle. Flint, chert, quartzite and obsidian all work well.

In India, many early tools are made of quartzite, which is common in riverbeds. In parts of Africa and the Middle East, flint was the favourite. Knowing your rocks mattered. Archaeologists have found tools made from stone that came from quarries many kilometres away, so people must have carried good stone with them.

The art of knapping

A skilled knapper holds the core stone firmly and hits near its edge with a hammerstone at a sharp angle. Each blow knocks off a flake. By turning the core and striking again and again, the knapper shapes it step by step.

Later toolmakers also used softer hammers of bone, antler or wood, which gave more control. They pressed tiny flakes off with a pointed antler tip to sharpen edges. Modern researchers who learn knapping say it takes months of practice, and plenty of cut fingers, to make even a rough hand axe. That tells us early humans had to plan, teach and learn.

Hand axes and tiny blades

About 1.7 million years ago, a new style appeared in Africa: the hand axe. It is shaped like a teardrop, sharp all round, and fits neatly in the palm. This style, called Acheulean, spread across Africa, Europe and Asia and lasted for more than a million years. It may be the longest-used tool design in history.

Much later, people made microliths, tiny stone blades only a centimetre or two long. Several could be stuck into a wooden handle with resin to make a saw or a barbed arrow. Microliths have been found at many sites in India, including around the Bhimbetka rock shelters in Madhya Pradesh.

India's stone tool story

On 30 May 1863, the geologist Robert Bruce Foote found a stone hand axe at Pallavaram, near what is now Chennai. It was one of the first such tools recognised in India, and it showed that early humans had lived there in the deep past.

Nearby, at Attirampakkam, scientists later dated hand axes to around 1.5 million years ago. Some of that dating is still being checked, but it suggests toolmakers reached India surprisingly early.

Why stone tools matter

Bones rot away, but stone lasts. Scattered tools are often the only sign that people once lived in a valley or along a river. By studying how the flakes were struck, archaeologists can work out whether a person was right-handed, what they were cutting and even how many steps they planned ahead.

The Stone Age did not end because people ran out of stones. It slowly faded as people learned to smelt copper and bronze, a few thousand years ago. Even then, sharp stone blades stayed in use for a long time in many places.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Cave people just picked up any rock and used it as a tool.

Truth: Making a good stone tool takes skill and planning. Toolmakers chose the right stone, struck it at the right angle and shaped it in a set order. Modern experts who try it need months of practice.

Try this

Collect a few pebbles from a garden or riverbank and sort them by how they feel. Which are gritty, which are smooth, which are heavy for their size? Early toolmakers did this kind of sorting by eye and hand. Don't try to break stones yourself, because flying chips can hurt your eyes.
