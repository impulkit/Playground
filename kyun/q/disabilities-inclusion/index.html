How can the world be fair for disabled people?

About 1 in 6 people on Earth has a disability. Often the biggest problem is not their body but a staircase with no ramp.

The short answer

A disability is a long-term condition that affects how a person sees, hears, moves, thinks or learns. The World Health Organization says about 1 in 6 people on Earth live with a significant disability.

Many disability campaigners point out something important. A person in a wheelchair is disabled most by a building with only stairs. A blind student is held back most by a book that comes only in print. Remove the barrier, and much of the problem goes away.

So making the world fair means designing it for everyone from the start: ramps and lifts, captions and sign language, Braille and screen readers, and teachers who adapt lessons.

Surprising but true

Ramps cut into pavements were pushed for by wheelchair users, but they are used by many more people with prams, suitcases, trolleys and bicycles. Planners call this the "curb-cut effect".

Two ways of thinking

For a long time, disability was seen mainly as a medical problem, something wrong with a person that doctors should fix. Campaigners call this the medical model.

From the 1970s, disabled activists offered another view, the social model. It says people are disabled by barriers around them: steps, narrow doors, missing information and other people's attitudes. Both ideas are useful. Medicine can help a lot. But the social model reminds us that society can change, too.

Crawling up the steps

In March 1990, dozens of disabled activists in Washington, D.C., left their wheelchairs and crutches at the bottom of the US Capitol steps. They pulled themselves up the 83 marble steps with their arms, to demand a new law. People called it the "Capitol Crawl".

Months later, the Americans with Disabilities Act became law. It requires buildings, buses and workplaces to be accessible. In 2006, the United Nations agreed a treaty on disability rights, and India passed its own law in 2016.

Small changes, big difference

Many fixes are simple. Yellow tactile paving on railway platforms, with raised bumps, warns blind travellers of the edge. Talking buttons in lifts announce each floor. Captions on videos help deaf viewers, and also anyone watching on a noisy bus.

In 2015, India launched the Accessible India Campaign to make government buildings, stations and websites easier to use. Many Indian banknotes and coins have marks or sizes that help people who can't see well tell them apart.

At school

India's 2016 law says disabled children have the right to study in ordinary schools with support, instead of being sent away. This is called inclusive education. It might mean a ramp to the science lab, a classmate who takes notes, extra time in exams or textbooks in large print.

In the USA, a 1975 law, now called IDEA, promised every disabled child a free public education suited to their needs. Before it, many disabled American children were kept out of school altogether.

Researchers have found that inclusive classrooms tend to help everyone. Children learn to work with people who are different from them, and teachers find new ways to explain things that help all their pupils.

Champions on the field

Sport has shown what disabled athletes can do. In 1972, Murlikant Petkar, who was badly wounded as a soldier, won India's first Paralympic gold, in swimming. Shooter Avani Lekhara won gold at both the Tokyo and Paris Paralympics.

Including, not just helping

Inclusion means disabled people take part in decisions, in schools, offices, films and government. The disability rights movement has a motto: "Nothing about us without us." It means no plan for disabled people should be made without asking them first.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Most disabilities are easy to see, like using a wheelchair.

Truth: Many disabilities can't be seen at all. Hearing loss, dyslexia, autism, long-term illness and some visual impairments are often invisible. Wheelchair users are only a small share of disabled people.

Try this

With a friend, do an access check of your school. Pretend you use a wheelchair, or cannot see well, and plan a route from the gate to your classroom and the toilets. Count steps, look for ramps, handrails and large, clear signs. Write down one change that would help, and share it with a teacher.
