What is a day in the life of kids around the world?

In Japan, children scrub their own classrooms every day, and in the Australian Outback, some kids go to school without ever leaving home.

The short answer

Around the world, most children's days follow a familiar pattern: wake up, eat, go to school, play, help at home and sleep. But the details vary in surprising ways. Some children clean their own classrooms. Some learn over the internet from a sheep farm hundreds of kilometres from the nearest town. Some take a boat to school.

At the same time, not every child gets a school day at all. UNESCO estimated in 2025 that about 272 million children and teenagers were out of school, many because of poverty, conflict or because they have to work.

Surprising but true

In Bangladesh, some children go to school on boats that sail from village to village during the floods.

Brooms in Japan, school at seven in Finland

In Japan, there are usually no cleaners in primary schools. After lunch, pupils grab brooms and cloths and sweep floors, wipe desks and even clean toilets. It's called o-soji, and the idea is that everyone looks after shared spaces. Many Japanese children also serve lunch to their classmates, wearing white coats and caps.

In Finland, children usually start formal school at seven, later than in most countries. School days for young children are short, homework is light and children spend long breaks playing outdoors, even in snow. Yet Finland has often scored highly in international tests.

Lunch, buses and boats

In India, more than 10 crore children get a free hot lunch at school through PM POSHAN, often called the mid-day meal. For many, it's the most filling meal of the day. In the USA, about 25 million children ride the yellow school bus, which is designed to be one of the safest ways to travel on roads.

In parts of Bangladesh, rivers flood for months each year. A group called Shidhulai Swanirvar Sangstha runs boat schools with solar panels and computers. The boats stop at villages, pick up children, hold class on board and drop them home.

What is the mid-day meal scheme?Every school day, India serves a hot lunch to more than 10 crore children, the largest school meal programme in the world.

School from home in the Outback

Australia's Outback is so empty that some families live hundreds of kilometres from a school. Since 1951, the School of the Air in Alice Springs has taught them from a distance. Children once used two-way radios to talk to their teachers. Today they log in online. Students meet their classmates in person only a few times a year.

In Germany, children starting school on their first day get a Schultüte, a big paper cone filled with sweets, pencils and small presents. In France, many primary schools close on Wednesday afternoons, or all Wednesday, for sports and clubs.

Chores, play and time zones

In many countries, children help with chores before or after school, like fetching water, feeding animals, minding younger siblings or helping in a family shop. In rural areas of many countries, including parts of India, some children walk several kilometres each way to school. Bicycle schemes, like those that gave free cycles to schoolgirls in Bihar from 2006, helped many more girls stay in class.

Children everywhere also play. Tag, marbles, skipping and hide-and-seek turn up on every continent with different names and rules. And while one child is sitting down to breakfast, another, on the other side of the world, is going to bed. The Earth's time zones mean that somewhere, at every moment, a school day is just beginning. Kids' rights to school and play are part of the rights of every child, agreed by almost every country in 1989.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Children in other countries all go to school in the same way, with the same subjects and timings.

Truth: School days differ hugely. Some children start at 7 a.m., others at 9. Some start formal school at 4, others at 7. Many children travel by boat, bus, bicycle or on foot, and some learn online from home.
