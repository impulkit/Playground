Why is Alice in Wonderland still loved?

A maths teacher made up a story on a summer boat trip to keep three girls amused, and the world has been falling down the rabbit hole ever since.

The short answer

Alice's Adventures in Wonderland is a children's book written by Lewis Carroll and published in England in 1865. A girl called Alice follows a white rabbit with a pocket watch down a hole and lands in a strange world. She shrinks and grows, attends a tea party that never ends, and plays croquet with flamingos as mallets.

It is still loved because it was one of the first famous children's books that didn't try to teach a lesson. It is silly, clever and sometimes a little scary. The jokes work for children and for adults, and the dream-like world lets readers find their own meanings.

Surprising but true

The book never calls him the Mad Hatter. Lewis Carroll just calls him the Hatter, though the Cheshire Cat does say that everyone there is mad.

A story told on a river

On 4 July 1862, Charles Dodgson, a young maths teacher at Christ Church, Oxford, went rowing on the River Thames. With him were a friend and three sisters: Lorina, Alice and Edith Liddell. Their father was the head of his college. To pass the time, Dodgson invented a story about a girl called Alice.

Ten-year-old Alice Liddell asked him to write it down. He did, by hand, adding his own drawings, and gave it to her as a gift called Alice's Adventures Under Ground. That handmade book is now kept at the British Library in London. Dodgson then expanded the story and hired the artist John Tenniel, whose drawings are still the ones most people picture.

Jokes made of logic

Dodgson was a mathematician who loved games, codes and logic puzzles. Wonderland is full of them. Characters take words literally, argue in circles and follow rules that make no sense. The Duchess finds a moral in everything, which is Carroll poking fun at the preachy children's books of his day.

At the Mad Tea-Party the Hatter asks, "Why is a raven like a writing-desk?" Alice gives up, and the Hatter admits he has no idea either. Readers sent Carroll so many guesses that he later suggested an answer of his own. Riddle lovers still argue about better ones.

Words that went into the dictionary

In the sequel, Through the Looking-Glass (1871), Alice climbs through a mirror into a back-to-front world. There she finds the poem Jabberwocky, full of invented words like "slithy", "brillig" and "frabjous". Humpty Dumpty explains that some of them are two words packed into one, like a suitcase.

Two of Carroll's inventions, "chortle" and "galumph", became real English words found in ordinary dictionaries. The mirror world also plays games with reflection. Alice has to walk away from a place to reach it, and has to hold a book up to a mirror to read it.

How do mirrors work?A mirror doesn't swap your left and right. It swaps your front and back, and that's a much stranger idea.

Alice around the world

The book has been translated into well over a hundred languages, including Hindi, Tamil and Bengali. Translators have a hard job. Many of the jokes are puns that only work in English, so they often invent new jokes in their own language.

Alice has also been turned into countless plays, ballets, comics and films. Walt Disney's animated version came out in 1951 and gave many people their picture of a blue-dressed Alice.

Alice Liddell grew up, married and had children. In 1932, aged 80, she travelled to New York to celebrate the hundredth anniversary of Dodgson's birth, and she received an honorary degree from Columbia University. The girl in the boat had become one of the most famous children in literature.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The character is called the Mad Hatter in the book.

Truth: Carroll only ever calls him "the Hatter". The name Mad Hatter came later, from films, plays and the old English saying "mad as a hatter".

Try this

Make up a nonsense word by squashing two real words together, the way Carroll made "chortle" from chuckle and snort. Give your word a meaning, use it in a sentence at dinner, and see if anyone in your family starts using it too.
