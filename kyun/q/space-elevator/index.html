Could we build a lift to space?

Scientists have worked out how a lift to space could stay up. The problem is that no one can yet make a cable strong enough.

The short answer

In theory, yes. A space elevator would be a cable stretching from the equator to more than 36,000 km above the Earth. Robot climbers would ride up and down it, carrying people and cargo to space without rockets.

The idea works because of a special height called geostationary orbit, 35,786 km up. A satellite there circles the Earth exactly once a day, so it seems to hover over one spot. Fix a cable there, run it down to the ground and up beyond, add a heavy counterweight at the top, and Earth's spin keeps the whole thing pulled tight. The catch is the cable. No material we can make in long lengths is strong enough yet.

Surprising but true

A space elevator wouldn't stand on the ground like a tower. It would hang down from space, held tight by Earth's spin, like a stone whirled on a string.

A tower in a Russian dream

In 1895 the Russian scientist Konstantin Tsiolkovsky was inspired by the new Eiffel Tower in Paris. He wondered what would happen if a tower kept going up and up. At the right height, he realised, anything on it would float, because it would be moving fast enough to stay in orbit.

In 1960 another Russian engineer, Yuri Artsutanov, flipped the idea around: hang a cable down from orbit instead of building up. In 1979 the science-fiction writer Arthur C. Clarke made it famous in his novel The Fountains of Paradise. He set it on an island called Taprobane, based on Sri Lanka, where Clarke lived.

Why it stays up

Whirl a stone on a string and the string goes tight. The stone wants to fly off in a straight line, and the string keeps pulling it back. A space elevator works the same way. The Earth spins, the counterweight beyond geostationary orbit is flung outwards, and the cable between them stays taut.

Gravity pulls the lower part of the cable down. The counterweight pulls the upper part out. As long as they balance, the cable hangs like a plumb line, straight up from the equator. It would be in tension, like a rope in tug of war, rather than squashed like a pillar.

The material problem

That tension is enormous. A steel cable long enough would snap under its own weight long before reaching orbit. Even the strongest fibres used in ropes and bulletproof vests are nowhere near good enough.

The best hope is carbon in special forms. Carbon nanotubes are tiny tubes of carbon atoms, and graphene is a sheet of carbon one atom thick. In the lab, they are many times stronger than steel for their weight. But scientists can only make them in short pieces, well under a metre long. A space elevator would need a flawless ribbon tens of thousands of kilometres long.

Even with the perfect cable, there'd be problems. Thousands of satellites and pieces of space junk cross the path, and any of them could cut it. Near the ground, storms, lightning and strong winds would batter it. The cable would also need to be guarded, because damage anywhere along it could bring it down.

Is anyone trying?

A Japanese construction company, Obayashi, has published plans for a space elevator by 2050, though it admits the material doesn't exist yet. Students and engineers hold contests to build robot climbers and test them on cables hanging from balloons and cranes.

Some scientists think a space elevator might be built first on the Moon, where gravity is weaker and existing materials might just be strong enough. For now, getting to space still means riding a rocket, though reusable rockets have already made that much cheaper. A trip up the cable, if it ever happens, would be slow and quiet: about a week's ride, looking down at the Earth turning beneath you.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A space elevator would be a super-tall tower built up from the ground.

Truth: No tower could hold its own weight that high. A space elevator would be a cable hanging down from a counterweight in space, kept tight because Earth's spin flings the counterweight outwards. It would be pulled, not squashed.

Try this

Tie a small soft toy to a piece of string about a metre long. Outdoors or in an open space, with nobody nearby, spin around slowly and feel the string pull tight. The toy is like the counterweight, and the string is like the cable. Spin faster and feel how much harder it pulls.
