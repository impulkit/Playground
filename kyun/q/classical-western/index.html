What is Western classical music?

Do, re, mi comes from a hymn sung by monks a thousand years ago, and it works a lot like sa, re, ga in Indian music.

The short answer

Western classical music is the tradition of composed music that grew up in Europe over the past thousand years or so, from church chants to symphonies, operas and concertos. It is usually written down carefully in musical notation, so performers centuries later can play it as the composer intended.

Famous composers include Bach, Mozart, Beethoven, Chopin and Tchaikovsky. Their music is played by soloists, small groups and full orchestras of up to about 100 musicians. A key feature is harmony: many notes and melodies sounding together to make chords. Indian classical music, by contrast, centres on a single melody line, a raga, explored in rich detail. The word "classical" is also used for one particular period, from about 1750 to 1820.

Surprising but true

A conductor born in Mumbai, Zubin Mehta, led the New York Philharmonic for 13 years, longer than anyone else in its history.

From monks to manuscripts

Much of the earliest Western music that survives was sung in churches. Monks sang plainchant, slow melodies in Latin, all on one tune with no harmony.

Around the year 1000, an Italian monk named Guido of Arezzo found a clever way to help singers learn new chants. He used a hymn in which each line began one step higher, and took the first syllable of each line: ut, re, mi, fa, sol, la. Later, ut became do and ti was added. He also helped develop the staff, the set of lines on which notes are written. Indian students learn the same idea with sa, re, ga, ma, pa, dha, ni.

How are maths and music connected?Halve a guitar string and it plays the same note, only higher. That simple fact links music to maths, from ancient Greece to a tabla player's tihai.

The great periods

Historians divide the music into periods. In the Baroque period, from about 1600 to 1750, Johann Sebastian Bach wove many melodies together. Antonio Vivaldi wrote The Four Seasons, with music that imitates birds, storms and shivering in winter.

In the Classical period, from about 1750 to 1820, Haydn and Mozart wrote music that was elegant and balanced. The symphony, a long piece for orchestra in several parts, became popular. In the Romantic period of the 1800s, composers such as Beethoven, Chopin and Tchaikovsky poured big emotions into their music. In the 20th century, composers broke many old rules and experimented with new sounds.

Harmony and the orchestra

Western classical music builds tall towers of sound. A composer might write separate parts for violins, cellos, flutes, trumpets and drums, each playing something different, all fitting together. The conductor stands at the front, keeping time and shaping the performance.

Beethoven's Ninth Symphony, first performed in 1824, ends with a choir singing the "Ode to Joy". Its melody is now the anthem of the European Union. Maths is hidden everywhere in this music, from the ratios that make notes sound pleasant together to the patterns of rhythm.

India and Western classical music

Indian musicians have long had links with Western classical music. Zubin Mehta, born in Mumbai in 1936, became one of the world's best-known conductors. He led the New York Philharmonic from 1978 to 1991 and the Israel Philharmonic for about 50 years.

The Symphony Orchestra of India, based at the NCPA in Mumbai, was founded in 2006. Film composers, from the old Bollywood studios to A.R. Rahman, have used string sections and Western harmony in their songs. The sitar player Ravi Shankar wrote concertos for sitar and orchestra, joining the two great traditions on one stage.

Still being written

Classical music did not stop with the old masters. Composers today still write symphonies, operas and pieces for small groups. Film scores, such as John Williams's music for Star Wars, use the same orchestras and techniques, which is how many young people first hear a symphony orchestra.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Listening to Mozart makes you smarter.

Truth: A 1993 study found a small, short-lived boost on one kind of puzzle after listening to Mozart, and the idea spread widely. Later studies mostly failed to find lasting effects. Learning to play an instrument, though, does take real practice and concentration.

Try this

Sing "do, re, mi, fa, so, la, ti, do" slowly, and then "sa, re, ga, ma, pa, dha, ni, sa". Notice that both climb through seven notes and arrive back at a higher version of the first. Try matching each syllable in one set with the other.
