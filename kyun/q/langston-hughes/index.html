Who was Langston Hughes?

At 19, on a train crossing the Mississippi, he scribbled a poem on the back of an envelope. Americans still learn it by heart.

The short answer

Langston Hughes was an American poet and writer, and one of the leading voices of the Harlem Renaissance. That was a burst of Black art, music and writing in the Harlem neighbourhood of New York in the 1920s and 1930s.

He wrote about ordinary Black Americans: cooks, lift operators, students and musicians. He wanted his poems to sound like the people he knew, so he borrowed the rhythms of jazz and the blues. His poems are short, clear and musical, which is why children in American schools still read them aloud.

Surprising but true

Langston Hughes was working as a busboy in a Washington hotel when he left three poems beside the plate of a famous poet, who praised them in public the next day.

A boy who moved a lot

Hughes was born in Joplin, Missouri, in 1901. His parents separated, and he spent much of his childhood with his grandmother in Lawrence, Kansas. She told him stories about the fight against slavery, and he read everything he could find. Books, he later wrote, became a place to escape to.

At grammar school in Lincoln, Illinois, his classmates elected him class poet. In his memoir he says they assumed a Black boy must have rhythm. Later, at high school in Cleveland, Ohio, he wrote for the school magazine and began to take poetry seriously.

A poem on a train

In 1920, at 19, Hughes took a train to visit his father in Mexico. As it crossed the Mississippi near St. Louis at sunset, he wrote "The Negro Speaks of Rivers". The poem links Black history to the oldest rivers people have lived beside: the Euphrates, the Congo, the Nile and the Mississippi.

It was published in 1921 in The Crisis, the magazine of the NAACP, a civil rights group. Hughes was still a teenager when he wrote it.

What was the Harlem Renaissance?In the 1920s, one neighbourhood in New York became the centre of a burst of Black poetry, novels, painting and jazz that changed American culture.

The busboy poet

In 1925 Hughes was working as a busboy, clearing tables, at a hotel in Washington DC. The well-known poet Vachel Lindsay was dining there. Hughes slipped three of his poems beside Lindsay's plate. That evening Lindsay read them to an audience and told them he had found a new poet. Newspapers picked up the story. Hughes had left Columbia University a few years earlier, unhappy with the racism he met there. Now he went back to studying, at Lincoln University in Pennsylvania.

The next year Hughes published his first book, The Weary Blues. Some Black critics thought he made Black life look rough by writing about clubs and working people. Hughes answered that he wanted to show people as they really were.

Poems that keep travelling

His poem "I, Too" ends with a Black man who is sent to eat in the kitchen, but says one day he will sit at the table. It is a quiet, confident poem about belonging in America. "Harlem" (1951) asks what happens to "a dream deferred". Lorraine Hansberry took a line from it for her play A Raisin in the Sun.

Hughes travelled widely, to Africa, Europe, the Soviet Union and Japan. In the 1950s he was questioned by a US Senate committee about his left-wing poems from the 1930s, a hard time for many American writers.

Harlem remembers

Hughes lived for about twenty years in a brownstone house on East 127th Street in Harlem, now a protected landmark. He died in 1967. His ashes rest under the floor of the Schomburg Center for Research in Black Culture, part of the New York Public Library. The floor there has a design based on "The Negro Speaks of Rivers", so visitors walk over his poem.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Langston Hughes wrote only poetry.

Truth: He also wrote novels, plays, newspaper columns, children's books, song lyrics and two books about his own life. His funny stories about a Harlem man called Jesse B. Semple ran in a newspaper for about twenty years.

Try this

Write a four-line poem about your street, but make it follow the beat of a song you know. Clap the beat as you read it aloud. Hughes did something like this when he wrote poems that sound like blues songs.
