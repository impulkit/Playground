What happens at a film festival?

In 1956 Satyajit Ray's first film, Pather Panchali, won a prize at the Cannes festival in France and made the world notice Indian cinema.

The short answer

A film festival is a gathering where new films are shown, often for the first time anywhere. Directors, actors, critics, film buyers and fans come together for a week or two of screenings, talks and parties. Many festivals have a jury that hands out prizes.

Festivals help small films find an audience. A film made on a tiny budget in a village can win a prize, get bought by a distributor and end up in cinemas or on streaming services worldwide. For many famous directors, a festival screening was the moment their career took off.

Surprising but true

The Venice Film Festival, first held in 1932, is the oldest film festival still running. It happens on a beach island next to the city of canals.

The big ones

Venice, in Italy, held the first film festival in 1932, on the Lido, a long sandy island next to Venice. It still runs every late summer. Cannes, on the French Riviera, was meant to start in 1939 but was cancelled when the Second World War began. It finally opened in 1946. Its top prize, the Palme d'Or, or Golden Palm, is one of the most famous awards in cinema.

Berlin started in 1951 and gives out Golden Bears. Toronto, in Canada, is known for sharing the public's favourite films, and many go on to win Oscars. Sundance, in the United States, is famous for independent films made outside the big studios.

A day at a festival

Mornings often begin with press screenings for critics, who rush out to write reviews. In the evenings come premieres, where the director and cast walk a red carpet in front of photographers before the film starts.

After a screening, there might be a question-and-answer session with the filmmakers. At Cannes, a much-loved film can get a standing ovation lasting several minutes. Behind the scenes, there is also a film market, where companies buy the rights to show films in different countries. Many films need subtitles to be shown abroad, so festivals help them travel.

India at the festivals

In 1956 Satyajit Ray's Pather Panchali, about a poor family in a Bengal village, won an award at Cannes. Critics around the world started paying attention to Indian films. Ray went on to win prizes in Berlin and Venice too.

In 2024 Payal Kapadia's All We Imagine as Light, about nurses living in Mumbai, won the Grand Prix at Cannes, its second-highest award. It was the first Indian film in the main competition there in 30 years.

India runs festivals of its own. The International Film Festival of India began in 1952 and has been held in Goa since 2004. Its top prize is the Golden Peacock. Kerala, Kolkata and Mumbai hold big festivals too.

Festivals for young viewers

Many festivals now have sections just for children. Some, like the Giffoni festival in Italy, let children sit on the jury and choose the winners. India has its own children's film festival, whose prize is the Golden Elephant.

Short films, documentaries and animation get their own festivals as well, such as Annecy in France for animated films. Some school film clubs in India and the US send pupils' short films to youth festivals, and a few have won prizes against adults.

Why they matter

Big studios can fill cinemas with adverts. Small filmmakers can't. A festival gives them a stage, judges who watch closely and journalists who write about what they find. A film in Marathi, Persian or Korean can reach people who would never have heard of it.

Festivals have critics too. Some say they favour a small circle of famous directors, or that tickets and travel cost too much for ordinary film lovers. But for a first-time director, getting a film chosen at all can change everything.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Anyone can walk into the Cannes Film Festival and watch the new films.

Truth: Most Cannes screenings need a badge given to film workers and the press. There are some public screenings, but it isn't like a normal cinema. Other festivals, such as Toronto and Goa, are much more open to the public.

Try this

Hold a mini film festival at home or school. Ask friends to make a one-minute film on a phone about the same theme, like "lost and found". Watch them all together, give each a prize with a silly name, and let a jury of three pick a winner.
