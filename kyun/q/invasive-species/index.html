What happens when animals move to new places?

In 1935, about a hundred toads were shipped to Australia to eat beetles. Today there are many millions, spread across a huge part of the country.

The short answer

When plants or animals arrive somewhere new, most don't survive, or settle in quietly. But a few take over. These are called invasive species. Away from the predators, diseases and competitors that kept them in check at home, they can multiply fast. They may eat local animals, crowd out local plants or spread disease.

People have moved species around for thousands of years, sometimes on purpose, as crops, pets or garden plants, and sometimes by accident, in ships and cargo. A 2023 global report estimated that invasive species cost the world more than 423 billion US dollars in the year 2019, through damage to crops, forests, fisheries and health.

Surprising but true

Lantana, a pretty garden shrub brought to India by the British in the 1800s, now covers large areas of India's forests and is very hard to remove.

The toad that backfired

In 1935, Australian sugarcane growers had a problem with beetles. Someone suggested cane toads, large toads from Central and South America. About a hundred were shipped to Queensland from Hawaii, where they had already been introduced. They were bred, and a few thousand young toads were released into the cane fields.

The toads ignored most of the beetles. Instead, they ate almost anything else they could fit in their mouths and bred quickly, laying thousands of eggs at a time. Worse, their skin glands hold a strong poison. Native Australian snakes, lizards and small hunting marsupials called quolls had never met them and died after eating them. Today the toads have spread across a large part of northern and eastern Australia.

Invaders in India

India has its own invaders. Lantana, a shrub with clusters of pink, orange and yellow flowers, was brought from the Americas by the British as a garden plant in the 1800s. It escaped and now forms thick tangles in many forests, crowding out the grasses and plants that deer and elephants eat.

Water hyacinth, a floating plant from South America with lilac flowers, can cover lakes in a green carpet, blocking sunlight and boats. In Kutch, a thorny tree called vilayati babul, or Prosopis, was planted to stop deserts spreading. It has since invaded the Banni grasslands, one of the largest grasslands in Asia, where herders graze their buffaloes.

Some people are turning these weeds into something useful. In parts of southern India, craft workers bend lantana stems into chairs and baskets, and in Assam and elsewhere, dried water hyacinth is woven into mats and bags.

Invaders in America

In 1890, a man named Eugene Schieffelin released about 60 European starlings in Central Park, New York. Today tens of millions of starlings live across North America, competing with native birds for nest holes.

In the late 1980s, zebra mussels, small striped shellfish from the region around the Black and Caspian Seas, turned up in the Great Lakes, probably carried in water from ships. They now clog water pipes and smother native mussels. In Florida, Burmese pythons, many likely escaped or released pets, have spread through the Everglades.

Not all newcomers are villains

Many of the world's most loved foods are introduced species. After Columbus crossed the Atlantic in 1492, plants and animals began moving between the continents. Chillies, potatoes and tomatoes came to India from the Americas, mostly through Portuguese traders. Wheat, cows and horses went the other way to the Americas.

Scientists now try to stop new invaders before they arrive, by checking cargo, cleaning ship ballast water and asking travellers not to carry plants or animals across borders. Stopping an invader early is far easier than removing it later.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Any plant or animal from another country is harmful.

Truth: Most introduced species never cause serious problems, and many, like potatoes, tomatoes and chillies, are now valued crops. A species is called invasive only when it spreads fast and harms nature, farming or health in its new home.

Try this

Pick five foods in your kitchen, such as potato, tomato, chilli, rice and tea. With a grown-up, find out where each one first grew. Mark them on a world map. You'll discover that many ordinary Indian and American foods are introduced species that turned out to be useful, not harmful.
