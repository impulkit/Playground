What happened when Spain invaded the Americas?

When Hernán Cortés marched on the Aztec capital in 1519, most of his army was not Spanish at all. It was tens of thousands of local people who hated Aztec rule.

The short answer

In 1492, Christopher Columbus, sailing for Spain, reached islands in the Caribbean. Over the next 50 years, Spanish soldiers and settlers conquered huge parts of the Americas. They toppled the Aztec Empire in Mexico in 1521 and the Inca Empire in Peru in the 1530s.

The results were devastating for the people who already lived there. Diseases from Europe, especially smallpox, killed a very large share of the population. Survivors were forced to work in mines and on farms, and their religions and languages were pushed aside. At the same time, plants and animals crossed the ocean in both directions and changed what the whole world eats.

Surprising but true

Tomatoes, potatoes, chillies, maize and chocolate all came from the Americas. Before the 1500s, nobody in India had ever tasted a chilli.

Columbus and the islands

Columbus was looking for a sea route to Asia, which is why he called the people he met "Indians". The first lands he reached were islands such as Hispaniola, now Haiti and the Dominican Republic. The Taíno people who lived there were made to dig for gold and grow food for the newcomers.

Within a few decades, war, overwork and disease had almost wiped them out. A Spanish priest named Bartolomé de las Casas, who had once owned Indigenous labourers himself, became their fierce defender. He wrote angry reports to the Spanish king describing the cruelty he had seen. Spain passed laws to protect Indigenous people, but far from the king's eyes they were often ignored.

Cortés and the Aztecs

In 1519, Hernán Cortés landed on the coast of Mexico with about 500 soldiers. He soon learned that many peoples resented the Aztecs, who demanded heavy tribute from them. The Tlaxcalans and others joined him in large numbers. A young Indigenous woman known as Malinche, who spoke several languages, became his interpreter and adviser.

Cortés entered Tenochtitlan, the Aztec capital, as a guest of the emperor Moctezuma II, then took him prisoner. Fighting broke out. In 1520 a smallpox epidemic swept through the city. After a long siege, Tenochtitlan fell on 13 August 1521.

How did the world get rid of smallpox?Smallpox killed hundreds of millions of people over the centuries. Then, in 1980, it became the first human disease ever wiped out.

Pizarro and the Inca

The Inca Empire stretched about 4,000 km along the Andes mountains. When Francisco Pizarro arrived in 1532, it had just been torn apart by a civil war between two royal brothers, and smallpox had already killed many people, including the previous emperor.

At the town of Cajamarca, Pizarro's small force ambushed the new emperor, Atahualpa, and captured him. Atahualpa offered to fill a room with gold and two more with silver to win his freedom. The Spanish took the treasure and executed him anyway. Inca resistance continued for decades, but the empire was broken.

Silver, sugar and slavery

Spain grew rich from the Americas. A single mountain at Potosí, in today's Bolivia, produced enormous amounts of silver, mined by Indigenous workers forced to labour there. Spanish silver coins spread so widely that they were used as money in China and in Indian ports.

As Indigenous populations collapsed, Spanish and Portuguese colonists turned to enslaved Africans to work on sugar plantations and in mines. Millions of people were carried across the Atlantic in chains, a history told in slavery and freedom.

The great swap

Historians call the movement of plants, animals and germs between the hemispheres the Columbian Exchange. Horses, cattle, wheat and sugar cane went west to the Americas. Maize, potatoes, tomatoes, cocoa, peanuts and chillies went east.

Portuguese traders brought chillies to India in the 1500s. Within a century they had become part of Indian cooking from Goa to Bengal. Today Mexico and Peru mix Spanish and Indigenous cultures. Millions of people still speak Indigenous languages such as Nahuatl, the language of the Aztecs, and Quechua, the language of the Inca.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A handful of Spanish soldiers defeated huge empires on their own, because of guns and horses.

Truth: Steel weapons and horses helped, but the Spanish relied on tens of thousands of Indigenous allies, on rivalries inside the empires and, above all, on epidemics that had already killed many defenders.
