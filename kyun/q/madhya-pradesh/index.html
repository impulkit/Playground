Why is Madhya Pradesh called the heart of India?

Madhya Pradesh has more wild tigers than any other Indian state, and painted cave walls that people decorated thousands of years ago.

The short answer

Madhya Pradesh means "Central Province", and the name is a good description. The state sits in the very middle of India, with no coastline and borders with five other states. The Tropic of Cancer runs right across it. For decades, until Chhattisgarh was split off in 2000, it was India's biggest state by area. Today it's second, after Rajasthan.

It is "the heart of India" in other ways too. Its forests hold more wild tigers than any other state. Some of India's most famous ancient places are here: the painted rock shelters of Bhimbetka, the Buddhist stupas of Sanchi, and the carved temples of Khajuraho.

Surprising but true

In the rock shelters of Bhimbetka, some paintings are thought to be around 10,000 years old, made long before the first Indian cities were built.

The tiger state

India's 2022 tiger count found 785 tigers in Madhya Pradesh, more than in any other state. Many live in national parks such as Kanha, Bandhavgarh, Pench and Satpura. Kanha is also known for saving the barasingha, a swamp deer with many-pointed antlers, from almost vanishing.

These forests may sound familiar. Rudyard Kipling set Mowgli's adventures in The Jungle Book in the Seoni hills of central India, near today's Pench reserve. Shere Khan the tiger, Baloo the bear and Bagheera the black panther were all based on animals that really live here. Kipling never actually visited Seoni. He built his jungle from the reports and books of people who had.

Paintings from the Stone Age

Near Bhopal, among forested hills, are the Bhimbetka rock shelters. They were discovered by an archaeologist, V. S. Wakankar, who noticed the rocks from a train window in 1957. Inside, hundreds of overhanging rocks are covered with cave paintings in red and white.

They show hunters, dancers, horse riders, deer, bison and elephants. Some are thought to be around 10,000 years old, and people kept painting here for thousands of years afterwards. UNESCO listed Bhimbetka as a World Heritage Site in 2003.

Stupas and temples

At Sanchi, the emperor Ashoka built a great stupa, a dome-shaped Buddhist monument, in the 3rd century BCE. Later rulers enlarged it and added four beautifully carved stone gateways covered with stories of the Buddha's life. Strangely, the Buddha himself isn't shown as a person in these carvings. He is represented by symbols, such as a tree, a wheel or footprints.

Khajuraho, in the north of the state, has temples built about a thousand years ago by the Chandela kings. Their walls are covered with thousands of carved figures of gods, musicians, dancers and animals. Only a couple of dozen of the original temples survive, out of many more. Because they stood in quiet countryside, far from big cities, they escaped much of the damage that hit other temples, and they became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1986.

Rivers, forests and the Gond

The Narmada river begins at Amarkantak and flows west through a valley between two ranges of hills. At Bhedaghat, near Jabalpur, it cuts through gleaming white marble rocks. Many Hindus consider the Narmada sacred, and some pilgrims walk all the way along both banks, a journey of thousands of kilometres.

The state is home to many Adivasi communities, including the Gond and the Bhil. Gond art, with its animals and trees made of dots and fine lines, has travelled from village walls to galleries around the world. Painters like Jangarh Singh Shyam helped make it famous, and his style lives on in a new generation of Gond artists.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Jungle Book was set in the forests of Africa.

Truth: Rudyard Kipling set Mowgli's stories in central India, in the Seoni hills, close to today's Pench Tiger Reserve. Many people think Kanha's forests, nearby, look much like the jungle he described.
