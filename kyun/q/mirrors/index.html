How do mirrors work?

A mirror doesn't swap your left and right. It swaps your front and back, and that's a much stranger idea.

The short answer

Light bounces. When it hits a very smooth, shiny surface, it bounces off in a neat way. It leaves at the same angle it arrived, like a carrom striker rebounding off the edge of the board. Because every ray keeps its pattern, the light coming off a mirror carries a clear picture.

Most surfaces, like paper or a wall, are rough when seen very close up. They scatter light in every direction, so you see the paper but not your face in it. A mirror is smooth all the way down to sizes far smaller than the waves of light. Modern mirrors are a sheet of glass with a very thin layer of silver or aluminium on the back, protected by paint. The glass keeps the metal flat and clean.

Surprising but true

Astronauts left mirrors on the Moon in 1969. Scientists still bounce lasers off them, and the timing shows the Moon drifting away from Earth by about 3.8 cm a year.

Mirrors of stone and bronze

The oldest known mirrors are about 8,000 years old. They were made from obsidian, a black volcanic glass, polished until it shone, and were found at Çatalhöyük in present-day Turkey. Later, people in Egypt, the Indus Valley, China and Mesoamerica polished bronze, copper and other stones.

In 1835, the German chemist Justus von Liebig found a way to coat glass with a thin, even layer of real silver using chemicals. Mirrors soon became cheap enough for ordinary homes.

Kerala's secret mirror

In the village of Aranmula in Kerala, a few families make a special metal mirror called the Aranmula kannadi. It is cast from a mix of metals whose exact recipe is a closely guarded secret, then polished for days by hand.

Because the shiny surface is on the front, with no glass on top, the reflection has no faint second image. Aranmula kannadi received a Geographical Indication tag, which means only mirrors made in that region may use the name. They are often given as gifts and are part of some Kerala wedding traditions.

Mirrors in space

The biggest telescopes use curved mirrors to collect starlight, because a large mirror is easier to build and support than a huge glass lens. The Hubble Space Telescope's main mirror is 2.4 metres across. The James Webb Space Telescope has 18 gold-coated mirror segments. They unfolded in space to make one mirror 6.5 metres wide, about the length of two autorickshaws parked end to end. Gold reflects the infrared light Webb is built to see especially well.

In 1969, the crew of Apollo 11 set up a panel of special mirrors on the Moon. Scientists on Earth fire lasers at it and time how long the light takes to return, about two and a half seconds. That's how they know the Moon is creeping away from us.

Who's that in the mirror?

Most animals treat their reflection as a stranger, or ignore it. A few, such as chimpanzees, dolphins and magpies, seem to recognise themselves. In 2006, researchers at the Bronx Zoo in New York painted a white mark on the head of an Asian elephant named Happy. Looking into a giant mirror, she touched the mark with her trunk again and again, as if she knew the mark was on her own head.

That simple test hints at how clever elephants are. A mirror, after all, only bounces light. Knowing that the face in it is yours takes a brain.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Mirrors swap left and right.

Truth: A mirror flips front and back, not sideways. Your right hand stays on the right of the picture. It only looks swapped because we imagine turning round to stand where the reflection is.

Try this

Write your name on a card and hold it up to a mirror. Then try writing it so it reads correctly in the mirror, looking only at the reflection, not at the card. It is much harder than it sounds. Next, stand two small mirrors upright at a right angle and look into the corner. That image is the way other people really see you.
