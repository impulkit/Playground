What is the Hitopadesha?

Hundreds of years ago, a teacher was given six months to turn some lazy princes into wise ones. His secret weapon was a book of animal stories.

The short answer

The Hitopadesha is a collection of fables in Sanskrit, written by a scholar named Narayana several hundred years ago. Its name means "good advice". It is full of animals who talk, scheme, make friends and fall out: crows, deer, jackals, tigers, pigeons, mice and tortoises.

The book has a clear purpose: to teach wisdom, good sense and the art of ruling. Each story is packed with short verses giving practical advice about friendship, trust, war and peace. For centuries, Indian children learned their first Sanskrit from it. It is still one of the most loved story collections in India.

Surprising but true

In 1787, the Hitopadesha became one of the first Sanskrit books ever translated directly into English, by the scholar Charles Wilkins.

Teaching princes with stories

The book opens with a king named Sudarshana, who ruled at Pataliputra, near today's Patna. He is upset because his sons are lazy and ignorant. A wise teacher called Vishnusharma promises to make them wise within six months.

Instead of boring lessons, he tells them stories. Each story contains other stories inside it, with animals explaining their choices by telling tales of their own. This nested style is very old in India. The Panchatantra, which uses the same teacher, works the same way, and so do the Vikram and Betal stories.

Four books of advice

The Hitopadesha has four parts. The first is about gaining friends. The second is about losing them, and how clever enemies can break up good friendships. The third is about war, and the fourth is about making peace.

In the first book, a crow, a mouse, a tortoise and a deer become firm friends and keep rescuing each other from danger. In the second, a sly jackal turns a lion against his loyal friend, a bull. The later books follow two kingdoms of birds, the geese and the peacocks, as they fight and then make peace.

The pigeons and the net

One famous story begins with a flock of pigeons spotting rice scattered on the ground. The young pigeons rush down to eat, and are caught in a hunter's net. As the hunter approaches, the pigeon king calls out a plan. If they all flap at once, they can lift the net together.

They rise into the sky, net and all, and fly to the king's old friend, a mouse named Hiranyaka. The mouse chews through the threads and sets them free, starting with his friend's subjects before the king himself. A watching crow is so impressed that he asks to become the mouse's friend too, and the next story begins.

Short verses, big ideas

What makes the Hitopadesha special is its verses. Between the stories, characters quote neat little sayings, often two lines long, much like Aesop's morals but sharper. They warn against trusting flatterers, praise learning above wealth, and point out that a true friend shows up in hard times.

Some sayings are gentle and some are crafty. The book was meant for future rulers, so it teaches how to spot a trick as well as how to be kind. Readers today sometimes find its advice surprisingly modern, especially about choosing friends carefully.

A book that travelled

In 1787, Charles Wilkins, an English scholar working in Bengal, published an English translation of the Hitopadesha. It was one of the first Sanskrit books translated directly into English. Many more versions followed, in English, German, French and other languages.

For a long time, Sanskrit students in India and abroad began with this book, because its language is fairly simple and its stories are fun. Today, picture books, cartoons and school readers retell its tales in Hindi, Bengali, Tamil and many other languages. Many children who know the pigeons and the net have never heard the book's name.

Then and now

The Hitopadesha shows how much old Indian storytellers valued cleverness and good company. Its animals are not heroes or villains in a simple way. They make mistakes, trust the wrong people and sometimes learn too late. That mix of humour and honesty is why its stories still get told around Indian dinner tables, centuries after Narayana first wrote them down.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Hitopadesha is just another name for the Panchatantra.

Truth: The two are related, and many Hitopadesha stories come from the Panchatantra. But Narayana arranged them differently, added new tales and many verses of his own, and wrote a separate book with its own character.

Try this

Pick a short story you know well, like one from the Panchatantra or a fairy tale. Write a four-line verse that sums up its lesson, the way the Hitopadesha does. Read the story and your verse aloud to someone and ask if they think the lesson fits.
