Which animal is the strongest for its size?

A dung beetle smaller than your fingernail can pull more than 1,000 times its own weight, like a child dragging a train of loaded trucks.

The short answer

For its size, the strongest known animal may be a tiny mite that lives in soil. In a 2007 study, one held on against a pull of about 1,180 times its own body weight. Close behind is a horned dung beetle, which in a 2010 study pulled about 1,141 times its weight. For a person, that would be like dragging about 80 tonnes, as heavy as a dozen elephants.

Ants are famous for strength too. Asian weaver ants can cling upside down to glass while holding 100 times their own weight. But the winners change depending on whether you measure pulling, carrying, holding or biting.

Surprising but true

A tiny soil mite, smaller than a grain of salt, was measured holding on against a pull of about 1,180 times its own body weight.

The beetle with horns

The horned dung beetle lives in tunnels under cow pats. Males fight over females in narrow underground passages, pushing and shoving with the horns on their heads. That's why they need such strength.

Scientists at the University of Western Australia tied a thread to beetles and measured how hard they could pull before being dragged out of a tube. The strongest beat 1,141 times their weight. Smaller, hornless males, which avoid fights and sneak past the big ones instead, turned out to be much weaker. Dung beetles have other talents too, like rolling balls of dung in straight lines by following the stars.

Why tiny means mighty

There's a simple rule behind small animals' superpowers, called the square-cube law. The strength of a muscle depends on how thick it is, its cross-section. But an animal's weight depends on its whole volume.

Make an animal twice as long, keeping the same shape, and its muscles become four times as strong. Its weight, though, goes up eight times. So bigger animals get relatively weaker. A beetle carries almost no weight of its own, so nearly all its muscle power is free for pushing.

How do muscles move you?Every muscle in your body can only pull. Not one of them can push.

Necks, punches and bites

Ants aren't just strong, they're tough. In 2014 engineers in the USA studied a type of mound ant and found its neck joint could withstand forces of about 5,000 times its body weight before breaking.

The mantis shrimp is a champion puncher. It swings its club-like arm so fast that the water boils into tiny bubbles, which pop and hit the prey a second time. It can smash snail shells, and aquarium owners say it has cracked glass tanks.

For the strongest bite ever measured, the winner is the saltwater crocodile, whose jaws snap shut harder than any other living animal tested.

Jumpers and kickers

Strength isn't only about lifting. Some small animals are champions at turning muscle into sudden movement. A froghopper, an insect about 6 millimetres long, can leap around 70 centimetres into the air, more than 100 times its own length. Fleas and grasshoppers store energy in springy parts of their bodies, then release it all at once like a catapult.

In the sea, tiny crustaceans called copepods kick themselves away from danger at hundreds of body lengths a second.

These feats follow the same square-cube rule: small bodies are light, so a little muscle goes a long way. But tiny animals face a problem big ones don't. To a very small insect, air feels thick and sticky, a bit like swimming through syrup.

Strong in a different way

If you only count raw power, big animals win easily. An African elephant's trunk has tens of thousands of tiny muscle bundles and no bones. It can rip branches off trees and lift heavy logs, yet pick up a single peanut.

Humans, by the way, are quite weak for our size compared with insects or even chimpanzees. Our bodies trade raw strength for control and endurance. And the strangest part? Some ants team up and use their own bodies as building blocks, making living bridges for the rest of the colony to cross.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If ants were as big as people, they could lift cars.

Truth: Small animals look super strong partly because of their size. A muscle's strength depends on how thick it is, but an animal's weight grows much faster as it gets bigger. A human-sized ant would struggle to stand.

Try this

Test the square-cube law with modelling clay. Make a small clay animal with thin legs, then a much bigger one with legs scaled up the same way. Stand both up. The big one's legs bend or snap far more easily, because its weight has grown faster than the thickness of its legs.
