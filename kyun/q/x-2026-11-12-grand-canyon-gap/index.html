Why is a billion years missing from the Grand Canyon's rocks?

Put your hand on one spot in the Grand Canyon and you can touch rocks that formed more than a billion years apart.

The short answer

Rock layers are like pages in Earth's diary, with the oldest at the bottom. At the Grand Canyon, very old rock sits right under much younger rock, and about 1.2 billion years of pages are missing.

The rock was not lost by magic. It was probably worn away by erosion over a very long time. The big question is what lifted the land up so it could be worn down.

Surprising but true

The same kind of gap turns up far from America, in northern Europe and in northern China, as if pages had been torn from the same chapter of Earth's history book all over the planet.

Why it's in the news

On 11 November 2026, Live Science reported a new study published in the journal Nature on 4 November. Two geologists, Santosh Dhungana and Nicolas Flament of the University of Wollongong in Australia, used a computer model of how Earth's insides move over hundreds of millions of years.

Their model suggests that slow, hot currents in the mantle, the thick layer under Earth's crust, pushed parts of ancient continents upwards. Wind, rain and rivers then scraped those high lands down. Around 540 million years ago, the same regions began to sink, and new layers started piling up on top.

Reading the canyon walls

The Grand Canyon is about 1.8 km deep at its deepest. The Colorado River cut it, exposing layer after layer like a sliced cake.

Near the bottom lie dark, twisted rocks about 1.7 billion years old, some of the oldest rocks you can see in North America. Directly above them are flat layers of sandstone only about 500 million years old. The line where they meet is called the Great Unconformity. "Unconformity" is a geologist's word for a break in the record.

The timing is interesting. Just after the gap ends, life on Earth suddenly became far more varied in the Cambrian period. Some scientists think minerals washed off the eroding land into the sea may have helped.

Why scientists still argue

Other teams have different ideas. Some think enormous glaciers, during times when much of Earth was covered in ice, ground the rock away. In August 2026, a team at the University of Southampton suggested that a giant cliff once stretched across ancient North America and slowly ate its way backwards as it eroded. The Wollongong team agrees glaciers probably helped, but says the land had to be lifted first.

What happens next

Geologists will test the new model against more places where the gap appears, from Scandinavia to China. Each answer helps explain how Earth's surface and the life on it changed together.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Rock layers always build up steadily, so every year of Earth's history is stored somewhere in a canyon wall.

Truth: Rock layers are more like a diary with many pages ripped out. Layers form only where sediment settles, and they can later be worn away. Most places on Earth keep only a patchy record.

Try this

Make a "rock book" with five slices of bread stacked up, each one a layer of time. Now take off the top three slices, as erosion would, and put one new slice on top. Look from the side. The gap between the bottom slices and the new top slice is your own Great Unconformity.
