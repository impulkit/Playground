What is the Guru Granth Sahib?

The holy book of the Sikhs is treated as a living Guru, and it includes poems by Hindu and Muslim saints alongside those of the Sikh Gurus.

The short answer

The Guru Granth Sahib is the holy scripture of Sikhism. Sikhs believe it is the living Guru, the spiritual teacher who guides them. It is a book of hymns: poems written to be sung, praising one God and describing how to live a good, honest and humble life.

It is written in the Gurmukhi script and has 1,430 pages. Most of the hymns are by the Sikh Gurus, starting with Guru Nanak, who founded the faith about 500 years ago in Punjab. It also includes poems by Hindu and Muslim saints whose ideas the Gurus shared.

Surprising but true

Every printed copy of the Guru Granth Sahib has the same 1,430 pages, so a hymn is always found on the same page number in any copy in the world.

How the book was made

Guru Nanak and the Gurus who came after him composed hymns and sang them with their followers. These were written down and passed on. Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth Guru, gathered them into one book with the help of a scholar named Bhai Gurdas. In 1604, this first version, called the Adi Granth ("the first book"), was placed in the Harmandir Sahib in Amritsar, now known as the Golden Temple.

The tenth Guru, Guru Gobind Singh, later added the hymns of his father, Guru Tegh Bahadur. In 1708, shortly before he died, Guru Gobind Singh declared that there would be no more human Gurus. From then on, the scripture itself would be the Guru, and it became the Guru Granth Sahib.

What is inside

The book opens with the Mul Mantar, a short statement of belief that begins Ik Onkar: there is one God. Its hymns talk about remembering God, working honestly, sharing with others and seeing every person as equal, whatever their caste, religion or whether they are a man or a woman.

Most of the book is arranged by raga, the musical mode in which a hymn is sung, with about 31 ragas in all. Many hymns name which raga to use, and some ragas are traditionally sung at particular times of day. The language is mostly old Punjabi, mixed with Braj, Persian and other languages spoken across north India at the time.

Why are some ragas sung only at certain times?Bhairav at sunrise. Yaman at dusk. Malhar when the monsoon clouds roll in.

Voices from many backgrounds

The Gurus included poems by saints called Bhagats. Among them are Kabir, a weaver from Varanasi, and Ravidas, a leather worker. There is Namdev, a tailor-poet from Maharashtra, and Sheikh Farid, a Sufi Muslim from Punjab who lived about 300 years before Guru Nanak.

For Sikhs, this shows a central belief: that truth and devotion are not owned by any one group. People of very different faiths and backgrounds, some from communities that were looked down on in their time, share the same pages.

How it is treated

In a gurdwara, the Sikh place of worship, the Guru Granth Sahib rests on a raised platform under a canopy, wrapped in cloth when not being read. An attendant often waves a whisk called a chaur over it, a sign of respect once shown to kings. At night, in many gurdwaras, it is carried to a separate room to rest, and it is brought back in the morning.

Each morning the book is opened at a random page, and a hymn from the top of the left-hand page is read aloud. This is called the hukamnama, the order or guidance for the day. The hukamnama from the Golden Temple is now shared online every day, so Sikhs in Toronto, London or California can read the same hymn as worshippers in Amritsar.

Visitors of any faith are welcome. They cover their heads, take off their shoes and sit on the floor. On special occasions, a team of readers takes turns to read the whole book aloud without stopping, which takes about 48 hours. This is called an Akhand Path. Afterwards, everyone may share a free community meal called langar, cooked and served by volunteers.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Guru Granth Sahib contains only writings by Sikh Gurus.

Truth: Besides the hymns of six Sikh Gurus, it includes verses by saints called Bhagats, from different faiths and backgrounds. They include Kabir, a weaver; Ravidas, a leather worker; Namdev, from Maharashtra; and Sheikh Farid, a Sufi Muslim from Punjab.

Try this

Look at the opening words "Ik Onkar" written in Gurmukhi (ੴ). The symbol joins the number one (੧) with a letter for the sound "O". Try copying it slowly with a pencil, then ask a Sikh friend or look up how it is pronounced and what it means to them.
