What was the Silk Road?

Silk from China reached Rome, and Buddhism reached China, along roads that almost nobody travelled from end to end.

The short answer

The Silk Road was a huge network of trade routes that linked China with Central Asia, India, Persia, Arabia and the Mediterranean. It was busiest from about the 2nd century BCE to the 15th century CE.

Merchants carried Chinese silk west, and brought back horses, glass, gold and wool. Indian spices, cotton and gems joined the flow. Along with goods came ideas. Buddhism travelled from India to China, paper-making moved west from China, and numbers, foods, music and diseases spread too. Most traders covered only one stretch, then sold their goods on, so the Silk Road worked like a giant relay rather than a single journey.

Surprising but true

The name "Silk Road" is quite new. A German geographer, Ferdinand von Richthofen, made it popular in 1877.

A secret wrapped in silk

The Chinese learned to make silk thousands of years ago, from the cocoons of silkworms that feed on mulberry leaves. For centuries they kept the method secret. Rich Romans paid high prices for this smooth, light cloth and had no idea how it was made.

The Byzantine historian Procopius wrote that, around the 550s CE, monks smuggled silkworm eggs to Constantinople, the capital of the eastern Roman Empire, perhaps hidden in hollow walking sticks. After that, Europe began making its own silk.

Camels, deserts and oasis towns

Some of the hardest stretches crossed the Taklamakan Desert and the high Pamir mountains. Traders travelled in caravans for safety, often with two-humped Bactrian camels that can cope with freezing nights and blazing days.

Along the way grew oasis towns such as Dunhuang, Kashgar and Samarkand. They had markets, inns called caravanserais, temples and wells. These towns became rich by serving travellers, and their people spoke many languages. Samarkand, in today's Uzbekistan, sat at a crossroads where routes from China, India and Persia met, and it grew into one of the great cities of Central Asia.

How do camels survive the desert?A thirsty camel can drink about 100 litres of water in a quarter of an hour, about five big buckets' worth.

Monks on the move

Buddhist monks used the same routes as traders. Statues, cave temples and texts appeared along the way, mixing Indian, Greek and Chinese styles.

The Chinese monk Xuanzang walked to India in the 7th century to study at Nalanda, and returned with hundreds of texts. Near Dunhuang, the Mogao Caves hold hundreds of painted Buddhist shrines. In one sealed cave, a copy of the Diamond Sutra was found, printed in 868 CE. It is the oldest dated printed book known, and it is now in the British Library in London.

Routes across the sea

Goods also moved by sea, from Chinese ports past Southeast Asia to India, Arabia and East Africa. Sailors used the monsoon winds, which blow one way in summer and the other way in winter.

Indian ports on the Malabar and Coromandel coasts were busy stops. Pepper from Kerala was so prized in Rome that Roman coins have been dug up in south India. Hoards of them have turned up in Tamil Nadu and Kerala, some still in the pots their owners buried.

At Arikamedu, near Puducherry, archaeologists found pieces of Roman wine jars and fine Roman pottery. Ships sailing with the summer monsoon could cross from the Red Sea to India in a few weeks, and return with the winter winds.

Why the routes faded

From the 15th century, European ships found sea routes around Africa to India and China. Land routes became less important, and empires along them rose and fell.

Today, the old routes have a new fame. In 2014 UNESCO listed a 5,000 km section running from China into Central Asia as a World Heritage Site. Silk still comes from China and India, but now it travels in shipping containers.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Silk Road was one long paved road.

Truth: It was a web of tracks, mountain passes, river crossings and sea routes. Routes shifted with wars, weather and new markets, and many never had paving at all.
