How are crayons and paints made?

The first box of Crayola crayons, sold in 1903, had just eight colours and cost five cents. All of them were made from wax and coloured powder.

The short answer

Crayons are made by melting wax, usually paraffin wax from petroleum, and stirring in coloured powder called pigment. The hot coloured wax is poured into moulds shaped like rows of thin tubes. It cools and hardens in a few minutes. The crayons are pushed out, wrapped in paper labels and boxed.

Paints are made by mixing pigment with a binder, something sticky that holds the colour together and glues it to a surface. Different binders make different paints. Watercolours use gum from trees. Oil paints use oil from seeds. Wall paints use plastic resins. Water or another liquid is added to make the paint flow.

Surprising but true

Some of the oldest paints in the world are red and yellow ochre, coloured earth that people were using more than 100,000 years ago. Ochre is still used in paints today.

Colour from the earth

People have been making paints for a very long time. In a cave in South Africa, archaeologists found shells used as paint pots about 100,000 years ago, with traces of ground red ochre, an iron-rich clay. Ochre, charcoal and white chalk gave early artists red, yellow, black and white.

In India, painters at Ajanta worked more than 1,500 years ago. Their colours included ochres, black from soot and green from a mineral called terre verte. Many experts think they also used a little precious blue from lapis lazuli, a stone brought from far away. Mixing these pigments with glue made from plants or animals turned them into paint for walls.

From rocks to factories

For centuries, artists or their assistants made paint by hand. They ground pigments with a stone on a slab, then mixed them with egg yolk, oil or gum. Some colours were very costly. Ultramarine blue, made from lapis lazuli, could cost more than gold.

In the 1800s, chemists began making new pigments in laboratories. Bright synthetic blues, greens, purples and reds appeared and were much cheaper. In 1841, the American painter John Goffe Rand invented the squeezable metal paint tube. Painters could now carry paint outdoors easily, which helped the Impressionists in France paint landscapes on the spot.

Who made the very first paintings?Long before paper, pencils or paintbrushes, people were painting on rock. Some of their pictures are more than 50,000 years old.

The crayon box

In 1903, a company in New York called Binney & Smith sold its first box of crayons for children, under the name Crayola. The box had eight colours: black, brown, blue, red, purple, orange, yellow and green. It cost five cents. The name came from the French word craie, meaning chalk, and "ola" from oily.

Today, a Crayola factory in Pennsylvania can make millions of crayons a day. Indian companies, such as Camlin, which began making ink in Mumbai in the 1930s, now make crayons, oil pastels and paints for schoolchildren across the country.

Why some colours fade

Pigments are tiny solid grains that sit on the surface, held in place by the binder. Some are very stable, like ochre, and stay bright for thousands of years. Others break down in sunlight, a problem artists have struggled with for centuries.

Children's paints have to be safe. In the past, some pigments contained poisons like lead and arsenic. Modern school paints and crayons in many countries are tested and labelled non-toxic, so a crayon accidentally chewed by a toddler won't harm them.

Paint you can wear and throw

The colours of Holi were once made from flowers, leaves and spices: yellow from turmeric, red from the flowers of the palash tree, green from neem or henna leaves. Many of today's Holi powders are made in factories, and some contain harsh chemicals, so some families have gone back to making their own colours from plants.

Paint on house walls, cars and ships works the same basic way as an artist's paint, with pigment and binder, but with extra ingredients that help it resist rain, heat and scratches.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Crayons and paints get their colour from dye, like food colouring.

Truth: Most crayons and paints get their colour from pigments, tiny solid particles that don't dissolve. Dyes do dissolve, and they are used in some inks and markers, but they fade faster in sunlight.

Try this

Make your own paint with an adult. Crush a spoonful of coloured chalk or turmeric powder into fine dust, then stir in a little white glue and a few drops of water until it's smooth. Paint a stripe on paper and compare it with your normal paint once it dries.
