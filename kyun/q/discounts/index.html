How do shop discounts really work?

Fifty per cent off plus an extra twenty per cent off sounds like seventy per cent. It's actually sixty, and shops know most people won't notice.

The short answer

A discount is a cut in price, usually written as a percentage. "25% off" means you take away a quarter of the price. On a ₹400 book, that's ₹100 off, so you pay ₹300. On a $40 game, it's $10 off.

The maths is simple when there's one discount. It gets tricky when shops stack them, mix them with free gifts or hide them behind words like "up to". That's not an accident. Shops know that a big, round number grabs attention faster than a careful sum.

Learning a few tricks with percentages lets you see straight through most of them.

Surprising but true

Two discounts of 20% and 10%, one after the other, take off only 28% in total, not 30%.

The stacking trap

Say a shoe shop offers 50% off, plus an extra 20% at the till. It sounds like 70% off. It isn't.

The first discount turns ₹2,000 into ₹1,000. The second takes 20% of ₹1,000, which is ₹200. You pay ₹800, so you saved 60%. Each new discount works on a smaller number, so the total is always less than simply adding the percentages.

There's a quick way to check. Turn each discount into what you keep: 50% off means you pay 0.5, and 20% off means you pay 0.8. Multiply them: 0.5 × 0.8 = 0.4. You pay 40% of the price. The same trick works for any pair of discounts.

Free isn't always free

"Buy one, get one free" is the same as 50% off each item, but only if you wanted two in the first place. If you needed just one, you paid full price and got something extra you might not use.

"Buy two, get one free" works out at a third off each item. "Up to 70% off" means at least one thing in the shop is 70% off. Most things may be 10% or 20% off. "Free gift worth ₹500" tells you what the shop says the gift is worth, which may not be what anyone would pay for it.

What price are they cutting from?

A discount is only as honest as the price it starts from. In India, packaged goods must show an MRP, the maximum retail price, and shops may not charge more than that. It's one of the basic consumer rights every shopper has. A sale is usually measured against the MRP.

In the USA there's no MRP. Shops set their own prices, and the government's Federal Trade Commission warns against fake "was" prices that the item never really sold at. There's another twist for American shoppers: in most states, sales tax is added at the till. A $20 shirt "on sale" may cost more like $21.50 once tax goes on. Five states have no statewide sales tax at all.

Why so many 99s?

Prices like ₹499 or $9.99 are called charm pricing. We read numbers from the left, so ₹499 feels closer to ₹400 than to ₹500, even though it's just one rupee short. In India you'll see ₹999 and ₹1,499 everywhere, and in the USA prices like $19.99 are so common that a round $20 can look odd. Studies of shoppers have found that these prices often do sell more.

Advertisers also use red tags, countdown clocks and words like "last chance". Big sales cluster around festivals: Diwali in India, and Black Friday, the day after Thanksgiving, in the USA.

A shopper's sum

Before you buy, ask two questions. What will I actually pay, in rupees or dollars? Would I buy this at that price if there were no sale sign? If the answer to the second question is no, the best discount is 100%, and you keep all your money.

Myth or fact?

Myth: 50% off plus an extra 20% off means 70% off.

Truth: The second discount is taken from the already reduced price. Half of ₹1,000 is ₹500, and 20% of ₹500 is ₹100, so you pay ₹400. That's 60% off, not 70%.

Try this

Collect three sale leaflets or online offers. For each one, work out the actual price in rupees or dollars and the real total percentage off. Then rank them from best deal to worst and see whether the one that looked biggest really was.
