Are hyenas really scavengers?

In some parts of Africa, lions steal more food from spotted hyenas than hyenas steal from lions.

The short answer

Not mostly. The spotted hyena of Africa, the largest kind, is a powerful hunter. In many places it kills well over half of what it eats, chasing down wildebeest, zebra and gazelles in packs. In the Ngorongoro Crater in Tanzania, scientists found that lions often scavenged from hyena kills, not the other way round.

Hyenas do scavenge too, and they are very good at it, cracking bones that other animals leave behind. But the image of a hyena as a cowardly thief is mostly wrong.

India's own hyena, the striped hyena, is different. It is shy, mostly solitary and does live mainly by scavenging, along with insects, fruit and small animals.

Surprising but true

Spotted hyena droppings can turn chalky white, because hyenas eat so much bone that their poop is full of calcium.

Built to crack bones

A spotted hyena's jaws are among the strongest of any mammal for its size. Its big back teeth and huge jaw muscles can crunch through a giraffe's leg bone. It can digest bone, hoof and even horn, leaving very little behind. That's why its droppings often turn chalky white, packed with calcium from all the bone.

Hyenas also have tough stomachs. They can eat meat that would make most animals sick. By clearing away carcasses, they help keep disease down on the savanna, much like vultures do in India.

Who's the real thief

In the 1960s, the scientist Hans Kruuk spent years watching spotted hyenas in the Serengeti and Ngorongoro, often at night. He found that hyenas killed most of their own food. Lions, arriving later, often drove them off and ate it.

Hyenas can run at about 60 km/h and keep going for kilometres. They hunt in groups, and a clan can finish a zebra in under half an hour. Fights between hyenas and lions over a kill are common, and the winner is usually whichever side has more animals there.

Queens of the clan

Spotted hyenas live in clans that can number up to about 80, though they often hunt in smaller groups. Females are larger than males and in charge. The top female rules, her cubs rank next, and males that join from other clans sit at the bottom.

Their famous "laugh" is not about anything funny. It is a high giggle hyenas make when they are excited, frustrated or being pushed away from food by a stronger hyena. Scientists have found that the sound carries information about the hyena's age and rank. Hyenas also whoop, a loud call that can be heard several kilometres away, to call clan members.

India's striped hyena

The striped hyena lives across dry parts of India, from the scrublands of Rajasthan and Gujarat to the Deccan. It is smaller than the spotted hyena, with grey fur, black stripes and a shaggy mane along its back that stands up when it is frightened, making it look bigger.

It mostly comes out at night, travelling long distances alone in search of carcasses. Near villages it may eat livestock remains and rubbish. People rarely see it, and old stories made it a creature of fear, though it almost never attacks humans.

It shares some of the same scrublands as India's wolves, and both struggle as grasslands are ploughed up or built over.

The city that feeds hyenas

In the old walled city of Harar, in Ethiopia, hyenas have wandered the streets at night for centuries. People there leave out scraps, and for decades some families have fed wild hyenas by hand after dark, calling them by name.

The hyenas help clean the city of food waste, and many residents see them as good neighbours. The smallest hyena of all, the aardwolf, would not be interested in those scraps. It eats almost nothing but termites, lapping up many thousands of them a night with a long, sticky tongue.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Hyenas are a kind of wild dog.

Truth: Hyenas look a bit like dogs, but they belong to their own family. Their closest living relatives are mongooses, civets and cats.
