What are jet streams?

About 10 km above your head, rivers of wind race around the planet as fast as a bullet train.

The short answer

Jet streams are narrow bands of very fast wind, high in the sky, that wind their way around the planet. They blow roughly 9 to 16 km up, near the height where passenger jets fly, often at 150 to 300 km/h and sometimes faster.

They form where cold air from the poles meets warm air from the tropics. The big difference in temperature creates strong winds high up. Earth's spin then bends those winds so they blow mostly from west to east. There are two main jet streams in each half of the world: a polar jet and a subtropical jet.

Surprising but true

In February 2020, a passenger jet riding a strong jet stream flew from New York to London in under five hours, more than an hour faster than usual.

Rivers in the sky

Jet streams are not straight lines. They snake around the globe in great loops and waves, a few hundred kilometres wide but only a few kilometres deep. They shift north and south with the seasons and from week to week.

Their path matters for weather. Storms and rain systems tend to form and travel along them. When the jet stream loops far south, it can drag icy polar air with it. When it loops north, warm air follows. If a loop gets stuck in one place, the same weather can sit over a region for days, bringing heat waves or floods.

Discovered by balloon

In the 1920s, a Japanese weather scientist named Wasaburo Oishi released more than a thousand small balloons from an observatory north-east of Tokyo and tracked them with a telescope. He found strong winds blowing steadily from the west, high above Japan. He published his results in Esperanto, an invented language, and few scientists elsewhere read them.

The winds became famous during the Second World War. American bomber pilots flying high over Japan found themselves battling headwinds so strong that their planes almost stood still. Weather scientists soon named these winds jet streams.

How do computers forecast the weather?In 1922, a scientist dreamed of a giant hall where 64,000 people with pencils would calculate tomorrow's weather. Today one supercomputer does far more, in minutes.

Free speed for planes

Airlines plan routes around jet streams every day. Flying east across the North Atlantic, planes ride the wind as a tailwind. Flying west, they avoid its strongest part. That's why a flight from New York to London is usually shorter than the trip back.

In February 2020, during a powerful winter storm, a British Airways jumbo jet caught an unusually fast jet stream. It flew from New York to London in 4 hours and 56 minutes, more than an hour faster than normal, a record for a regular passenger jet.

Balloonists use jet streams too. In 1999, the first nonstop balloon flight around the world rode them for much of its journey.

Jet streams and the monsoon

In winter, a subtropical jet stream flows over northern India and the Himalaya. It steers weather systems called western disturbances, which bring winter rain to the plains and snow to the mountains.

As summer comes, this jet shifts north of the Himalaya and the Tibetan Plateau. That shift is one of the signals that the monsoon is ready to arrive. During the monsoon, a different jet appears, blowing from the east, high above southern India. Weather scientists at the India Meteorological Department watch both closely.

A changing pattern

The jet streams are driven by the temperature difference between the poles and the tropics. The Arctic is warming faster than most of the planet, which shrinks that difference.

Some scientists think this is making the polar jet stream slower and wavier, so weather gets stuck more often. Others say the evidence is not yet clear. Computers that make weather forecasts and track climate change are helping to test both ideas.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Jet streams are made by jet planes.

Truth: Jet streams are natural winds, named because they are narrow, fast streams of air, like a jet of water from a hose. They were blowing long before planes existed. Pilots simply use them, or avoid them.
