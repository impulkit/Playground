How can technology clean water?

A clear plastic bottle of water left in strong sunshine for about six hours can kill most of the germs inside, and the World Health Organization recommends it.

The short answer

Dirty water can carry three kinds of trouble: bits of dirt you can see, germs you can't, and dissolved chemicals such as salt, arsenic or lead. Different technologies tackle different problems, so treatment plants and home purifiers often use several steps one after another.

Filters strain out dirt. Boiling, chlorine and ultraviolet light kill germs. Reverse osmosis forces water through a sheet with holes so tiny that salts and most chemicals stay behind. Used well, these tools turn river water, groundwater and even sea water into water that is safe to drink.

Surprising but true

Chennai turns sea water into drinking water. Its desalination plants, the first of which opened in 2010, push sea water through filters so fine that salt can't pass.

Why clean water matters

Before people understood germs, dirty water spread diseases like cholera in cities everywhere. In 1854, a London doctor named John Snow traced one cholera outbreak to a single water pump. His work helped show that diseases could travel in water, and cities began building proper water systems and sewers.

Today, according to the WHO and UNICEF, about 2.1 billion people still lacked safely managed drinking water at home in 2024, about one in four people on Earth. Clean water is one of the Sustainable Development Goals the world has set for 2030.

Which diseases changed history?Mosquitoes helped stop France from building the Panama Canal. A few years later, the USA finished it by fighting the mosquitoes first.

Step by step at the treatment plant

A big city's water plant works like a series of sieves and shields. First, chemicals are added that make tiny dirt particles clump together. The clumps sink to the bottom of big tanks. Next, the water trickles through deep beds of sand and gravel, which catch what is left.

Finally, the water is disinfected, usually with chlorine. A small amount stays in the water as it travels through pipes, so germs can't grow on the way to your tap. Jersey City in the United States became the first American city to chlorinate its supply all the time, in 1908, and deaths from typhoid fell sharply wherever chlorine was used.

Purifiers at home

In many Indian homes, a purifier sits on the kitchen wall. Some use ultraviolet (UV) light, which damages the genes of germs so they can't multiply. Others use reverse osmosis, or RO, which also removes dissolved salts. RO is useful where groundwater is salty, but it throws away a lot of water and strips out helpful minerals too. Experts say it isn't needed where tap water is already low in salts. A good water purifier is chosen to match the water you actually have.

Clever, cheap ideas

Not every solution needs electricity. Clay pot filters, fired with sawdust mixed into the clay, leave tiny holes that trap germs. In the SODIS method, people fill clear plastic bottles and leave them in strong sun for about six hours. Ultraviolet rays and heat together kill most germs.

Boiling remains one of the surest methods, which is why doctors advise it during floods. Powdered seeds of the moringa tree, the drumstick tree common in Indian gardens, can make mud clump and sink. Small household filters of ceramic or cloth now cost very little in many countries.

Water from the sea

Coastal cities short of rain are turning to desalination, removing salt from sea water. Chennai opened its first large plant in 2010 and has added more since. In California, a plant near San Diego produces around 190 million litres a day. Desalination uses a lot of energy and pumps very salty water back into the sea, so engineers are working to make it cleaner and cheaper.

Technology is only part of the story. In 2014, the city of Flint in Michigan switched its water supply, and old pipes leaked lead into homes. Good public health also needs well-kept pipes, regular testing, and officials who act when something goes wrong.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If water looks clear, it is safe to drink.

Truth: The germs that cause diseases like cholera and typhoid are far too small to see, and so are chemicals like arsenic or lead. Clear water can still make you ill. Only proper treatment or testing tells you it is safe.

Try this

With an adult, make a simple filter. Cut the bottom off a plastic bottle, turn it upside down, plug the neck with cotton wool, then add layers of clean sand and small gravel. Pour muddy water through and compare it with the muddy water you started with. It looks cleaner, but don't drink it, because a simple filter can't remove germs.
