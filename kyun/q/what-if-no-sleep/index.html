What happens if you never sleep?

In 1963, a 17-year-old in California stayed awake for 11 days for a school science fair. By the end, he was seeing things that weren't there.

The short answer

You can't skip sleep for ever. Your brain will take it, whether you agree or not. After a day or two without sleep, people get grumpy, clumsy and forgetful. After several days, they start falling asleep for a few seconds at a time without noticing, and some begin to see things that aren't there.

Scientists think sleep lets the brain sort memories, repair itself and clear away waste. Nobody has tested how long a person could go with no sleep at all, because it would be dangerous. The best-recorded attempt, by a teenager in 1964, ended after 11 days.

Surprising but true

Frigatebirds can sleep while flying, shutting down one half of their brain at a time for a few seconds, high above the ocean.

The boy who stayed awake

In December 1963, Randy Gardner, a 17-year-old in San Diego, decided to break the record for staying awake for his school science fair. Friends kept him moving with basketball and car rides. A sleep researcher from Stanford University, William Dement, came to watch.

By day four, Randy thought a street sign was a person. Later he struggled to count backwards from 100 and forgot what he was saying halfway through sentences. He stopped at about 264 hours, or 11 days. Then he slept for about 14 hours and recovered.

Guinness World Records later stopped accepting attempts to stay awake, because they are too risky.

What goes wrong, hour by hour

After about 24 hours awake, your reactions slow down as much as if you were very tired after a long journey. Your mood drops.

After two or three days, the brain starts grabbing tiny naps called microsleeps. They last a few seconds. You might be staring at a page and not know you've switched off. This is why tired drivers are so dangerous.

Longer than that, memory gets patchy, speech gets muddled and people may see shapes in the corners of their eyes.

Why do we need sleep?You will spend about a third of your life asleep, and scientists still argue about exactly why.

The night shift inside your head

In 2013, scientists at the University of Rochester in the USA watched the brains of sleeping mice. They found fluid flowing faster between brain cells during sleep, washing out waste chemicals. It's a bit like the cleaning crew who come into a school once everyone has gone home.

Sleep also seems to be when the brain sorts the day. Things you practised, from times tables to a cricket shot, are often better the morning after. Some of this happens during dreams.

Sleep also helps you grow. Much of the growth hormone your body makes each day is released in deep sleep, early in the night. Your immune system works better after a good night too. People who are short of sleep catch colds more easily, which is one reason doctors tell sick children to rest.

Animals that bend the rules

No animal studied has been found to need no sleep at all, but some find clever ways to fit it in. Dolphins rest one half of their brain at a time, so the other half can steer them up to breathe.

Frigatebirds, which glide over the ocean for weeks, take naps of a few seconds while flying. Common swifts may stay in the air for ten months, and scientists believe they sleep on the wing too.

How much do you need?

Doctors in the USA recommend 9 to 12 hours a night for children aged 6 to 12, and 8 to 10 hours for teenagers. Many children in both India and America get less, thanks to early school buses, tuition classes and screens at night.

If you feel tired all the time, it's worth talking to a parent or doctor. By the time you are ten, you have spent around four years of your life asleep. That sounds like a waste, until you find out what your brain was busy doing all that time.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You can train yourself to need much less sleep.

Truth: People who sleep too little often feel used to it, but tests show their attention and memory are still worse. A very small number of people have genes that let them do well on less sleep, but most of us cannot learn it.

Try this

Keep a sleep diary for one week. Each morning, write down when you went to bed, when you woke and how you felt from 1 to 5. At the end of the week, look for a pattern between your hours of sleep and your mood.
