What is India's Samudrayaan mission?

India is building a small submarine called Matsya-6000 to carry three people 6 km down into the Indian Ocean, as deep as about 80 Qutub Minars stacked one on top of another.

The short answer

Samudrayaan is India's plan to send people deep into the ocean. "Samudra" means ocean and "yaan" means vehicle. The mission is building a submersible, a small deep-diving craft, called Matsya-6000, after the fish avatar of Vishnu. It is designed to carry three people down to 6,000 metres below the surface of the Indian Ocean.

The mission is part of India's Deep Ocean Mission, run by the Ministry of Earth Sciences. The craft is being built by the National Institute of Ocean Technology (NIOT) in Chennai, with help from ISRO, the space agency behind Chandrayaan. As of 2026, the first crewed dive to full depth was aimed at about 2027.

Surprising but true

In August 2025, two Indians dived deeper than any Indian before them, reaching about 4,025 m and 5,002 m in a French submersible in the Atlantic, as training for Samudrayaan.

Reasons to go so deep

Six kilometres down, the sea floor is cold, dark and mostly unexplored. Scientists want to study the animals living there, the shape of the sea bed and hot springs called vents, where life feeds on chemicals instead of sunlight.

There is also something valuable lying on the floor of the Central Indian Ocean: potato-sized lumps called polymetallic nodules. They contain metals such as manganese, nickel, cobalt and copper, used in batteries and electronics. India has had permission since 1987 to explore a large patch of sea floor there. Some scientists warn that mining could harm deep-sea life, so studying it carefully first matters.

A ball of titanium

The most important part of Matsya-6000 is a sphere just 2.1 metres wide, about the size of a small car's inside. All three crew members sit inside it. At 6,000 metres, the water pushes on every part of the sphere with about 600 times the pressure of air at sea level. So the walls are made of titanium alloy, a strong, light metal, 80 millimetres thick.

Outside the sphere are batteries, thrusters, lights, cameras and robotic arms for collecting rocks and animals. The crew will look out through small, thick windows. A normal dive is planned to last about 12 hours, with enough air and supplies for up to 96 hours in an emergency.

Sinking and rising

A submersible has to sink, hover and come back up. Matsya-6000 does this by changing its buoyancy, how strongly water pushes it upward. It carries heavy weights to help it sink. At the bottom, it uses thrusters to move around. To return, it drops weights and floats back up to its mother ship.

The craft must also be found again in the open ocean. Like submarines, it uses sound signals underwater, since radio waves don't travel far through seawater.

Step by step

India has tested the mission slowly, much like ISRO tests rockets before letting people fly on Gaganyaan. In 2021, an uncrewed steel sphere was lowered to about 6,000 metres to test equipment. In early 2025, Matsya-6000 completed wet tests in a harbour in Tamil Nadu, with crew inside. Shallow sea trials, to about 500 metres, were planned next.

In August 2025, two Indians trained by diving in the French submersible Nautile in the Atlantic. NIOT scientist Raju Ramesh reached about 4,025 metres, and retired Navy commander Jatinder Pal Singh reached about 5,002 metres, the deepest any Indian had gone.

Joining a small club

Only a handful of countries have built crewed submersibles that can reach 6,000 metres or more, among them the USA, France, Russia, Japan and China. If Matsya-6000 succeeds, India will join them.

The sea covers about 70% of our planet, but as of 2024 only around a quarter of the sea floor had been mapped in detail. Every dive will help fill in those blank spaces, and may reveal creatures nobody has ever seen in the deep sea.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Scientists have already mapped the whole ocean floor in detail.

Truth: As of 2024, only about a quarter of the sea floor had been mapped in high detail. Large parts of the Indian Ocean are still known only from rough satellite estimates.

Try this

Push an empty plastic bottle with its cap screwed on down into a bucket of water. Feel how hard the water pushes it back up. Then let a little water into the bottle and try again. Submarines and submersibles control how they sink and rise by changing how much water and air they carry.
