Why does Greenland look bigger than India on most maps?

On many world maps, Greenland looks huge. It's actually smaller than India.

The short answer

Earth is round, and a map is flat. You can't flatten a ball without stretching some parts. Try it with an orange peel.

A famous map from 1569, the Mercator map, keeps directions correct for sailors but stretches places near the poles. The further from the equator, the bigger things look.

So Greenland, near the North Pole, looks enormous, about as big as Africa. In fact, Africa is roughly 14 times larger, and India is about one and a half times the size of Greenland. Different maps choose different things to get right.

Surprising but true

India is bigger than Greenland, even though most maps show the opposite.

Every map lies a little

A way of turning the round Earth into a flat map is called a projection. There are hundreds of them, and every one distorts something: area, shape, distance or direction.

Some maps keep areas correct but squash shapes. Others keep shapes but stretch areas. Globes are the only maps that get everything right, but you can't fold a globe into your pocket or see the whole world at once.

Maps for sailors

Gerardus Mercator was a Flemish mapmaker. His 1569 map keeps compass directions as straight lines. A sailor could draw a line from port to port, read off the angle and steer by it. That made it valuable for navigation on the spice routes and beyond.

The price was size. To keep directions true, Mercator stretched the map more and more towards the poles. Antarctica looks like a giant white continent stretched along the bottom, and Alaska seems bigger than Mexico, though Mexico is larger.

Checking for yourself

You can test any map. Find the equator, then compare a country near it with one far from it. On Mercator, India looks small beside Russia and Canada. In reality, India is about a fifth the size of Russia, which is still huge, but not the giant gap the map suggests.

Maps also freeze a moment in time. Over millions of years, continents drift, and India itself was once an island far to the south. A map of Earth 10 crore years ago would look strange to us, whichever projection you used.

The map arguments

In the 1970s, a German historian named Arno Peters promoted a map that showed true areas. Africa and South America looked long and thin, but correctly sized. He argued that Mercator's map made Europe and North America seem more important than they were.

Mapmakers pointed out that a similar map had been drawn over a century earlier, and that it badly squashed shapes. The debate got people thinking. In 2017, public schools in Boston, USA, began using the Peters-style map in classrooms.

The compromise maps

Many maps today try to balance things. National Geographic used the Robinson projection for years, then switched to one called the Winkel Tripel in 1998. Neither is perfect, but neither stretches the poles as much as Mercator.

In 2016, a Japanese architect, Hajime Narukawa, won a design award for the AuthaGraph map, which folds the globe into a rectangle with sizes kept fairly true.

Maps on your phone

Online maps use a version of Mercator, because when you zoom in on a street, shapes and angles stay correct. That matters when you are trying to turn left at the right corner. Since 2018, Google Maps on a computer has shown a globe when you zoom all the way out, so Greenland shrinks back to its real size. Places are found using coordinates, latitude and longitude, which work the same on any map.

Myth or fact?

Myth: North belongs at the top of a map, because that is the right way up.

Truth: Putting north at the top is only a custom. Many medieval European maps put east at the top, and the Arab mapmaker al-Idrisi's famous world map of 1154 put south at the top. Space has no up or down.

Try this

With a marker, draw a rough face or a few continents on an orange. Peel it carefully by hand in as few pieces as you can, and try to lay the peel flat on a table. Notice where it tears or gaps open. Every flat world map has to solve this same problem, and each one tears in a different place.
