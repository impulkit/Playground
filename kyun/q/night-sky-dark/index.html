Why is the night sky dark?

If the universe were endless, old and full of stars, every point in the sky should blaze with starlight. It doesn't, and that tells us the universe had a beginning.

The short answer

The night sky is dark because the universe had a beginning, and because it is expanding.

Light travels fast, but not instantly. The universe is about 13.8 billion years old, so light from very distant stars and galaxies simply hasn't had time to reach us. We only see stars in a limited bubble around us, and in between them, the sky looks black. On top of that, the universe is stretching. Light from the farthest galaxies gets stretched as it travels, shifting it into colours our eyes can't see, and making it fainter.

This simple question, why is it dark at night, turns out to be one of the clues that the universe isn't eternal.

Surprising but true

The space between the stars does glow, faintly, in every direction. The glow is the leftover heat of the Big Bang, stretched into microwaves that our eyes can't see.

A puzzle older than telescopes

Picture standing in a huge forest. If the forest goes on far enough, every direction you look ends at a tree trunk. You can't see past them.

Astronomers in the 1600s and 1700s realised the same should be true of stars. If space went on for ever, filled evenly with stars, and had always existed, then every line of sight would eventually end on the surface of a star. The whole sky would be as bright as the Sun's surface, day and night. The German astronomer Heinrich Olbers wrote about this in 1823, and the puzzle is named after him, though others raised it earlier.

Some people suggested that dust between the stars blocks the light. But dust soaking up starlight for long enough would heat up until it glowed as brightly as the stars.

The answer: a beginning

The real answer came slowly. Surprisingly, one of the first people to describe it was the American writer Edgar Allan Poe, in an essay in 1848. He suggested that the sky is dark because light from the most distant stars has not yet had time to arrive.

In the 20th century, astronomers discovered that galaxies are rushing apart and that the universe began with the Big Bang. Light from beyond a certain distance hasn't reached us yet. When we look far away, we look back in time, as explained in light-years. Look far enough and we see back to when there were no stars at all.

A glow we can't see

The sky between the stars isn't completely dark. In 1964 and 1965, two radio astronomers in New Jersey, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, kept picking up a faint hiss with their antenna, coming from every direction. At first they thought it was caused by pigeons nesting in the antenna. They cleaned it out, but the hiss stayed.

It turned out to be the afterglow of the Big Bang, light released when the universe was about 380,000 years old. Over billions of years, the stretching of space has turned it into faint microwaves, a kind of radio wave. Penzias and Wilson won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1978. If your eyes could see microwaves, the whole sky would glow dimly.

Losing the dark

Today many people can't see a properly dark sky at all. Streetlights, signs and buildings throw light upwards, and it scatters in the air, washing out the stars. A 2016 study found that about 80 per cent of the world's people live under light-polluted skies, and that the Milky Way is hidden from more than a third of humanity. In the United States and Europe, the figure for polluted skies is over 99 per cent.

What is life like in Ladakh?Ladakh is a desert, but a freezing one. Its main town is about 3,500 m up, it gets less rain in a year than Mumbai can get in a day, and winter nights fall far below freezing.

Better outdoor lighting can help, such as light bulbs shielded so they shine down, not up. Some places are protected on purpose. In 2022, India declared its first dark sky reserve around the observatory at Hanle in Ladakh, more than 4,000 m above sea level. Villagers there use shaded lights so the stars stay bright.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The sky is dark at night simply because the Sun has set.

Truth: That explains why our side of Earth is in shadow, but not why the sky between the stars is black. In an endless, unchanging universe full of stars, every direction would end on a star and the whole sky would shine. The darkness needs a deeper explanation.

Try this

On a clear night, go outside with an adult and look up through a cardboard tube from a kitchen roll. Count how many stars you see in the circle. Try it in different places, such as a city street, a park and a village. The fewer stars you count, the more light pollution there is.
