Who designs everyday things?

Someone decided the shape of your toothbrush, the buttons on a TV remote and the symbol ₹ on Indian money. Most of the time, you never notice them.

The short answer

Almost everything made by people was designed by someone. Industrial or product designers plan the shape of objects like chairs, kettles, phones and toothbrushes. Graphic designers create logos, signs, posters and the symbols on money. UX designers (UX stands for user experience) work out how apps and websites should work, so they feel easy.

Designers think about how people will use something, what it should be made of, how it can be built cheaply and safely, and how it looks. When design is good, you hardly notice it. When it is bad, you push a door that should be pulled, or lose your way in an app.

Surprising but true

The Indian rupee symbol ₹ was chosen in 2010 from thousands of entries in a public competition, and was designed by D. Udaya Kumar, then a young design student.

Objects that explain themselves

In 1988 the American scientist Don Norman wrote a book now known as The Design of Everyday Things. Its first edition had a slightly different title, The Psychology of Everyday Things. His big idea was that objects should show you how to use them, without needing instructions.

A button that sticks up says "press me". A handle says "pull me". A flat metal plate on a door says "push here". When an object sends the wrong message, people blame themselves, but Norman said the real fault usually lies with the design. Confusing doors are now jokingly called "Norman doors".

Designed by accident

Some designs we use every day are surprisingly old. The QWERTY layout on keyboards comes from typewriters made in the United States in the 1870s. One popular explanation is that it spread out common letter pairs so the metal arms inside would not jam. Even now, phones and laptops use it, simply because so many people learned it. You can read more in Why are keyboard letters in such a strange order?.

Other everyday objects, like the zip, took many inventors and many years to get right.

How does a zip work?Two rows of tiny teeth, a little sliding wedge, and a problem that took inventors about twenty years to solve.

Designing for everyone

Good designers try to make things that work for as many people as possible, including children, older people and people with disabilities. This is called universal or inclusive design.

Kerb ramps at street corners were made for wheelchair users, but they also help people with prams, suitcases and bicycles. Large, soft-grip kitchen tools were first designed for people with stiff hands, and many others found them easier too. Ideas like these come up again and again when people think about how the world can work better for everyone.

A school for Indian design

In 1958 the Government of India invited two famous American designers, Charles and Ray Eames, to travel around India and suggest how to train designers. They admired the humble lota, the round brass water pot, saying its shape had been perfected over centuries of use.

Their report led to the National Institute of Design in Ahmedabad, founded in 1961. Its graduates now design everything from buses to banknotes.

A new symbol for money

Until 2010, the Indian rupee had no symbol of its own. The government held a competition, and thousands of people sent in ideas. The winner was D. Udaya Kumar, then a postgraduate student at IIT Bombay.

His ₹ blends the Devanagari letter "र" (ra) with the Roman letter "R". The two lines across the top suggest India's flag, and the gap between the top line and the letter below hints at the equals sign, standing for balance. It now appears on banknotes, coins, price tags and phone keyboards everywhere.

Designing what you can't touch

Today many designers work on screens. They decide where buttons go in an app, what happens when you tap, and how to show information clearly. They test early versions with real users and change what confuses them, the same way app makers test new features. A good app, like a good door handle, should make you feel clever without ever explaining itself.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Design is just about making things look pretty.

Truth: Looks matter, but most design is about how something works, how easy it is to use and how safe it is. A well-designed object often feels so natural that you don't notice the design at all.

Try this

Pick one everyday object at home, such as a water bottle, a school bag or a TV remote. Write down three things that are annoying about it. Then draw a better version that fixes them, and test your idea by asking a family member how they would use it.
