How does a tabla make so many sounds?

A pair of small drums can ring like a bell, thump like a heartbeat and swoop like a voice. The secret is a black circle.

The short answer

A tabla is a pair of hand drums. The smaller, wooden drum on the right, the dayan, plays crisp, ringing notes. The bigger drum on the left, the bayan, made of metal or clay, plays deep, booming sounds.

Both drums have a black circle on their skin called the syahi. This heavy paste makes the skin vibrate in a special way, so the drum produces a clear musical note instead of a dull thud. A player strikes different parts of the skin with different fingers, and each spot makes its own sound. Mix them, and the tabla seems to have a hundred voices.

Surprising but true

In 1920, the Indian physicist C.V. Raman showed that the black paste on Indian drums makes them ring with musical notes, which most drums in the world cannot do.

The black circle

The syahi is made by mixing iron filings, soot or charcoal, flour and a sticky gum into a paste. A craftsman spreads it on in many thin layers, polishing each one with a smooth stone. It can take days.

In 1920, the physicist C.V. Raman and a colleague studied Indian drums like the tabla and mridangam. Most drums vibrate in a messy way that sounds like a thump. Raman showed that the weighted centre makes the extra vibrations line up neatly, like the notes on a string. That is why a tabla can be tuned to match a singer.

Speaking in syllables

Tabla players learn their sounds as spoken syllables called bols. "Na" is a sharp ring from the rim of the right drum. "Ge" is a deep boom on the left. "Dha" is both together. Students recite whole patterns aloud before they ever play them.

Rhythm patterns follow cycles called taal. The most common, teentaal, has 16 beats grouped as 4 + 4 + 4 + 4. The first beat, sam, is the strongest, and musicians often finish a dazzling passage exactly on it. You can learn to clap teentaal in rhythm.

Bending a note

The left drum can do something surprising. By pressing the heel of the hand into the skin and sliding it, a player makes the pitch swoop up or down, a bit like a voice saying "whoa". This sliding sound gives the tabla its playful, talking quality.

The right drum is tuned to a particular note, usually the main note of the song. Players tap small wooden blocks under the leather straps with a little hammer to tighten or loosen the skin until it matches.

Where it came from

A favourite story says the tabla was invented in the 1200s by Amir Khusrau, a poet at the court of Delhi. Historians doubt it. No written record from his time mentions the instrument. The tabla probably grew from the older pakhawaj and other drums, and became popular in the 1700s as lighter music suited it better.

Different teaching families, called gharanas, grew up in Delhi, Lucknow, Banaras, Punjab and elsewhere. Each has its own favourite strokes and pieces, passed down from teacher to student.

Tabla around the world

The tabla accompanies singers, sitar players and dancers of Kathak. It is also heard in countless Hindi film songs.

Ustad Alla Rakha toured the world with the sitar player Ravi Shankar in the 1960s. His son, Zakir Hussain, became one of the most famous tabla players ever. He lived for many years in California, played with musicians from jazz to rock, and won several Grammy awards before his death in 2024. Thanks to them, children in San Francisco can now learn the same bols as children in Kolkata.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The poet Amir Khusrau invented the tabla in the 1200s.

Truth: This is a popular story, but no writing from his time mentions the tabla. Most historians think it developed in the 1700s, partly from an older barrel drum called the pakhawaj.

Try this

Stretch a piece of balloon tightly over the mouth of an empty tin and fix it with a rubber band. Tap the centre and then near the edge. Now stick a small blob of play dough in the middle and tap again. Listen for how the extra weight changes the sound.
