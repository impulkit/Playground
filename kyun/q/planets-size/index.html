How big are the planets?

Line them up. Earth isn't the big one.

The short answer

The planets come in two very different sizes. The four inner planets, Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars, are small and rocky. The four outer planets, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune, are giants made mostly of gas, liquid and ice.

Jupiter is by far the biggest. It is about 11 times as wide as Earth, and about 1,300 Earths could fit inside it. All the other planets put together would fit inside it too. Mercury is the smallest, a little more than a third as wide as Earth. Earth is the biggest of the rocky planets, but next to the giants it is tiny.

Surprising but true

Saturn would float in water, if you could find a bathtub big enough.

The rocky four

Mercury, the closest to the Sun, is barely bigger than our Moon. Venus is almost Earth's twin in size, only about 5% narrower, but its thick air makes it the hottest planet. Mars is about half as wide as Earth, yet it has the tallest volcano known in the solar system. Olympus Mons rises about two and a half times higher than Mount Everest.

All four have solid surfaces you could stand on, with mountains, valleys and craters. In old Indian astronomy, Mars is called Mangal, Venus is Shukra and Mercury is Budh. Some days of the week in Hindi are named after them, like Mangalvar for Tuesday.

The giants

Beyond Mars and the asteroid belt lie the giants. Jupiter and Saturn are mostly hydrogen and helium, the same gases that make up the Sun. Neither has a solid surface to land on. Jupiter's Great Red Spot is a storm wider than Earth that has been swirling for at least 150 years. Go down into their clouds and the gas just gets thicker and hotter.

Saturn is the second-biggest planet, about nine times as wide as Earth. Yet it is so light for its size that, on average, it is less dense than water. That's where the bathtub joke comes from. Like a huge steel ship that floats, what matters is how heavy something is for its size.

Uranus and Neptune are about four times as wide as Earth. They contain more water, ammonia and methane ice, so they are sometimes called the ice giants.

How small is an atom?Line up about a million atoms side by side and they would only just stretch across a single human hair.

Bigger than a planet

Size alone doesn't make something a planet. Jupiter's moon Ganymede and Saturn's moon Titan are both wider than the planet Mercury. Ganymede is the largest moon in the solar system.

Pluto, on the other hand, is smaller than our own Moon, which is one reason astronomers rethought what counts as a planet.

Scale and distance

The hardest thing to picture is how far apart the planets are. Suppose the Sun were a football. Earth would be a peppercorn about 24 m away. Jupiter would be a large marble about 120 m away. Neptune, the farthest planet, would be a small bead more than 700 m away, the length of a long walk through a park.

At that scale, the nearest star would be another football thousands of kilometres away. Space is mostly empty.

And the Sun?

Even Jupiter is small next to the Sun. The Sun is about 109 times as wide as Earth and holds more than 99% of all the mass in the solar system. Everything else, every planet, moon, asteroid and comet, is crumbs left over from the cloud that formed it about 4.6 billion years ago.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The planets are fairly close together, like the pictures in books.

Truth: Book pictures squash the planets together so they fit on a page. Real distances are huge compared with the planets' sizes. Most of the solar system is empty space.

Try this

Make a pocket solar system. Use a football (about 22 cm wide) for the Sun. Earth is then a peppercorn or a mustard seed about 2 mm wide, which you place about 24 m away, roughly the length of a cricket pitch plus a bit more. Jupiter, about the size of a large marble, would sit more than 120 m away.
