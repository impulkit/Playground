What is zardozi embroidery?

Some royal robes in Mughal India were so thick with gold thread that they could be heavy to wear, and their glitter lit up a candlelit court.

The short answer

Zardozi is a kind of heavy embroidery made with metal thread. The name comes from Persian: zar means gold and dozi means embroidery. Artisans stitch shining wire, sequins, beads and tiny coiled springs onto silk or velvet to make raised patterns that glitter like jewellery.

It was a favourite of the Mughal emperors, who wore it on robes, turbans and slippers, and used it on tents, cushions and the saddles of their horses. Today zardozi is used most on wedding clothes: lehengas, sherwanis, dupattas and bridal bags. Lucknow, Bareilly, Agra, Varanasi and Bhopal are among its biggest centres.

Surprising but true

The "gold" in zardozi is metal drawn into a wire thinner than a hair, then wound around silk thread or coiled into tiny springs.

Gold you can stitch

Metal can't be pulled through cloth like cotton thread. So zardozi workers use metal in clever shapes. Gold or silver is drawn through smaller and smaller holes until it becomes a wire finer than a hair. Some of it is wound around a silk core to make a thread called kasab.

Other wire is coiled tightly into hollow springs called salma and dabka. Workers cut these into tiny pieces and stitch them down like beads. Add little metal discs called sitara (stars) and glass beads, and a flat cloth becomes a raised, shining surface. Gold is so soft that a single gram can be drawn into a wire more than two kilometres long. Because metal work is heavy and scratchy, zardozi is usually stitched onto strong fabrics like velvet, satin or thick silk that can bear the weight.

Working on the frame

The cloth is stretched tight on a wooden frame called an adda, low enough that workers sit cross-legged around it. Several people often work on one piece at once. They use a regular needle and an ari, a hooked needle that pulls thread up through the fabric quickly.

The design is first printed onto the cloth using a perforated paper and powder. Then the workers fill it in, often a flower, a vine or a paisley, building up layers. A heavy bridal lehenga can take a team weeks or even months.

The Mughal golden age

Gold embroidery is old in India. Ancient texts describe garments worked with gold. But zardozi reached its peak in the 1500s and 1600s, when emperors like Akbar ran royal workshops full of craftspeople. Robes, canopies and even elephant coverings were covered in gold and silver.

When royal money dried up in the 1700s, many artisans lost their patrons. Some moved to the courts of nawabs and other rulers, including Lucknow and Hyderabad. The craft survived, but it became smaller and quieter for a long time.

Lucknow and its needles

Lucknow is famous for two embroideries. One is delicate white chikankari, stitched in white thread on light cotton. The other is glittering zardozi. In 2013, Lucknow zardozi received a GI tag, a legal label linking the craft to the region around the city.

Tens of thousands of families in and around Lucknow, Bareilly and nearby towns work in zardozi, many from home. Much of the work is done by men, though more women now work too.

From weddings to uniforms

Most zardozi now goes into Indian weddings, where a gold-heavy outfit is a family tradition. It also turns up on film costumes and fashion runways in Paris and New York. Some military badges and crests worn on uniforms around the world are stitched with the same kind of metal thread work.

And the strangest part? All the gold ever mined, more than 200,000 tonnes by 2024, would fit in a cube about 22 metres on each side. Every gold thread in a royal robe was a sliver of something very rare.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Zardozi is the same as zari.

Truth: They are related but different. Zari is the metallic thread itself, often woven into fabric like the borders of Banarasi saris. Zardozi is heavy embroidery stitched on top of cloth with that thread plus sequins, beads and coiled wire.

Try this

Draw a simple flower or paisley shape on a piece of cardboard. Glue on bits of shiny foil, sequins or old bindis along the outline, then fill the inside with small loops of golden wool or string. Hold it under a lamp and tilt it to see how raised shapes catch the light.
