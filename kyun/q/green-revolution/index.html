How did India grow enough food?

In the 1960s India depended on shiploads of foreign wheat. Within about a decade it was growing enough to feed itself.

The short answer

In the mid-1960s, India could not grow enough grain for its people. Two years of failed monsoons, in 1965 and 1966, made things worse, and the country relied on wheat shipped from the USA.

The answer was a set of changes called the Green Revolution. Indian scientists and farmers adopted new high-yielding seeds of wheat and later rice, which produced far more grain per field. With these came more irrigation, chemical fertilisers and pesticides. Harvests jumped, especially in Punjab and Haryana. By the late 1970s, India no longer needed food imports to survive. It came at a price, though, which farmers are still dealing with.

Surprising but true

In 1966, India imported about 18,000 tonnes of a new Mexican wheat seed, one of the largest seed shipments in history at the time.

Ship to mouth

After independence, India's population grew quickly, but farm yields stayed low. Under an American programme, the USA sent huge amounts of wheat to India. In the worst years, grain was unloaded at ports and sent almost straight to ration shops. People called it living "from ship to mouth".

Prime Minister Lal Bahadur Shastri asked Indians to skip one meal a week to save grain. His slogan "Jai Jawan, Jai Kisan", "Hail the soldier, hail the farmer", honoured both.

Short wheat from Mexico

In Mexico, American scientist Norman Borlaug had bred new wheat varieties. They were short, resisted disease and gave much more grain when watered and fertilised well. He later won the Nobel Peace Prize for his work against hunger.

The Indian plant scientist M.S. Swaminathan saw what these seeds could do. He invited Borlaug to India in 1963, and trials began on test plots. Agriculture Minister C. Subramaniam pushed for change. In 1966, India imported about 18,000 tonnes of Mexican seed.

Indian scientists then crossed the new wheat with local varieties to suit Indian tastes and soil. Some Mexican wheat had reddish grain, and Indian breeders developed amber-coloured types that made better rotis.

Fields of gold

Punjab and Haryana had flat land, canals and many farmers who could afford tube wells and fertiliser. They adopted the new seeds quickly. India's wheat harvest nearly doubled between 1965 and 1971.

In 1966 a new rice variety, IR8, arrived from a research centre in the Philippines. It was nicknamed "miracle rice" for its harvests. Rice production rose too, a little more slowly.

The government built a system to buy grain from farmers at a guaranteed price and store it for ration shops. That system still feeds hundreds of millions of people today.

The costs

The new seeds needed lots of water. Punjab's farmers dug ever deeper tube wells, and groundwater in many districts is now falling year after year. Heavy use of fertiliser and pesticides has damaged soil and polluted water in some places.

Farmers with more land and money gained most at first. Many farms grew wheat and rice every year, and crops like millets and pulses, which need less water and are very nutritious, were planted less.

Scientists, including Swaminathan himself, called for an "evergreen revolution", one that keeps harvests high while protecting soil, water and farmers' incomes.

Feeding a billion

Today India grows more than 10 crore tonnes of wheat a year and is one of the world's biggest rice exporters. Swaminathan died in 2023 and was awarded the Bharat Ratna in 2024. In the same years, India was looking again at millets, the old grains the Green Revolution had pushed aside.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Green Revolution was all good, with no downside.

Truth: It ended the threat of famine, but in places like Punjab, heavy use of tube wells has made groundwater levels fall fast, and overuse of fertilisers and pesticides has harmed soil and water. Many farmers also switched away from nutritious crops like millets and pulses.

Try this

Plant a few wheat or moong seeds in two cups of soil. Water both the same, but put one in a sunny window and one in a dim corner. After two weeks, compare their height and colour. Farmers have to think about light, water and soil for every field.
