How did people walk on the Moon?

In July 1969, two astronauts stepped onto the Moon. Their footprints are still there.

The short answer

On 20 July 1969, NASA's Apollo 11 mission landed on the Moon. Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin climbed down from their landing craft, the Eagle, while Michael Collins circled above. Armstrong's first words were: "That's one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind."

Between 1969 and 1972, twelve astronauts walked on the Moon. Nobody has been back since.

Getting there took the most powerful rocket ever flown at the time, a spacecraft in three parts, and the work of around 400,000 engineers, scientists and factory workers across the United States.

Surprising but true

Footprints left on the Moon could last millions of years. There is no wind or rain there to wipe them away.

A race to the Moon

In April 1961, the Soviet Union sent Yuri Gagarin into space, the first person ever to go there. The United States was badly shaken. A few weeks later, President John F. Kennedy told the US Congress that America should land a man on the Moon and return him safely before the decade was out.

It was a huge gamble. At the time, the US had sent just one astronaut into space, on a 15-minute hop. The Moon landing became the biggest prize of the space race between the two countries.

What was the space race?In just twelve years, two rival countries went from launching a beeping metal ball to putting people on the Moon.

Getting there

They rode the Saturn V, a rocket about 110 m tall, taller than a 30-storey building. Apollo 11 lifted off from Florida on 16 July 1969. It took about three days to travel about 384,000 km.

The spacecraft had three parts. The command module, called Columbia, was the cone-shaped cabin where the crew lived and which would bring them home. The service module behind it carried the engine, air and power. The lunar module, Eagle, was the spidery lander. It carried two astronauts down, while one stayed in orbit.

A nervous landing

The landing almost went wrong. As Eagle descended, its computer kept flashing alarms because it was overloaded. The Apollo guidance computer had a tiny fraction of the memory of a cheap mobile phone today. Engineers in Houston quickly decided the alarms were safe to ignore.

Then Armstrong saw that the computer was steering them towards a field of boulders. He took manual control and flew the lander further on, looking for a flat spot. When he touched down in the Sea of Tranquillity, Eagle had less than a minute of landing fuel left. "Houston, Tranquility Base here," he radioed. "The Eagle has landed."

Hundreds of millions of people around the world watched the grainy black-and-white pictures of the first steps on television. Armstrong and Aldrin spent about two and a half hours outside, collecting rocks, setting up experiments and planting a US flag.

What they brought back

The Apollo missions brought back 382 kg of Moon rock. Those rocks helped scientists work out how the Moon formed. Some samples are still kept sealed, waiting for better instruments.

On Apollo 15, in 1971, astronaut David Scott held up a hammer and a falcon feather and let them go together. With no air to slow the feather, they hit the ground at the same moment, just as Galileo had predicted falling objects would. The last three missions also took an electric buggy, the Lunar Roving Vehicle, to drive across the surface.

Not every mission landed. In 1970, an oxygen tank exploded on Apollo 13 on the way to the Moon. The crew swung around the Moon and made it safely home in a damaged spacecraft.

Back to the Moon

Countries are returning. In 2023, India's Chandrayaan-3 landed near the Moon's south pole, where no one had landed before.

NASA's Artemis programme plans to send astronauts back to the Moon, and China has said it aims to land its own astronauts there by 2030. When they arrive, the Apollo footprints will still be waiting. So will the initials of his daughter that Eugene Cernan, the last astronaut to climb back into his lander, traced in the dust in December 1972.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Moon landings were faked in a film studio.

Truth: Scientists around the world have studied the 382 kg of Moon rock, which is different from any rock on Earth. Mirrors left by the astronauts still reflect laser beams fired from observatories today, and a NASA spacecraft has photographed the landing sites, with equipment and tracks still visible.

Try this

Hold a flat sheet of paper and a coin at the same height and drop them together. The paper flutters down slowly. Now crumple the paper into a tight ball and try again. They land almost together. On the Moon, with no air in the way, even the flat sheet would fall as fast as the coin.
