What is the Antikythera mechanism?

In 1901, divers pulled a lump of corroded bronze from a Greek shipwreck that sponge divers had found the year before. Inside were gears from a 2,000-year-old machine that predicted eclipses.

The short answer

The Antikythera mechanism is a hand-cranked machine made in ancient Greece, probably more than 2,100 years ago. Inside a wooden box about the size of a shoebox were at least 30 interlocking bronze gears. Turning a handle moved pointers on dials that showed the positions of the Sun and Moon, the phases of the Moon, and when eclipses would happen.

It is often called the world's oldest known analogue computer. Not a computer with a screen, but a machine that calculated by turning one movement into another through gears. It was found in a shipwreck in 1901, and scientists are still working out all its secrets.

Surprising but true

Nothing as complicated as the Antikythera mechanism is known from anywhere in the world for more than a thousand years after it was made.

Found by sponge divers

In 1900 a group of Greek sponge divers sheltered from a storm near the small island of Antikythera, between Crete and the Greek mainland. Diving, they found a shipwreck full of bronze and marble statues. Over the next year, they and the Greek navy brought up statues, pottery and coins.

What can shipwrecks teach us?A shipwreck is a moment frozen in time, with dinner plates, tools and cargo left exactly where they were on the day it sank.

Among the finds was a shapeless lump of corroded bronze and wood. In 1902 an archaeologist, Valerios Stais, noticed a gear wheel inside. At first, many people thought it must be a later object that had fallen into the wreck. Nothing like it was known from the ancient world.

Peering inside

For decades the fragments were hard to study. The British scientist Derek de Solla Price spent years on them, and in 1974 published a book, Gears from the Greeks, using early X-ray pictures.

In 2005 a team of scientists brought powerful new X-ray scanners and special cameras to the museum in Athens. The 3D scans showed gears hidden inside the lumps and thousands of tiny Greek letters engraved on the surfaces, a kind of instruction manual. Their results, published in 2006, changed what we knew about the machine.

What it could do

The front had a big dial showing the zodiac and the 365-day Egyptian calendar. Pointers showed where the Sun and Moon were in the sky. A small ball, half silver and half dark, turned to show the Moon's phase.

The back had two spiral dials. One tracked a 19-year cycle that matches the Moon's months to the solar year. The other predicted eclipses over an 18-year cycle, marking the month and even the hour. Another small dial showed the four-year cycle of athletic games, including the Olympics.

One clever part used a pin and slot between two gears to make the Moon's pointer speed up and slow down. This copied the way the real Moon moves faster at some points in its orbit. The inscriptions suggest it also showed the five planets visible to the naked eye, though those gears are lost.

Who made it?

Nobody knows. Some researchers link it to astronomers on the island of Rhodes, such as Hipparchus. Others suggest workshops in Corinth or its colonies, perhaps following a tradition going back to Archimedes. The Roman writer Cicero described a bronze sphere made by Archimedes that showed the movements of the Sun, Moon and planets, so similar machines may once have existed. We just haven't found any.

Lost and found again

Gear machines this complicated don't appear again in the records for more than a thousand years, until astronomical clocks were built in the Islamic world and in medieval Europe. Why the knowledge was lost is a mystery. Perhaps only a few were ever made, and bronze was often melted down and reused.

Today the fragments are displayed at the National Archaeological Museum in Athens. Scientists and model makers keep building working copies, each one filling in a few more of the missing gears.

Made
probably in the 2nd or early 1st century BCE

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Antikythera mechanism is too advanced for its time, so it must be from aliens or time travellers.

Truth: It is covered in Greek writing, uses Greek astronomy, and fits with ancient writers such as Cicero, who described similar devices. It shows how skilled Greek craftsmen and astronomers were, not that it came from somewhere else.

Try this

Cut two circles from cardboard, one with 10 notches around the edge and one with 20, and pin them so their notches mesh like gears. Turn the small one all the way round once and count how far the big one turns. The Antikythera mechanism used dozens of gear ratios like this to copy the movements of the Sun and Moon.
