How is ghee made?

Ghee is butter with the water cooked out of it, which is why a jar can sit in an Indian kitchen for months without a fridge.

The short answer

Ghee is made by heating butter gently until all its water boils away. Butter is about four fifths fat, but it also holds water and a little milk protein. As it simmers, the water bubbles off and the proteins sink to the bottom and turn golden brown. The cook pours off the clear, golden fat above and strains it. That is ghee.

The browned bits give ghee its nutty smell. Without its water, ghee keeps for months, and it can be heated much hotter than butter without burning. Those two things made it perfect for India's warm kitchens.

Surprising but true

In Ladakh and Tibet, monks carve detailed sculptures out of butter, and some are made for festivals and then left to melt away.

First, make butter

Milk contains tiny drops of fat, each wrapped in a thin skin. Shake or churn cream hard and those skins break. The drops stick together into bigger and bigger clumps until they form butter, leaving a watery liquid called buttermilk.

In many Indian homes, the traditional way is to set milk into curd first and then churn the curd with a wooden churner, a madhani, spun with a rope. The butter that floats up is called makkhan. Making ghee from cultured butter like this gives it a slightly tangy, rich smell.

Cooking out the water

To make ghee, the butter goes into a heavy pan over low heat. It melts, foams and crackles as the water escapes. After a while, the crackling stops. That silence tells the cook the water is gone.

The milk solids at the bottom turn the colour of light toast. Some families save that brown residue and mix it with sugar or atta as a treat. The ghee is then strained through a cloth into a clean jar. When it cools, it often turns grainy and pale, a sign of fat crystals forming.

Holy and everyday

Ghee has been part of Indian life for thousands of years. The hymns of the Rigveda, from the Vedic age, describe ghee being poured into sacred fires. It is still offered in many Hindu rituals and burned in brass lamps.

In stories, the young Krishna is a famous butter thief who steals makkhan from the pots of the cowherd women. During Janmashtami, in Maharashtra and elsewhere, young people form human pyramids to break a pot of curd hung high in the air, a festival called Dahi Handi.

Ghee around the world

India is not the only place that clarifies butter. In Egypt and the Middle East it is called samna. In Ethiopia, cooks make a spiced version called niter kibbeh. French chefs make clarified butter for frying. In North America, ghee has become popular in health food shops in recent years.

Butter in the mountains

In Ladakh and Tibet, yak or cow butter is churned into salty tea for warmth and energy in the cold. Buddhist monks also shape coloured butter into delicate sculptures of flowers and figures, called torma, for prayers and festivals.

Far away, the Iowa State Fair in the USA has displayed a life-size cow carved from butter almost every year since 1911. Whether sculpted in a Himalayan monastery or a Midwestern fair, butter works for art for the same reason: chilled fat holds its shape, then softens with warmth.

Why ghee is good for frying

Butter burns quickly in a hot pan because its milk proteins scorch. Ghee has had those proteins removed, so it can get much hotter before it smokes. That is why it works so well for a tadka of crackling cumin seeds or for frying puris until they puff.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ghee goes off quickly unless you keep it in the fridge.

Truth: Because ghee contains almost no water, it keeps well at room temperature for months if the jar is clean and closed. Families in India have kept it this way for thousands of years.

Try this

Pour some fresh cream into a clean jar until it is about a third full. Screw the lid on tight and shake hard for 10 to 15 minutes, taking turns with friends. First it turns to whipped cream, then suddenly a lump of butter appears in a thin liquid, which is buttermilk.
