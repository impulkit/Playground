What toilets did ancient people use?

In Roman public toilets, people sat side by side on long stone benches with holes, chatting with no walls between them.

The short answer

Ancient people used everything from simple holes and pots to surprisingly clever toilets with running water. About 4,500 years ago, in Indus Valley cities like Mohenjo-daro and Harappa, many houses had a toilet connected to brick drains under the streets. The Romans built public toilets where dozens of people sat side by side, with water flowing below to carry waste away.

Most people in history, though, used chamber pots, pits, fields or rivers. Toilets with pipes and drains were a luxury for cities and the rich. Some of these ancient systems were better than what many European towns had a thousand years later.

Surprising but true

About 4,500 years ago, many homes in Indus Valley cities like Mohenjo-daro had toilets linked to covered drains running under the streets.

The Indus Valley's clean streets

The cities of the Indus Valley, in today's India and Pakistan, were planned with great care for drains. Houses in Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, Lothal and Dholavira often had a bathing area and a toilet. Waste water flowed through clay pipes into covered brick drains along the streets.

The drains had removable covers so workers could clean them. Some houses even had seats made of brick over a pot or a chute. Archaeologists think few ancient cities managed waste this well. You can explore how these cities were laid out in Indus Valley cities.

A village in the far north

On the island of Orkney, off the north of Scotland, lies Skara Brae, a stone village about 5,000 years old. Its houses had drains built under the floors. Some small side rooms may have been toilets, flushed by a nearby stream.

That makes Skara Brae older than the Egyptian pyramids. Its people built stone beds and shelves too, all still standing today.

Rome: toilets with company

Rome had public toilets called latrines. A typical one was a long stone bench with a row of keyhole-shaped holes, and no walls between seats. A channel of water ran underneath to wash waste into the sewers. Another small channel ran at people's feet.

Many historians think Romans cleaned themselves with a tersorium, a sea sponge on a stick, rinsed in that foot channel. Others think the sponge was for cleaning the toilets. The waste went into sewers, including the Cloaca Maxima, Rome's great drain, parts of which are still used today. That city's power and engineering are told in ancient Rome.

How do astronauts go to the toilet?On the International Space Station, yesterday's urine becomes today's drinking water, and the machines now recycle about 98% of it.

China's 2,400-year-old flush

In 2023, archaeologists working at the ruins of an ancient palace near Xi'an, in China, found a toilet with a pipe running outside. It was about 2,400 years old. They believe servants poured water into it to flush it away, making it one of the oldest known flushing toilets.

Elsewhere in ancient China, some toilets sat above pig pens, and the pigs ate the waste. Clay models of these "pig toilets" were placed in tombs.

Castles and chamber pots

Medieval castles in Europe had small toilet rooms called garderobes, built into the outer wall. Waste dropped straight down the side of the castle, often into the moat. Most townspeople used chamber pots and emptied them into the street.

In 1596, an English courtier named Sir John Harington built a flushing toilet for his godmother, Queen Elizabeth I. It didn't catch on. Flush toilets became common only in the late 1800s, once cities built proper sewers.

Toilets today

Clean toilets save lives by keeping germs out of drinking water. Yet in 2022, about 3.5 billion people still lacked a safely managed toilet. India's Swachh Bharat Mission, launched in 2014, built more than 100 million household toilets. The United Nations marks World Toilet Day on 19 November.

From Indus drains to the sewers under your street, the problem has always been the same. Even astronauts have to solve it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Thomas Crapper invented the flush toilet, and the word "crap" comes from his name.

Truth: Flushing toilets existed long before Crapper, a London plumber of the late 1800s. He did improve parts of them and sold many. The word "crap" was used in English before he was famous.
