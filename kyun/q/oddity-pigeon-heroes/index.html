Which pigeons won medals?

In 1918 a wounded pigeon with one leg shot away flew through gunfire with a message that saved about 194 American soldiers.

The short answer

Dozens of pigeons have won medals for bravery, mostly for carrying messages during wars. The most famous is Cher Ami, a pigeon of the US Army in the First World War. In October 1918, though badly wounded, he delivered a message that saved about 194 trapped soldiers.

In the Second World War, Britain's Dickin Medal, a medal for animals, went to 32 pigeons. Some carried news from crashed aircraft at sea. Others flew secrets from behind enemy lines. Pigeons were used because they are fast, reliable, and could fly when radios failed or would have given positions away.

Surprising but true

In 1999, when a super cyclone cut power and phone lines in coastal Odisha, police there still had carrier pigeons, and say the birds helped carry messages.

Cher Ami and the Lost Battalion

In October 1918, more than 500 American soldiers were trapped in the Argonne Forest in France, surrounded by German troops. They became known as the Lost Battalion. Then things got worse: their own side's guns began shelling them by mistake.

Their commander, Major Charles Whittlesey, sent pigeons with messages. The first ones were shot down. The last bird was Cher Ami. His note said, roughly, that American guns were firing on their own men, and begged for them to stop.

Cher Ami was hit as he took off. He lost much of a leg, was hurt in one eye and took a bullet in his chest. Still he flew about 40 km back to his loft in around 25 minutes. The shelling stopped, and about 194 men survived to be rescued.

A wooden leg and a French medal

Army medics saved Cher Ami's life and carved him a small wooden leg. France awarded him the Croix de Guerre, a military medal. He was shipped back to the USA but died in 1919 from his wounds.

His body was preserved and is now kept at the Smithsonian National Museum of American History in Washington.

Pigeons of the Second World War

In 1943 a British charity started the Dickin Medal for animals that showed great bravery in war. In its early years, pigeons won more of them than any other animal.

One of the first winners was Winkie. In 1942 a British bomber crashed into the North Sea. The crew released Winkie, who flew about 190 km home to Scotland, oily and exhausted. From the time of her arrival, rescuers worked out roughly where the plane had come down, and the crew was saved.

An American pigeon, G.I. Joe, carried an urgent message in Italy in 1943. Allied planes were about to bomb a village that British troops had just taken. G.I. Joe's message arrived in time to call off the raid, saving many soldiers and villagers.

How do pigeons find home?

Homing pigeons are bred to fly back to their own loft, even from hundreds of kilometres away. They often travel at 80–100 km/h, faster than a car on most highways.

Scientists think they use several clues at once. They track the Sun, sense Earth's magnetic field, and recognise landmarks near home. Experiments in which tiny magnets were attached to pigeons confused some of them on cloudy days, when they couldn't see the Sun.

Pigeon post in India

People have sent messages by pigeon for thousands of years. Mughal emperors kept pigeon lofts, and so did many Indian kingdoms.

Odisha's police still keep more than 100 Belgian Homer pigeons. Police say the birds were a lifeline at least twice: after floods in 1982 and after the super cyclone of 1999, which cut phone lines along the coast. Today they mostly fly in ceremonies on Independence Day and Republic Day. The modern post office carries most of India's letters now, but Odisha's pigeons are kept, police say, for future generations.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Carrier pigeons can fly a message to any address you choose.

Truth: Homing pigeons only fly back to their own home loft. To send messages, armies carried pigeons away from their lofts in cages, then released them with a note so they would fly home to headquarters.
