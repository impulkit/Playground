How do your eyes see?

The picture at the back of your eye is upside down. Your brain flips it the right way up without you ever noticing.

The short answer

You see because light bounces off things and into your eyes. It passes through the clear front window of the eye, the cornea, and through the black hole in the middle, the pupil. Behind the pupil sits a soft, clear lens that focuses the light into a sharp picture.

That picture lands on the retina, a thin layer at the back of the eye packed with light-sensing cells. They turn light into electrical signals, which travel along the optic nerve to the back of your brain.

Your brain does the rest. It flips the image the right way up, joins the views from both eyes, and works out what you're looking at.

Surprising but true

Each eye has a blind spot where the nerve leaves it, with no light sensors at all. Your brain fills in the gap with a good guess.

A camera made of jelly

An eye works a lot like a camera. The cornea and lens focus light, as a camera's lens does. The iris, the coloured ring, is a muscle that widens or narrows the pupil, letting in more light in a dim room and less in bright sunshine. The retina is like the camera's sensor.

The eyeball is filled with a clear jelly that keeps its round shape. An adult eye is about 2.4 cm across, about the size of a large grape.

To focus on something close, like this screen, tiny muscles change the lens's shape. As people reach their forties, the lens stiffens, which is why many grandparents hold a newspaper at arm's length or wear reading glasses.

Rods and cones

The retina has two kinds of light sensors. About 120 million rods in each eye work in dim light but can't see colour. That's why a garden at night looks grey. About 6 million cones work in brighter light and let you see colours and fine detail.

Cones are packed tightly in a small spot in the centre of the retina. Whatever you look straight at lands there, which is why the middle of your view is sharpest.

The blind spot

Where the optic nerve leaves the eye, there's no room for rods or cones. That patch is blind. You don't notice because your other eye covers it, and your brain paints in the gap using the colours and patterns around it. Try the card trick below to find yours.

Light goes in, not out

Some ancient Greek thinkers believed the eyes sent out rays to touch what they saw. Around 1,000 years ago, the scientist Ibn al-Haytham, working in Cairo, argued from experiments that light travels into the eye instead. His Book of Optics shaped science in the Islamic world and Europe for centuries.

In ancient India, the surgical text linked to Sushruta describes an operation for cataract, when the lens becomes cloudy. Today cataract surgery replaces the cloudy lens with a plastic one, and it's one of the most common operations in both India and the USA.

Eyes that need help

Many children are short-sighted: they see near things clearly but faraway things look blurry, like the board at school. The eyeball has grown a little too long, so light focuses in front of the retina. Glasses fix this by bending the light before it enters. Studies suggest that spending more time playing outdoors in daylight lowers children's chances of becoming short-sighted.

Eyes in the animal world

Owls have huge eyes packed with rods for hunting at night, but they can't move them in their sockets, so they swivel their heads instead. Eagles see fine detail much farther away than we can. Every one of these eyes uses the same basic trick: a lens, a screen of sensors and a brain to make sense of it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Eating lots of carrots gives you super night vision.

Truth: Carrots contain a substance the body turns into vitamin A, which the eyes need to see in dim light. People who lack vitamin A can develop night blindness, so carrots help them. But if you already get enough, extra carrots won't let you see in the dark.

Try this

On a card, draw a small X on the left and a dot on the right, about 8 cm apart. Close your left eye, hold the card at arm's length and stare at the X with your right eye. Slowly bring the card closer. At one point the dot vanishes, because it has landed on your blind spot.
