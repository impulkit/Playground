How does India decide how many MPs each state gets?

Uttar Pradesh sends 80 MPs to the Lok Sabha and Tamil Nadu sends 39. The numbers were last worked out using the 1971 census, and they have been frozen ever since.

The short answer

India decides how many MPs each state gets through a process called delimitation. An independent commission looks at how many people live in each state, shares out the seats, and then draws the borders of each constituency.

The idea is simple: every MP should speak for roughly the same number of people. But since 1976, the number of seats per state has been frozen, still based on the 1971 census.

Surprising but true

India's Lok Sabha seats are still shared out between states using population figures from 1971, more than 50 years ago, even though the country has grown hugely since then.

How it works

Delimitation has two big steps.

  • 1. Share out the seats. The Lok Sabha has a fixed number of seats. These are divided among states by population. A state with more people gets more MPs. Articles 82 and 170 of the Constitution say this should be done after a census.
  • 2. Draw the lines. Inside each state, the land is cut into constituencies, each with about the same number of people. Some seats are kept for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, as the entry on reservation explains.

The job is done by a Delimitation Commission, headed by a judge. Its final orders cannot be challenged in any court.

Think of a school choosing class monitors. If Class 6 has 60 children and Class 7 has 30, it seems fair for Class 6 to have two monitors and Class 7 one. Now imagine Class 6 grows to 90 children but is still allowed only two. That is the puzzle India faces.

Where it came from

After independence, India redrew its seats after each census. The first commission, in 1952, used the 1951 census and set the Lok Sabha at 494 seats. The next, in 1963, raised it to 522. The third, in 1973, used the 1971 census and raised it again. The 1977 election had 542 seats, and every election since 1989 has had 543.

Then came a big change. In 1976, the 42nd amendment froze the number of seats each state had until the 2001 census. The reason was family planning. Some states were working hard to slow population growth. Leaders did not want those states to lose seats for succeeding.

In 2001, the 84th amendment stretched the freeze until the first census after 2026. A commission led by Justice Kuldip Singh did redraw constituency borders using the 2001 census. Those changes came into force in 2008, but no state gained or lost seats.

India's story

Over five decades, India's states grew at very different speeds. Southern states such as Kerala and Tamil Nadu slowed their growth early. States such as Uttar Pradesh and Bihar kept growing faster.

Because seats were frozen, constituency sizes drifted far apart. According to one widely used count, the biggest constituency at one point had more than 30 lakh voters, while the smallest had fewer than 50,000.

In 2023, Parliament passed a law to keep a third of seats in the Lok Sabha and state assemblies for women. But the law said this would start only after the next census and the delimitation that follows. That census had been due in 2021, but it was put off because of COVID-19, so the wait grew longer. In April 2026, the government brought a bill to start the redraw sooner, using the latest published census (from 2011) and raising seats to as many as 850. It failed in the Lok Sabha because it did not get the two-thirds support a change to the Constitution needs.

The numbers that matter

  • - 543: Lok Sabha seats at every election since 1989. Uttar Pradesh has 80 and Tamil Nadu 39.
  • - 454 to 2: the vote in the Lok Sabha on 20 September 2023 that passed the women's reservation law. The idea was first brought to Parliament in 1996.
  • - 298 to 230: the vote on 17 April 2026. A change to the Constitution needs two-thirds of the MPs voting, which that day meant 352 votes, so it failed.
  • - 74: women elected to the Lok Sabha in 2024, about 13.6% of seats. A third of seats would mean about 181.
  • - 80 to 143: how much Uttar Pradesh's seats might grow in a 2019 study by the Carnegie Endowment. The study worked out that, by 2026, the Lok Sabha would need about 848 seats for seats to follow population without any state losing seats.

What people argue about

This is one of India's hardest political questions, and people disagree strongly.

Many southern leaders say sharing seats by today's population would be unfair to states that slowed their population growth through schools, health care and family planning. Tamil Nadu Chief Minister M.K. Stalin said in February 2025 that his state's 39 seats could fall to 31. Many opposition parties also said women's reservation should not be tied to delimitation.

The government says no southern state will lose seats. Home Minister Amit Shah told the Lok Sabha in April 2026 that, under the government's plan, seats in five southern states would rise from 129 to 195. Supporters of redrawing say a voter in a crowded state should not count for less than a voter elsewhere.

Some suggest a middle path: raise every state's seats by the same share, keeping today's balance. Others want a fixed number of seats, like the US House of Representatives. The debate is really about fairness in a federal country: fair to each voter, and fair to each state.

Myths and mix-ups

"The ruling party draws the map." The borders are drawn by an independent commission, not by any party. Parliament decides the bigger rules, such as how many seats there are and which census to use.

"Women's reservation is already in force." The law was passed in 2023, but it only starts after a delimitation. So far, none has happened since then.

"More seats means a bigger state on the map." Seats follow people, not land. A big state with few people can have fewer MPs than a smaller, more crowded one.

What it means for you

Delimitation decides whose voice is heard in Parliament, and how loudly. If your constituency is redrawn, you could find yourself voting with a different set of towns, or for a seat kept for a woman candidate.

It also shapes which states have more say over national laws, budgets and the next government. That is why a quiet-sounding word like delimitation can spark loud arguments.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Delimitation always changes how many MPs a state has.

Truth: Not always. The redraw that came into force in 2008 moved the boundaries of constituencies inside each state, but every state kept the same number of Lok Sabha seats it had before.
