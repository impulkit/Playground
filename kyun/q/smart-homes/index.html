What is a smart home?

Ask a smart speaker to switch off the fan, and your words may travel to a computer hundreds of kilometres away and back in about a second.

The short answer

A smart home is a home where everyday devices are connected to a network, usually Wi-Fi, so they can be controlled from a phone, by voice or automatically. Lights can switch on when you walk in. An air conditioner can cool a room before you get home. A doorbell camera can show who's at the gate while you're at school.

Each smart device has sensors, a tiny computer and a radio to talk to the network. Many also connect to the company's computers far away, called servers, which do the heavy thinking. Smart homes can save effort and energy and help people who find switches hard to reach. They also raise real questions about privacy and safety.

Surprising but true

In 2016, a piece of malicious software called Mirai took control of hundreds of thousands of poorly protected home gadgets, such as cameras and routers, and used them together to jam some of the internet's busiest websites.

An old dream

People have imagined automatic homes for a long time. World fairs in the 1930s and 1950s showed "homes of tomorrow" with push-button kitchens. In the 1970s, a system called X10 let people switch lamps on and off by sending signals through a house's electrical wiring.

The modern smart home took off in the 2010s. Cheap chips, fast Wi-Fi and smartphones arrived together. In 2011, a thermostat that learned a family's habits went on sale in the USA. In 2014, Amazon launched the Echo speaker with its voice assistant, Alexa. Soon there were smart bulbs, plugs, locks, cameras, fridges and robot vacuum cleaners. All of them belong to a bigger trend called the Internet of Things.

How it all connects

Most smart devices follow a simple pattern: sense, decide, act. A motion sensor notices someone in the hallway. A rule, set up in an app, says "if motion after sunset, turn on the light". The bulb switches on.

Voice assistants add speech. When you say "Alexa, turn off the fan", the speaker records the request and sends it to a server. Software there turns the sound into words, works out what you mean and sends a command back to the fan's smart plug. It all happens in a second or two.

How does a voice assistant understand you?When you say "Hey" to a smart speaker, a tiny chip has been listening for that one word all day, ignoring everything else you said.

For years, devices from different companies often refused to work together. In 2022, many big tech firms launched Matter, a shared standard meant to let a bulb from one brand obey a speaker from another.

Who it helps

For someone who uses a wheelchair, or an older person with shaky hands, being able to say "lock the door" or "switch on the lights" can make daily life much easier. Smart smoke alarms can send a warning to a phone, even if nobody is at home.

In India, smart plugs are popular for turning on water heaters before a bath and for scheduling air conditioners. Voice assistants now understand Hindi and several other Indian languages, so you don't need to speak English to use them.

The worries

A home full of microphones and cameras collects a lot of information. Companies may store recordings and data about when you come and go. That makes privacy a big issue, and it's worth checking what each device records.

Security is the other worry. In 2016, a malicious program called Mirai searched the internet for cameras and routers still using factory passwords like "admin". It took over hundreds of thousands of them and used them together to flood websites with traffic, knocking popular services offline for hours.

There's also the question of what happens when a company gives up. In 2022, a US smart-home firm called Insteon shut its servers down with almost no warning, and many customers' lights and switches stopped working through the app overnight. A smart home, it turns out, is only as reliable as the company and the network behind it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Smart speakers record everything you say, all day long.

Truth: They listen locally for their wake word and only start sending audio when they think they hear it. But they do sometimes wake by accident, and companies have used recorded clips to improve their systems, so privacy settings are worth checking.

Try this

Make a list of every device in your home that runs on electricity. Mark which ones connect to the internet or an app. Then, with an adult, check whether the router and any cameras still use the password they came with. Changing default passwords is one of the simplest ways to keep a home safer.
