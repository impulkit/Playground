What did Rosa Parks do?

One evening in 1955, a woman on a bus in Alabama refused to stand up. Her arrest set off a protest that lasted 381 days.

The short answer

Rosa Parks was a Black woman from Montgomery, Alabama. On 1 December 1955 she refused a bus driver's order to give up her seat to a white passenger, and she was arrested.

At the time, Montgomery's laws kept Black and white passengers apart on buses. Her arrest sparked the Montgomery bus boycott. For more than a year, Black residents refused to ride the city's buses. In 1956, the US Supreme Court agreed that bus segregation was against the Constitution, and the boycott ended in victory. Parks became known as "the mother of the civil rights movement".

Surprising but true

The actual bus Rosa Parks rode is now on display in a museum in Michigan, and visitors can sit in it.

How the buses worked

In Montgomery, the front seats of each bus were kept for white passengers. Black passengers, who made up most of the riders, sat at the back. The middle rows were in between. If the white section filled up, the driver could order Black passengers in the middle to stand.

Black passengers often had to pay at the front, get off, and walk around to enter through the back door. Sometimes the bus drove off before they got back on.

The evening of 1 December

Parks worked as a seamstress, sewing and altering clothes at a department store. That evening she boarded the bus home and sat in the first row of the middle section. When more white passengers got on, the driver told her row to move. Three people stood up. Parks did not.

The driver called the police, and she was arrested. She was later fined 10 dollars, plus 4 dollars in court costs.

Not just a tired seamstress

Parks was calm and polite, but her choice was not random. She had been secretary of the local branch of the NAACP, a civil rights group, since 1943. That summer she had attended a training centre for activists in Tennessee.

She had also been thrown off a bus by the same driver, James Blake, twelve years earlier, in 1943.

She was not the first to refuse. Nine months earlier, a 15-year-old named Claudette Colvin had done the same thing on a Montgomery bus. Leaders felt Parks, a respected and well-known member of the community, would be the right person to rally around.

381 days of walking

Within days, Black leaders called a boycott and chose a young minister, Martin Luther King Jr., to lead it. Black residents walked, shared rides and used church-organised cars. The bus company lost most of its passengers.

The boycott held through a year of threats and arrests. In November 1956, the Supreme Court upheld a ruling that bus segregation was illegal. The boycott ended on 20 December 1956.

After the bus

Parks paid a price. She lost her job, received threats and in 1957 moved with her husband to Detroit. There she worked for many years in the office of a US congressman, John Conyers.

She received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1996 and the Congressional Gold Medal in 1999. When she died in 2005, she became the first woman to lie in honour in the US Capitol building.

The bus itself was found rusting in a field and was bought in 2001 by The Henry Ford, a museum in Dearborn, Michigan. After careful restoration, it sits in the museum today. Visitors can climb aboard and sit in the row where she refused to stand.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Rosa Parks refused to move because she was physically tired after work.

Truth: She wrote later that the only tired she was, was tired of giving in. She was an experienced activist who had worked for years with the civil rights group NAACP.
