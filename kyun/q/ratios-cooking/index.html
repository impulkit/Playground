How do ratios help in cooking?

Many cooks remember recipes as ratios, not lists. Two cups of water for one of rice, or one part urad dal to about three or four parts rice for idli batter.

The short answer

A ratio compares two or more amounts. If a recipe uses 2 cups of water for every 1 cup of rice, the ratio of water to rice is 2:1. The real amounts can change, but the ratio stays the same. Two cups of rice need four of water. Half a cup needs one.

That is why ratios are so useful in the kitchen. They let you cook for one person or for twenty, without a new recipe each time. Many experienced cooks don't follow written recipes at all. They carry a handful of ratios in their heads and adjust by look, smell and taste.

Surprising but true

The pound cake got its name from its recipe. It was traditionally made with a pound each of flour, butter, sugar and eggs, a ratio of 1:1:1:1.

Rice, dal and batter

Cooking plain rice is a lesson in ratios. A common starting point is 1 part rice to 2 parts water, but it depends on the rice. Basmati often needs a little less water, and many cooks soak it first. Sticky short-grain rice needs a different amount, and a pressure cooker needs less again, because less water escapes as steam.

In South India, idli and dosa batter starts with a ratio. Families often grind about 1 part urad dal to 3 or 4 parts rice, then leave the batter to ferment overnight. More dal gives softer, fluffier idlis. More rice gives crisper dosas. Each family argues that its own ratio is the right one.

Bakers love ratios

Bakers are especially strict about ratios, because small changes can ruin a cake or loaf. The old pound cake used a pound each of flour, butter, sugar and eggs. Change one and the cake comes out dry, greasy or flat.

Many bread bakers use "baker's percentages". Flour is always 100%, and everything else is measured against it. A simple dough might be 100% flour, 65% water, 2% salt and 1% yeast. So 500 g of flour needs 325 g of water. This way, a baker in Mumbai and a baker in Chicago can share a recipe in any size.

Weighing ingredients is more reliable than using cups. A cup of flour can weigh quite different amounts depending on how tightly it is packed.

Scaling up and down

Suppose your recipe for 4 people uses 200 g of paneer and you need to feed 6. The ratio of new people to old people is 6:4, or 1.5. So you multiply each ingredient by 1.5 and use 300 g of paneer.

But some things don't scale perfectly. Spices and salt are often added more carefully when cooking in large amounts, then adjusted to taste. Cooking time and heat also change. A big pot takes longer to heat through, and a large cake may need a cooler oven so the outside doesn't burn before the middle is cooked. This is where cooking science comes in.

The perfect cup of chai

Even a cup of chai is a ratio problem. One home might use equal parts milk and water, another mostly milk. Then there is the ratio of tea leaves to liquid, and of sugar to everything. Change one and the tea tastes different.

Ratios also explain why lemonade can be too sour or too sweet. A recipe of 1 part lemon juice, 1 part sugar syrup and 4 parts water stays balanced however much you make. Add an extra cup of water without adding juice and you have changed the ratio, and the taste. Drinks factories work the same way, only with tanks instead of jugs.

Quick facts

Ratio
a comparison of amounts, written like 2:1
Plain rice
often about 1 part rice to 2 parts water (varies by rice and pot)
Idli batter
roughly 1 part urad dal to 3 or 4 parts rice
Pound cake
flour, butter, sugar and eggs in equal weights
Bread dough
often about 60–75 g water for every 100 g flour

Myth or fact?

Myth: To make a recipe twice as big, you just double everything, including the cooking time.

Truth: Doubling the ingredients keeps the ratios right, but cooking time does not simply double. A bigger cake may need a lower heat and only a bit longer, and a big pot of rice may need slightly less than double the water, because less steam escapes for each cup.

Try this

With an adult's permission, make three small glasses of a drink such as lemon water or rooh afza, using ratios of 1:2, 1:4 and 1:8 of syrup to water. Taste each and decide which ratio you like best. Then work out how much syrup you'd need to make a whole jug at your favourite ratio.
