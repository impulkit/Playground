How has women's cricket grown?

Women were playing cricket in England more than 270 years ago, and held a World Cup two years before the men did.

The short answer

Women's cricket is very old but has grown fastest in the last ten years. Women played in English villages in the 1700s, and the first Women's World Cup took place in 1973. For decades, though, players paid their own way, played in front of tiny crowds and were barely shown on television.

Since about 2017, that has changed sharply. Big audiences, professional leagues, better pay and television deals have turned women's cricket into a major sport, especially in India, Australia and England.

Surprising but true

The first Cricket World Cup of any kind was the women's World Cup in England in 1973. The first men's World Cup was in 1975.

Cricket in long skirts

The first recorded women's match took place in Surrey, England, in 1745, between two villages. Newspapers reported that the women "bowled, batted, ran and catched" as well as most men.

There is a popular story that round-arm bowling was invented by a woman, Christina Willes, in the early 1800s, because her wide skirt got in the way of underarm bowling. Historians say it is a nice tale but hard to prove.

The first World Cup

The first Women's World Cup was held in England in 1973, two years before the men's. It was organised by the England captain Rachael Heyhoe Flint with support from a businessman, Jack Hayward. England won. By then women had been playing international cricket for almost 40 years: the first women's Test, between Australia and England, was played in Brisbane in December 1934.

Australia has since become the most successful team, winning the 50-over World Cup seven times. Women's Test matches are still played, but rarely, and most international games are one-day or T20 matches.

India's pioneers

The Women's Cricket Association of India was set up in 1973, and India's women played their first Test in 1976, against the West Indies in Bengaluru. Shantha Rangaswamy captained that first Test side. For years players travelled in unreserved train compartments and stayed in cheap lodgings. Things improved after 2006, when the women's association merged into the BCCI, which ran men's cricket and had far more money.

Two players defined the next era. Mithali Raj, a batter from Hyderabad, played international cricket for 23 years and scored more one-day runs than any other woman. Fast bowler Jhulan Goswami, from a small town in West Bengal, took more one-day wickets than anyone else when she retired in 2022.

The 2017 turning point

India's women reached the 2017 World Cup final in England. In the semi-final, Harmanpreet Kaur hit 171 not out against Australia. India then lost the final at Lord's in London to England by just nine runs.

Millions watched in India, many of them seeing women's cricket for the first time. Sponsors, broadcasters and the BCCI took notice.

Pay, leagues and a trophy

In 2022 the BCCI announced that India's women would earn the same match fees as men. Their yearly contracts are still smaller. In 2023 the Women's Premier League began, an IPL-style league with city teams and a player auction. Mumbai Indians won its first season, and several players earned more in one auction than in years of playing before.

In November 2025, India's women won their first 50-over World Cup, beating South Africa in the final at Navi Mumbai. Captain Harmanpreet Kaur's team joined the men's heroes in India's World Cup story. Girls' cricket academies across India report more players than ever, and the women's game now writes fresh chapters of cricket history every season.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Women's cricket is new.

Truth: Women have played cricket since at least 1745, and the first Women's World Cup was held in 1973. What is new is the money, the television coverage and the crowds.
