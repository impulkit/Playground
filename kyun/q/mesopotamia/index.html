What was Mesopotamia?

Every time you check a clock, you use an idea from Mesopotamia, where people counted in sixties more than 4,000 years ago.

The short answer

Mesopotamia was the land between two great rivers, the Tigris and the Euphrates, mostly in what is now Iraq. Its name comes from Greek words meaning "between the rivers". Around 5,000 to 6,000 years ago, people there built some of the world's first cities, and began to write.

Mesopotamia was never one country for long. It was home to many peoples over thousands of years: Sumerians, Akkadians, Babylonians and Assyrians, among others. They gave the world cuneiform writing, written laws, early maths and astronomy, and one of the oldest long stories ever written down.

Surprising but true

Clay tablets from Mesopotamia mention trade with a faraway land called Meluhha. Most historians think Meluhha was the Indus Valley civilisation.

Life between the rivers

Southern Mesopotamia is hot and dry, with very little rain. But the rivers flooded and left rich mud. Farmers dug canals to carry river water to their fields of barley and dates. Organising all that digging needed leaders, records and workers, and that helped cities grow.

There was no stone or much timber, so people built with mud bricks. Each city had a great temple on a stepped platform called a ziggurat. The Ziggurat of Ur, built about 4,100 years ago, still rises above the desert in southern Iraq.

Watering fields in a hot land had a hidden cost. As water evaporated, it left salt in the soil, and over centuries some farms in the south grew less and less.

Wedges in clay

At first, officials in cities like Uruk drew small pictures on clay to count grain and animals. Over time, the pictures became groups of wedge-shaped marks pressed in with a cut reed. This is called cuneiform, from the Latin word for "wedge". It was used for about 3,000 years, for many languages.

Hundreds of thousands of tablets survive, because clay lasts, especially when baked. They include shopping lists, school exercises, letters and complaints. In one, a man named Nanni grumbles to a merchant called Ea-nasir about bad copper. It is often called the oldest written customer complaint. That is how writing began: with business.

Who invented the wheel?The wheel came surprisingly late. People were farming, making pots and building towns for thousands of years before anyone rolled a cart.

Wheels, sixties and stars

Some of the earliest known wheels come from Mesopotamia and nearby lands, more than 5,000 years ago. The wheel was probably used first for spinning clay pots, then for carts.

Mesopotamian scribes counted in groups of 60. We still use their system every time we tell the time. They also watched the sky carefully and recorded the movements of the Moon and planets on clay, helping later astronomers.

Kings and laws

Around 2300 BCE, Sargon of Akkad conquered many cities and ruled them together, one of the first empires in history. Later, Hammurabi of Babylon had about 280 laws carved on a black stone pillar over 2 m tall. Some are harsh, such as "an eye for an eye". Others set fair prices and protect widows and children. The pillar now stands in the Louvre in Paris.

Mesopotamians also wrote stories. The Epic of Gilgamesh tells of a king of Uruk who searches for a way to live forever. It includes a great flood, and it is one of the oldest stories in the world.

Links with India

Seals made in the Indus cities have been found in Mesopotamian cities such as Ur. Mesopotamian texts talk of ships from Meluhha bringing carnelian beads, timber and ivory. Most historians think Meluhha was the Indus region, which means Indian goods reached Iraq over 4,000 years ago.

The Assyrian king Ashurbanipal later collected thousands of clay tablets in his palace at Nineveh. When the city was destroyed in 612 BCE, the fire baked many of them hard, and that helped them survive to be read today.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Hammurabi's code was the first set of written laws.

Truth: It is the most famous, but older law codes existed. The laws of Ur-Nammu, a king of Ur, were written more than 300 years earlier.

Try this

Make your own clay tablet from play dough or atta dough rolled flat. Use the end of an ice-cream stick or a pencil to press wedge-shaped marks into it. Invent a mark for "one sheep" and one for "ten sheep", then record your class's favourite fruit like a Sumerian accountant.
