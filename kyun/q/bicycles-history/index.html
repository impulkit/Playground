How was the bicycle invented?

The first bicycle, built in Germany in 1817, had no pedals. You sat on it and pushed along the road with your feet.

The short answer

The bicycle was invented in stages over about 70 years. In 1817, a German inventor named Karl Drais built a wooden two-wheeled "running machine". It had a seat and handlebars but no pedals. Riders pushed off the ground with their feet and coasted.

In the 1860s, French makers added pedals to the front wheel. In the 1870s came the tall penny-farthing. Then in 1885, an English inventor, John Kemp Starley, built the Rover, with two wheels of the same size and a chain driving the back wheel. That design, with air-filled tyres added soon after, is still the basic bicycle you ride today.

Surprising but true

The Wright brothers, who built the first successful powered aeroplane, paid for their experiments by running a bicycle shop in Ohio.

A machine with no pedals

Karl Drais lived in Mannheim, in what is now Germany. On 12 June 1817, he rode his running machine about 13 km, there and back, in a little over an hour, faster than walking. People called it a "dandy horse" or "hobby horse".

Some historians think Drais was looking for a way to travel without horses. In 1815, a huge volcanic eruption at Mount Tambora in Indonesia threw ash high into the sky. The next year was so cold in Europe that it was called the Year Without a Summer. Crops failed and horses were costly to feed. Whether this inspired Drais is debated, but it is a striking link between a volcano and a vehicle.

Boneshakers and penny-farthings

In the 1860s, French makers fixed pedals and cranks to the front wheel. These iron-tyred bikes rattled so hard on cobbled streets that English riders called them boneshakers.

Because the pedals turned the front wheel directly, a bigger front wheel meant more speed. So front wheels grew, until some were as tall as a grown man's shoulder. These were penny-farthings, named after a large British penny coin and a small farthing coin. They were fast, but the rider sat so high that hitting a stone could send him flying over the handlebars.

The safety bicycle

The answer was the "safety bicycle". Starley's Rover of 1885 had two wheels of similar size and a chain linking the pedals to the back wheel. Gears did the job the giant wheel had done, and the rider sat low enough to put a foot down.

In 1888, John Boyd Dunlop, a Scottish vet living in Belfast, made air-filled rubber tyres for his young son's tricycle so it would ride more smoothly. Air tyres made cycling far more comfortable. A bicycle craze swept Europe and the United States in the 1890s.

Why doesn't a moving bicycle fall over?Stand still on a bicycle and you topple. Ride it and you're fine. Scientists argued about this for over 100 years.

Freedom on two wheels

Bicycles changed lives. Workers could live further from factories. Women in particular found new freedom. They could travel alone, and many switched from long skirts to looser clothes for riding. The American campaigner Susan B. Anthony said in 1896 that the bicycle had done more for women's freedom than almost anything else, at a time when women were fighting for the vote.

Bicycle shops became workshops for inventors. In Dayton, Ohio, Wilbur and Orville Wright built and repaired bicycles, and used their skills and profits to build the first successful aeroplane.

India's love of cycles

In India, the bicycle became the vehicle of postmen, milkmen, newspaper boys and schoolchildren. Hero Cycles, founded in Ludhiana, Punjab, grew into one of the biggest bicycle makers in the world. Many Indian states have given free bicycles to schoolgirls, and a study in Bihar found that more girls enrolled in secondary school after the scheme began.

There are now more than a billion bicycles in the world, and most of them still follow Starley's design from 1885.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Leonardo da Vinci drew a design for a bicycle in the 1490s.

Truth: A bicycle sketch was found in one of Leonardo's notebooks in the 1970s, but most experts think it was added much later by someone else. There is no good evidence Leonardo designed a bicycle.

Try this

Look closely at a bicycle with an adult. Count the teeth on the front gear (by the pedals) and on the back gear. Turn the pedal slowly once and count how many times the back wheel turns. A bigger front gear and smaller back gear make the wheel spin more times, just like the penny-farthing's giant wheel, but safely.
