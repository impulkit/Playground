What caused the Bengal famine of 1943?

In 1943, millions of people in Bengal died of hunger and disease, even though the region's harvests had not completely failed.

The short answer

The Bengal famine happened in 1943, during the Second World War, when Bengal was part of British India. Historians estimate that about 2 to 3 million people died, mostly from hunger and the diseases that spread among weakened people.

There was no single cause. Japan had captured Burma, cutting off rice imports. A cyclone and a crop disease damaged the harvest. War spending pushed prices sky-high. People with money bought and stored rice, while the poorest could not afford it. The colonial government was slow to admit there was a famine and slow to bring in food. Together, these turned a shortage into a disaster.

Surprising but true

The economist Amartya Sen, who later won the Nobel Prize, saw the famine as a nine-year-old boy and spent years studying why it happened.

A war on the doorstep

In 1942 Japanese forces conquered Burma, now Myanmar, which had sent rice to Bengal. They reached the border of India. Fearing invasion, the British carried out a "denial policy" in coastal Bengal. They removed or destroyed thousands of boats, and moved stocks of rice, so an invading army could not use them.

For fishermen and boatmen, losing their boats meant losing their living. Meanwhile, the government made sure soldiers and war workers in Calcutta, now Kolkata, had enough food, which pulled rice away from the countryside.

Wind, water and a fungus

In October 1942, a powerful cyclone struck the coast of Midnapore and nearby districts. Storm surges flooded fields with seawater and destroyed crops and homes.

Soon after, a fungal disease called brown spot attacked rice plants across the region. The winter harvest was smaller than usual. How much smaller is still argued over. Some researchers think the crop failure was serious. Others say there was still a fair amount of rice in Bengal, but it was in the wrong hands.

Why does India's east coast get more cyclones?The Bay of Bengal is smaller than the Arabian Sea, yet over the past century it has brewed about four cyclones for every one in the west.

When prices outrun wages

The economist Amartya Sen studied the famine closely. He grew up in Santiniketan in Bengal and remembered starving people coming to the town when he was nine.

In his 1981 book Poverty and Famines, Sen argued that the main problem was not a lack of food. Wartime spending had caused rapid inflation. The price of rice rose to several times its normal level. Landless farm workers, fishermen and craftspeople found that their earnings could no longer buy enough to eat. Traders and some better-off families stored rice, expecting prices to rise further, which made things worse.

Slow help

Officials in Bengal and in London disagreed about how bad things were. Newspapers were censored in wartime, but in August 1943 the Calcutta newspaper The Statesman published photographs that shocked readers in India and Britain.

The British War Cabinet, led by Winston Churchill, turned down or delayed several requests to send more grain, saying ships were needed for the war. How much Churchill personally was to blame is one of the most argued parts of the story. In October 1943, a new Viceroy, Lord Wavell, ordered the army to help move food and run relief camps, and conditions slowly improved.

What changed afterwards

The famine left deep scars on Bengal. Artists such as Zainul Abedin and Chittaprosad made drawings that recorded the suffering, and writers wrote about it for years.

After independence, India made food security a top priority. The Green Revolution of the 1960s helped the country grow far more grain. Sen later pointed out that no major famine has occurred in a working democracy with a free press, because governments that face elections and newspapers cannot easily ignore hunger.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Famines happen only because there is not enough food.

Truth: Many famines, including Bengal's in 1943, happen when some people lose the ability to buy or grow food, even while food is still available elsewhere. Prices, jobs and government decisions matter as much as harvests.
