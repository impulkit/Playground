How big is a crore, really?

Count one number every second, without stopping. How long to reach a crore?

The short answer

India counts in lakhs and crores: 1 lakh = 1,00,000 and 1 crore = 1,00,00,000.

Much of the world counts in thousands, millions and billions: 1 lakh is a hundred thousand, and 1 crore is 10 million. So 100 crore make a billion.

The numbers are the same. Only the names and the commas change. The trouble is that our brains are bad at feeling the difference between big numbers. A crore and a billion both just sound like "a lot". Turning them into time, distance or rice grains helps.

Surprising but true

Counting to 1 billion, one number a second, would take about 32 years.

Same number, different commas

Write ten million the Indian way and you get 1,00,00,000. Write it the international way and you get 10,000,000. After the first three digits, the Indian system puts a comma every two digits. The international system puts one every three.

Each system matches its names. Indian names jump by a hundred: thousand, lakh, crore. International names jump by a thousand: thousand, million, billion. Older Indian names go further, with arab for 100 crore and kharab for 10,000 crore, though few people use them now. The words are old too: lakh comes from the Sanskrit laksha, and crore from koti.

Turning numbers into time

Count one number every second, with no sleep or meals. You reach a thousand in about 17 minutes and a lakh in about 28 hours. A crore takes almost four months.

A billion takes about 32 years. A child who started on her eighth birthday would finish at around 40. That gap is the clearest way to see how much bigger each step is. Every time you add a zero, the counting takes ten times longer, which is why zero was such a powerful invention.

Where the big numbers live

India has about 140 crore people, and the USA about 34 crore, according to recent estimates. Together, that is more than one in every five humans on Earth. The whole world's population passed 800 crore (8 billion) in 2022.

Go bigger and the numbers stop being about people. Our galaxy may hold between 100 and 400 billion stars, so astronomers measure space in light years instead of kilometres. Earth itself is about 4.5 billion years old, a number geologists squeeze into a single day to make it feel real in Earth's age.

Feeling the size

Scientists who study how children learn maths have found that most of us picture numbers on a mental line. Small numbers get lots of space, and big ones get squashed together at the far end. That is why a crore and a billion can feel almost the same.

One fix is to use a ruler. If one lakh were a single millimetre, a crore would be 10 centimetres, about the width of your palm. A billion would be 10 metres, about the length of a school bus. The step from a crore to a billion is a hundred palms, not one.

A billion that changed size

Until 1974, a "billion" in Britain meant a million million, the number Americans call a trillion. Many European languages still use it that way. Britain's government switched to the American meaning, and most English speakers followed.

Ancient Indian thinkers loved huge numbers. A Buddhist text called the Lalitavistara tells of the young Siddhartha, later the Buddha, naming numbers far past a crore, up to a 1 followed by 53 zeros. Nobody needed such a number for shopping or counting cattle. People invented it because they enjoyed thinking about it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A billion is only a bit bigger than a million.

Truth: A billion is a thousand times bigger. A million seconds is about 11 and a half days. A billion seconds is about 32 years.

Try this

Fill a small cup with dry rice. Count the grains in one level teaspoon, then count how many teaspoons fill the cup. Multiply the two numbers to estimate the grains in the cup, then work out how many cups would hold one lakh grains.
