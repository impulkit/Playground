How did the USA grow from 13 states to 50?

In 1803 the USA bought a slice of land about two-thirds the size of India, and in 1867 it paid about 2 US cents an acre for Alaska.

The short answer

The USA began in 1787 with 13 states along the Atlantic coast. Over the next 172 years it added 37 more, by buying land, signing treaties, fighting wars and drawing new borders. The last two, Alaska and Hawaii, joined in 1959.

The rules came early. In 1787 Congress passed the Northwest Ordinance, which said new territories could become full states, equal to the old ones, once enough people lived there. That promise turned a strip of coastal colonies into a country stretching 4,500 km across a continent, and then into the Pacific.

Surprising but true

The 50-star US flag was designed as a school project by a 17-year-old in Ohio, who says his teacher first gave it a B minus.

The biggest shopping trip ever

In 1803 the young USA wanted to buy one city, New Orleans, from France. The French ruler, Napoleon, needed money for his wars and offered something huge instead: the entire Louisiana territory. It cost about 15 million dollars and roughly doubled the size of the country overnight.

Parts of 15 future states came from that one deal. President Thomas Jefferson sent Lewis and Clark to explore it, though Native nations had lived there for thousands of years. Later, Texas joined after being its own independent republic for nine years, from 1836 to 1845. A war with Mexico then brought in California and the Southwest.

Gold, railways and a race

California became a state in 1850, just two years after gold was found there. The gold rush brought so many people so fast that it skipped the usual long wait as a territory. Railways and wagon trails pulled settlers further west, and new states followed: Oregon, Kansas, Nevada, Colorado.

Every new state stirred up a fierce question: would slavery be allowed there? The fight over the balance between free and slave states grew so bitter that it helped spark the Civil War in 1861. During that war, West Virginia split away from Virginia and became a state of its own.

Some borders were drawn with a ruler on a map, often by people who had never seen the land. That is why Colorado and Wyoming are almost perfect rectangles. Others follow rivers, like the Mississippi between several states. In the Southwest, four states meet at a single point called Four Corners. A visitor can put a hand or foot in each of them at the same time.

The people already living there

The map grew at a terrible cost to the people already on it. Native nations were pushed off their lands, often by force, and many treaties promising them land were later broken. In the 1830s, tens of thousands of Cherokee and other people were forced to march west, a journey now called the Trail of Tears.

Hawaii was an independent kingdom until 1893, when American businessmen overthrew its queen. In 1993, Congress formally apologised for that. Historians today tell the story of statehood alongside these losses, because both are true at once.

The last two stars

The final mainland states, New Mexico and Arizona, joined in 1912. Arizona's date was 14 February, so it is sometimes called the Valentine State. Then the map stood still for almost 50 years.

Alaska became the 49th state in January 1959 and Hawaii the 50th in August. A teenager in Ohio, Robert Heft, had sewn a 50-star flag for a school project. By his own account his teacher gave it a B minus, then raised it after the government picked his design. It has flown since 4 July 1960.

Will there ever be a 51st star? Washington, DC and Puerto Rico have both held votes on the idea, but Congress has not agreed. Meanwhile, the cheapest state of all keeps surprising people. Russia sold it for pocket change, yet Alaska turned out to hold gold, oil and a coastline longer than all the other states put together.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The USA grew by settling land that was empty.

Truth: Almost every part of it was already home to Native American nations, and to Mexican, Hawaiian and other peoples. Much of the land changed hands through wars and broken treaties.
