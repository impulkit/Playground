What are the weirdest foods in the world?

In Odisha, people grind red ants into a spicy chutney, and in 2024 it won a special government label for its fame.

The short answer

"Weird" depends on where you grow up. A child in Sweden might find a mango pickle shocking. A child in Gujarat might find a cheese full of blue mould odd. Every country has foods that look strange to outsiders and feel like home to the people who eat them.

Some of the most famous examples are fermented fish that can clear a room, eggs with green yolks, soup made from bird spit and chutney made from ants. Most of them came from clever ways to preserve food, use every part of an animal or find protein where it was plentiful.

Surprising but true

Swiftlets build nests out of their own hardened spit, and in parts of Asia those nests are cooked into soup that sells for very high prices.

A chutney with a bite

In the forests of Similipal in Odisha, people of the Mayurbhanj region collect red weaver ants and their eggs from leaf nests in the trees. The ants are ground with salt, ginger, garlic and chilli into a sour, spicy paste called kai chutney. It's said to taste a bit like lemon, because the ants contain an acid.

In January 2024, Similipal kai chutney received a GI tag, a label given to products linked to a special place, like Darjeeling tea. In Chhattisgarh, a similar dish called chaprah is eaten too. Across the Northeast, people eat silkworm pupae, crickets and other insects. The UN's food agency estimates about 2 billion people around the world eat insects.

Room-clearing fish

In Sweden, surströmming is Baltic herring left to ferment in barrels, then sealed in tins where it keeps fermenting. The cans can bulge with gas. People usually open them outdoors, because the smell is extremely strong.

Iceland has hákarl, Greenland shark meat. Fresh, it is poisonous. So it's fermented for weeks and then hung in sheds to dry for months, until it's safe to eat. Its smell has been compared to strong cleaning products. Both foods come from cold places where people needed to store fish for winter, and fermentation let them do it without fridges.

How do tiny microbes make food tasty?Curd, idli, bread, cheese and chocolate all need help from creatures far too small to see.

Eggs, nests and maggots

Century eggs from China are duck or chicken eggs coated in clay, ash, salt and lime for weeks or months. The whites turn into brown, see-through jelly and the yolks turn dark green. They're often served with tofu or rice porridge.

In Southeast Asia, swiftlets build nests from strands of their own saliva, glued to cave walls. Bird's nest soup has been a luxury in China for centuries. Collecting nests from tall caves can be dangerous work.

On the Italian island of Sardinia, there's casu marzu, a sheep's cheese deliberately left for cheese flies to lay eggs in. The larvae soften it. Food safety rules in the European Union mean it can't be sold in normal shops.

Why does your brain say "eww"?

Disgust probably helped our ancestors avoid rotten food. That's why strong smells hit so hard: your nose is checking for danger. But disgust is also learned. Children copy the faces adults make at the dinner table.

Scientists have found that people often start to like a new taste after trying it many times. Plenty of foods now loved around the world once seemed odd. Raw fish in sushi, blue cheese and even tomatoes were once thought strange or risky by many people. And somewhere in an Odisha forest, a family is enjoying ant chutney made from just a few of the roughly 20 quadrillion ants scientists estimate live on Earth.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Some cultures eat strange foods only because they had nothing else.

Truth: Many unusual foods are prized, careful creations eaten by choice. Fermented and preserved foods let people store food through long winters, and insects are rich in protein. Today many of these dishes are celebration foods.

Try this

Hold a blind taste test with a grown-up's help. Put small spoons of familiar foods, like curd, lime pickle, jaggery and cheese, on a plate. Taste each with eyes closed and describe it in three words without naming it. Notice how much "weird" depends on what you're used to.
