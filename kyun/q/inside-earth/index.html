What's inside the Earth?

The deepest hole humans ever dug went only 12 km down. The centre is 6,371 km away.

The short answer

Nobody has been to Earth's centre. Scientists ‘see’ inside using earthquake waves, which bend and bounce differently through solid and liquid layers.

The mantle slowly churns, carrying the plates on top.

The liquid outer core makes Earth a giant magnet.

Surprising but true

Earth's inner core is about as hot as the surface of the Sun.

Four layers

Earth is built in layers, a bit like a mango with its skin, flesh and stone, but with two parts to the stone.

The crust is the thin rocky skin we live on. It's 5 to 10 km thick under the oceans and up to about 70 km under big mountains like the Himalayas. Below it is the mantle, nearly 2,900 km of hot rock. It is solid, but it creeps slowly over millions of years, carrying the plates with it.

Next comes the outer core, a layer of liquid iron and nickel about 2,200 km thick. Its swirling makes Earth's magnetic field. At the very centre is the inner core, a solid ball of iron and nickel about 2,400 km across. That's about two-thirds the width of the Moon.

Seeing with earthquakes

When a big earthquake strikes, its waves travel through the whole planet. Seismometers around the world time their arrival. Some waves, called P waves, can pass through both solids and liquids. Others, called S waves, can only travel through solids.

Scientists noticed that S waves never reach the far side of Earth from an earthquake. Something in the middle blocks them. It must be liquid. That's how they found the outer core.

In 1936, a Danish scientist named Inge Lehmann spotted faint P waves arriving where none should be. She worked out that they were bouncing off something solid inside the liquid core. She had discovered the inner core, working from paper records of earthquake waves collected at stations around the world. She kept working on Earth's insides for decades and lived to be 104.

The deepest hole

People have tried to dig down. In the 1970s, scientists in the Soviet Union began drilling the Kola Superdeep Borehole near the Arctic. After about 20 years, they reached 12,262 m, the deepest artificial hole in the world. The hole is only about 23 cm wide, narrower than a dinner plate.

They had to stop because it was getting too hot, about 180 °C at the bottom. Even so, the hole barely scratched the crust. The deepest mines, in South Africa, go about 4 km down. Digging tunnels through mountains is hard enough.

Why it's so hot inside

Earth is still holding heat from when it formed, when rocks crashed together and melted. While the young planet was largely molten, heavy iron sank to the middle to form the core, and lighter rock floated up. A giant crash with another young planet, which probably also made the Moon, added even more heat. Some of the heat comes from radioactive atoms deep inside, which give off energy as they slowly change.

The pressure is enormous too. At the centre, it is more than three million times the air pressure at the surface. That pressure squeezes the iron of the inner core so hard that it stays solid, even though it's as hot as the surface of the Sun.

That inner heat drives almost everything that shapes our planet's surface. It powers volcanoes and hot springs, moves the plates, and builds mountains.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The inside of the Earth is a giant ball of liquid lava.

Truth: Most of Earth's inside is solid. The mantle is solid rock that flows very slowly, like extremely stiff toffee. Only the outer core is fully liquid, and it's metal, not lava. Magma forms in small pockets near the surface.

Try this

Make a model Earth from play dough. Roll a small yellow ball for the inner core, wrap it in a layer of orange for the outer core, then a thick layer of red for the mantle, and a very thin layer of brown for the crust. Ask an adult to help you slice it in half with a piece of string or dental floss to see the layers.
