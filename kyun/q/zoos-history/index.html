How did zoos begin?

Over 5,000 years ago, rulers in Egypt kept hippos, baboons and elephants. The zoo you visit today grew out of these private animal collections.

The short answer

Zoos began as private collections of rare animals kept by kings and nobles to show off their power and wealth. Over thousands of years they turned into public parks where anyone could pay to see lions, giraffes and bears. In the last hundred years they changed again, with many zoos now saying their main job is protecting animals from extinction.

The oldest zoo still open is in Vienna, Austria. It started in 1752 in the gardens of an emperor's palace. In India, the first public zoo opened in Madras, now Chennai, in 1855.

Surprising but true

In 1987 the last wild California condor was caught and taken into a zoo. Thanks to breeding in zoos, hundreds of these giant vultures now fly free again.

Kings and their animals

Archaeologists digging at Hierakonpolis, an ancient Egyptian site, found the graves of animals buried around 3500 BCE, including hippos, baboons and an elephant. Some had healed broken bones, a sign that someone cared for them for years.

Many later rulers kept animals too. Chinese records describe royal parks full of deer and birds. In India, Emperor Ashoka carved rules on stone pillars around 2,200 years ago that protected many kinds of animals from being killed. The Mughal emperors kept cheetahs for hunting, and Akbar is said to have owned hundreds of them.

Parks for the public

The idea of a zoo as a place of science grew in Europe in the 1700s and 1800s. In 1828 the Zoological Society of London opened its gardens in Regent's Park, at first only for scientists and members. Londoners began calling it "the zoo", and the short word stuck around the world.

India's zoos followed. Alongside Madras, the Alipore Zoological Gardens in Kolkata opened in 1876. In the USA, the Philadelphia Zoo opened in 1874 and calls itself America's first zoo.

Not everything in that era was kind. Animals were often kept alone in small, bare cages. Some fairs and zoos in Europe and America even displayed people from Africa and Asia, which is now seen as deeply wrong.

Which animals are endangered today, and why?In the 1990s, India's vultures almost vanished in about ten years. The cause was hiding in a common painkiller.

Taking away the bars

In 1907 a German animal dealer named Carl Hagenbeck opened a park near Hamburg with no cages for many animals. Instead, deep ditches called moats kept lions and visitors apart. From the path, it looked as if the animals were roaming free. Zoos around the world copied the idea.

Today good zoos try to give animals space, things to do and company of their own kind. Keepers hide food so animals must search for it, the way they would in the wild.

Saving species

Some animals exist today because of zoos. The Arabian oryx, a white antelope, was hunted out of the wild by the early 1970s. Zoos bred a herd, and oryx were returned to Oman in 1982. The California condor, a vulture with wings about 3 m across, longer than a car is wide, was saved the same way.

India runs breeding centres too. Zoos and centres help breed vultures, red pandas and the tiny pygmy hog for release.

The argument today

Not everyone agrees zoos should exist. Critics say large animals such as elephants, which can walk many kilometres a day, suffer in even big enclosures. In 2009, India's Central Zoo Authority told zoos to move their elephants to forest camps and sanctuaries where possible. Supporters say zoos teach millions of children to care about wildlife they would never otherwise see. Most experts agree that a zoo is only as good as the care it gives.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Most animals in zoos today were caught in the wild.

Truth: In well-run zoos, most animals were born in zoos. Catching wild animals for display is now rare and strictly limited by law in many countries, including India.
