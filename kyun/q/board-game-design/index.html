How are board games designed?

Monopoly began as a game meant to show how unfair it is when a few landlords own everything, the opposite of how most people play it today.

The short answer

A board game is a set of rules, pieces and a board that together create a challenge. A designer's job is to make that challenge fun. They decide what players are trying to do, what choices they get on each turn, how much luck is involved and how the game ends.

Most designers start with either a theme, such as building a city or racing to a temple, or a mechanic, such as rolling dice, trading cards or placing tiles. They make a rough version from paper and cardboard and test it with friends again and again. A finished game may have been played hundreds of times before anyone buys it.

Surprising but true

Snakes and ladders comes from an old Indian game where the ladders stood for good deeds and the snakes for bad ones.

Old games, big ideas

Some of the world's favourite games began in India. Ludo comes from pachisi, played on a cross-shaped cloth board with cowrie shells instead of dice. At Fatehpur Sikri, the city built by the emperor Akbar, a giant pachisi board is laid into a courtyard. Stories say he played with people as the pieces.

Snakes and ladders comes from Gyan Chaupar, also called Moksha Patam. Players climbed ladders of virtues, such as generosity, and slid down snakes of vices, such as anger. The goal at the top was moksha, freedom from the cycle of rebirth. When the British took the game home in the 1800s, they kept the snakes and ladders and dropped most of the religion.

The game with a message

In 1903, an American woman named Lizzie Magie designed The Landlord's Game. Players moved around a square board buying property and charging rent. She wanted to show how renting land let a few owners grow rich while everyone else grew poor. She even included a second set of rules where wealth was shared.

People made their own boards and changed the rules. One version reached a man named Charles Darrow, who sold it to the company Parker Brothers as Monopoly in 1935. It became one of the best-selling games ever, and for decades Magie's part was nearly forgotten.

Luck, skill and balance

Every game sits somewhere between pure luck and pure skill. Snakes and ladders is all luck: you never make a choice. Chess has no luck at all. Most modern games mix the two, so that beginners have a chance but good players usually win.

Designers spend a lot of time on balance. If one strategy always wins, players stop exploring. Some games add a catch-up rule that helps whoever is behind, so nobody gives up halfway. Understanding probability helps here. A designer must know that rolling a seven with two dice happens far more often than rolling a twelve.

The settlers of a new style

In 1995, Klaus Teuber, a German dental technician who designed games in his spare time, released The Settlers of Catan, now simply called Catan. Players collect wood, brick and wheat, trade with each other and build roads and towns on an island made of hexagon tiles.

Catan had no player knocked out early, a board that changed every game and a lot of talking and trading. It sold tens of millions of copies and helped start a boom in modern board games. Germany even has a famous yearly prize for the best new game, the Spiel des Jahres, which began in 1979.

Testing and the rulebook

Designers watch people play their games without explaining anything. Do players understand what to do? Do they look bored while waiting for their turn? Does the game drag on too long?

Many designers say the hardest part is the rulebook. Rules must be short, clear and impossible to misread, because the designer won't be in the room when you open the box. In the end, a good game is a conversation between a designer and players they will never meet.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Monopoly was invented by Charles Darrow in the 1930s.

Truth: Darrow sold the game to Parker Brothers in 1935, but it was based on The Landlord's Game, designed by Lizzie Magie and patented in 1904. Versions of her game had been played and changed by many people before Darrow saw it.
