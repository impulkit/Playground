Who was Savitribai Phule?

On her way to teach girls in 1840s Pune, Savitribai Phule was pelted with mud, so she carried a spare sari and changed at school.

The short answer

Savitribai Phule was a teacher, poet and social reformer from Maharashtra. In 1848, with her husband Jyotirao Phule, she started a school for girls in Pune at a time when most people thought girls did not need to read. She is often called India's first woman teacher in a modern school.

She also opened schools for children from communities that had been shut out of education because of their caste. She cared for widows, wrote poems, and died in 1897 helping people sick with plague.

Surprising but true

Savitribai Phule could not read when she married at about nine years old. Her husband, Jyotirao, taught her at home, and she went on to run schools.

A girl who learned late

Savitribai was born in 1831 in Naigaon, a village near Satara. Like most girls of her time, she was married very young, at about nine, to Jyotirao, who was about thirteen. She had never been to school.

Jyotirao believed education could change lives, and he taught her at home. Later she trained as a teacher in Ahmednagar and Pune. In the 1840s this was almost unheard of for an Indian woman.

The school at Bhide Wada

On 1 January 1848 the Phules opened a school for girls in Bhide Wada, a house in Pune. Savitribai taught there. Every day, as she walked to school, some people shouted at her and threw mud, stones or dung. They believed she was breaking the rules of society.

She carried a spare sari in her bag and changed when she arrived. Then she taught. Some accounts say a man once blocked her path, and she slapped him. The harassment slowly eased after that. Her friend Fatima Sheikh, who also became a teacher, worked with her. By the early 1850s the Phules were running several schools in Pune, with well over a hundred girls.

Their lessons were practical. Pupils learned reading, arithmetic, grammar, geography and some science, much like a school today. The Phules also worried about children dropping out, a problem India still works on. They tried paying small stipends to keep poor pupils in class, and they held regular meetings with parents to explain why learning mattered.

Schools for everyone

Indian society was divided by caste, and people from the lowest-ranked groups were often not allowed to study at all. The Phules opened schools for their children too, and taught them alongside others.

Many in their own community turned against them. At one point the couple had to leave Jyotirao's family home. They kept going. In 1852 the British government in Bombay Presidency publicly honoured them for their work in education.

Caring for women in need

In the nineteenth century, many young widows were treated harshly and some were driven from their homes. In 1863 the Phules opened a shelter where widows could safely give birth and leave their babies to be cared for. They later adopted a widow's son, Yashwantrao, who grew up to become a doctor.

Savitribai also wrote poetry. Her first book, Kavya Phule, came out in 1854. Its poems urged people, especially girls and the poor, to learn to read. After Jyotirao died in 1890, she led the Satyashodhak Samaj, the society he had founded to fight caste unfairness.

Her last work

In 1897 bubonic plague swept through Pune. Savitribai and her son set up a clinic outside the city. She carried a sick boy there herself, caught the disease and died on 10 March 1897.

Today her birthday, 3 January, is celebrated in Maharashtra as Balika Din, or Girls' Day. In 2014 the University of Pune was renamed Savitribai Phule Pune University. Thinkers like B.R. Ambedkar later carried forward the Phules' fight for equal rights, and Jyotirao Phule and Savitribai are often remembered together.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Girls in India always had the same chance as boys to go to school.

Truth: In the early 1800s very few Indian girls learned to read, and many people opposed it. Reformers like the Phules had to fight hard, and girls' schooling only spread widely in the twentieth century.
