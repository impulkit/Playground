What is chaat?

Chaat comes from the Hindi "chaatna", to lick, as in licking your fingers after something sweet, sour, salty and spicy.

The short answer

Chaat is a family of savoury snacks from North India, sold at street stalls and eaten at all times of day. A typical plate mixes something crunchy, like fried papdi or puffed rice, with something soft, like boiled potatoes or chickpeas. Then come cool curd, a sweet tamarind chutney, a sharp green chutney, crunchy sev and a sprinkle of tangy chaat masala.

The magic is in the contrast. Each bite is sweet, sour, salty, spicy, hot and cold, crunchy and soft. Your tongue and nose get many signals at once, which is part of why chaat is so hard to stop eating.

Surprising but true

Chaat masala gets its eggy smell from kala namak, a black salt that contains sulphur.

A plate of opposites

Chaat-wallahs, the cooks who make chaat, build each plate by hand in seconds. For papdi chaat, they crack crisp flour wafers onto a leaf plate, add potatoes and chickpeas, pour on whisked curd, then splash on two chutneys.

The sweet chutney is made from tamarind and jaggery or dates. The green one is coriander, mint and green chilli. On top go sev, pomegranate seeds, chopped onion, coriander leaves and a pinch of chaat masala. The whole thing is eaten quickly, before the papdi goes soggy.

The secret spice mix

Chaat masala gives chaat its tangy, slightly funky taste. It usually contains dried mango powder (amchur) for sourness, roasted cumin, black pepper, dried ginger, asafoetida and kala namak.

Kala namak, or black salt, is a rock salt heated with other ingredients. The process creates sulphur compounds, which give it a smell like boiled eggs. A tiny pinch makes a big difference, partly because much of what we call taste is really smell.

So many chaats

Delhi and Uttar Pradesh have aloo tikki, crisp potato patties topped with chutneys and curd, and dahi bhalla, soft lentil dumplings soaked in curd. In Lucknow, cooks serve chaat in a crisp edible basket called a tokri.

Mumbai has its own chaat family. Bhel puri mixes puffed rice, sev, onion, potato and chutneys, and it's sold on the sands of Juhu and Chowpatty beaches. Sev puri tops flat puris with potato and chutneys. Ragda pattice pairs potato patties with a white pea curry.

Kolkata loves jhalmuri, puffed rice tossed with mustard oil, onions and green chillies, and sold in paper cones.

Where it came from

Nobody knows exactly where chaat began. A popular story says it started in Delhi in the time of the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan, in the 1600s. Royal doctors supposedly advised people to eat spicy, tangy food to fight the effects of water from the Yamuna. There's no clear evidence for this tale.

What is clear is that chaat grew up on the streets of North Indian cities. Chandni Chowk in Old Delhi has been famous for its chaat stalls for generations.

Eating it safely

Because chaat is street food, many people worry about hygiene. Good stalls use clean water, fresh chutneys and cover their food. It's sensible to choose busy stalls where food doesn't sit around for long, and to ask an adult before trying somewhere new.

Chaat has also spread far beyond the street. You can find it at weddings, in restaurants and in Indian cafés in cities like London and New York. Some American restaurants now serve chaat dishes, introducing new eaters to the sweet-sour-crunchy mix that North Indian children have loved for generations.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Different parts of your tongue taste different flavours, with sweet at the tip and bitter at the back.

Truth: This "tongue map" is wrong. Taste buds all over the tongue can sense sweet, sour, salty, bitter and umami. Some areas may be slightly more sensitive, but every part tastes every flavour.

Try this

Make a mini chaat taste test with an adult. Put a few boiled chickpeas on a plate and add a different topping to each little group: a drop of lemon, a pinch of sugar, a pinch of salt, a pinch of chaat masala. Taste each, then mix them all. Notice how the combined spoonful tastes stronger than any one alone.
