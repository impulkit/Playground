How did the Incas build Machu Picchu?

High in the Andes, Inca builders fitted giant stones together so tightly, without any cement, that you can't slide a knife blade between them.

The short answer

Machu Picchu is a stone city built on a narrow ridge high in the Andes mountains of Peru, about 2,430 metres above sea level. The Incas built it around 1450, most likely as a royal estate for their emperor Pachacuti.

Inca builders used the granite of the mountain itself. With stone and bronze tools, they shaped blocks so precisely that they fit together without mortar, which is the cement-like paste that normally holds bricks together. They built terraces down the steep slopes, laid hidden drainage under the city, and designed walls that rock gently during earthquakes instead of falling down. Most of the work was done by teams of labourers who owed work to the state as a kind of tax.

Surprising but true

The Spanish conquerors never seem to have found Machu Picchu, which is one reason it survived so well.

An empire in the clouds

The Incas built the largest empire in the Americas. At its height around 1500, it stretched about 4,000 km along the Andes, through today's Peru, Ecuador, Bolivia, Chile and Argentina. Its capital was Cusco.

They had no horses, no iron and no wheeled carts. Yet they ruled millions of people. A road network of about 30,000 km, with rope bridges across gorges, linked the empire. Relay runners called chasquis carried messages along it, covering great distances in a few days.

Stones that fit like puzzle pieces

The finest walls at Machu Picchu are made of granite blocks, each shaped to fit its neighbours exactly. Builders pounded the stone with harder hammerstones and ground the faces smooth. They may have tried each block in place, lifted it off, trimmed it and tried again.

Walls lean slightly inwards. Doors and windows are trapezoid-shaped, wider at the bottom than the top. These shapes help the city stand firm in a land of frequent earthquakes. When the ground shakes, the stones can shift and settle back into place.

Building on a knife edge

The ridge is steep and wet, with heavy rains for part of the year. A city here could easily slide away. So builders put much of their effort below ground, into foundations and layers of crushed rock that let rainwater drain away.

The terraces on the slopes held soil in place and gave farmland. Llamas, relatives of camels, carried loads up and down, though each one can only carry about 25 to 30 kg. Most of the heavy lifting was done by people, with ropes, ramps and levers.

A hidden city

About a century after it was built, Machu Picchu was abandoned, around the time Spanish soldiers led by Francisco Pizarro conquered the Incas in the 1530s. The Spanish destroyed or rebuilt many Inca sites, but there is no sign they ever reached Machu Picchu.

Local farmers knew it was there. In 1911, a farmer named Melchor Arteaga led the American explorer Hiram Bingham up the mountain. Bingham's photographs in National Geographic made it world-famous. He also shipped thousands of objects to Yale University in the USA, which returned them to Peru from 2011 onwards.

Visiting today

Machu Picchu became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1983 and is one of the most visited places in South America. In 2007 it was voted one of the New Seven Wonders of the World.

Peru now limits visitor numbers to protect the site. Many people still walk the Inca Trail there over several days, following old stone paths. The Incas also domesticated the potato in these mountains, a crop that eventually reached Indian kitchens as the humble aloo.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Hiram Bingham discovered Machu Picchu in 1911.

Truth: Local farmers knew the site well and led him to it, and some families were growing crops on its terraces. Bingham's photographs and articles made it famous around the world.
