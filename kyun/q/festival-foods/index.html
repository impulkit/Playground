Which foods go with which festivals?

In Tamil Nadu, families cook rice and milk in a pot until it boils over, and everyone shouts "Pongalo Pongal" to welcome a year of plenty.

The short answer

Almost every festival in the world has foods that belong to it. Diwali comes with boxes of sweets. Eid comes with bowls of sweet vermicelli. Thanksgiving in the USA means roast turkey, and Lunar New Year in China means dumplings.

These foods usually have a reason behind them. Many follow the seasons, because festivals often mark a harvest or a change in weather. Some carry a meaning, such as sweetness for a happy year or a dish that recalls an old story. Others simply became traditions because families made them year after year. Sharing them with neighbours and friends is often as important as eating them.

Surprising but true

In Japan, eating fried chicken from KFC has become a popular Christmas tradition, thanks to an advertising campaign from 1974.

Harvest on a plate

Many Indian festivals celebrate the harvest, so the food uses fresh crops. In Tamil Nadu, the harvest festival of Pongal shares its name with its main dish. New rice is cooked with milk and jaggery in a clay or brass pot until it bubbles over the rim. The overflow is a sign of plenty, and people shout "Pongalo Pongal".

In Kerala, the Onam feast called sadya is served on a banana leaf, often with more than 20 dishes. There is rice, sambar, avial, thoran, pickles and crispy banana chips, with payasam, a sweet pudding, at the end. Each dish has its own place on the leaf. Guests eat with their right hand, and the order in which dishes are served is part of the tradition.

Sweet beginnings

Sweets are a way of wishing someone a sweet year. During Diwali, homes fill with laddoos, barfis and kaju katli, and families exchange boxes with neighbours. In Maharashtra, people also make a festive snack platter called faral, with crunchy chakli, chivda and sweet karanji.

At Holi, many north Indian homes fry gujiya, little pastry pockets stuffed with khoya and dry fruit, and serve cold, spiced thandai. In winter, Makar Sankranti brings sesame and jaggery sweets, handed out with a saying that means "take this and speak sweetly".

Why is Diwali called the festival of lights?Millions of small oil lamps glow on the darkest night of the month.

Fasting and feasting

Some festivals involve fasting before the feast. During the holy month of Ramadan, many Muslims eat nothing from dawn to sunset. They often break each day's fast with a date and water, following a practice linked to the Prophet Muhammad, before a bigger evening meal called iftar.

When the month ends with Eid al-Fitr, many families in South Asia make sheer khurma or seviyan, vermicelli cooked in sweet milk with nuts and dates. During Navratri, Hindus who fast often skip grains and eat sabudana khichdi or rotis made from buckwheat flour instead.

Food that tells a story

At Passover, Jewish families eat matzo, a flat bread made without yeast. It recalls the story of the Israelites leaving Egypt in such a hurry that their bread had no time to rise. Each food on the special Passover plate stands for a part of that story.

In China and among Chinese families everywhere, Lunar New Year brings dumplings, often shaped like old silver money for good fortune, and long noodles for a long life. Thanksgiving in the USA, held on the fourth Thursday of November, centres on turkey, cranberry sauce and pumpkin pie, foods linked to the American autumn harvest.

New traditions

Festival foods keep changing. In India, plum cake has become part of Christmas, and bakeries in Kerala, Goa and Kolkata are busiest in December. In Japan, where few people are Christian, an advertising campaign in 1974 suggested fried chicken for Christmas. Today, many Japanese families order buckets of it weeks in advance.

Families also bring traditions with them when they move. An Indian family in New Jersey might serve gujiya at Holi and turkey at Thanksgiving. A dish only becomes a festival food when people keep making it, year after year, and teaching it to the next cook in the family.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Eating turkey at Thanksgiving makes you sleepy because turkey is full of a sleep chemical called tryptophan.

Truth: Turkey has tryptophan, but no more than chicken or many other foods. The sleepiness usually comes from eating a very large meal, often with plenty of rich food, and relaxing afterwards.
