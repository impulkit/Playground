What is special about Howrah Bridge?

Howrah Bridge carries a river of people across the Hooghly every day, and it is held together by rivets, without a single nut or bolt.

The short answer

Howrah Bridge is a giant steel bridge across the Hooghly River, joining the city of Howrah to Kolkata in West Bengal. It opened in 1943 and is one of the busiest bridges in the world.

It is special for several reasons. It is a cantilever bridge, with no pillars in the river between its two big towers, so ships could pass freely. It was built without nuts and bolts, held together by rivets. And it was made almost entirely of Indian steel, during the Second World War.

Since 1965 its official name has been Rabindra Setu, after the poet Rabindranath Tagore. Most people still call it Howrah Bridge.

Surprising but true

Some of the greatest damage to Howrah Bridge has come from people spitting chewed tobacco and paan, whose juices ate into the steel so badly that the port had to cover the pillar bases.

Replacing a floating bridge

In the 1800s Calcutta, as Kolkata was then called, was the capital of British India and one of the busiest ports in Asia. Howrah, across the river, had the big railway station.

In 1874 a pontoon bridge was opened between them. It floated on boats and had to be opened in the middle to let ships through, stopping all traffic. As the cities grew, it became hopelessly crowded.

A new fixed bridge was planned. The river was busy with ships and the riverbed was soft, so engineers chose a design with no supports in the water at all.

Built in wartime

Work started in the late 1930s, with a firm called Braithwaite, Burn and Jessop Construction Company doing the building. The Second World War broke out in 1939. Steel from Britain became hard to get.

So the builders turned to Tata Steel in Jamshedpur. Of about 26,500 tonnes of steel in the bridge, around 23,000 tonnes was a special high-strength kind made by Tata.

The bridge opened to traffic in February 1943, with no grand ceremony. Japan's armies were then fighting in nearby Burma, and the city feared air raids. The war shaped the bridge from start to finish.

How it stands up

Howrah Bridge is about 705 m long. Its main span, the part between the two towers, is about 457 m, longer than four football fields placed end to end.

Each tower holds out a long arm over the river, balanced by a shorter arm on the land side, like a see-saw. The two river arms don't meet directly. A middle section hangs between them. Together they make one smooth line across the water.

The whole steel frame is joined with rivets, hot metal pins hammered flat at each end. Rivets were the usual way to build big steel structures then, but people still marvel that such a giant has no nuts or bolts.

A bridge that never sleeps

Every day, huge numbers of people cross. Estimates from the 2010s put it at around a lakh vehicles and well over a lakh people on foot, along with handcarts, cycles and porters carrying loads on their heads.

Beneath it, ferries cross the river and the busy Mallik Ghat flower market spreads along the bank. At night the bridge is lit up, and it has starred in many Bengali and Hindi films.

Spit and rust

The bridge's toughest enemy turned out to be people. Many travellers chew paan or gutkha, a tobacco mixture, and spit the red juice on the bridge. The juice mixed with rain made the steel at the base of the pillars rust and thin out.

Around 2011 engineers found that some steel covers had lost about half their thickness. The port fixed the damage and wrapped the pillar bases in fibreglass to protect them. Signs now ask people not to spit.

Today the bridge is more than 80 years old. Newer bridges, such as Vidyasagar Setu, carry some of the load, but Howrah Bridge is still the most famous crossing in Kolkata.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Howrah Bridge was the first bridge over the Hooghly.

Truth: Before it, a floating pontoon bridge, opened in 1874, joined Howrah and Calcutta. It had to open to let ships pass and was badly crowded, which is why a new bridge was built.
