Are good manners the same everywhere?

In Japan, slurping your noodles loudly can show you are enjoying them. At many dinner tables in the USA, it would earn you a frown.

The short answer

Some manners are shared by almost everyone. Most people like to be greeted, thanked and offered food when they visit. But the details of how to do those things change from place to place, sometimes in opposite directions.

Slurping noodles, eating with your hand, leaving food on your plate, tipping a waiter, keeping your shoes on indoors: each of these is polite somewhere and rude somewhere else. Manners are a kind of local language. They tell people, without words, that you respect them. Like any language, they have to be learned, and nobody is born knowing the rules.

Surprising but true

In New Zealand, a traditional Māori greeting called the hongi involves two people pressing their noses and foreheads together.

Hands, forks and chopsticks

Across much of India, eating with your right hand is normal, even at weddings and fancy dinners. People mix rice and dal with their fingertips and lift it neatly to the mouth. The left hand stays out of the food. In Ethiopia, diners tear pieces of spongy injera bread and use them to scoop up stews.

In Japan, China and Korea, chopsticks have their own rules. Don't point with them. Don't pass food from one pair to another. Never stand them upright in a bowl of rice, because that looks like a funeral offering.

In much of the USA and Europe, forks and knives rule. Even there, habits differ. Many Americans cut their food, put the knife down and switch the fork to the right hand. Many Europeans keep the fork in the left hand throughout.

Noises and plates

In Japan, slurping hot noodles is fine and even shows you like them. It also cools each mouthful on the way in. In Britain or the USA, children are often told to eat quietly with their mouths closed.

Plates send signals too. In many Indian and American homes, a clean plate tells the cook you enjoyed the meal. In parts of China, leaving a little food can show the host gave you more than enough. The same empty plate can mean "delicious" in one house and "I'm still hungry" in another.

What is daily life like in Japan?In most Japanese schools there are no cleaners. After lunch, the students sweep, mop and scrub their own classrooms, corridors and even toilets.

Hellos and feet

Greetings vary hugely. Indians often say namaste with palms pressed together, and many touch an elder's feet for a blessing. In Japan, people bow, more deeply for someone important. In France, friends often kiss each other lightly on both cheeks. In New Zealand, a traditional Māori greeting called the hongi involves pressing noses and foreheads, sharing a breath. Visitors to a Māori meeting ground may be welcomed this way.

Feet carry strong meanings in parts of Asia. In India, if you accidentally touch a book or a person with your foot, you may touch them with your hand and then touch your own eyes or forehead as an apology. In Thailand, pointing your feet at someone or at a Buddha image is very rude.

Money and time

Tipping confuses travellers. In the USA, restaurant staff often depend on tips, and diners usually add 15–20% to the bill. In Japan, tips are rare, and staff may chase you down the street to return money you "forgot". India falls somewhere in between. In Britain, people are known for queueing patiently, and pushing in is frowned on almost everywhere.

Ideas about time differ as well. A meeting at 10 means 10 sharp in some offices and "around 10" in others. A party invitation for 7 might mean guests arrive at 8. When people travel, these small differences cause most of the awkward moments. Asking someone "What's the custom here?" is nearly always welcome, and it's a habit that respecting other cultures depends on. Even a simple thali meal has its own order and rules, and every family seems to know a slightly different version.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Eating with your hands is messy and bad manners.

Truth: In much of India, Sri Lanka, Ethiopia and the Middle East, eating with your hand is the proper way, with careful rules of its own. You usually use only the right hand, keep the food to your fingertips and wash before and after.

Try this

Make a "manners map". Ask family, friends or classmates from different places one question each, such as "What do you do when you meet an elder?" or "Is it rude to be ten minutes late?" Write each answer on a sticky note and place it on a world map. See how many different answers you get to the same question.
