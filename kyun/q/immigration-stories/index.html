Why do people move to new countries?

More Indians live outside India than people from any other country live outside theirs, about 1.8 crore of them.

The short answer

People move to new countries for many reasons. Most go for work, hoping for better pay or a job that matches their skills. Others go to study, to join family who moved earlier, or to marry. Some have no choice: they flee war, persecution or disaster.

Experts talk about "push" and "pull" factors. Push factors drive people away from home, like poverty, conflict or a failed harvest. Pull factors draw them somewhere new, like jobs, safety, good universities or relatives already there. In 2020, about 28 crore people, roughly 1 in every 28 people on Earth, were living outside the country where they were born.

Surprising but true

More than 12 million immigrants passed through one small island in New York harbour, Ellis Island, between 1892 and 1954.

India on the move

India has the largest group of people living abroad of any country, about 1.8 crore. Many work in the Gulf countries, such as the United Arab Emirates and Saudi Arabia, building, nursing and running businesses. Others live in the USA, the UK, Canada and Australia. Indian workers abroad send home more than 100 billion dollars a year, more money than is sent to any other country. Families use it for houses, school fees and medical bills.

Some Indians who went to study abroad became famous there. Kalpana Chawla moved from Karnal, Haryana, to study in the United States, and later became the first woman of Indian origin to fly in space.

Ships, sugar and indenture

A painful chapter of migration began in 1834. After slavery was abolished in the British Empire, plantation owners needed new workers. Over the next 80 years or so, more than a million Indians signed contracts called indenture. They sailed to Mauritius, Fiji, Trinidad, Guyana, South Africa and other places to work on sugar plantations. Conditions were often harsh, and many could never afford to return.

Almost half a million of them first landed at Aapravasi Ghat in Port Louis, Mauritius, now a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Their descendants make up a large share of the people in several of those countries today. In South Africa, a young lawyer named Mohandas Gandhi first began his fight against unfair treatment of Indians.

How did Gandhi fight without weapons?A shy lawyer who was once thrown off a train in South Africa went on to challenge the British Empire with a spinning wheel and a handful of salt.

A golden door

The United States is often called a nation of immigrants. Between 1892 and 1954, more than 12 million people arrived through Ellis Island in New York harbour, many from Italy, Ireland, Germany and Eastern Europe. As their ships came in, they passed the Statue of Liberty. A poem by Emma Lazarus on its base speaks of welcoming "huddled masses yearning to breathe free".

Today the US has more immigrants than any other country, around 5 crore. New York is home to people speaking hundreds of languages.

Hard choices

Migrants often bring their food, music and festivals with them, and these change their new homes. Chicken tikka masala is a favourite dish in Britain, and Diwali is celebrated in New York's Times Square. In Trinidad, people of Indian descent play chutney music that mixes Bhojpuri songs with Caribbean rhythms.

Moving is rarely easy. Migrants may leave behind parents, friends and familiar food, and face a new language, a new climate and sometimes unkind treatment. Countries argue about how many newcomers to let in and on what terms, and opinions differ widely, in India and the USA alike.

Most migration does not cross borders at all. Far more people move within their own country, especially from villages to growing cities. Almost every family's story, if you go back far enough, includes someone who packed a bag and left home for somewhere new.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Most people who move to another country are refugees.

Truth: Refugees are fewer than one in ten international migrants. Most people move for jobs, studies or family. Also, far more people move within their own country, from villages to cities, than move abroad.

Try this

Ask the adults in your family where your grandparents and great-grandparents were born. Mark each place on a map and draw arrows showing each move. Ask why they moved. Many families find that almost everyone moved at least once, even if only from a village to a city.
