What is gilli-danda?

All you need is a stick and a smaller stick, and children across India have played this game for centuries, with dozens of names for it.

The short answer

Gilli-danda is played with two pieces of wood. The danda is a stick about as long as an arm. The gilli is a short piece, about the length of a hand, sharpened at both ends like a double-pointed pencil.

A player lays the gilli on the ground or across a small dip and taps one end with the danda. It flips up into the air. Then, in a flash, the player swings and whacks it as far as possible. The fielders try to catch it. If they do, the hitter is out. If not, the distance is measured in danda-lengths, and that's the score. It's quick, noisy and needs almost no money.

Surprising but true

Premchand, one of Hindi's most famous writers, wrote a short story called "Gulli-Danda" about two childhood friends who meet again as adults to play.

A game with many names

Gilli-danda is played in villages, lanes and open grounds across South Asia, and every language has its own name. In Maharashtra it's viti-dandu. In Karnataka, chinni-dandu. In Tamil Nadu, kitti-pullu. In Bengal, dang-guli. In parts of north India, it's gulli-danda, and in Pakistan and Nepal it's played too.

Rules change from village to village. Some players get three tries to flip and hit the gilli. Some games let a fielder throw the gilli back at the danda laid on the ground to get the hitter out. Children argue about the rules as fiercely as any cricket umpire.

The physics of the flip

Gilli-danda is a lesson in levers. When you tap one end of the gilli, the ground acts as a pivot, and the gilli spins up like a seesaw. Hit it at the right spot and it rises nearly straight up.

The second hit is the hard part. The gilli is spinning and moving fast, and you have less than a second to swing. Good players can send it tens of metres. The angle matters too: hit it upwards at a moderate angle and it travels farthest, a bit like a javelin throw.

Cousin of cricket and baseball?

Similar games have been played around the world. In England, tip-cat was popular for centuries, with a short wooden "cat" hit with a stick. Italy has lippa, the Philippines has syatong, and versions exist in Cambodia and elsewhere.

Some people say gilli-danda may be an ancestor of cricket, or even of baseball, since all involve hitting an object with a stick and running or measuring. It's an interesting idea, but no written record proves a direct link. Bat-and-ball games probably grew up in many places, the way people everywhere invented tag.

Premchand's story

The Hindi writer Premchand wrote a short story called "Gulli-Danda" in the 1920s. In it, the narrator remembers playing with a poor boy named Gaya who was the best player in the village. Years later, the narrator returns as an engineer, an important man, and plays again. Gaya quietly lets him win, and the narrator realises that the friendship of childhood has been changed by the difference in their status.

The story is read in many Indian schools. It shows how a simple game can hold big feelings.

Is it dangerous?

The gilli can fly fast, and a hit can hurt, especially near the eyes. That's why many parents and schools warn children to play in open spaces and keep spectators well back. Some families now use softer gillis.

Still, gilli-danda remains popular. Some states have included it in festivals of traditional sport. And though gilli-danda isn't played professionally, the same basic skill of watching an object and hitting it hard is at the heart of every cricket bat swing.

Quick facts

Equipment
a danda (stick, about 60 cm) and a gilli (short stick, sharpened at both ends)
Other names
viti-dandu (Marathi), chinni-dandu (Kannada), kitti-pullu (Tamil), dang-guli (Bengali)
Similar games
tip-cat (England), lippa (Italy), syatong (Philippines)
Players
two or more, often in teams
Famous story
Premchand's 'Gulli-Danda'

Myth or fact?

Myth: Gilli-danda is just a poor version of cricket.

Truth: It's an old game in its own right, with its own rules, skills and scoring. Some people even suggest it may be older than cricket, though historians can't prove a direct link.

Try this

With an adult, find a short stick about as long as your hand and a longer one about as long as your arm. Use a soft foam or rolled-paper "gilli" for safety and play in an open space away from windows and people. Practise flipping it up and hitting it, and measure your hits in stick lengths.
