How do mountain rescue teams work?

In 2013, a helicopter pilot plucked a climber off Mount Everest at about 7,800 m, dangling him from a long rope in air so thin the rotors barely bit.

The short answer

Mountain rescue teams find and help people who are lost, hurt or stuck in the mountains. When someone calls for help, the team gathers fast. They work out where the person might be, then go in on foot, on skis, by helicopter or with search dogs.

Once they reach the person, they give first aid, keep them warm and carry them out, often on a stretcher lowered down cliffs by ropes. In the USA and Britain, many rescuers are trained volunteers. In India and Nepal, rescues often involve the army, the air force, local guides and special disaster teams.

Surprising but true

Many mountain rescuers in the USA and Britain are unpaid volunteers who leave their jobs at a moment's notice when the phone rings.

Racing the clock

Mountains punish delay. Cold, injury and thin air all get worse with time. For avalanches, the numbers are stark. Most people buried in snow survive if dug out within about 15 minutes. After about 35 minutes, the chances fall steeply.

So the first rescuers are often the victim's own friends. Skiers and climbers carry avalanche beacons, small radios that beep to show where a buried person lies. They also carry folding probes to poke through the snow and shovels to dig. A good team can find and dig out a person in minutes.

Help from the sky

Helicopters have changed mountain rescue. A flight of 20 minutes can replace a two-day carry. But high up, the air is thin, so rotor blades have less air to push against. Above about 6,000 m, only special helicopters flown by very skilled pilots can manage.

In 2013 a pilot rescued a climber on Everest at about 7,800 m. He hung the climber from a long rope below the helicopter instead of trying to land. In India, the 2013 floods around Kedarnath trapped huge numbers of pilgrims. The Indian Air Force and army flew rescue missions for days, and tens of thousands of people were evacuated.

How does a helicopter fly?A helicopter's blades are really wings, whirling round several times every second.

The Himalayan way

On popular routes in Nepal, the Himalayan Rescue Association runs aid posts staffed by volunteer doctors. Its clinic at Pheriche, on the trail to Everest Base Camp, has treated trekkers suffering from altitude sickness since the 1970s. Every season, the doctors give talks to walkers about the danger signs.

The best cure for altitude sickness is simple: go down. Even a few hundred metres lower can make a big difference, as the science of altitude explains. Sherpa guides and porters, who know the mountains best, often carry out rescues long before any helicopter arrives.

Four-legged rescuers

Search dogs can sniff out a person buried under snow or rubble far faster than a line of people with probes. A trained avalanche dog can search an area in minutes that would take a team hours. Dogs smell the tiny traces of scent that rise through the snow, then dig furiously at the spot. Many ski resorts in the Alps and the Rockies keep dogs on duty all winter, sometimes riding chairlifts with their handlers.

The most famous mountain dog is Barry, a St Bernard who lived at the Great St Bernard Hospice in the Swiss Alps in the early 1800s. Monks there had kept dogs to guide travellers through the snowy pass. Barry is credited with saving about 40 people. His preserved body is now in a museum in Bern.

How you can help rescuers

Rescuers say most rescues could be avoided with simple steps. Tell someone where you are going and when you'll be back. Carry warm clothes, water, a torch and a charged phone. Turn back if the weather turns.

They also ask people to learn basic first aid, because the person who helps first is usually a friend, not a rescuer. Barry was one of many animal heroes, and the stories of dogs who changed history include a few more who saved lives.

Myth or fact?

Myth: St Bernard rescue dogs carried little barrels of brandy around their necks.

Truth: The monks of the Great St Bernard Hospice say their dogs never carried brandy barrels. The idea seems to come from a painting by the English artist Edwin Landseer in the 1820s. Alcohol is also a bad idea for someone who is freezing.

Try this

Play a beacon game. Hide a small ticking kitchen timer or a phone playing quiet music under cushions. With eyes covered, a friend must find it by sound alone while you time them. Now try it with three hidden objects and see how much longer it takes.
