Why can't we quit fossil fuels?

Coal, oil and gas still give the world about four of every five units of its energy. Giving them up means some countries giving up their biggest earner.

The short answer

Coal, oil and gas still give the world about four of every five units of its energy. They run power plants, trucks, ships, planes and factories. They are also cheap to move and easy to store. But burning them is the main cause of global warming.

Stopping is hard because every country is in a different place. Some earn much of their money by selling oil and gas. Some, like India, still need lots of cheap energy to lift people out of poverty. Others, like small island nations, could lose their land to rising seas. At UN climate talks, all of them must agree before anything is decided.

Surprising but true

In 2023, India burned more coal than all of Europe and North America put together, for the first time.

How it works

Fossil fuels are energy that was stored underground over millions of years. A lump of coal or a litre of diesel packs a lot of power into a small space. You can dig it up, ship it across the world and burn it whenever you like, day or night.

That is why the modern world was built around them. Most of the world's electricity has long come from burning coal and gas. Nearly all cars, buses, ships and planes run on oil. Steel, cement and fertiliser factories need huge amounts of heat. Even most plastic starts as oil.

Think of a family that has cooked on one gas stove for 50 years. Now it must move to a new kitchen. The new kitchen may be cleaner and cheaper to run. But someone has to pay for it, and the cook must learn new habits. Meanwhile, dinner must still be served every evening.

Now picture almost 200 countries doing this together. Some export the gas and earn a living from it. Some can barely afford the new stove. And at the UN, nothing is agreed unless every country accepts it. This rule is called consensus.

Where it came from

Coal powered the Industrial Revolution in Britain from the late 1700s. Oil took off after 1859, when one of the first modern oil wells was drilled in Pennsylvania, USA. Cars, planes and plastics followed in the 1900s.

In 1992, countries signed the UN climate treaty. It said every country must act, but with "common but differentiated responsibilities". In plain words, everyone shares the problem, but richer countries that polluted more should do more.

For years, climate deals hardly named fossil fuels at all. At Glasgow in 2021, a draft said coal power should be "phased out". In the final hours, India, backed by China and other developing countries, asked for "phased down" instead. The softer words went in.

At Dubai in 2023, countries agreed on "transitioning away from fossil fuels in energy systems". It was the first time a UN climate summit had agreed to move away from fossil fuels. They also agreed to triple renewable energy by 2030. The deal did not say "phase out", which many countries had wanted.

India's story

India is one of the world's biggest coal users. In 2024–25, it mined more than 1 billion tonnes of coal in a single year for the first time. Around 74% of its electricity came from coal-fired power plants. About 5 lakh people worked in more than 350 coal mines.

Whole towns in states such as Jharkhand, Odisha and Chhattisgarh depend on those mines for jobs. India also buys more than 85% of the crude oil it uses. That oil from abroad keeps its buses and trucks moving.

At Glasgow in 2021, India's environment minister, Bhupender Yadav, put India's view simply. "How can anyone expect that developing countries can make promises about phasing out coal and fossil fuel subsidies?" he asked. India argues that rich countries grew wealthy by burning fossil fuels for well over a century. It says they should cut first, and help pay for poorer countries to switch.

India is also building clean power fast. It has some of the world's largest solar parks, such as Bhadla in Rajasthan. In 2021, it promised to reach net zero emissions by 2070.

The numbers that matter

  • - About 80%: the share of the world's energy that came from coal, oil and gas in the early 2020s. Renewables were growing quickly but supplied only about 15%.
  • - About 24%: the United States' share of all carbon dioxide from fuels and industry since 1750. That is more than any other country. China comes next, at about 15%. India's share is under 4%, though about one in six people on Earth is Indian.
  • - 90%: how much the cost of power from big solar farms fell between 2010 and 2023. The figure comes from the International Renewable Energy Agency. That fall is a big reason solar power is spreading so fast.
  • - About 50 million tonnes: the carbon dioxide all the world's carbon capture plants could trap in a year, around 2025. That is well under 1% of what burning fossil fuels releases.
  • - 1: in theory, the number of countries needed to block a decision at UN climate talks, because every decision needs consensus.
What happens at a world climate summit?Once a year, almost every country on Earth sends people to one city to argue about the weather of the future. Nothing can be agreed unless nearly everyone says yes.

What people argue about

Phase out, phase down or transition away? These words sound alike but mean different things. "Phase out" means ending use completely. "Phase down" means using less over time. "Transition away" means shifting to other energy, usually without a fixed end date. Island nations and the European Union want the strongest words. Many oil producers and some developing countries prefer softer ones.

Who should move first? Small island nations, such as the Marshall Islands and the Maldives, say rising seas threaten their very existence. They want everyone to cut fast. Developing countries, including India, say rich nations caused most of the warming, so those nations should cut first and help others switch.

What about the sellers? Countries such as Saudi Arabia have built their economies on selling oil and gas. They resist promises to end these fuels. Instead, they want room for carbon capture, so fuels can keep being used while their pollution is trapped.

Can carbon capture help? Carbon capture and storage means trapping carbon dioxide from chimneys and burying it deep underground. Supporters, including many oil producers, say it lets the world keep some fuels while cutting pollution. Critics say it is costly, still tiny, and a reason to keep drilling.

Who pays? Switching costs huge sums. Poorer countries say they need grants, not loans, from rich countries. Rich countries say big emitters such as China should help pay too.

Myths and mix-ups

"If a summit fails, nothing changes." Countries keep their own climate plans either way. Solar farms, electric buses and new power lines keep getting built, often because they save money.

"Phase down and phase out mean the same thing." They do not. Phase out means reaching zero. Phase down only means using less. In 2021, that one word was fought over until the last hours of the Glasgow summit.

"Countries that resist phase-out deny the science." Almost every country has joined the Paris Agreement. It is built on climate science. The fight is mostly about speed, fairness, jobs and money.

What it means for you

The fan and lights in your home probably run partly on coal power. Your school bus and the family scooter run on oil. The cooking gas cylinder in many kitchens comes from oil and gas fields. So the world's choices about fossil fuels touch your bills and your daily life.

As India builds more solar and wind power, more of your electricity will come from sunshine and wind. Less coal burning means cleaner air in many towns. Electric scooters and buses are already common in many Indian cities.

Whatever words the world agrees on, the real change happens in power plants, on roads and in kitchens like yours.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The world could stop using fossil fuels quickly if leaders just agreed to.

Truth: Even with full agreement, power plants, cars, factories and power lines take decades to replace. Agreeing is only the first step. Building the new energy system, and paying for it, takes much longer.
