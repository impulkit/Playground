What happens at a book fair?

Every winter, Kolkata holds a book fair so popular that people queue at the gates, eat fish fry between stalls and go home with bags of books.

The short answer

A book fair is a giant market for books. Publishers and booksellers set up stalls, sometimes hundreds or thousands of them, and visitors wander between them, flicking through new titles and old favourites. Authors give talks and sign copies. There are often children's corners, quizzes, music and plenty of food.

There are two main kinds. At trade fairs, like the one in Frankfurt, publishers mostly do business with each other, buying and selling the rights to print books in other countries. At public fairs, like the ones in Kolkata and New Delhi, the main visitors are readers, families and school groups who come to buy and browse.

Surprising but true

In 1997, a fire destroyed many stalls at the Kolkata Book Fair. The fair reopened within days, and readers turned up in huge numbers to support it.

Frankfurt and the printing press

Soon after Johannes Gutenberg began printing with movable type in Mainz in the 1450s, printers began taking their books to the trade fairs in nearby Frankfurt. Booksellers came from all over Europe to buy them. The printing press had made books cheaper, and Frankfurt became the place to sell them.

Today's Frankfurt Book Fair was restarted in 1949, after the Second World War. Each October, thousands of publishers from more than 100 countries meet there. The main business is rights. A deal made over coffee in Frankfurt can mean a Hindi novel being read in Spanish a year later, thanks to translation.

Kolkata's winter festival of books

The International Kolkata Book Fair began in 1976 and has grown into one of the biggest public book fairs in the world. Held over about two weeks in late January and early February, it draws visitors in the millions. People in Kolkata talk about it the way some cities talk about a cricket match.

Each year a different country is the theme, and its pavilion is often designed to look like a famous building. Bengali publishers launch new titles, little magazines sell poetry for a few rupees, and students argue about authors over cups of tea. When a fire destroyed many stalls in 1997, the fair reopened within days.

The New Delhi World Book Fair

India's other giant fair, the New Delhi World Book Fair, has been organised by the National Book Trust since 1972. It is held at the exhibition grounds at Pragati Maidan in Delhi, now home to a new convention centre called Bharat Mandapam. Publishers come from India and abroad, and books are sold in English, Hindi, Urdu, Tamil, Bengali and many other languages.

School groups arrive by the busload. Children's halls are packed with comics, picture books and science kits, and storytellers perform for small crowds sitting on the floor.

Book fairs in schools

In the USA, many children meet their first book fair in the school library. Scholastic, a children's publisher, has run book fairs in American schools since 1981. Cases on wheels are unfolded into shelves, and children browse during class time with a little money from home. Many adults remember the smell of new books and the shiny posters and pencils sold alongside them.

Italy hosts the Bologna Children's Book Fair, started in 1964, where illustrators and children's publishers from around the world trade ideas and rights. A picture book drawn in Bengaluru may find a publisher in Brazil there.

Why fairs still matter

Online shops can deliver almost any book. But fairs let you discover books you would never have searched for. Small publishers, poets and translators get a stall next to the big names. And for many families, a day at the fair, with a full bag of books and a plate of snacks at the end, is a tradition that has lasted generations.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Book fairs are only for people who already love reading.

Truth: Book fairs are planned with music, talks, food, quizzes and children's corners, partly to draw in people who don't think of themselves as readers. Many children pick up their first favourite series at one.

Try this

Hold a book swap with friends or classmates. Each person brings one or two books they have finished with and writes a one-line review on a slip of paper. Lay them out on a table like stalls, and let everyone take as many books as they brought.
