How do climbers scale cliffs?

The fastest speed climbers race up a 15-metre wall, as tall as a five-storey building, in under five seconds.

The short answer

Climbers scale cliffs by finding small holds for their hands and feet: cracks, ledges, bumps and pockets in the rock. They push up with their legs, balance their weight over their feet and use their arms mostly to stay close to the wall. Sticky rubber shoes and chalked hands give them grip.

For safety, most climbers use a rope tied to a harness. As they climb, they clip the rope into metal bolts or devices jammed into cracks. A partner on the ground, called a belayer, holds the rope with a braking device. If the climber slips, they fall only a short way before the rope catches them.

Surprising but true

Climbing chalk is not school chalk at all. It is magnesium carbonate, the same powder gymnasts and weightlifters use to keep their hands dry.

Grip from rubber and powder

Climbing shoes are tight, flat and made of special rubber that grips like a pencil eraser on paper. A good climber can stand on a ledge narrower than a coin. That is friction doing the work. The more weight pressed onto the rubber, the better it holds.

Hands sweat, and sweat makes rock slippery. So climbers carry a bag of chalk on their belt. The white powder is magnesium carbonate, which soaks up moisture. Look at a popular cliff and you can spot the routes by the white smudges left on the holds.

Three ways to climb a wall

The Olympics split climbing into three kinds. In bouldering, climbers tackle short walls about 4 metres high with no ropes, only thick crash mats below. Each "problem" is a puzzle of a few hard moves. In lead climbing, climbers clip a rope as they go up a tall wall and try to get as high as possible in six minutes.

Speed climbing is a straight race. Two climbers go side by side up identical 15-metre walls that lean out at 5 degrees. The holds are always in the same places, all over the world, so climbers can practise the exact route. In 2026, China's Zhao Yicheng set the men's record at 4.54 seconds. America's Emma Hunt set the women's record at 5.99 seconds. That's faster than most people can climb a ladder one storey high.

The big walls

In California's Yosemite valley stands El Capitan, a smooth granite wall about 900 metres high. That is taller than the Burj Khalifa, the world's tallest building. Most teams take three to five days to climb it. They sleep in hanging tents called portaledges, bolted to the cliff, with hundreds of metres of air below.

In 2017, Alex Honnold climbed El Capitan with no rope at all, in under four hours. This is called free soloing, and almost no one tries it, because a single mistake would be deadly. Most climbers, even world champions, prefer a rope.

Climbing in India

India has some superb rock. Hampi in Karnataka is famous for its giant granite boulders, piled up around ancient temple ruins. Climbers from around the world come to try its short, tricky problems in the winter months. Badami's red sandstone cliffs and the rocky hills near Bengaluru also draw climbers.

Indoor climbing gyms have opened in cities like Mumbai, Delhi, Pune and Bengaluru. Many schools now have small walls too. Indian climbers compete at Asian and world events, and the sport is growing fast.

Learning from animals

The best climbers on Earth aren't human. Mountain goats balance on cliff ledges as narrow as a hand. Geckos run up glass and across ceilings with no claws or glue, using millions of tiny hairs on their toes. Engineers are copying gecko feet to make sticky pads for robots that might one day climb buildings without any rope at all.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Climbing is mostly about having strong arms.

Truth: Good climbers push mostly with their legs and keep their arms straight to save energy. Balance, footwork and planning a route matter as much as strength.

Try this

Test grip and friction at home. Put a rubber-soled shoe and a sock on a smooth board, then slowly lift one end of the board. Note the angle at which each one starts to slide. Rubber holds on much steeper slopes, which is why climbing shoes are made of extra sticky rubber.
