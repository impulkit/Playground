How does a pufferfish puff up?

When danger comes, a pufferfish gulps water so fast that in about 15 seconds it swells into a ball two or three times its normal size.

The short answer

A pufferfish puffs up by swallowing water, fast. It pumps water into a stomach that can stretch to many times its normal size. In about 15 seconds, a long, ordinary-looking fish becomes a round ball two or three times bigger.

This makes it hard for a predator to swallow. Trying to gulp it down would be like trying to bite into a football. Many pufferfish also have spines that stick out when they inflate, and most carry a deadly poison. Together, these defences mean very few animals try to eat them twice.

Surprising but true

Many pufferfish carry a poison called tetrodotoxin that is far deadlier than cyanide, yet the fish don't make it themselves. It comes from bacteria in their food.

How the puff works

A pufferfish's stomach is folded like an accordion. When it is scared, it opens its mouth and throat and uses strong muscles to pump in water, gulp after gulp. A special valve stops the water flowing back out.

To make room, the fish has unusual skin and bones. Its skin is very stretchy and folded. It has no ribs, which in most fish would hold the body in shape and block the stomach from swelling. Its spine can bend into a curve to make space too.

When the danger has passed, it pushes the water out through its mouth and shrinks back. Puffing takes a lot of energy, and a pufferfish may take hours to fully recover.

A poison from bacteria

Most pufferfish carry tetrodotoxin, one of the strongest natural poisons known. It blocks the signals that nerves use to talk to muscles. A tiny amount can stop someone breathing. It is often described as more than a thousand times deadlier than cyanide.

The fish doesn't make the poison itself. Bacteria make it, and the pufferfish builds it up by eating shellfish and other animals that carry those bacteria. Pufferfish raised in tanks on clean food are often not poisonous at all.

The same poison is found in blue-ringed octopuses and some newts. This is a poison, not a venom, because it harms you if you eat it rather than if it bites you.

Dangerous dinner

In Japan, pufferfish is a famous and expensive dish called fugu. Only specially trained chefs may prepare it. They study for years and pass difficult tests, learning to remove the poisonous liver, ovaries and skin.

Even so, a few people get sick each year, usually after trying to prepare it at home. In parts of India, the US and elsewhere, people also catch pufferfish, and doctors warn that eating them without expert preparation can be deadly.

A beak for crunching

Pufferfish have four large teeth fused into a strong beak, two on top and two below. That is where their family name comes from: Tetraodontidae means "four teeth". They use the beak to crack crabs, clams, snails and sea urchins. Some species nibble on coral reefs, scraping off the tiny animals and algae that grow there.

The teeth never stop growing. Crunching hard shells keeps them worn down, a bit like a rabbit's teeth.

Underwater artists

In 1995, divers off Amami Ōshima in southern Japan found strange circles in the sand on the seabed, about 2 m across, with ridges and grooves like a flower. For years, nobody knew who made them.

Around 2011, scientists finally filmed the answer. A small male white-spotted pufferfish, only about 12 cm long, swims back and forth for about a week, flapping its fins to shape the sand. Females visit the circle and lay eggs in the middle.

The patterns are so neat and regular that people once guessed they were made by humans. Nature is full of such patterns, from the spiral of a snail to the six sides of a honeycomb, as the maths of nature's shells shows.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pufferfish should be helped to puff up because it is fun to watch.

Truth: Puffing is a stress response and uses a lot of energy. Divers and aquarium keepers are asked never to make pufferfish inflate, and lifting one out of the water can make it gulp air instead, which can harm it.
