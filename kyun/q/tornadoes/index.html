How does a tornado start spinning?

The USA gets more tornadoes than any other country, over a thousand a year.

The short answer

A tornado is a spinning column of air that reaches from a thunderstorm down to the ground. It starts when winds blow at different speeds and directions at different heights. The air between them begins to roll, like a pencil rolled between your hands.

A big thunderstorm's rising air can tip this rolling air upright. If it tightens and touches the ground, it becomes a tornado.

Surprising but true

The strongest tornado winds can top 400 km/h, faster than a Formula 1 car.

From roll to funnel

The most dangerous tornadoes come from giant, long-lasting thunderstorms called supercells. Inside them, the whole column of rising air slowly rotates. On weather radar, this rotation shows up as a hook shape at one corner of the storm.

As the spinning air stretches and narrows, it spins faster, the way a figure skater speeds up by pulling in their arms. Often a funnel of cloud drops from the base of the storm. Once the spinning reaches the ground, it's officially a tornado. Even so, most rotating storms never make one, and scientists are still working out why some do.

Tornado Alley

The central USA is perfect for tornadoes. Warm, wet air flows north from the Gulf of Mexico, while cold, dry air comes down from Canada and the Rocky Mountains. When they crash together in spring, giant storms grow.

That stretch of flat land, from Texas up through Oklahoma and Kansas, is nicknamed Tornado Alley. No mountains stand in the way, so the air masses meet head-on. Many homes there have storm cellars, and schools practise tornado drills the way schools in Japan or Gujarat practise earthquake drills.

How strong?

Tornadoes are rated from EF0 to EF5. The strongest have winds over 400 km/h and can flatten houses. Most last just a few minutes. Luckily, about three-quarters of tornadoes are weak, rated EF0 or EF1, and the most violent ones are rare.

The scale was first designed in 1971 by Ted Fujita, a Japanese-American scientist who studied storm damage in great detail. Since nobody can easily put instruments inside a tornado, ratings are based on what it destroys. In 1999, a radar truck near Oklahoma City measured winds of about 480 km/h inside one tornado, among the fastest ever recorded on Earth.

Warnings and radar

In the USA, the Storm Prediction Center in Norman, Oklahoma, watches for the right ingredients every day. When conditions look risky, it issues a tornado "watch". When radar shows rotation or someone spots a funnel, local forecasters issue a "warning", and sirens and phones go off. Better radar and weather forecasts have given people precious minutes to take shelter.

Forecasters talk in chances, not certainties. A "30% chance of tornadoes" means that in many similar situations, about three in ten produce one nearby.

Beyond the USA

Tornadoes happen on every continent except Antarctica. Bangladesh, with its flat land and warm, wet air from the Bay of Bengal, gets some powerful ones. In 1989, a tornado near Dhaka was one of the deadliest ever recorded. India gets tornadoes now and then too, mostly in the east, and waterspouts sometimes spin over the sea.

Not a hurricane

A tornado is small, usually less than a kilometre wide, and lasts minutes. A hurricane can be hundreds of kilometres wide and last for days.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Opening windows during a tornado stops a house from exploding.

Truth: This old idea has been tested and rejected. Opening windows wastes precious time and lets in wind and flying debris. The safe thing is to get to a basement or a small room in the middle of the lowest floor, away from windows.

Try this

Fill a clear plastic bottle about three-quarters full of water and add a drop of washing-up liquid. Screw the lid on tightly. Hold it upside down and swirl it quickly in circles for a few seconds, then stop. A spinning funnel forms inside, narrow at the bottom, just like the shape of a tornado.
