How does pumping your legs make a swing go higher?

In a cathedral in Spain, eight men swing a giant incense burner, as heavy as a grown man, almost to the ceiling, using the same trick you use on a playground swing.

The short answer

A swing is a pendulum: a weight hanging from a fixed point, free to rock back and forth. Left alone, it slowly loses energy to air and friction and comes to a stop.

When you pump, you add energy back in. Sitting down, you lean back and stretch your legs out as you swing forward, then sit up and tuck your legs under as you swing back. Standing up, you crouch at the high points and stand up tall as you whoosh through the bottom.

Each time, you shift your body's weight closer to or farther from the top bar, at just the right moments. That small push of your own muscles, timed with the swing, adds up to bigger and bigger arcs.

Surprising but true

A swing takes the same time to go back and forth whether a small child or a heavy adult sits on it. Only the length of the ropes changes the timing.

The magic of the pendulum

Around 1600, the Italian scientist Galileo noticed something odd about pendulums. A popular story says he watched a lamp swinging in Pisa cathedral and timed it with his pulse. Whether or not that happened, his experiments showed that a pendulum keeps nearly the same rhythm, whether it swings wide or small.

The time depends on the length of the rope. A swing with ropes about 2 metres long takes roughly 2.8 seconds to go forward and back. A longer swing is slower. The rider's weight makes almost no difference, because gravity pulls harder on heavier things but they are harder to move by the same amount.

Pendulums keep time so well that, for nearly 300 years, the best clocks in the world used them.

Why standing pumping works

The standing trick is the easiest to understand. When you crouch at the top of the swing, your body is far from the top bar. As you rush down, you stand up straight, pulling yourself closer to the bar right at the bottom, where you are moving fastest.

Standing up there takes work, since your legs must push against both gravity and the swing's outward pull. That work goes into the swing as extra energy. Then you crouch again at the next high point, when moving costs almost nothing. Do it twice per full back-and-forth swing and you climb higher each time.

Who was Galileo?In January 1610, an Italian professor pointed a home-made telescope at Jupiter and saw four tiny dots that changed how people saw the universe.

Why sitting pumping works

Seated pumping is a little different. When you lean back and kick your legs forward, your body rotates around the seat. That twisting pushes on the ropes and gives the swing a small extra shove in the direction it is already going.

The timing is everything. Lean back too early or too late and you fight the swing instead of helping it. Children usually learn the rhythm by feel, long before anyone explains it. Physicists have written whole research papers about it.

Festival swings and a flying incense burner

Swings are part of festivals across India. In the monsoon, during Teej, swings are hung from tree branches and decorated with flowers. In Kerala, swings called oonjal are put up for Onam. Many stories show the young Krishna on a swing.

In Spain, the cathedral of Santiago de Compostela has the Botafumeiro, a giant silver-coloured incense burner of about 53 kg hanging from ropes about 20 metres long. A team of men called tiraboleiros pull on the rope in rhythm, shortening it at the bottom of each swing and letting it out at the top. Within a few minutes it swings almost from wall to wall, reaching about 65 km/h. It is exactly the standing-pumping trick, done by a team.

The same timing trick turns up everywhere. Pumping a swing is a cousin of bouncing on a trampoline: in both, a well-timed push at the right moment adds a little energy each time, until you are flying.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You can only go high on a swing if somebody pushes you.

Truth: A rider can pump a swing from standing still, just by moving their body at the right moments. A push helps get started, but good pumping alone can take you very high.

Try this

Tie a small weight, such as a key or a washer, to a piece of string and let it swing from your hand. Count how many swings it makes in 15 seconds. Then try a heavier weight on the same length of string, and then a shorter string. Which change makes a difference?
