When did people start using forks?

When a Byzantine bride ate with a little golden fork in Venice about a thousand years ago, a church writer called it shocking vanity.

The short answer

People have eaten with knives and spoons for thousands of years, but the table fork is a newcomer. Romans had large forks for serving and cooking, and small eating forks were used in the Byzantine Empire, the eastern half of the old Roman world. From there they reached Italy around the year 1000.

The rest of Europe took centuries to catch on. Forks were rare in England until the late 1600s and only became normal in the USA in the 1800s. Meanwhile, much of the world never needed them at all: around a fifth of people eat with chopsticks, and many more eat with their hands.

Surprising but true

Knives are about 2.5 million years older than forks. The first ones were sharp stone flakes used by early humans to cut meat.

Knife first, by a very long way

The knife is the oldest eating tool there is. Early humans in East Africa were chipping sharp stone flakes about 2.5 million years ago and using them to slice meat off bones. Spoons came much later, but they are old too. People scooped with shells, gourds and bits of bone, and ancient Egyptians carved fine spoons from wood and ivory.

For most of history in Europe, guests brought their own knife to dinner. They speared food with its point and used fingers and bread for everything else. Medieval feasts often served food on thick slices of stale bread called trenchers, which soaked up the gravy.

Why do people eat with chopsticks?The oldest chopsticks ever found are made of bronze and are over 3,000 years old, and they were probably used for cooking, not eating.

The scandal in Venice

Around the year 1004, a Byzantine princess married the son of the ruler of Venice. At the wedding feast she would not touch food with her fingers. Instead, a servant cut it up and she ate using a little two-pronged golden fork.

A churchman called Peter Damian later wrote about her with disgust, calling such delicacy a sin of pride. When she died young of an illness, some writers hinted it was a punishment. Forks, at the time, looked like a silly luxury from the East.

Laughing at Mr Coryat

Italians adopted forks anyway, partly because they ate so much slippery pasta. In 1608 an Englishman named Thomas Coryat walked across Europe and was amazed to see Italians using forks. He brought the habit home and described it in his travel book of 1611. His friends mocked him for it.

The early forks had two straight prongs, good for holding meat still while you cut. Food kept sliding off, though. By the 1700s, makers in Germany and France were adding a third and fourth prong and curving them slightly, until a fork could scoop as well as stab.

The American zig-zag

Forks arrived in the American colonies late, and for years many families ate with a knife and a big spoon. When forks did spread in the 1800s, Americans developed their own habit. They cut food with the knife in the right hand, put the knife down, then swap the fork into the right hand to eat. Europeans keep the fork in the left hand throughout. Etiquette writers still argue over which is proper.

Cheap steel and factory production finally put forks in every kitchen drawer. Stainless steel, invented in the early 1900s, made them shiny and rust-free.

Cutlery in India

In India, eating with the right hand remained the normal way for most meals, and still is. Spoons were used for serving and for some foods, and the British brought the full set of knives and forks into colonial clubs and hotels. Today many Indian families switch happily: fingers for rice and dal, a spoon for curd, a fork for noodles.

That choice isn't just habit. Some people swear food tastes better eaten by hand, and there is a little science behind the idea, explored in eating with your hands.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Catherine de' Medici brought the fork to France when she married the future French king in 1533.

Truth: This story is told everywhere, but historians have found little evidence for it. Forks spread through France slowly, over more than a century, and nobody can pin it on one person.

Try this

Eat one meal at home with only a spoon, and one with only a fork (with a grown-up's OK). Notice which foods get hard. Then think about which tool was invented to solve which problem. Peas, noodles and rice will tell you a lot.
