How do bats see in the dark?

They shout, and listen to the echoes.

The short answer

Echolocation means finding things by listening to echoes. A bat flying at night sends out short, loud calls through its mouth or nose. The sound bounces off a moth, a branch or a wall, and the bat's large ears catch the echo.

From that echo, the bat works out how far away something is, how big it is, which way it is moving and even what its surface is like. All this happens in a flash. Most bat calls are ultrasound, pitched too high for human ears. If we could hear them, a summer evening would be surprisingly noisy.

Surprising but true

Some blind people have learned to echolocate by clicking their tongues and listening to the echoes from walls, cars and trees.

An old puzzle, solved

In the 1790s, an Italian scientist named Lazzaro Spallanzani noticed something odd. Bats that could not see still flew around obstacles. But bats with their ears plugged crashed. He could not explain how ears could help a bat "see".

The answer came in the late 1930s. At Harvard University, a student named Donald Griffin brought bats to a physicist who had a machine that could detect ultrasound. Suddenly the silent bats were full of calls. At first, many scientists doubted him. It seemed impossible that a small animal could make such fast judgements from sound. In 1944 Griffin gave the skill its name, echolocation.

Closing in

A hunting bat starts with slow calls to scan the air. Once it locks on to a moth, it calls faster and faster, up to about 200 times a second. Scientists call this the "feeding buzz". The fast calls give the bat a quick stream of updates as the moth dodges. At the last moment, some bats scoop the insect into a pouch of skin between their back legs, or catch it with a wingtip, and eat it in flight.

Some moths fight back. Many can hear bat calls and dive out of the way. Some tiger moths make clicks of their own, which seem to confuse the bat's sonar. How fast sound moves matters here, and you can read more in how sound travels.

Different calls for different jobs

Bats tune their calls to where they hunt. Bats that chase insects in open sky use long, lower calls that carry far. Bats that hunt among leaves use short, sharp calls, so the echoes don't blur together. Horseshoe bats call through their noses, and a fleshy, leaf-shaped flap around the nostrils helps focus the sound into a beam, like the light from a torch.

Each kind of bat has its own style of call. Scientists use bat detectors, which turn ultrasound into sounds we can hear, to work out which bats are flying over a park at night. A few birds echolocate too. Swiftlets that nest in dark caves in the Andaman Islands, and oilbirds in South America, find their way with clicks that people can hear.

Echoes in water, and in people

Sound travels well underwater, so many toothed whales use it too. Dolphins send clicks through a fatty bump on their forehead. India's national aquatic animal, the Ganges river dolphin, has tiny eyes and lives in muddy water. It depends almost entirely on echolocation to find fish.

Some blind people have taught themselves to do something similar. The American Daniel Kish, blind since he was a baby, clicks his tongue and listens to the echoes. He can ride a bicycle and hike this way, and he teaches others. Ships and submarines use the same idea, called sonar, to measure depth and find objects in the dark sea.

Myth or fact?

Myth: All bats find their way using echoes.

Truth: Most small insect-eating bats echolocate, but most big fruit bats do not. They rely on good eyes and a strong sense of smell instead.

Try this

Stand about 20 big steps from a tall wall, such as a school building, with an adult. Clap loudly once and listen for the echo. Walk closer and clap again. Notice how the gap between clap and echo shrinks as you get nearer.
