How do modern books retell old myths?

The Greek gods have moved to New York. In Rick Riordan's books, Mount Olympus floats above the Empire State Building, on the 600th floor.

The short answer

Modern writers retell old myths by bringing the gods, monsters and heroes into new settings, often into today's world. They keep the old stories' bones and change the clothes.

In Rick Riordan's Percy Jackson books, the Greek gods are alive and living in America. Percy, a 12-year-old in New York, finds out that his father is Poseidon, god of the sea. He goes to a summer camp for children of gods, battles monsters from Greek myths and travels across the USA on quests.

Retelling myths is not new. People have done it for thousands of years. What modern writers add is humour, modern problems and heroes young readers can recognise.

Surprising but true

Percy Jackson began as bedtime stories for Rick Riordan's son, who had ADHD and dyslexia. Riordan gave Percy the same, and made them signs that he was a demigod.

Bedtime stories for a son

Rick Riordan was a middle-school English and history teacher in California and Texas. His son Haley had ADHD and dyslexia and found school hard, but he loved Greek myths. When Riordan ran out of myths to tell at bedtime, Haley asked him to make up new ones.

So Riordan invented Percy Jackson, a boy with ADHD and dyslexia. In the books, these are signs that he is a demigod. His restlessness is a fighter's battle reflexes. His dyslexia happens because his brain is wired to read ancient Greek, not English. The Lightning Thief came out in 2005, and four more books followed by 2009.

Gods with phones and bad tempers

Riordan's gods are funny, vain and sometimes terrible parents, just like in the old stories. Mount Olympus floats above the Empire State Building in New York. The underworld's entrance is in Los Angeles.

But the monsters and myths are real, taken from ancient sources. Medusa still turns people to stone, though she runs a garden-gnome shop. Readers learn who the Minotaur, the Fates and the Titans were while laughing.

Riordan went on to write series based on Roman, Egyptian and Norse myths, and books retelling Greek myths in Percy's own voice. The first books were made into films in 2010 and 2013. Riordan was unhappy with them, and he worked closely on a later TV series that began in 2023 and stayed nearer to the books.

An ancient habit

Myths have always been retold. In ancient Greece, poets such as Homer and Hesiod, and later the playwrights of Athens, told the same stories in different ways. The Roman poet Ovid retold hundreds of Greek myths in his Metamorphoses, around 8 CE, and changed many of them.

In India, the Ramayana and Mahabharata have been retold in nearly every Indian language, in songs, dances, plays, TV serials and films. Amar Chitra Katha turned them into comics. Writers like Devdutt Pattanaik retell them for modern readers, and the 1980s TV Ramayan drew huge audiences across India.

Your myth, your voice

In 2018, Riordan started a publishing imprint, Rick Riordan Presents, for authors from different cultures to retell their own myths. The first book was Aru Shah and the End of Time by Roshani Chokshi. In it, a 12-year-old Indian-American girl in Atlanta accidentally frees an ancient demon. Then she discovers she is the reincarnation of one of the Pandava brothers from the Mahabharata. Another series, by Sayantani DasGupta, draws on Bengali folk tales.

Other authors have retold Norse, Mayan, Korean and West African myths for young readers. Each new version changes the old story a little. That is exactly how myths have survived so long: by being told again, to new listeners, in new ways.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Each old myth has one true, fixed version.

Truth: Myths were told and retold for centuries, and often exist in many versions. The Ramayana alone has hundreds of retellings across Asia. Modern authors like Riordan are joining a very old tradition.
