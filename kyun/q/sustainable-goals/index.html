What are the Sustainable Development Goals?

In 2015, every country in the United Nations agreed to the same to-do list for the planet, with 17 items and a deadline of 2030.

The short answer

The Sustainable Development Goals, often called the Global Goals, are a list of 17 aims that all 193 member countries of the United Nations agreed on in September 2015. Together they describe the kind of world countries promised to build by 2030.

The first goal is to end poverty everywhere. Others include zero hunger, good health, quality education, equal rights for girls and women, clean water and toilets, clean energy, and action on climate change. The last goal is about countries working together, because none of the others can be met by one country alone.

"Sustainable" means meeting people's needs today without wrecking the chances of people who come later.

Surprising but true

The 17 goals are broken into 169 smaller targets, and the UN's own 2024 report found that only about one in six of them was on track.

A to-do list with 169 boxes

Each big goal is split into smaller targets, 169 in all. Goal 4, quality education, includes a target that all girls and boys finish primary and secondary school. Goal 6 includes clean drinking water for everyone.

Each target also has numbers that can be measured, such as how many children are in school or how many homes have a toilet. Every year the UN publishes a report on how the world is doing. That turns a list of hopes into something closer to a school report card.

The goals are not laws. No country is punished for missing them. They work more like a promise made in public, which governments, charities and companies can be held to.

Many targets are linked. Clean water cuts disease, healthier children miss fewer school days, and children who finish school tend to earn more as adults. Progress on one goal often helps several others, and failure on one can drag the rest down.

Where the goals came from

They replaced an earlier list, the Millennium Development Goals, which ran from 2000 to 2015. That list had 8 goals and focused mainly on poorer countries. Some of it went well. The share of the world's people living in extreme poverty fell sharply during those years.

For the new list, the UN held talks and surveys that asked millions of people around the world what mattered to them. The result was longer and broader. This time, rich countries like the USA had goals to meet too, on things like waste, fair pay and pollution.

The goals even got their own look: 17 bright coloured squares, each with a simple icon, which you may have spotted on school walls.

How India keeps score

India's planning body, NITI Aayog, began ranking states on the goals in 2018 with its SDG India Index. Each state gets a score out of 100 for things like health, schooling, clean energy and hunger. Kerala has often come out near the top.

Some Indian programmes connect directly to the goals. The free mid-day meal in government schools helps with hunger, health and education all at once. A child who eats lunch at school is more likely to keep coming to class.

Halfway and behind

By 2024 the deadline was getting close. The UN's report that year found that only about 17% of the targets were on track. The COVID-19 pandemic, wars and rising food prices had pushed many countries backwards.

That does not make the goals useless. Targets give people something to measure, argue about and push for. A village that gets clean drinking water, or a girl who stays in school for two more years, counts toward the list whether or not the whole world finishes on time.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Sustainable Development Goals are only about the environment.

Truth: Only some of the goals are about nature, like climate, oceans and forests. Others are about ending hunger, getting girls into school, decent jobs, fair courts and safe cities.

Try this

Look at the list of 17 goals with your family or class. Pick one, such as clean water or less waste, and keep a one-week diary of every time it touches your own day. At the end, choose one small thing your home or classroom could change.
