How does a washing machine clean clothes?

A washing machine does what people once did by hand at the river, using water, soap, rubbing and time.

The short answer

A washing machine cleans clothes by doing four jobs at once. It soaks them in water. It adds detergent, a kind of soap that loosens dirt and grease. It moves the clothes around so they rub against each other and the water. And it gives all this enough time to work. Many machines also heat the water, because warm water helps dissolve grease.

When washing is done, the machine drains the dirty water, rinses the clothes in clean water, and then spins them very fast to throw most of the water out.

People did the same things for thousands of years, scrubbing and beating clothes on stones by rivers. The machine just does the hard work for you.

Surprising but true

In a fast spin, the drum of a front-loading machine can turn more than 1,000 times a minute, flinging water out through hundreds of tiny holes.

How detergent grabs dirt

Water alone is not good at removing grease, because oil and water don't mix. Detergent solves this. Each detergent molecule is long and thin. One end loves water. The other end hates water but loves oil and grease.

When you wash, the oil-loving ends dig into greasy dirt, while the water-loving ends stick out into the water. Thousands of molecules surround each speck of grease, lift it off the cloth and keep it floating so it can be rinsed away. This is the same trick that makes soap pull germs off your hands. Modern detergents also contain enzymes that break down stains such as egg, blood and grass.

Tumbling and churning

The two main kinds of machine move clothes differently. In a top-loader, you drop clothes in from above. A spinning post or disc at the bottom churns them round and round in deep water.

In a front-loader, the drum lies on its side like a rolling barrel. As it turns, paddles lift the clothes to the top, and they drop back down into a shallow pool of water. That tumbling, over and over, does the scrubbing. Front-loaders usually use less water and energy, though they take longer.

The rubbing matters. Friction between fibres and water knocks dirt loose, which is why a quick rinse with no movement leaves clothes still dirty.

The spin

At the end, the drum spins very fast, often more than 1,000 times a minute in a front-loader. The clothes are pressed against the drum wall. The water, which tends to keep moving in a straight line, escapes through hundreds of small holes and is pumped away. Clothes come out damp, not dripping, so they dry much faster on the line.

Washing by hand in Mumbai

Not everyone uses machines. At Mahalaxmi Dhobi Ghat in Mumbai, an open-air laundry more than a hundred years old, hundreds of washers called dhobis still scrub and beat clothes in rows of concrete washing pens. Hotels and hospitals send them sheets by the sackful. Visitors watch from a bridge above.

In many homes across the world, washing is still done by hand, often by women and girls. The Swedish doctor Hans Rosling, in a famous talk in 2010, described the day his family got a washing machine. He said it gave his mother hours each week to read and to take him to the library. Some economists and historians argue that household machines like this changed women's lives as much as many laws did, freeing time for school and paid work.

The machine needs a lot of water and electricity, though. In places where water is scarce, a bucket and a scrubbing brush may still make more sense.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The more detergent you use, the cleaner your clothes will be.

Truth: Extra detergent doesn't clean better. It can leave a sticky film on clothes, make too many suds and need extra rinsing. Following the amount on the packet works best.

Try this

Put a few drops of cooking oil in a jar of water, close the lid tightly and shake. The oil soon floats back up in blobs. Now add a drop of washing-up liquid and shake again. Watch how the oil breaks into tiny droplets that stay mixed in, just like grease leaving your clothes.
