How do you guess a number well?

In 1906, about 800 people at a country fair guessed the weight of an ox. None of them got it exactly, but the middle guess was almost perfect.

The short answer

Estimating means finding a number that is close enough to be useful, when counting exactly would be too slow or impossible.

The trick is not to guess the whole answer at once. Break the big question into smaller ones that you can guess sensibly. How many classrooms are in your school? How many children in each? Multiply, and you have a decent estimate of the whole school.

Round your numbers to make the sums easy. Thirty-two children per class becomes 30. Eleven classes becomes 10. You aren't aiming to be exact. You want to be in the right range, not wrong by ten times.

Surprising but true

At a 1906 fair in England, the middle guess of a crowd was within about 1% of the ox's real weight, closer than most of the individual guesses.

The physicist who estimated everything

The Italian-American physicist Enrico Fermi, who won the Nobel Prize in 1938, loved this kind of question. He would ask his students things like "How many piano tuners are there in Chicago?"

Nobody knows that number offhand. But you can reason it out. Guess how many people live in Chicago, how many homes have pianos, how often a piano needs tuning and how many pianos one tuner can manage in a year. Multiply and divide, and you get an answer that is often surprisingly close.

Questions like this are now called Fermi problems. Scientists, engineers and business people use them to check whether an idea is even possible before spending time on it.

Counting your heartbeats

Here is a Fermi problem you can do in your head. How many times will your heart beat in your life?

A resting heart beats about 70 times a minute. Round it to 70. There are 60 minutes in an hour, so that's about 4,000 beats an hour. There are 24 hours in a day, so about 1 lakh (100,000) beats a day. A year has about 365 days, so roughly 3.5 crore beats a year.

If you live 80 years, that's about 300 crore, or 3 billion, heartbeats. The real number depends on your heart and how active you are, but the estimate is in the right ballpark.

The wisdom of the crowd

In 1906, the English scientist Francis Galton visited a farm fair in Plymouth. Visitors paid a small fee to guess the weight of an ox, with prizes for the closest. Galton collected about 800 tickets.

Some guessers were butchers and farmers. Most were ordinary visitors who knew little about cattle. Galton expected the crowd to be poor at it. Instead, when he lined up all the guesses, the middle one was 1,207 pounds. The ox weighed 1,198 pounds. The crowd's median guess was off by less than 1%.

The lesson is that many independent guesses, each wrong in different ways, can average out to something close to the truth. It works best when people guess on their own without copying each other.

Estimating crowds and countries

Police and event organisers estimate crowds at festivals and marches. They work out the area of a space, then guess how many people fit into each square metre. Loosely packed crowds might have one or two people per square metre. Very tightly packed ones can reach four or more.

Governments sometimes estimate population between censuses using birth and death records. A full census is the careful count that checks those estimates.

Checking with a quick guess

Estimating is also a way of catching mistakes. If you work out a shopping bill and get ₹4,500 for a few vegetables, a quick estimate tells you something has gone wrong. Engineers do the same with bridges and buildings. If the calculator and the rough guess disagree by ten times, it's time to check again.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Estimating is just wild guessing.

Truth: A good estimate is careful reasoning with round numbers. You break the problem into parts you can sensibly guess, then multiply them together. Errors in different parts often cancel out, so the final answer can be surprisingly close.

Try this

Fill a jar with dry beans, marbles or toffees. Ask as many family members or classmates as you can to guess how many are inside, without telling each other. Write down every guess, then count the real number. Line the guesses up and find the middle one. Is it closer than most individual guesses?
