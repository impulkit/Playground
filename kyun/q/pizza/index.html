Where did pizza come from?

Pizza began as cheap street food for the poorest families of Naples, sold by the slice and eaten folded in the hand.

The short answer

Pizza began in Naples, in southern Italy. People around the Mediterranean had baked flatbreads with toppings for thousands of years, but the pizza we know took shape in the 1700s and 1800s. Naples was a crowded port city, and many families were poor. Street sellers sold cheap flatbreads topped with garlic, oil, cheese, small fish or, later, tomatoes.

You could buy a piece for a few coins and eat it while walking. Rich people and visitors often looked down on it. Over the next 200 years, this humble street food spread around the world.

Surprising but true

The art of the Neapolitan pizza maker, spinning and baking dough, was added to UNESCO's list of the world's intangible cultural heritage in 2017.

The tomato arrives

Pizza needed the tomato, which came to Europe from the Americas in the 1500s. Italians were suspicious of it at first, but by the 1700s poor Neapolitans were putting tomato on bread.

Mozzarella, a soft white cheese made from the milk of water buffalo in the Campania region around Naples, added the stretchy layer. Today, many pizzerias use cow's milk mozzarella, but buffalo mozzarella is still a local speciality. Pizza was baked in domed ovens fired with wood, where the heat could reach over 450°C.

The queen's pizza

According to a famous story, in 1889 a pizza maker named Raffaele Esposito made a special pizza for Queen Margherita of Italy, who was visiting Naples. He used tomato, mozzarella and basil, the red, white and green of the Italian flag, and named it after her.

Historians aren't sure the story is true. Pizzas with those same toppings were described in Naples before 1889. Whatever its origin, the pizza Margherita became a classic. In 2017, UNESCO listed the art of the Neapolitan pizza maker as part of the world's cultural heritage, alongside other traditions you can explore in world heritage.

Across the ocean

Millions of Italians moved to the USA in the late 1800s and early 1900s, many from the south. They brought pizza with them. Lombardi's in New York, often called America's first licensed pizzeria, dates from 1905, though some food historians question that claim.

For decades, pizza was mostly eaten in Italian neighbourhoods in cities like New York. After the Second World War, American soldiers who had eaten pizza in Italy came home wanting more. Pizza shops spread across the country. Chicago became known for its deep-dish pizza, baked in a tall pan.

Pizza in India

Pizza chains opened in India in 1996, starting in Delhi and Bengaluru. They soon changed their recipes for Indian tastes. Paneer tikka, tandoori chicken and spicy vegetable toppings joined the menu, and many shops have separate kitchens for vegetarian pizzas.

Some Indian toppings would surprise people in Naples. But pizza has always changed wherever it went, and Italians themselves invented hundreds of versions, from the thin crispy pizza of Rome to the fried pizza of Naples.

Slicing fairly

Pizza is often cut into 8 slices, which makes it easy to share among 2, 4 or 8 people. Sharing it among 3 is harder, because a circle needs careful cuts to make thirds. That makes pizza a favourite way for teachers to explain fractions.

Why do we need fractions?An American burger chain once lost customers because people thought a third of a pound of meat was smaller than a quarter.

Square pizzas, popular in some American cities, are often cut into small squares instead of wedges. Mathematicians have even written papers on the fairest ways to slice a pizza, proving that some clever cutting patterns give everyone exactly the same amount.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pizza was invented in the USA.

Truth: Pizza was born in Naples in Italy. Italian immigrants brought it to New York and other US cities in the late 1800s and early 1900s, and America made it famous around the world after the Second World War.

Try this

Cut a large paper circle and draw a pizza on it. Cut it into 8 equal slices. Share it among 2, then 4, then 8 people. Then try sharing it among 3. What's tricky about thirds? Can you work out how to make 6 equal slices?
