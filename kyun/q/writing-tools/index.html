How did people write before pens?

Many South Indian scripts are full of round, curly letters, possibly because straight lines would have split the palm leaves people wrote on.

The short answer

Before pens, people wrote with whatever their land gave them. In Mesopotamia, scribes pressed a cut reed into wet clay. In Egypt, they painted on papyrus with reed brushes. In India and Southeast Asia, they scratched palm leaves with an iron stylus. Romans scratched wax tablets with a pointed metal or bone stick.

The tool shaped the writing. Wedges pressed in clay made cuneiform. Brushes made flowing Chinese characters. Scratches on palm leaves may have made some Indian scripts curly. Every material had its own tricks and problems.

Surprising but true

A pencil has never contained lead. The "lead" is graphite, a soft form of carbon, mixed with clay.

Clay, reeds and wedges

The oldest writing we know of comes from Mesopotamia, in today's Iraq, around 3200 BCE. Scribes cut the end of a reed into a sharp edge and pressed it into soft clay. Each press left a wedge-shaped mark. That is why the script is called cuneiform, from the Latin word for wedge.

Clay was cheap and everywhere. A tablet could be smoothed and reused while it was wet, or dried in the sun to keep. Important ones were baked hard.

In Egypt, scribes used thin reeds chewed or cut into brushes. They dipped them in black and red ink and wrote on papyrus, made from strips of a river plant. They wrote everyday documents in fast scripts, and carved hieroglyphs on temple walls.

Palm leaves and birch bark

In South and Southeast Asia, people wrote on the dried leaves of palm trees for well over a thousand years. A scribe used an iron stylus to scratch letters into the leaf. Then they rubbed soot mixed with oil over it. The black stuck in the scratches, and the rest was wiped away.

Holes were made in each leaf, and a string threaded through them to tie the pages together between wooden covers. That is why some Indian books are still called granth, from a word linked to tying.

In Kashmir and the Himalaya, writers used birch bark, called bhojpatra, with ink and a reed pen. Many ancient Sanskrit and Buddhist texts survive on it.

Wax, slate and the takhti

Roman schoolchildren carried wax tablets: wooden boards coated with wax. They wrote with a stylus that had a point at one end and a flat end for rubbing out mistakes.

Many Indian children learned to write on a slate with chalk or a slate pencil. In parts of north India and Pakistan, children used a takhti, a wooden board coated with clay, and wrote with a reed pen called a qalam. The board could be washed and re-coated every day.

Feathers, nibs and pencils

In Europe, from about the 500s CE, people wrote with quills, made from the long wing feathers of geese and swans. The tip was sharpened and split, and it wore out quickly. The word "pen" comes from the Latin word for feather.

In the 1500s, a big deposit of graphite was found at Borrowdale in England. People wrapped sticks of it in string or wood to write with. Pencils were born. In the 1700s and 1800s, makers learned to mix powdered graphite with clay, which lets pencils be harder or softer.

Pens with their own ink

In the 1800s, factories in Birmingham, England, made cheap steel nibs by the million. Fountain pens, which hold ink inside, became practical in the 1880s.

The Hungarian journalist László Bíró noticed that newspaper ink dried quickly, and worked with his brother to make a pen with a tiny rolling ball at the tip. He patented it in the 1930s. The ballpoint is now the most common pen in the world. Yet artists still love reed pens for calligraphy, because a cut reed makes lines no ballpoint can.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pencils contain lead, so chewing them can poison you.

Truth: Pencil "lead" is graphite and clay, not the metal lead. People called it lead long ago because graphite looked like it. Chewing pencils still isn't a good idea, but not for that reason.

Try this

Make a clay tablet. Flatten a ball of play dough or clay and press marks into it with the end of an ice-cream stick or chopstick, as Sumerian scribes did. Try writing your name in wedges, then try writing it in curves. Which is easier?
