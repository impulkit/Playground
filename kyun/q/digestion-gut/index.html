What do the bacteria in your gut do?

Tens of trillions of bacteria live in your gut. They eat what you can't digest, make vitamins and help train your body to fight germs.

The short answer

Your gut, especially the large intestine, is home to tens of trillions of microbes, mostly bacteria. Scientists call this community the gut microbiome.

These bacteria live off the parts of your food that your own body can't break down, such as the tough fibre in vegetables, dal and whole grains. In return they make useful substances, including vitamin K, which helps blood clot, and chemicals that feed the cells lining your gut. They also help teach your immune system which microbes are friends and which are enemies, and they take up space that harmful germs might otherwise grab.

Surprising but true

Your body probably holds about as many bacterial cells as human cells, roughly 38 trillion bacteria to 30 trillion of your own cells, by a 2016 estimate.

Who lives down there?

Each person carries hundreds of different kinds of bacteria, and no two people have exactly the same mix. Most live in the colon, the last wide part of the gut, where food moves slowly and there is almost no oxygen. Your stomach, full of strong acid, holds very few.

All together, your gut bacteria weigh only about 0.2 kg, roughly as much as a cup of uncooked rice. Yet some estimates suggest they carry far more genes than your own cells do.

Nobody knew any of this until the 1670s, when the Dutch cloth merchant Antonie van Leeuwenhoek peered through tiny lenses he had ground himself. These early microscopes showed him "little animals" swimming in pond water and in the gunk scraped from his own teeth. They were bacteria, seen for the first time.

How bacteria help digest food

Your own body makes enzymes that break down starch, protein and fat, mostly in the small intestine. But humans can't digest most fibre. It reaches the colon almost untouched.

There, bacteria feed on it. As they break it down, they release gases (which is why beans and rajma can make you windy) and small molecules called short-chain fatty acids. The cells of your colon use these as food. This is one reason doctors and dietitians suggest eating plenty of fruit, vegetables, pulses and whole grains.

How do tiny microbes make food tasty?Curd, idli, bread, cheese and chocolate all need help from creatures far too small to see.

Where your first bacteria come from

Before birth, a baby's gut has few or no bacteria. During birth and the first days of life, babies pick them up from their mother, from milk, from skin and from the world around them. Breast milk even contains special sugars the baby can't digest, which feed helpful bacteria instead.

By about the age of three, a child's gut community looks much like an adult's. It keeps changing with food, illness and medicine.

When the balance tips

Antibiotics kill harmful bacteria, which can save lives. But they also kill many helpful ones, which is why some people get an upset stomach while taking them. The gut community usually recovers over the following weeks.

Foods like curd and yogurt contain live bacteria. Scientists are still studying how much they help, since most of these bacteria pass through rather than settling down.

Still being explored

The gut microbiome is one of the busiest areas of medical research today. In the USA, the National Institutes of Health ran the Human Microbiome Project from 2007 to 2016 to map these communities.

Some studies, many in mice, hint that gut bacteria may affect weight, allergies and even mood. These links are interesting but not yet proven in people. Be wary of products that promise big health changes from a pill of bacteria. If you have tummy troubles that keep coming back, a doctor is the person to ask.

Myth or fact?

Myth: All bacteria are germs that make you ill.

Truth: Only a small number of kinds of bacteria cause disease. Most bacteria in your gut are harmless or helpful, and you would struggle to stay healthy without them.

Try this

Look at the label on a cup of curd or yogurt from a shop. Many say "live cultures" or name the bacteria inside, such as Lactobacillus. With an adult, make curd at home by stirring a spoonful of old curd into warm milk and leaving it overnight. The bacteria from the spoonful do all the work.
