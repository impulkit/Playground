How did a failed medicine experiment create a new purple?

In 1856, an 18-year-old in London tried to make a malaria medicine from coal tar, failed, and invented a purple so popular that Queen Victoria wore it to a royal wedding.

The short answer

In the spring of 1856, William Henry Perkin, an 18-year-old chemistry student, was working in a home lab in London during his Easter holidays. His teacher had challenged students to make quinine, a medicine for malaria, from chemicals found in coal tar, the gooey waste left when coal was turned into gas.

Perkin's attempt failed. He got a black sludge. But when he cleaned it out with alcohol, the liquid turned a brilliant purple. He tested it on silk, and the colour didn't wash out or fade in the sun. He had made one of the first synthetic dyes, made from chemicals rather than plants or animals. It became known as mauve.

Surprising but true

Before mauve, the most famous purple dye came from sea snails, and it took thousands of them to make a single gram.

Why quinine mattered

Malaria, spread by mosquitoes, was a huge killer in the 1800s, including in India and other places ruled by Britain. Quinine, from the bark of the South American cinchona tree, could treat it. But the bark was expensive and hard to get.

Chemists knew quinine contained carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen and oxygen. They hoped they could build it from simpler chemicals. Nobody then understood how atoms were arranged inside molecules, so Perkin's plan had little chance. Quinine wasn't made in a lab until 1944.

From sludge to fashion

Perkin decided his purple was more exciting than his failed medicine. Against his teacher's advice, he left college, patented the dye at 18 and, with his father's savings, built a factory at Greenford near London in 1857.

At first, dyers weren't sure. Then fashion helped. In 1858, Queen Victoria wore a mauve gown to her daughter's wedding. In France, Empress Eugénie loved the colour too. Soon mauve dresses, ribbons and gloves were everywhere. The craze was so strong that the magazine Punch joked about "mauve measles" spreading across London.

The snail purple it replaced

Purple had always been precious. In the ancient Mediterranean, the best purple came from murex sea snails. Each snail gave just a drop of a special gland's fluid, so it took thousands of snails for a single gram of dye. Roman emperors wore it, and for a time ordinary people were barred from wearing the finest purple.

Now anyone could buy purple cloth. Perkin's discovery started a rush to make new colours from coal tar: magenta, aniline blue and many more. Chemistry became an industry, especially in Germany.

A shock for India's indigo farmers

The rush to make dyes from chemicals changed India too. For thousands of years, India had dyed cloth with plants, using madder for red and indigo for blue, in textiles and block prints traded worldwide. British planters forced many Indian farmers to grow indigo, often unfairly.

In 1897, the German company BASF began selling synthetic indigo. Within a few decades, natural indigo exports collapsed. Many planters left, though in Champaran in Bihar the struggle of indigo farmers continued, and in 1917 Gandhi took up their cause.

Dyes that became medicine

Here's the twist. Perkin failed to make a medicine, but synthetic dyes later led to many. Scientists found that some dyes stained germs but not human cells. The German doctor Paul Ehrlich used that idea to search for "magic bullets" that would attack only germs. His work helped begin modern drug design, a big chapter in the history of medicine.

Perkin became rich, was knighted in 1906 and died in 1907. Each year, the Perkin Medal honours top chemists in the USA, named after the teenager who couldn't make quinine.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Purple was always a cheap, everyday colour.

Truth: For thousands of years, a lasting purple was rare and costly. Tyrian purple from sea snails was so expensive that, in ancient Rome, it was linked to emperors and the rich. Perkin's mauve helped make purple affordable for ordinary people.

Try this

Make your own natural dye with an adult. Simmer chopped red cabbage or beetroot in water, let it cool and strain it. Dip strips of white cotton cloth in it and compare the colours after drying. Wash one strip and see how well the colour stays.
