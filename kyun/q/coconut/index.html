Why is the coconut called the tree of life?

A coconut can float across the sea for weeks and still sprout when it washes up on a beach.

The short answer

The coconut palm is called the tree of life because almost every part of it is useful. The water inside a young coconut is a refreshing drink. The white flesh is eaten fresh, grated into curries, or squeezed to make coconut milk. Dried flesh is pressed for oil, used in cooking and on hair.

The tough husk gives coir fibre for ropes, mats and brushes. The hard shells become cups, spoons and fuel. The leaves are woven into roofs, mats and baskets, and their midribs are tied into brooms. The trunks are used as timber. In Kerala, the palm is called kalpavriksha, the tree that grants every wish.

Surprising but true

The coconut isn't a true nut. Botanists call it a drupe, a fruit with a hard stone inside, like a mango or a peach.

A seed built for the sea

A coconut is the seed of the coconut palm, wrapped in a thick, fibrous husk. The husk is full of air, so the coconut floats. Inside, the hard shell protects the seed, and the water and flesh feed the young plant when it sprouts.

This design lets coconuts drift on ocean currents for weeks and still grow when they wash up on a beach. That's one reason coconut palms line tropical shores from India to Hawaii. Scientists think people also spread coconuts, carrying them on long sea voyages as food and water.

A fruit, not a nut

Despite its name, a coconut isn't a true nut. Botanists call it a drupe, a fruit with a fleshy or fibrous outer layer and a hard stone inside. Mangoes, peaches and cherries are drupes too. In a coconut, the outer layer is the husk, and the "stone" is the brown shell we usually see in shops.

Where coconuts grow

Coconut palms love warm, wet, coastal places. Indonesia, the Philippines and India are among the world's biggest producers. In India, Kerala, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh grow the most.

On India's coral islands of Lakshadweep, coconut palms are everywhere, and coconut is central to food and income. Studies of coconut DNA suggest that cultivated coconuts were developed separately in two regions, one around the Pacific and one around the Indian Ocean.

A kitchen staple

In South India and along the western coast, coconut is in almost everything. Grated coconut goes into thoran and poriyal. Coconut milk makes stews and curries creamy. Coconut chutney is served with idli and dosa. Kerala cooks fry many dishes in coconut oil.

Sweets use coconut too, from Maharashtra's modak to Goa's bebinca. Tender coconut water is sold on beaches and roadsides, often by vendors who slice the top off with a few swift strokes.

In rituals and festivals

Coconuts play a big part in Hindu rituals. Breaking a coconut is a common offering at temples and at the start of new ventures, from opening a shop to launching a ship. In Maharashtra, fishing communities celebrate Narali Purnima by offering coconuts to the sea before the fishing season begins.

Coir and craft

The husk fibre, called coir, is soaked, beaten and spun into strong, rough rope. Coir mats and ropes are made in large numbers in Kerala, especially around Alappuzha. Coir resists salt water, so it was traditionally used for ships' ropes and fishing nets.

Weavers twist and weave coir into doormats, carpets and even mattresses. Nothing from the palm goes to waste, which is why it earned its name.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Falling coconuts kill 150 people every year, more than sharks do.

Truth: This number is widely repeated but was never based on proper records. Falling coconuts can and do injure people, but no one has reliably counted deaths worldwide.

Try this

Test whether a coconut floats. With an adult, place a whole, dry coconut (with or without its husk) in a bucket of water. Does it float? Now try a stone of a similar size. Think about how a floating seed could spread a plant to new islands.
