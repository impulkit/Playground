How do you make a box from flat paper?

There are exactly 11 different ways to cut a cube out of one flat piece of paper. Most people guess far fewer.

The short answer

A box is a 3D shape, but it can start life flat. If you cut along some of a box's edges and unfold it, you get a single flat piece called a net. Fold the net back along the same lines and the box returns.

Every cardboard box, sweet box and cereal packet is made this way. Factories print and cut nets from big flat sheets, then fold and glue them. For a cube, the net is six squares joined edge to edge. But not just any six squares will do. Only 11 arrangements fold into a cube, and finding them all is a good puzzle.

Surprising but true

Nobody has proved whether every shape with flat faces that bulges outward everywhere can be cut along its edges and unfolded into one flat piece without parts overlapping. Mathematicians have been stuck on this since at least the 1970s.

Counting the cube nets

The most famous cube net looks like a cross: four squares in a column, with one square on each side of the second square. Fold the side squares up and the top square over, and you have a cube.

There are 10 others. Some look like a staircase. Some have three squares in a row with the rest hanging off at odd places. Mathematicians call any shape made of six joined squares a hexomino. There are 35 hexominoes in all, but only 11 are cube nets. A good test is to picture folding one face at a time and check that no two faces end up in the same place.

Faces, edges and corners

Shapes with flat faces and straight edges, like cubes and pyramids, are called polyhedra. A cube has 6 faces, 12 edges and 8 corners. In the 1700s, the Swiss mathematician Leonhard Euler noticed a pattern: for these shapes, faces plus corners minus edges always equals 2. For a cube, 6 + 8 - 12 = 2.

That rule helps when you design a net. To unfold a cube you must cut along exactly 7 of its 12 edges, leaving 5 as folds holding the faces together. A triangle-based pyramid is simpler: it has only 2 different nets.

A painter's idea

In 1525, the German artist Albrecht Dürer published a book about measuring and drawing. In it, he showed solid shapes unfolded flat, so readers could copy them, cut them out and fold their own models. His is one of the earliest known books to show nets this way.

Dürer's idea left a puzzle that is still open. For any shape with flat faces that bulges outward everywhere, can you always cut it along its edges into one flat net with no overlaps? It seems likely, and it works for every example tried. Yet nobody has proved it must always work.

Boxes in real life

Real boxes need more than the plain net. Packaging designers add tabs, which are thin flaps that get glued or tucked in, and they plan where the box will be weakest. A pizza box folds from one cut sheet with no glue at all, using slots and tabs. A mithai box often has a lid that is a second, slightly bigger net.

Designers also think about waste. They fit as many nets as they can onto one big sheet, a bit like fitting tiles on a floor, so less cardboard is thrown away. Once you've opened a box flat and counted its tabs, every packet in a shop starts to look like a puzzle someone has already solved.

Folding goes to space

Folding flat things into shapes isn't only for boxes. Engineers borrowed ideas from origami to fold solar panels and sunshields so they fit inside rockets, then open in space. Bees, meanwhile, build their honeycomb from six-sided cells that pack together with no gaps. Working out how much a box can hold is a volume problem, and working out how to make it from one flat sheet is a nets problem.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Any six squares joined together will fold up into a cube.

Truth: Only 11 of the 35 possible six-square shapes work. A straight row of six squares, for example, rolls into a tube with two open ends.

Try this

Take an empty cereal box or toothpaste box and gently pull its glued flaps apart until it lies flat. That flat shape is its net. Count the faces and the extra tabs, then fold it back up and see which tab is glued to which face.
