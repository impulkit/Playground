Why does Gol Gumbaz echo seven times?

Clap once inside Gol Gumbaz in Karnataka and the sound comes back again and again, while a whisper on one side can be heard about 37 metres away on the other.

The short answer

Gol Gumbaz is a giant tomb in Vijayapura, in northern Karnataka, finished around 1656 for Sultan Mohammed Adil Shah. Its dome is about 44 metres across on the outside, one of the largest built anywhere before modern steel and concrete.

The dome echoes because sound bounces off its hard, curved inside again and again. A clap travels up, hits the dome, comes back down, bounces off the floor and goes up again. Each round trip takes a fraction of a second, so you hear the clap repeat several times before it fades. Guides often say seven. Around the base of the dome runs a balcony called the whispering gallery, where even a soft whisper travels round the curved wall to the far side.

Surprising but true

Gol Gumbaz's dome is about 44 metres wide on the outside, and it stands with no pillars inside to hold it up.

A sultan's giant tomb

The Adil Shahi sultans ruled Bijapur, in the Deccan, from the late 1400s to 1686. They were great builders. Mohammed Adil Shah began his own tomb early in his reign, and it took about 30 years to finish.

The building is a huge cube with a seven-storey tower at each corner, each topped with a small dome. Above sits the main dome. The single hall inside covers about 1,700 square metres, bigger than six tennis courts, with no pillars at all. The sultan's grave lies in the middle.

How do you hold up a dome without pillars?

A round dome doesn't sit easily on a square room. The builders solved this with arches across the corners that turn the square into an eight-sided shape, then into a circle. These overlapping arches spread the dome's huge weight down into the thick walls.

The dome is made of bricks and lime mortar, a few metres thick. It is a little like the domes of cathedrals in Europe, where builders used similar tricks. Some people say Gol Gumbaz's dome is second in size only to St Peter's in Rome among old domes, though such rankings depend on how you measure.

Why the echoes repeat

Sound is a wave of pushes travelling through air at about 343 metres a second. When it hits a hard, smooth surface, it bounces back as an echo. In a normal room, the bounces come so fast that they blur together. Gol Gumbaz is so big that each bounce takes long enough for your ear to hear separately.

The dome's curve also gathers the sound and sends it back down towards the centre, like a giant bowl. Plaster and stone absorb very little, so each bounce is only a bit weaker than the last. That is why one clap can come back seven times, or even more in a quiet hall.

The whispering gallery

Visitors climb a long, narrow staircase inside one of the corner towers to reach the gallery. It is a narrow balcony that runs right round the base of the dome, about 33 metres above the floor.

If you whisper close to the wall, the sound clings to the curved surface and bounces along it all the way round. A friend on the opposite side, about 37 metres away, can hear you clearly. Many visitors clap and shout too, which is why the gallery is rarely quiet.

Whispering walls around the world

Gol Gumbaz is not alone. St Paul's Cathedral in London has a famous whispering gallery, and in the 1870s the British scientist Lord Rayleigh studied it to explain how such waves work. In New York, a curved tiled arch in Grand Central Terminal lets two people whisper to each other from opposite corners.

Builders who want quiet rooms do the opposite. They use soft, rough or angled surfaces that stop sound from bouncing. And the strangest part? Recording studios spend a lot of money to stop exactly the echoes that make Gol Gumbaz famous, using soundproofing tricks.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The echo in Gol Gumbaz was made by a hidden trick or machine.

Truth: There is no trick. The echoes come from the shape of the dome and its smooth, hard plaster, which reflect sound many times before it fades. Any similar dome would echo too.

Try this

Stand near a long, smooth wall or the corner of a big empty hall with a friend at the other end. Each of you put your face close to the wall and whisper. Then try it standing away from the wall. Notice how much clearer the sound is when it can travel along the surface.
