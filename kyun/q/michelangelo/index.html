How did Michelangelo paint the Sistine Chapel?

Michelangelo thought of himself as a sculptor, not a painter, yet he spent four years on scaffolding painting one of the most famous ceilings in the world.

The short answer

Michelangelo painted the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel in the Vatican, in Rome, between 1508 and 1512. He stood on a wooden scaffold about 20 metres above the floor and painted onto fresh, wet plaster, a method called fresco.

He covered the curved ceiling with more than 300 figures, telling stories from the beginning of the Bible, including the creation of the world and of Adam, the first man. He had a few helpers to grind colours and spread plaster, but he designed the whole scheme and painted most of it himself. It took about four years.

Surprising but true

Michelangelo wrote a funny poem complaining about the job, describing his beard pointing at the sky and paint dripping onto his face.

A sculptor told to paint

Michelangelo Buonarroti was born in 1475 near Florence. By his twenties he was already famous for sculpture. His marble David, more than 5 metres tall, made him a star in Florence. He thought of carving stone as the highest art and of himself as a sculptor.

So when Pope Julius II asked him to paint the chapel ceiling, Michelangelo was not pleased. He had been working on a huge marble tomb for the pope. Some people at the time suspected his rivals had suggested the job, hoping he would fail at painting. Still, you did not easily say no to a pope.

Painting on wet plaster

Fresco is a tricky method. A thin layer of fresh plaster is spread on the ceiling each morning. The painter has to paint it while it is still wet, before it dries, usually in a single day. As the plaster dries, the colours become locked into the surface, which is why frescoes can last for centuries.

Each day's patch is called a giornata, from the Italian for "a day's work". Experts have counted the joins between these patches to work out how many days Michelangelo spent on different parts. Mistakes were hard to fix. A bad patch had to be chipped off and redone.

Why is Rome called the Eternal City?Rome's people were calling it eternal over 2,000 years ago. They had a point. It is still a capital, and a Roman aqueduct still feeds one of its fountains.

The scaffold

Getting up to the ceiling was a puzzle of its own. Michelangelo did not want a scaffold standing on the floor, because the chapel was still being used. He designed a wooden platform supported by beams fixed into holes near the top of the walls. It reached across the chapel like a bridge.

He stood on this platform and leaned backwards to paint. In a poem to a friend he joked that his stomach was pushed up under his chin, his beard pointed at the sky and paint dripped all over his face. He even drew a little sketch of himself at work, arm stretched upwards.

The creation of Adam

The most famous scene shows God reaching out to give life to Adam. Their fingers almost touch, but not quite. That tiny gap has become one of the most copied images in the world, used in films, adverts and cartoons.

Michelangelo painted the figures with powerful, muscular bodies, like sculptures come to life. He had studied anatomy carefully. From the floor, 20 metres below, they still look solid and strong. Years later he returned to paint The Last Judgement on the chapel's altar wall.

Cleaning off the centuries

Over 450 years, candle smoke, dust and old varnish turned the ceiling dark and brownish. Between 1980 and 1994 a team of restorers cleaned it carefully. Underneath the grime were bright pinks, greens and oranges.

Some critics argued that the cleaning had removed parts of Michelangelo's own shading. Most experts now accept the bright colours as his. The debate showed how hard it is to care for old art, a job that art detectives and scientists still do today. Thousands of people visit the chapel in Rome every day, looking up just as the pope's guests did five centuries ago.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Michelangelo painted the Sistine Chapel ceiling lying on his back.

Truth: He painted standing up, on a scaffold he designed himself, tilting his head back for hours. His own poem and sketch about the job show him standing with his head bent back.
