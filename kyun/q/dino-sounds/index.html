Did dinosaurs really roar?

Movie roars are made by mixing elephant, tiger and alligator sounds. The real thing may have been different.

The short answer

Sound doesn't fossilise. So scientists study dinosaurs' closest living relatives: birds and crocodiles.

Both can make deep, closed-mouth booming sounds. Dinosaurs probably could too. The sound may have been more like a deep rumble you feel in your chest than a movie roar.

The duck-billed Parasaurolophus had a hollow crest. Models of it make a low, trumpet-like hoot. Some dinosaurs may also have hissed, honked or clattered their beaks, but we can't hear any of it directly.

Surprising but true

A model of Parasaurolophus's head crest makes a sound like a deep trumpet.

Where movie roars come from

The roar of the T. rex in the 1993 film Jurassic Park was made by sound designers, who mixed recordings of real animals, including a baby elephant, a tiger and an alligator. It sounds terrifying, which was the point. It was never meant to be scientific.

Lions and tigers roar with their mouths open, pushing air through a voice box in the throat. Mammals have a different kind of voice box from birds, and dinosaurs were not mammals. So a lion's roar is not a good guide.

Asking the relatives

Scientists use a trick called the "living bracket". If birds and crocodiles both have a feature, their shared ancestors, including dinosaurs, probably had it too.

Crocodiles and alligators make deep bellows with their mouths closed. Male alligators can make the water dance on their backs with sounds partly too low for us to hear. Many birds, like pigeons, emus and ostriches, also call with closed mouths, cooing or booming. In 2016, a study showed that closed-mouth calling evolved several times in this family. So it is a good guess for big dinosaurs, but not a certainty.

A trumpet on its head

Parasaurolophus, a plant-eater that lived about 76 million years ago in North America, had a long, curved crest sweeping back from its head. Inside were tubes connected to its nose, looping up and back down. In 1981, a scientist argued that the crest worked like a wind instrument.

In the 1990s, researchers scanned a crest and built a computer model of the air passages. Blowing air through the model gave a low, resonant hoot. Different shapes of crest would make different notes. Males, females and youngsters may have sounded quite different. Young Parasaurolophus had much smaller crests, so their calls would have been higher, the way a small flute sounds higher than a big one. Its relatives, like Corythosaurus with its helmet-shaped crest, had different tube shapes and probably made different calls.

Voice boxes

Birds sing with an organ called a syrinx, deep in the chest where the windpipe splits. That is how some songbirds can sing two notes at once. The oldest syrinx known comes from a bird called Vegavis, found in Antarctica and about 67 million years old.

In 2023, scientists described the fossil voice box of Pinacosaurus, an armoured dinosaur from Mongolia. It looked more like a bird's than a crocodile's. That hints that some dinosaurs could change their calls in bird-like ways, though not necessarily sing.

Sounds too low to hear

Big animals can make very low sounds that travel far. Elephants rumble to each other across several kilometres with sounds partly below what human ears can catch, called infrasound. Giant dinosaurs had big bodies and long airways, so some may have done the same. If so, a herd of sauropods might have been talking all around you and you would have felt it more than heard it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Dinosaurs roared like lions with their mouths wide open.

Truth: There is no evidence for that. Their closest relatives, birds and crocodiles, often call with their mouths closed, making booms, hisses and coos. Many scientists think big dinosaurs did the same.

Try this

Hum a low note with your mouth closed, then put a hand on your chest and throat. You can feel the buzz. Now try with a cardboard tube held to your lips and hum again. A longer tube makes a deeper sound, which is how Parasaurolophus's long crest may have worked.
