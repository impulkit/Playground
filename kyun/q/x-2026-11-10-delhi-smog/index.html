Why does Delhi's sky turn grey every November?

Every winter, the air over Delhi gets trapped under an invisible lid, and everything that goes up stays put.

The short answer

Delhi's air is dirty all year, from vehicles, dust, factories and smoke. In summer and the monsoon, strong winds and rain sweep much of that away.

In November, the weather changes. Cool, still air settles over the city like a lid, and the smoke and dust have nowhere to go. Add farm fires and Diwali crackers, and the sky turns grey.

Surprising but true

The most harmful specks in smog, called PM2.5, are about 30 times thinner than a single hair from your head, small enough to slip deep into your lungs.

Why it's in the news

On Diwali night, 8 November 2026, 20 of Delhi's 59 air monitoring stations showed readings above 400 at 10 pm. That is in the "severe" range. Delhi's average Air Quality Index for the day was 376, which is "very poor", and it stayed at 367 at 3 am. Down To Earth, an environment magazine, found that Delhi's air was worse on the day after Diwali in eight of the past nine years.

The government's forecast system said vehicles made up about 19 per cent of Delhi's pollution on Diwali, and smoke from farm fires about 3.5 per cent.

The invisible lid

Usually, air near the ground is warmer than the air above it. Warm air rises, carrying smoke upwards, where wind blows it away.

On long, clear winter nights, the ground loses heat quickly and chills the air just above it. Now a layer of warmer air sits on top of colder air. Scientists call this a temperature inversion. The cold air cannot rise through the warm layer, so it stays trapped with everything in it. Winter winds over north India are also slow and gentle, so they cannot push the dirty air out.

Tiny specks, big trouble

Smog is a mix of smoke and fog. Its most dangerous part is PM2.5, specks made by combustion in engines, stoves, crackers and burning crop stubble. In October and November, after the rice harvest, some farmers in Punjab and Haryana burn leftover straw to clear their fields fast.

PM2.5 specks are so small they slip past the nose and throat and go deep into the lungs. That is why doctors advise children to play indoors on very smoggy days.

What happens next

Delhi follows a Graded Response Action Plan: as the air gets worse, rules get stricter, from limiting construction dust to keeping some trucks out. Stronger winds or rain usually bring relief, and the air tends to improve by late winter.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Delhi's smog is caused only by Diwali crackers.

Truth: Crackers add a sudden spike, but the haze comes from many sources at once, including vehicles, factories, dust, burning rubbish and farm fires. Winter weather then traps all of it near the ground.

Try this

Fill a clear glass with cold water and gently pour a little warm water coloured with food colouring on top using a spoon. The warm, coloured layer stays on top. This is upside down compared with the sky, but it shows how layers of different temperature refuse to mix, just like the air over Delhi in winter.
