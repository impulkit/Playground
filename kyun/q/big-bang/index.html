How did the universe begin?

A faint hiss that two scientists first blamed on pigeon droppings turned out to be the oldest light in the universe.

The short answer

Scientists think the universe began about 13.8 billion years ago, extremely hot and packed tightly together. Since then it has been expanding and cooling. This idea is called the Big Bang.

In the first few minutes, the simplest ingredients formed, mostly hydrogen and helium. Hundreds of millions of years later, gravity pulled that gas into the first stars and galaxies. Heavier elements, like the carbon in your body and the iron in your blood, were made later inside stars.

Nobody knows what, if anything, came before. That question is still open.

Surprising but true

The name "Big Bang" was coined on BBC radio in 1949 by Fred Hoyle, an astronomer who didn't believe the idea.

Galaxies racing away

In the 1920s, astronomers noticed that light from distant galaxies was stretched towards the red end of the rainbow. This "redshift" meant the galaxies were moving away from us. In 1927, a Belgian priest and physicist named Georges Lemaître suggested the universe was expanding. In 1929, the American astronomer Edwin Hubble showed that the farther away a galaxy is, the faster it moves away.

Run that film backwards, and everything gets closer and closer together. Long ago, everything we can see must have been squeezed into a tiny, hot, dense state. Ideas from Einstein about how space can stretch made the picture fit.

The hiss in the antenna

In 1964, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson were testing a huge horn-shaped radio antenna at Bell Labs in New Jersey. They kept hearing a faint hiss that came from every part of the sky, day and night. They checked their equipment. They even scrubbed out pigeon droppings. The hiss stayed.

Other scientists had predicted that the universe's early glow should still be around, cooled and stretched into radio waves. That is exactly what they had found. It is called the cosmic microwave background, and a small part of the fuzzy "snow" on old analogue televisions came from it.

When the fog cleared

For its first 380,000 years or so, the universe was a glowing fog of hot gas. Light couldn't travel far before bumping into something, a bit like car headlights in thick winter fog on a Delhi highway. Then it cooled enough for the first atoms to form, and the fog cleared. Light could suddenly travel freely.

The hiss that Penzias and Wilson heard is that very light, released when the fog lifted. It has been travelling for nearly 13.8 billion years, stretching all the way.

Looking back in time

Light takes time to travel. When we see a galaxy billions of light-years away, we see it as it was billions of years ago. Telescopes are time machines.

The James Webb Space Telescope looks for galaxies that formed in the first few hundred million years. The European Space Agency's Planck spacecraft mapped the leftover glow so precisely that scientists could pin down the universe's age to within less than one percent.

The doubters

Not everyone liked the idea at first. The British astronomer Fred Hoyle argued for a "steady state" universe with no beginning, constantly making new matter. He gave the Big Bang its name on a radio programme in 1949. He later said he only wanted a vivid picture for listeners, not an insult.

The Indian astrophysicist Jayant Narlikar worked closely with Hoyle and spent much of his career exploring other ideas. Today, most scientists accept the Big Bang because so many different clues agree. Doubters played a useful part, though: questioning an idea makes everyone check the evidence harder.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Big Bang was an explosion at one spot, throwing matter out into empty space.

Truth: It wasn't an explosion in space. Space itself has been stretching everywhere at once. There is no centre where the bang happened, which is why distant galaxies are moving away from us in every direction.

Try this

Draw a dozen dots on a balloon with a marker before blowing it up. Then blow it up slowly and watch the dots. Every dot moves away from every other dot, and none of them is the centre. That's how galaxies move apart as space itself stretches.
