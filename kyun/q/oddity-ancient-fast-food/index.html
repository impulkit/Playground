Did ancient Romans eat fast food?

In 2020, archaeologists in Pompeii uncovered a 2,000-year-old snack bar with painted ducks on the counter and food still stuck in the jars.

The short answer

Yes. Walk down a street in Pompeii in AD 79 and you would pass a snack bar every few minutes. The Romans called it a thermopolium, which roughly means "place that sells hot things". It had a stone counter facing the street, with big clay jars sunk into the top to keep stews, beans and wine warm. You pointed, paid, and ate standing up or walking away.

Archaeologists have found around 80 of these counters in Pompeii, a town of maybe 11,000 people. That is more snack bars per person than many modern towns have burger joints.

Surprising but true

Many Romans lived in flats with no kitchen at all, so buying hot food in the street was everyday life, not a treat.

The snack bar with ducks on the counter

In 2019, diggers in a part of Pompeii called Regio V uncovered a counter that still had its paint. Bright pictures covered the front: two ducks hanging upside down, ready for the pot, a rooster, and a dog on a lead. The pictures were probably a menu. A picture of a duck meant duck was for sale.

Inside the jars, scientists found tiny bits of duck bone, pork, goat, fish and snails. One jar held ground fava beans, which Romans sometimes added to wine to change its colour and taste. The volcano's ash had sealed everything in place, like a lunchbox nobody opened for 2,000 years.

Flats with no kitchens

Fast food made sense because of how Romans lived. In big cities, poorer families rented rooms in tall blocks of flats called insulae. Some were five or six storeys high, taller than most school buildings today, and built partly of wood.

Cooking on an open fire in a wooden flat was a good way to burn the whole block down. So many flats had no kitchen. Families bought hot food outside, the way many Mumbai office workers grab vada pav at a station stall. Rich Romans, who had big houses with kitchens and cooks, often sneered at snack bars as rough places for the poor.

What was on the menu

A Roman snack bar sold food you could eat fast: lentil and bean stews, porridge, sausages, cheese, olives, flat bread, and boiled eggs. Wine was usually mixed with water, sometimes served warm with spices.

Almost everything came with garum, a salty sauce made by leaving fish and salt in the sun for weeks until it broke down. That sounds gross, but it is close to the fish sauce still used in Thai and Vietnamese cooking. It works through fermentation, the same process that turns milk into curd.

Missing from the menu? Tomatoes, potatoes, chillies and corn. Those all came from the Americas, and Europe didn't see them until about 1,500 years later.

Emperors who disliked snack bars

Roman leaders didn't always trust these places. Crowds gathered there, drank, gambled and talked about politics. The writer Suetonius says that Emperor Nero once banned snack bars from selling cooked food, except beans and vegetables. Rules like that came and went, and the counters kept serving.

Rome was not alone. In China, around a thousand years ago, the city of Kaifeng had night markets where stalls sold noodles, dumplings and grilled meat until late. In India, travellers' accounts describe sellers of ready-cooked food at markets and pilgrim towns for many centuries. Wherever crowds gathered and people worked long hours, someone set up a stove and sold a quick meal.

Still being dug up

Only about two-thirds of Pompeii has been dug up. Every few years, archaeologists find another counter, another painted wall, another kitchen pot. Life in towns across ancient Rome is easier to picture because of finds like these. And the best clues sit right beside the counters: customers waiting for their stew scratched jokes, prices and insults into the plaster, and that Roman graffiti can still be read today.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Fast food is a modern invention that began with American burger chains.

Truth: Cities have sold ready-to-eat food for thousands of years. Roman towns had snack counters on busy streets, and Chinese cities had night markets selling noodles and dumplings a thousand years ago.
