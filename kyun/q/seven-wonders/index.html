What are the Seven Wonders of the World?

Of the seven wonders of the ancient world, only one is still standing, and it was already more than 2,000 years old when the list was made.

The short answer

The Seven Wonders of the World began as a list of must-see sights made by Greek writers over 2,000 years ago. It was a bit like a traveller's guidebook. The Greeks called them "theamata", which means "things to be seen", and they picked buildings and statues around the eastern Mediterranean, the part of the world they knew.

The usual list starts with the Great Pyramid of Giza and the Hanging Gardens of Babylon. Then come the Statue of Zeus at Olympia, the Temple of Artemis at Ephesus, the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus, the Colossus of Rhodes and the Lighthouse of Alexandria. Only the pyramid survives. In 2007, a public vote picked a "new" seven, which includes India's Taj Mahal.

Surprising but true

The word "mausoleum", for a grand tomb, comes from one of the ancient wonders, the tomb of a ruler named Mausolus.

The survivor

The Great Pyramid of Giza in Egypt was built around 4,500 years ago as a tomb for the pharaoh Khufu. It was made of more than two million stone blocks. At about 146 m, it was the tallest building made by humans for nearly 4,000 years, until a cathedral spire in England beat it in the 1300s.

By the time Greek writers listed it, the pyramid was already older to them than the Romans are to us.

How were the pyramids of Giza built?About 2.3 million stone blocks, stacked 4,500 years ago, without engines or cranes.

The lost six

The others are gone. The Statue of Zeus was a giant seated figure covered in ivory and gold. The Temple of Artemis was burned down, rebuilt and finally destroyed. The tomb of Mausolus, a ruler in what is now Turkey, was so grand that its name gave us the word "mausoleum".

The Colossus of Rhodes was a bronze statue of the sun god, about 33 m tall, roughly as high as a ten-storey building. It stood for only around 50 years before an earthquake toppled it. The Lighthouse of Alexandria guided ships into Egypt's busy harbour with a fire at the top, as you can read in lighthouses.

The gardens that may not have existed

The Hanging Gardens of Babylon, in what is now Iraq, are said to have been terraces of trees and flowers watered by clever machines. The trouble is that no Babylonian writing describes them, and archaeologists have not found them.

Some historians think the gardens were legendary. Others think they were real but stood in a different city, Nineveh, built by an Assyrian king. So one of the world's most famous wonders may be the one we know least about.

The new seven

In 2007, a Swiss-based foundation ran a worldwide vote by internet and phone. The winners were the Great Wall of China, Petra in Jordan and Christ the Redeemer in Brazil. Joining them were Machu Picchu in Peru, Chichén Itzá in Mexico, the Colosseum in Rome and the Taj Mahal in India. The Great Pyramid was given an honorary place.

Countries with big populations and keen campaigns had an advantage, so the list says as much about popularity as about greatness. UNESCO, which looks after World Heritage Sites, kept its distance from the vote.

Wonders worth adding

People argue about what is missing. Many would add Angkor Wat in Cambodia, the Kailasa temple at Ellora carved down into a single rock, or modern wonders like the Panama Canal.

Every list leaves something out. That is part of why people keep making new ones.

Myth or fact?

Myth: There is one official list of the Seven Wonders of the World.

Truth: Ancient writers made several different lists. The famous 2007 list came from a vote organised by a private foundation, and UNESCO did not take part. There is no single official list.
