Who sailed on the Mayflower?

In 1620, 102 passengers crammed into a small wooden cargo ship for a two-month crossing of the Atlantic. Two babies were born before they built a single house.

The short answer

The Mayflower was an English merchant ship that carried 102 passengers across the Atlantic Ocean in 1620. About a third belonged to a religious group who had broken away from the Church of England. They wanted to worship in their own way, and had lived for years in the Netherlands before deciding to start again in America. The rest were other settlers hoping for land and work.

They landed on the coast of what is now Massachusetts, on the homeland of the Wampanoag people, and founded Plymouth Colony. They are now called the Pilgrims. Their story is told every year in the United States at Thanksgiving.

Surprising but true

The passengers we call the Pilgrims did not call themselves that. The name became popular about 200 years later.

A rough crossing

The group had planned to sail in two ships, but the second one, the Speedwell, leaked so badly that it turned back. Everyone squeezed onto the Mayflower, which left Plymouth in England in September 1620.

The ship was probably about 30 metres long, roughly one and a half cricket pitches. Passengers lived in the dark, cramped space between decks for about two months. It relied entirely on the wind, like all sailing ships of its day, and autumn gales battered it. At one point a main beam cracked. They fixed it with a large iron screw they had brought along, perhaps from a printing press. A baby named Oceanus Hopkins was born at sea.

The Mayflower Compact

In November they sighted Cape Cod, far north of where they had permission to settle. Some passengers grumbled that no rules would apply there. So before landing, 41 men signed an agreement promising to make and obey fair laws together.

This Mayflower Compact was a short document, only about 200 words. Women did not sign, and neither did most servants. But it was an early example of settlers agreeing to govern themselves, and Americans still point to it as a step towards self-government.

A terrible first winter

The settlers began building at Plymouth in December, but they arrived too late to plant crops. Many lived on the ship through the cold. Disease spread, probably made worse by poor food and the long voyage. About half the passengers died before spring.

The land was not empty. It was Patuxet, a Wampanoag village whose people had been nearly wiped out by an epidemic, probably brought by earlier European visitors, a few years before.

The Wampanoag alliance

In March 1621 a Native man named Samoset walked into the settlement and greeted them in English, which he had learned from fishermen. He returned with Tisquantum, often called Squanto, who had been kidnapped and taken to Europe years earlier and had learned English there. He showed the settlers how to plant maize with fish as fertiliser.

The Wampanoag leader Massasoit, also known as Ousamequin, agreed an alliance with the colonists. Each side promised to help the other against enemies. Both had reasons: the Wampanoag had been weakened by disease and faced rival nations.

The harvest of 1621

That autumn, after their first harvest, the colonists held a celebration. About 90 Wampanoag men joined them, and the gathering lasted three days. They ate deer the Wampanoag brought, along with wild birds and other food. There is no record of pumpkin pie.

Peace between the two peoples lasted about 50 years. Then, as more settlers took more land, it broke down into a bloody war in 1675. For many Wampanoag and other Native people today, Thanksgiving is a day of mourning as well as memory. Their nation still lives in Massachusetts.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Pilgrims stepped ashore onto Plymouth Rock.

Truth: None of the Pilgrims' own writings mention the rock. It was pointed out as the landing spot more than 120 years later, in 1741, by an elderly man who said his father had told him.

Try this

Mark out a rectangle about 30 metres long and 7 metres wide with chalk or string in a playground. That is roughly the size historians estimate for the whole Mayflower. Ask your class to stand inside it, then remember that over 130 people, plus cargo and animals, shared a ship like that for over two months.
