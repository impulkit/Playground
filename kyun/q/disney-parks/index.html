How did theme parks like Disneyland begin?

Disneyland was built on orange groves in California in about a year, and its opening day in 1955 went so badly that staff called it Black Sunday.

The short answer

People have gathered to ride, play and eat at fairs and pleasure gardens for hundreds of years. Bakken in Denmark has welcomed visitors since 1583, and Tivoli Gardens in Copenhagen opened in 1843. By 1900, New York's Coney Island had roller coasters and bright electric lights.

What changed in 1955 was the idea of a theme. Walt Disney, a cartoon maker, opened Disneyland in Anaheim, California, on 17 July that year. Instead of a jumble of rides, each area told a story: a small-town main street, a jungle river, a castle from a fairy tale. That idea now shapes parks from Florida to Shanghai.

Surprising but true

Bakken, an amusement park near Copenhagen in Denmark, has been entertaining visitors since 1583, more than 370 years before Disneyland opened.

Before the mouse

The old pleasure gardens of Europe were green parks with music, lamps, dancing and food stalls. Fairs added swings and wooden carousels, turned first by horses or people and later by steam engines. In the 1890s, Coney Island at the edge of New York City became the place where working families went for a cheap day out. They rode early roller coasters, watched shows and stared at buildings covered in thousands of light bulbs.

The story goes that Walt Disney had the idea for his own park while sitting on a bench, watching his two daughters ride a merry-go-round. He thought parents deserved somewhere they could enjoy too, cleaner and better planned than the fairs he knew. He spent years sketching ideas, and even built a small steam railway in his own garden.

A park built like a film set

Disney had spent years making cartoons with his animation studio, and he planned the park the way a film director plans scenes. Visitors enter through a single gate onto Main Street, which leads straight to Sleeping Beauty Castle. From a central hub, paths fan out to different "lands".

Designers used a trick called forced perspective. Upper floors of buildings on Main Street are built smaller than the ground floor, so they look taller than they really are. Rubbish bins, trains and even staff costumes match each land. The aim was that you would never see a piece of the ordinary world from inside.

Black Sunday

The first day was chaotic. Tens of thousands of people turned up, many with forged tickets. The July heat softened fresh asphalt, there weren't enough drinking fountains, and some rides broke down. Newspapers were not kind.

Disney fixed the problems quickly. Within about two months the park had welcomed its millionth guest, and families were driving across the country to see it. It helped that Disney had a weekly TV show, so children in faraway states already knew the park before they arrived.

From one park to twelve

Walt Disney World opened near Orlando, Florida, in 1971, on land so large it could hold a city. Its Magic Kingdom park drew about 1.8 crore visitors in 2024, more than any other theme park on Earth. Disneyland in California came second.

Parks followed in Tokyo (1983), near Paris (1992), Hong Kong (2005) and Shanghai (2016). As of 2025, Disney runs 12 parks at six resorts. Tokyo has two of them side by side.

Rides, physics and fear

Theme parks sell a safe kind of fright. A drop ride lets you fall for a second or two, long enough for gravity to make your stomach lurch but never long enough to be dangerous. Engineers test every ride with sandbags and dummies before people climb aboard.

India has its own big parks, such as EsselWorld near Mumbai, which opened in 1989, and the Wonderla parks in cities such as Kochi, Bengaluru and Hyderabad. Most mix water slides with roller coasters, which suits a hot climate. The fairy-tale castle, though, still belongs to a mouse born in a 1928 cartoon.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Walt Disney invented the amusement park.

Truth: Amusement parks with rides and games are centuries older. Disney's new idea was a "theme park", where every part of the park tells a story and visitors can't see the outside world from inside.
