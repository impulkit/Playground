Which planet has a day longer than its year?

Venus takes 243 Earth days to spin round once, but only 225 Earth days to go all the way round the Sun.

The short answer

Venus is the planet whose day is longer than its year. It spins so slowly that one full turn takes about 243 Earth days, while one trip round the Sun takes about 225. It also spins backwards compared with Earth, so on Venus the Sun rises in the west.

There is a twist. Because Venus spins one way while travelling the other, the time from one sunrise to the next is shorter than one full spin, about 117 Earth days. Mercury has the opposite oddity: its sunrise-to-sunrise day lasts about 176 Earth days, twice as long as its 88-day year.

Surprising but true

On Mercury, from sunrise to the next sunrise takes 176 Earth days, which is two whole Mercury years.

Two kinds of day

On Earth, we use "day" to mean 24 hours from one noon to the next. That is a solar day. Earth actually finishes one full spin, measured against the distant stars, about 4 minutes sooner. The gap appears because Earth moves along its orbit while it spins, so it has to turn a little extra to face the Sun again.

On Earth this gap is tiny. On slow-spinning planets, it becomes enormous. Moving along the orbit can add or subtract a great deal of time, depending on which way the planet spins. That is why Venus and Mercury break our everyday ideas of day and night.

Venus, the backwards planet

Most planets, including Earth, spin in the same direction as they orbit the Sun. Venus spins the other way, and very slowly. If you could cycle at a gentle pace along its equator, you could keep up with the Sun. A Venus year holds fewer than two of its sunrise-to-sunrise days.

Why is it backwards? Nobody knows for sure. One idea is that a giant collision long ago knocked it over. Another is that the Sun's pull on Venus's thick atmosphere slowly changed its spin over billions of years. Venus's air is so heavy that standing on the surface would feel like being nearly a kilometre deep in the ocean, and it is about 465 °C, hot enough to melt lead.

Mercury's strange sunrises

Mercury spins three times for every two trips round the Sun. Because of how those numbers fit together, a full day from sunrise to sunrise lasts two of its years. A Mercury child would celebrate two birthdays between breakfast and the next breakfast.

Mercury's orbit is also stretched, not round. When it swings closest to the Sun, it speeds up so much that its orbit briefly outpaces its spin. From some places on the surface, the Sun would rise, stop, go backwards a little, then carry on across the sky.

Earth's days are getting longer

Earth's day is changing too, very slowly. The Moon's pull raises tides in the oceans. As water sloshes against the turning Earth, it acts like a gentle brake. Over a century, the day grows longer by roughly 2 thousandths of a second.

That adds up over deep time. Studies of ancient shells suggest that about 70 million years ago, when dinosaurs were alive, a day lasted about 23 and a half hours. The year had more than 370 days.

Worlds with endless day

Some planets around other stars may be tidally locked: their spin and orbit match exactly, so one side always faces their star. Our Moon does this with Earth, always showing us the same face.

On such a planet, one side would have endless daylight and the other endless night. Scientists wonder if life might survive in the twilight strip between them. And the strangest part? Even our calendars carry signs of these sky rhythms, because Earth's year doesn't fit a whole number of days.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A day is always the time a planet takes to spin once.

Truth: There are two kinds of day. One is a single spin compared with the distant stars. The other is from one sunrise to the next. On Earth they differ by only about 4 minutes, but on Venus and Mercury they are very different.

Try this

Use a lamp as the Sun and a ball with a sticker on it as a planet. Walk the ball slowly in a circle around the lamp while turning it very slowly the opposite way. Count how many times the sticker faces the lamp in one lap. You may find sunrise comes round at a surprising rate.
