How does the lead get inside a pencil?

There has never been any lead in a pencil. The grey stuff is graphite mixed with clay, baked like a pot and sandwiched between two strips of wood.

The short answer

The "lead" in a pencil is graphite, a soft, grey form of carbon, mixed with clay. It is made into long, thin rods and baked hard in an oven. Then the rods are laid in grooves cut into a flat strip of wood. A second grooved strip is glued on top, like a sandwich, and the whole thing is cut into pencils.

So nobody pushes the core into a hole. The pencil is built around it. The shape is cut, the outside is painted, and the end might get a metal band and an eraser. A big factory can make hundreds of thousands of pencils a day.

Surprising but true

In the 1500s, a huge lump of pure graphite was found at Borrowdale in England. It was so valuable that the mine was guarded and the graphite was carried away under armed escort.

Why it's called lead

When people first found graphite, they thought it was a kind of lead, because both are grey and leave a mark. They called it "black lead" or "plumbago". The name stuck, even after chemists showed in the 1700s that graphite is carbon.

The first big find was at Borrowdale, in the hills of north-west England, in the 1500s. The graphite there was so pure it could be sawn into sticks and used for writing. Shepherds used it to mark their sheep. Before that, people had written with tools like reed pens, quills and sticks of soft metal. Because it was so valuable, the mine was guarded, and stealing graphite became a serious crime.

Clay changes everything

Pure graphite was rare. In 1795, during a war that cut France off from English graphite, a French scientist named Nicolas-Jacques Conté found a solution. He ground poorer graphite into powder, mixed it with clay and water, and baked the paste in a kiln.

This had a bonus. More clay made a harder, lighter pencil. More graphite made a softer, darker one. That's where the pencil scale comes from. "H" means hard and "B" means black. An HB pencil is in the middle. A 6B is very soft and dark, good for drawing, while a 4H draws pale, thin lines.

How a pencil writes

Graphite is made of flat sheets of carbon atoms stacked on top of each other, like a deck of cards. The sheets slide apart very easily. When you drag a pencil across paper, the rough paper scrapes off flakes, which stick in its fibres and leave a grey line.

That's also why pencil marks can be rubbed out. The graphite sits on top of the paper instead of soaking in like ink, so an eraser can lift it off.

Diamond is carbon too, with its atoms locked in a rigid 3D pattern, which makes it the hardest natural material. Graphite and diamond are made of the same element, arranged differently. In factories, graphite squeezed under enormous heat and pressure can even be turned into small diamonds.

The wood around it

Pencil makers need wood that is soft enough to sharpen easily but doesn't splinter. In the US, incense cedar from California is popular. In India, most pencil wood is poplar, and much of it comes from the Kashmir Valley. Around Pulwama, many villages grow poplar trees for pencil factories.

Once glued and cut, pencils get several coats of paint. Many American pencils are yellow. In the 1890s, a company called Koh-i-Noor started selling fancy yellow pencils, and yellow came to suggest quality. Others copied the colour.

Pencils in space

There's a story that NASA spent millions on a space pen while Russian cosmonauts just used pencils. It isn't really true. Both used pencils at first. But pencil tips can snap off, and tiny flakes of graphite floating in a spacecraft could get into electronics, so both countries later switched to special pens made by a private company.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Chewing a pencil can give you lead poisoning.

Truth: Pencils contain graphite and clay, not lead, so the core isn't poisonous. In the past, some pencils had paint that contained lead, but that has been banned in many countries for decades.

Try this

Look at the end of an unsharpened pencil. You can often spot a faint line where the two halves of wood were glued together. Then rub a soft pencil hard on paper and shade a coin underneath. The slippery graphite shows how its layers slide off and stick to paper.
