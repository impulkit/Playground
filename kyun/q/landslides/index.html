Why do hillsides slide?

The largest landslide ever recorded happened in a few minutes in 1980, when the side of a volcano in Washington State collapsed.

The short answer

A landslide happens when rock, soil or mud on a slope suddenly moves downhill. Gravity is always pulling everything on a hillside down. Normally, friction and the strength of the ground hold it in place.

When something tips the balance, the slope gives way. Heavy rain is the most common trigger, especially in India's monsoon. Earthquakes, volcanoes, rivers eating away at the bottom of a slope, and people cutting into hillsides for roads and buildings can all set off landslides too.

Surprising but true

When Mount St. Helens erupted in 1980, its whole north side slid away first, in the largest landslide in recorded history. It moved enough rock to fill about a million Olympic swimming pools.

A tug of war on every slope

On every hillside, there is a quiet tug of war. Gravity pulls the soil and rock downhill. Friction between grains, the roots of plants, and the strength of the rock itself hold them back.

The steeper the slope, the harder gravity pulls. A gentle hill can stay put for thousands of years. A steep cliff of loose rock may only need a small push. Dry sand, for example, can't form a heap steeper than about 30 to 35 degrees before it starts to slide.

How rain sets things moving

A little water can help soil stick together. That's why you can build a sandcastle with damp sand but not with dry sand. But too much water does the opposite.

When heavy rain soaks into a slope, it fills the tiny gaps between grains of soil. The water pushes the grains apart and makes the ground heavier. Friction drops, while the weight pressing downhill goes up. If a layer of clay or solid rock lies below, water can collect on top of it like a slippery sheet, and everything above slides off.

That's why so many landslides happen after days of rain, not just one shower. In India, most come during the monsoon.

Why do things slow down?A football rolling across grass stops by itself. Voyager 1, launched in 1977, is still coasting through space. The difference is friction.

Kinds of landslides

Landslides come in many forms. In a rockfall, boulders break off a cliff and tumble down. In a slump, a chunk of hillside slides down along a curved surface, like a spoonful scooped from a pudding. In a mudflow or debris flow, soaked soil, rocks and trees rush downhill like a river, often following a valley. These can move faster than a car.

Some landslides are very slow, creeping a few centimetres a year. You can sometimes spot them by trees with curved trunks, fences out of line, or cracks in roads.

Landslides in India

India's most landslide-prone regions are the Himalaya, which are young and still rising, and the Western Ghats, where steep slopes meet some of the heaviest rain in the country. The Geological Survey of India has mapped which areas are most at risk.

In July 2024, after extremely heavy rain, huge landslides swept through villages in the Wayanad district of Kerala. It was one of the worst landslide disasters in India's recent history, and rescue teams worked for weeks. Many experts pointed to the very heavy rain, the steep slopes and changes to the land as reasons it was so severe.

Earthquakes and volcanoes

Strong earthquakes shake slopes and can loosen thousands of landslides at once. In mountains, landslides often cause much of the damage after a big quake.

Volcanoes can cause the biggest landslides of all. On 18 May 1980, an earthquake shook Mount St. Helens in Washington State. Its bulging north side collapsed in the largest landslide ever recorded, and the eruption burst out moments later.

Keeping slopes safe

People can make slopes safer. Planting trees and grasses helps, because roots bind the soil. Drains carry rainwater away before it soaks in. Walls and wire nets hold back loose rock along roads in the Himalaya and the Western Ghats.

Warning systems help too. Rain gauges and sensors on slopes can alert officials when the ground is getting dangerously wet. In the United States, the USGS works with weather forecasters to issue warnings when heavy rain could trigger debris flows, especially on hillsides recently burned by wildfires.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Landslides always happen suddenly, with no warning at all.

Truth: Some are sudden, but many give signs first, such as new cracks in the ground or walls, tilting trees or poles, or water bubbling up where it didn't before. Experts watch for these signs, and people in hilly areas are taught to report them.

Try this

Build a slope in a tray. Make a steep heap of dry sand or soil at one end of a baking tray and note its shape. Now slowly sprinkle water on top from a small cup. At first the slope holds together better. Keep adding water and watch what happens when it gets too wet. Try again with a few small sticks pushed in, like roots.
