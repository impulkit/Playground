How is sea salt made?

Every bucket of seawater holds a small handful of salt. All you need to get it out is sunshine, wind and a lot of patience.

The short answer

Sea salt is made by letting the sun and wind dry up seawater. Workers let seawater flow into a series of wide, shallow ponds called salt pans. As water evaporates, the water left behind gets saltier and saltier. Eventually it can hold no more salt, and white crystals begin to form on the bottom.

Salt workers then rake the crystals into piles, let them drain and dry, and send them off to be cleaned and packed. The whole process can take weeks, and it works best in places with hot sun, steady wind and little rain, such as the coasts of Gujarat and Tamil Nadu.

Surprising but true

Salt ponds near San Francisco turn red, orange and green, coloured by tiny living things that thrive in very salty water.

From sea to crystal

Seawater is about 3.5 percent salt. That doesn't sound like much, but it means each litre carries about 35 g, mostly sodium chloride, the same salt you sprinkle on food. Rivers wash tiny amounts of minerals off rocks into the sea, and over millions of years the ocean has grown salty.

In a salt works, seawater moves slowly from one pond to the next. In the first ponds, mud and sand settle out. Some minerals, such as gypsum, crystallise before common salt does. Only when the water has shrunk to about a tenth of its starting amount does sodium chloride start to form crystals. Workers try to collect the salt before other bitter-tasting minerals come out too.

India's salt country

India is one of the world's largest salt makers, and roughly three-quarters of its salt comes from Gujarat. Much of it is made along the coast. In the Little Rann of Kutch, salt workers called Agariyas use a different method. They pump salty water from under the ground into pans in the flat, baking desert and spend months tending them.

The work is hard. Temperatures can pass 45 °C, the glare off the white salt is fierce, and many families live in simple huts at the pans for most of the year. Tamil Nadu, especially around Thoothukudi, is India's other big salt producer. Farther north, the Great Rann dries into a white desert of natural salt that stretches to the horizon.

A handful of salt and a march

Salt has always mattered, because people need it to stay healthy and to preserve food. In British India, it was illegal for Indians to make or sell their own salt, and a tax was charged on it.

In 1930, Mahatma Gandhi and his followers walked about 385 km from Sabarmati Ashram to the seaside village of Dandi in Gujarat. On 6 April he picked up a lump of natural salt from the shore, breaking the law on purpose. The Salt March inspired people across India to make their own salt and helped build the movement for independence.

Rainbow ponds

Salt pans can look surprisingly colourful. As brine gets saltier, it suits different tiny living things. A kind of green alga thrives in fairly salty water and turns orange-red as the salt increases. In the saltiest ponds, microbes stain the water pink or deep red.

From a plane over the south of San Francisco Bay, old salt ponds look like a giant paint palette. Many of these ponds are now being turned back into wetlands for birds and fish. Some flamingos in India's salt flats and lagoons feed on tiny brine shrimp that also live in very salty water.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Sea salt is much healthier than ordinary table salt.

Truth: Spoon for spoon, sea salt and table salt contain about the same amount of sodium. Sea salt may have tiny traces of other minerals, but advice from doctors about eating less salt applies to both kinds.

Try this

With a grown-up's help, stir two spoonfuls of salt into half a cup of warm water. Pour a thin layer into a dark saucer and leave it on a sunny windowsill for a few days. As the water evaporates, small salt crystals appear, just as they do in a salt pan.
