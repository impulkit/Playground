Who was Bhaskara II, and what is the Lilavati?

In the 1100s an Indian mathematician wrote a maths book full of puzzles about bees, peacocks and snakes. It was still used in schools 700 years later.

The short answer

Bhaskara II, also called Bhaskaracharya, was one of medieval India's greatest mathematicians and astronomers. Born in 1114 CE, he became head of the astronomical observatory at Ujjain, a famous centre of learning where Brahmagupta had worked five centuries earlier.

Around 1150 he finished a huge work called the Siddhanta Shiromani, the "crown jewel of astronomy". Its first part, the Lilavati, teaches arithmetic through puzzles written as poems. Its second, the Bijaganita, covers algebra. Together they were copied, studied and taught across India for centuries.

Surprising but true

A puzzle that Bhaskara II solved in the 1100s was set as a challenge by the French mathematician Pierre de Fermat in 1657, who thought it would stump Europe's best minds.

A maths book in verse

The Lilavati speaks to a young reader, often addressed as a clever girl, and sets problems about everyday things. There are questions about bees among flowers, a peacock pouncing on a snake, the price of goods in a market and the size of a bamboo broken by the wind.

Each problem is a short verse in Sanskrit, so students could learn it by heart. Working out the answer meant using rules for fractions, square roots, interest, areas and volumes. That mix of story and maths kept the book popular. In 1587, the poet Faizi translated it into Persian for the Mughal emperor Akbar.

A much-told legend says Bhaskara named the book after his daughter, Lilavati. The story comes from long after his death and may not be true, but it has made the book even more famous.

Why is Akbar called 'the Great'?The emperor who never learned to read built a huge library, and invited Hindus, Muslims, Jains, Zoroastrians and Christians to argue in front of him.

Zero and infinity

Bhaskara wrote clear rules for working with zero. Adding zero changes nothing. Multiplying by zero gives zero. Then he tackled the hard case: dividing by zero.

He called the result khahara, which means "with zero as its divisor", and described it as an endless quantity. Adding to it or taking away from it, he wrote, makes no difference, just as the infinite god Vishnu is unchanged by the worlds that come and go. Mathematicians today say dividing by zero has no answer at all, but Bhaskara's thinking shows how seriously he took the puzzle of infinity.

The puzzle that stumped Europe

In the Bijaganita, Bhaskara solved problems where you hunt for whole numbers that fit equations like 61 × y² + 1 = x². The smallest answer is huge: x is 1,766,319,049 and y is 226,153,980.

He used a step-by-step method called chakravala, meaning "wheel", which improved on ideas from Brahmagupta and the later scholar Jayadeva. In 1657, Fermat set this exact equation as a challenge to other European mathematicians. Europe worked out a full general method only in the 1700s.

Bhaskara the astronomer

The later parts of the Siddhanta Shiromani deal with the movements of planets, eclipses, and the shape of the Earth. Bhaskara explained that the Earth is a sphere hanging in space, held in place by its own power, and that it attracts heavy objects towards itself.

Some people today say this means he discovered gravity before Isaac Newton. Historians are more careful. Bhaskara's idea was a description, not a law with a formula that explains orbits and tides. Even so, his astronomy tables were accurate enough to be used for calendars for centuries.

Remembered

In 1207, Bhaskara's grandson set up a school to study his works, recorded in a stone inscription in Maharashtra. Today his books are still read in their original Sanskrit and in translation, and his puzzles still turn up in Indian textbooks.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Bhaskara II discovered the law of gravity before Isaac Newton.

Truth: Bhaskara wrote that the Earth has a natural power of attraction that pulls heavy things towards it, building on older Indian ideas. But he didn't give a mathematical law for how gravity works across space. That came from Newton in 1687.
