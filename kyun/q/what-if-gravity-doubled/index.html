What if gravity were twice as strong?

Your school bag would weigh as much as two, a dropped glass would hit the floor 40 per cent faster, and every kitchen would work like a pressure cooker.

The short answer

If Earth's gravity doubled, everything would weigh twice as much. You would feel as if you were carrying another person on your back all day. Jumping, climbing stairs and even lifting your arms would be hard work.

Things would fall faster, so trips and spills would hurt more. The air would be pressed down harder too, roughly doubling the pressure at ground level. Rockets would struggle to reach space. Over time, plants and animals would probably evolve to be shorter, stockier and stronger. This can't really happen to Earth, but it's a great way to see how much gravity shapes our lives.

Surprising but true

With twice the gravity, air pressure at the ground would roughly double, so water would boil at about 120°C, just like inside a pressure cooker.

Your body under double weight

Your heart would have to pump harder to push blood up to your brain. Fighter pilots feel several times their normal weight in tight turns, and without special suits, blood drains from their heads and they can black out. Double gravity is gentler than that, but it would never let up.

Your bones and muscles would be under constant strain. Knees and backs would ache. Astronauts in space lose bone because gravity is too weak. Here, the problem would flip. Bones would likely grow thicker over a lifetime, as they do in people who carry heavy loads.

And your jump? It would be far less than half its usual height, because your legs would have to lift twice the weight, and gravity would drag you back twice as hard.

Falling faster

Gravity speeds up falling objects. Double it, and a dropped glass would hit the floor about 1.4 times faster. A fall from a tree or a wall would be much more dangerous.

Raindrops would fall faster and hit harder. Rivers would rush more quickly downhill.

How much would you weigh on other planets?A child who weighs 40 kg on Earth would weigh about 15 kg on Mars and about 101 kg on Jupiter, without gaining or losing a single gram.

A world in a pressure cooker

The air presses down on us because gravity pulls it towards the ground. Double gravity would roughly double that push.

That would change cooking. Water boils at 100°C at normal pressure. At double pressure, it boils at about 120°C. That's exactly what a pressure cooker does in an Indian kitchen to cook rice and dal faster. With double gravity, every open pot would act a bit like one.

Denser air would also push harder against anything moving through it, from cricket balls to birds.

Shorter trees, chunkier animals

Tall trees, like the redwoods of California, must lift water more than 100 metres up their trunks. Stronger gravity would make that much harder. Forests would probably be shorter.

Animals would feel it too. When an animal doubles in size, its weight grows much faster than the strength of its legs. That's why elephants have thick, pillar-like legs while spiders can have thin ones. With double gravity, the biggest animals, like elephants and giraffes, would struggle most. Over millions of years, life would likely become lower and sturdier.

Rockets in trouble

To leave Earth, a rocket must reach about 11.2 km every second. If Earth had twice the mass at the same size, that would rise to about 16 km a second. Rockets would need far more fuel, and big rockets might barely get off the ground.

A German scientist calculated in 2018 that on some heavy "super-Earth" planets, chemical rockets might hardly be able to reach space at all. If aliens live on such worlds, they may be stuck at home.

Where you can feel it

You can't double Earth's gravity, but you can borrow the feeling. A roller coaster pulling out of a dip, or a lift starting upward, briefly makes you feel heavier.

On the giant planets, the pull is real. At the cloud tops of Jupiter, gravity is about 2.5 times stronger than on Earth, so a 30 kg child would weigh as much as a 75 kg adult. Every world has its own pull, which is why your weight changes so much across the planets. Even a skydiver would plunge noticeably faster under Jupiter-like gravity.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Stronger gravity would make everything fall at the same slow speed, only heavier.

Truth: Stronger gravity makes things fall faster. With double gravity, a ball dropped from a table would hit the floor about 1.4 times faster than now, and falls of all kinds would hurt more.

Try this

Fill a school bag with books until it weighs about as much as a quarter of your body, with an adult checking it's safe. Wear it for five minutes while you walk and climb a few stairs. Now picture every part of you, including your arms and head, feeling heavier like that, all day.
