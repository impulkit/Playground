Why is Diwali called the festival of lights?

Millions of small oil lamps glow on the darkest night of the month.

The short answer

Diwali comes from the Sanskrit deepavali, meaning "a row of lamps". On Diwali night, families light clay lamps called diyas in rows along doorsteps, windows and rooftops. It falls on the new moon of the month of Kartik, the darkest night, so the lamps shine brightest.

The lights stand for light winning over darkness and good over evil. Homes are cleaned and decorated, people wear new clothes, share sweets and visit friends and relatives.

Because it follows the Moon, Diwali moves around the calendar, usually landing in October or November.

Surprising but true

Firework colours come from metals: strontium makes red, barium makes green, copper makes blue and sodium makes yellow.

Many stories, many people

For many Hindus, Diwali marks the return of Lord Rama to Ayodhya after 14 years in exile, as told in the Ramayana. People lit lamps to welcome him home. Many also worship the goddess Lakshmi, who is linked with wealth and good fortune.

In parts of South India, the main day celebrates Krishna's victory over the demon Narakasura. In Bengal, the same night is Kali Puja. Sikhs celebrate Bandi Chhor Divas, remembering Guru Hargobind's release from Gwalior Fort, and the Golden Temple in Amritsar glows with lights. Jains remember Mahavira, who attained nirvana, or final release, on this night.

Five days of festival

In much of north and west India, Diwali stretches over five days. It starts with Dhanteras, when many families buy something new, often metal pots or gold. Next comes Naraka Chaturdashi, sometimes called Choti Diwali.

The third day is the main festival, with Lakshmi Puja and the lamps. Then comes Govardhan Puja, and in some regions a new year begins. The last day, Bhai Dooj, celebrates brothers and sisters. Traditions differ from region to region, which is part of the fun.

Colours at the door

Families draw rangoli or kolam patterns with coloured powder, rice flour or flower petals to welcome guests. Many designs are symmetrical and built from dots and loops. Strings of marigolds and mango leaves hang over doors.

Kitchens are busy for days, making sweets like laddoos, karanji and barfi to share with neighbours.

Many families also play cards or board games on Diwali night, an old custom said to bring luck. Shops and businesses close their old account books and open new ones, which is why traders in Gujarat often treat Diwali as the start of a fresh year.

How do fireworks get their colours?Every firework colour comes from a metal. Strontium glows red, barium green, and copper makes the hardest one, blue.

The chemistry of sparkle

Firework colours come from metals heated until they glow. Each metal gives off its own colour of light, so firework makers mix different compounds to paint the sky. Most of India's fireworks are made in Sivakasi, in Tamil Nadu.

Fireworks also cause air and noise pollution, and they frighten pets and birds. In northern cities, Diwali often comes as the winter air is already smoggy, so smoke from crackers adds to the problem. Courts and city governments have restricted fireworks in some years, and many families now celebrate mostly with lamps and electric lights.

Diwali around the world

Today Diwali is celebrated across India and by communities around the world, from Singapore to New York. It is a public holiday in countries such as Nepal, Sri Lanka, Mauritius, Fiji and Trinidad and Tobago. In 2023, New York made Diwali a holiday for New York City's public schools, and they closed for it for the first time in 2024. Wherever it is held, the first lamp is usually lit just after sunset, as the moonless night begins.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Diwali is only a Hindu festival.

Truth: Sikhs celebrate Bandi Chhor Divas at the same time, Jains mark the day Mahavira attained nirvana, and some Buddhists, especially Newars in Nepal, celebrate too. Each has its own reasons for lighting lamps.

Try this

Make a rangoli on a sheet of paper or a tray using rice, lentils, flower petals or coloured chalk. Start with a dot in the middle and work outwards in rings. Fold a paper copy in half to check whether your pattern is symmetrical, the way many traditional kolam designs are.
