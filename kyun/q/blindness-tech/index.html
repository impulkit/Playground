How do blind people use phones?

Many blind people use a smartphone with the screen switched off, listening to it read aloud at speeds so fast that most sighted people can't follow.

The short answer

Blind people use phones with the help of a screen reader, software that reads aloud whatever is on the screen. Every modern smartphone has one built in: VoiceOver on iPhones and TalkBack on Android phones.

With a screen reader turned on, touch works differently. Sliding a finger over the screen makes the phone speak the name of each button or line of text. Swiping left or right moves from one item to the next. A double tap opens whatever was just read out. Users can type by touch and sound, dictate with their voice, or connect a braille keyboard or display.

Surprising but true

Experienced screen-reader users often set their phones to speak at two or three times normal talking speed, and understand every word.

Listening at top speed

New users usually start with the voice at normal speed. Over months, many speed it up, until it sounds like a blur to everyone else. Experienced users often listen at two or three times the speed of ordinary speech. The brain adapts, just as a sighted reader learns to skim a page.

Some people also use headphones so their messages aren't read out to the whole bus. Many use a trick called the screen curtain, which turns the display black while the phone still works. It saves battery and stops others peeking.

Feeling the words

Braille is a system of raised dots that people read with their fingertips. It was invented in France by Louis Braille, who was blinded in an accident as a small child. He published his system in 1829, after developing it from the age of 15.

A refreshable braille display is a flat device with rows of tiny pins that pop up and down to form braille letters. Connected to a phone by Bluetooth, it lets a person read texts and emails with their fingers. These displays are expensive, often costing more than the phone itself, so groups in many countries, including India, have worked on cheaper versions.

How do computers see?In 1966, a professor in Massachusetts thought teaching computers to see would make a good summer project for students. Experts are still working on it.

The phone as eyes

Phone cameras have become powerful helpers. Apps using computer vision can read printed text aloud, describe a scene, recognise banknotes or identify colours of clothes. Some apps connect a blind user by video call with a sighted volunteer, who can read a label or check whether a light is on.

Indian banknotes have small raised marks and different sizes to help people tell them apart. In 2020, the Reserve Bank of India also released an app called MANI that identifies notes using the phone camera.

Maps apps give spoken directions, and some describe what's around a corner. Combined with a white cane or a guide dog, they help many blind people travel on their own.

When apps get it wrong

A screen reader can only read what app makers tell it. If a button has no label, the phone may just say "button", with no clue what it does. Pictures without descriptions are silent. Badly designed apps can be impossible for a blind person to use.

That's why accessibility is now part of good design. Developers are encouraged to label every button, describe important images and test apps with a screen reader. In many countries, including India and the US, laws require government websites to be accessible to disabled people.

More than the phone

The World Health Organization estimated that about 43 million people worldwide were blind in 2020, and many more have weaker sight. Many live in countries like India, where cheap phones have made a big difference.

Blind people also use computers with screen readers. Free ones like NVDA, made by two blind programmers in Australia, are used around the world. For a lot of blind students, the phone in their pocket has become a library, a map and a scanner all in one.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Blind people can't use touchscreen phones because they can't feel the buttons.

Truth: Built-in screen readers turn touchscreens into talking devices. Many blind people use smartphones every day to text, read, shop, travel and take photos.

Try this

Ask an adult to help you turn on the screen reader on a phone or tablet (VoiceOver on Apple devices, TalkBack on Android). Close your eyes and try to open one app and find the clock using only touch and sound. Ask the adult to show you how to turn it off again before you start.
