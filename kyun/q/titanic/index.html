Why did the Titanic sink?

The biggest ship in the world hit an iceberg on its very first voyage and sank in under three hours.

The short answer

The Titanic sank because it hit an iceberg at night, at high speed, in the North Atlantic Ocean. The collision opened gaps along the right-hand side of the hull below the waterline. Seawater poured into the front of the ship. The Titanic was built to stay afloat with up to four of its front compartments flooded, but at least five began filling. The bow sank lower, water spilled over the walls into the next compartments, and the ship was doomed.

It struck the iceberg at about 11:40 pm on 14 April 1912 and sank at about 2:20 am on 15 April. Of about 2,200 people on board, around 1,500 died, many in the freezing water. There were not enough lifeboats for everyone.

Surprising but true

The Titanic carried lifeboats for only about half the people on board, and that was completely legal at the time.

A floating city

The Titanic was the largest moving object people had ever built. It was about 269 m long, nearly as long as three football fields, and as tall as a 17-storey building from keel to the tops of its funnels. It had a gym, a swimming pool, restaurants and grand staircases for its richest passengers. Most people on board, though, travelled in second and third class, including many families moving to America for a new life.

Its first voyage left Southampton in England on 10 April 1912, bound for New York. Steel ships like it float because their hulls push aside a huge weight of water, as you can read in how ships float.

Warnings and bad luck

That spring, unusually many icebergs had drifted south from the Arctic. Icebergs break off glaciers, mostly in Greenland, and float south on the cold Labrador Current. Several ships radioed warnings about ice to the Titanic that day. Not all of them reached the captain.

The night was calm and moonless. With no waves breaking at its base, the iceberg was hard to spot until it was very close. The lookouts in the crow's nest had no binoculars. By the time they shouted the warning, there was less than a minute to turn. The ship swerved but scraped along the ice.

Calling for help

The Titanic's wireless operators sent distress calls, including the new SOS signal, using radio, which was still a young invention. A nearby ship, the Californian, was probably close enough to help, but its radio operator had gone to bed and switched off his set.

The Carpathia, about 100 km away, heard the call and raced through the ice. It arrived about an hour and a half after the Titanic sank and rescued about 700 survivors from the lifeboats. Many lifeboats had left half empty, partly because at first many passengers did not believe the ship could sink.

What changed

The disaster shocked the world and changed the rules of the sea. In 1914, nations signed the first international agreement on safety at sea. Ships now had to carry enough lifeboats for everyone on board, hold lifeboat drills and keep their radios on day and night. An International Ice Patrol was set up to watch for icebergs in the North Atlantic. It still operates today.

The wreck lay hidden for 73 years. In 1985, a team led by Robert Ballard of the USA and Jean-Louis Michel of France found it about 3,800 m down, broken in two. Remote cameras and deep-diving vehicles have visited it since, as you can read in ocean explorers. Tiny living things are slowly eating away its iron, and one day little of it may be left.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Everyone at the time said the Titanic was unsinkable.

Truth: A few magazines and newspapers called ships like it "practically unsinkable", but its owners did not make it a big promise. The idea that everyone believed it grew much stronger after the disaster, in books and films.

Try this

Test watertight compartments. Take a plastic food tray with several sections and float it in a sink. Fill one section with water using a spoon. Does it still float? Keep filling sections one by one at the same end and see how many it takes before the tray tips and sinks.
