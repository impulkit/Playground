Could people live on Mars?

A machine the size of a toaster has already made oxygen out of the thin Martian air, enough in total for a small dog to breathe for about ten hours.

The short answer

People could probably visit Mars one day, and maybe stay for a while in sealed bases. Living there for a lifetime, as a real colony, would be extremely hard.

Mars is cold, with an average temperature of about −60 °C. Its air is mostly carbon dioxide, so nobody could breathe it, and so thin that liquid water left in the open would quickly boil away. There is no global magnetic field, so harmful radiation reaches the ground. Dust storms can cover the whole planet. Gravity is only about 38% of Earth's, and nobody knows what living in it for years would do to the human body.

On the other hand, Mars has a day almost the same length as ours, frozen water in the ground and a surface you can stand on. That makes it the most likely place in the solar system for people to try.

Surprising but true

Air pressure on Mars is so low, less than 1% of Earth's, that water in an open cup would boil away even though the planet is freezing cold.

Getting there

Earth and Mars line up well for a trip only about every 26 months. Even then, a journey takes about six to nine months each way. A crew would have to stay on Mars for many months before the planets lined up again for the trip home. A round trip could last around two and a half to three years.

During that time, astronauts would face radiation, weightlessness during the cruise and the strain of living in a tiny space far from home. Research on the International Space Station shows that months in space affect the body, weakening bones and muscles and even changing eyesight.

What happens to the body in space?Astronauts come home a few centimetres taller, with weaker bones and puffy faces, and some have to relearn how to walk.

Making air, water and food

Carrying everything from Earth would be hugely expensive, so settlers would need to make things on Mars. NASA's Perseverance rover carried an experiment called MOXIE, about the size of a toaster. Between 2021 and 2023, it pulled carbon dioxide from the Martian air and split off oxygen, proving the idea works on a small scale.

Water could come from ice buried in the soil, especially at middle and high latitudes. Food is harder. Martian soil contains salts called perchlorates that are harmful to people, so it would need cleaning before crops could grow. Plants would also need greenhouses for warmth, air pressure and protection, but they would use photosynthesis just as they do on Earth.

Practising on Earth

Space agencies test life on Mars without leaving home. In 2023, NASA sealed four volunteers inside a 3D-printed "Mars habitat" in Houston for a year to study how they coped. Other teams have lived in domes on the slopes of a Hawaiian volcano or in remote research stations. Scientists who spend winters in Antarctica face months of isolation and darkness that are a useful model too.

India has its own Mars connection. In 2014, ISRO's Mars Orbiter Mission, nicknamed Mangalyaan, reached Mars orbit on its first attempt, making India the first Asian country to get a spacecraft there. Its budget was famously small for a planetary mission.

The big argument

Some people, including the founder of the company SpaceX, say humans should become a "multi-planet species" and want to send large ships to Mars soon. Supporters say a base on Mars would teach us a great deal and protect humanity from disasters on Earth.

Many scientists are more cautious. They point out that even the harshest desert on Earth is far kinder than Mars, and that the money might do more good solving problems here. Others worry about bringing Earth microbes to Mars before we know whether it has life of its own. The first human footprint on Mars may still be decades away, and nobody can say yet whether a child will ever be born there.

Myth or fact?

Myth: We could give Mars a thick atmosphere and make it like Earth in a few years.

Truth: A 2018 NASA-funded study found Mars doesn't have enough carbon dioxide left that can be released to warm it up with today's technology. Turning Mars into a second Earth would take far longer than a human lifetime, if it is possible at all.
