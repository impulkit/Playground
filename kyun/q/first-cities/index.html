What were the first cities?

In one of the world's earliest towns, there were no streets. People walked across the rooftops and climbed down ladders into their homes.

The short answer

The first cities grew in places where farming produced more food than farmers needed. That spare food meant some people could do other jobs full time: making pots, trading, building, writing records or leading worship. Thousands of people living close together needed walls, streets, water, drains and rules.

Which was the very first city depends on what you call a city. Çatalhöyük in Turkey was a large town about 9,000 years ago. Uruk, in Mesopotamia, grew into what many historians call the first true city around 5,000 years ago. Soon after, cities rose along the Nile, the Indus, the Yellow River and, separately, in Peru.

Surprising but true

Archaeologists have not found a clear royal palace or a grand royal tomb in Mohenjo-daro, one of the largest cities of its time.

A town with no streets

Çatalhöyük was home to perhaps several thousand people from about 7100 BCE. Its mud-brick houses were packed so tightly that people moved around on the roofs. Inside, families slept on raised platforms, cooked at ovens, and painted walls with hunting scenes. They buried their dead under the floors.

Many experts call it a big village or town rather than a city. Nearly every house looked alike, and there are few signs of rich and poor, rulers or specialists.

Uruk: walls and writing

Uruk stood beside the Euphrates in what is now southern Iraq. By around 3000 BCE, it may have held tens of thousands of people. It had temples on raised platforms, workshops, and a city wall later said to be about 9 km long.

Running a place this big meant tracking grain, workers and taxes. Officials began pressing marks into clay tablets. Those marks grew into writing, one of the first systems in the world. Uruk was one of many city-states in Mesopotamia, each with its own ruler and god.

Planned cities of the Indus

Around 2600 BCE, cities appeared in the Indus valley, in today's Pakistan and north-west India. Mohenjo-daro and Harappa were laid out on a grid, with main streets running north to south and east to west. Houses were built of baked bricks of standard sizes. Many had bathrooms connected to covered drains, cleaner than many cities had thousands of years later.

The Indus cities stretched far. Dholavira in Gujarat, on an island in the salt flats of Kutch, had huge water tanks to catch rain. It became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2021. Rakhigarhi in Haryana may have been one of the largest of all.

Cities across the world

Egypt's cities grew along the Nile. In China, early cities rose along the Yellow River. In Peru, Caral was built around 2600 BCE, with stepped pyramids and sunken plazas, without any contact with Asia or Africa. People in different places invented cities on their own.

In 1950 the archaeologist V. Gordon Childe listed signs of a true city, such as full-time specialists, large public buildings, trade, writing and a surplus of food. Not every early city had all of them, which is why the debate continues.

What cities brought

Cities brought new inventions and ideas, as crowds of people swapped skills. They also brought new problems: rubbish, disease, crime and the need to share water. Today more than half of all people live in cities, and planners still wrestle with the same questions the builders of Mohenjo-daro faced: where clean water comes from and where waste goes.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Every ancient city had a king living in a great palace.

Truth: Many did, but not all. In the Indus cities, archaeologists have found big public buildings, granaries and a huge bath, but no clear palace or royal tombs. How these cities were run is still a mystery.
