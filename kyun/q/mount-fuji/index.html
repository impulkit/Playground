Why is Mount Fuji special in Japan?

Mount Fuji is Japan's tallest mountain and an active volcano. It last erupted in 1707, covering the city now called Tokyo in ash.

The short answer

Mount Fuji is Japan's highest mountain, at 3,776 metres. It is a volcano with an almost perfectly even cone, often topped with snow. On a clear day it can be seen from Tokyo, about 100 km away.

It is special for more than its height. For centuries, Japanese people have treated Fuji as sacred, a place where gods live. Pilgrims have climbed it to pray. Poets have written about it and artists have painted it again and again. It appears on banknotes, stamps and countless souvenirs. For many people in Japan and abroad, its outline means "Japan".

Surprising but true

Mount Fuji is a World Heritage Site for its culture, not its nature. UNESCO listed it in 2013 as a "place of worship and source of artistic inspiration".

A young volcano

Fuji sits where three of the Earth's tectonic plates meet. Its present cone is fairly young in geological terms. It grew on top of older volcanoes over roughly the past 10,000 to 17,000 years, as layer after layer of lava and ash piled up. A tall, steep cone built of layers like this is called a stratovolcano.

Fuji last erupted in 1707. The eruption didn't send lava pouring down the slopes. Instead it blasted out ash and cinders for about two weeks. Ash fell on Edo, the city that later became Tokyo, about 100 km away. Fuji has been quiet since, but it is still an active volcano, and scientists monitor it for small earthquakes and changes in shape.

A sacred mountain

In the Shinto religion, mountains are often seen as homes of spirits called kami. Fuji is linked with a goddess of blossoms, Konohanasakuya-hime, and many shrines are dedicated to her around the mountain. The summit crater is treated as a holy place.

For centuries, groups of pilgrims climbed Fuji wearing white robes, ringing bells and chanting. Until 1872, women were officially banned from climbing to the top, though a few went up anyway. Today, climbers of every background go up, some still stopping at shrines on the way.

The mountain in art

Fuji appears in Japanese poems that are well over a thousand years old. Later, short haiku poets wrote about it too.

The most famous pictures of Fuji are by the artist Katsushika Hokusai. In the 1830s he made a series of woodblock prints called Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji. One of them, The Great Wave off Kanagawa, shows a huge curling wave with tiny Fuji in the distance. It became one of the best-known images in the world and influenced European painters like Van Gogh and Monet.

Climbing to the top

About 200,000 people climb Fuji each summer, during the official season from about July to early September. There are four main trails, divided into ten stations. Most climbers take a bus to the fifth station, about halfway up, then walk the rest. The path is a zigzag of loose volcanic gravel, and the climb usually takes five to seven hours.

Many climb through the night, resting in mountain huts, to reach the summit for sunrise. The Japanese call this view goraiko. The air at the top has about a third less oxygen than at sea level, so climbers can get altitude sickness.

Crowds have become a problem. From 2025, the government charges climbers 4,000 yen each on the main trails, limits numbers and closes the gates to people who try to climb overnight without booking a hut.

Seen from afar

From the north, Fuji is often reflected in five lakes at its base. In spring, cherry blossoms frame it. The bullet train between Tokyo and Osaka passes close by, and passengers rush to the right-hand windows when it comes into view. Fuji is often hidden by cloud, so a clear sight of it still feels like luck.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Mount Fuji is an extinct volcano that will never erupt again.

Truth: Fuji is classed as active. It has been quiet since 1707, but scientists watch it closely, and Japanese authorities have plans ready in case it erupts again.
