How did the Pallavas build Mahabalipuram?

At Mahabalipuram, Pallava sculptors turned whole boulders into temples, elephants and gods, carving downwards from the top as if peeling fruit.

The short answer

The Pallavas were kings who ruled northern Tamil Nadu from their capital, Kanchipuram, and were at their strongest from about 600 to 900 CE. Their port, Mamallapuram, now usually called Mahabalipuram, lies on the coast about 60 kilometres south of Chennai.

There, over about a hundred years, Pallava sculptors cut caves into hillsides and carved temples out of single boulders. They covered a cliff with a giant scene of gods, animals and people, and finally built a stone temple by the sea. They were working out, step by step, how to make temples in stone, and their ideas shaped south Indian temple architecture for centuries.

Surprising but true

When the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami pulled the sea back at Mahabalipuram, people briefly saw carved rocks that had been hidden under the water and sand.

A king who loved caves

Before the Pallavas, most temples in the south were built of brick, wood and plaster, which rot and crumble. King Mahendravarman I, who ruled in the early 600s, ordered temples to be cut into solid rock instead.

One of his inscriptions proudly says that he made a temple without bricks, timber, metal or mortar. He was also a writer. He composed a comic play in Sanskrit, the Mattavilasa Prahasana, which pokes fun at drunk monks and squabbling holy men. It is still sometimes performed.

The wrestler king

His son Narasimhavarman I, who ruled from about 630 to 668, was nicknamed Mamalla, "the great wrestler". The port city was named Mamallapuram after him.

He is also remembered for a famous victory. In 642 he defeated the Chalukya king Pulakeshin II and captured his capital, Vatapi, today's Badami in Karnataka. During his reign, the Chinese monk Xuanzang visited Kanchipuram and described it as a large city of learning with many monasteries and temples.

Rocks that became temples

Mahabalipuram's most famous carvings are the Pancha Rathas, five temples carved out of a long granite outcrop. Each has a different shape: one is like a thatched hut, others like tall towers. Beside them stand a life-size stone elephant, a lion and a bull, carved from the same rock.

Granite is one of the hardest rocks to carve. Sculptors used iron chisels and hammers, working from the top down. Some rathas were never finished at the base, which lets visitors see how they worked.

Nearby is a huge carved cliff, about 27 metres long, longer than a cricket pitch. It is known as Arjuna's Penance or the Descent of the Ganga, and experts still debate which story it shows. A natural crack runs down the middle, and it may once have carried water like a river. Around it are more than a hundred figures, including elephants, a cat pretending to meditate and a crowd of mice.

The temple by the waves

The Shore Temple was built around 700 CE under King Narasimhavarman II, also called Rajasimha. Unlike the rathas, it is made of cut granite blocks fitted together. Its slim towers face the Bay of Bengal so that the first sunlight strikes the shrine.

For centuries, sailors used it as a landmark. Salt winds have worn away much of its fine carving, and a sea wall now protects it. After the 2004 tsunami, the retreating sea briefly revealed carved rocks and old walls in the sand, and archaeologists found new carvings along the shore.

What the Pallavas passed on

Later Pallava kings built the Kailasanatha temple in Kanchipuram, with its rows of small shrines. The Cholas, who took over the region in the late 800s, built on these ideas to make even larger temples. The pyramid-shaped towers found across Tamil Nadu today began as experiments in stone on a sandy beach at Mahabalipuram.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Shore Temple was carved from one rock, like the rathas.

Truth: The Shore Temple was built from cut blocks of granite stacked together, which is why it is called a structural temple. It came later, around 700 CE, after the rock-cut experiments nearby.

Try this

Try subtractive sculpture with a bar of soap. Using a blunt butter knife or a spoon handle, carve a simple shape, like an elephant, starting from the top. Notice that once you remove a piece, you can't put it back, just like the Pallava sculptors.
