What is Stonehenge?

About 5,000 years ago, farmers with no metal tools and no wheels dragged stones heavier than a loaded truck across southern England and stood them up in a ring.

The short answer

Stonehenge is a prehistoric monument on Salisbury Plain in southern England. It is a ring of giant standing stones, some with huge flat stones balanced across their tops like lintels over a doorway. It was built in stages, starting about 5,000 years ago, by farming people of the Stone Age.

Nobody left a written explanation, because the builders had no writing. But archaeologists have worked out a great deal from the stones, bones, tools and holes in the ground. Stonehenge was lined up with the Sun at midsummer and midwinter, and it was used as a burial place. It was almost certainly a place for ceremonies, where people gathered at special times of the year.

Surprising but true

Some of Stonehenge's smaller stones were brought from hills in Wales, about 225 km away, and its central Altar Stone came from north-east Scotland, about 750 km away.

Built in stages

At first, around 3000 BCE, Stonehenge was a circular ditch and bank, with a ring of pits. Some held the cremated remains of people, which makes it one of Britain's earliest large cemeteries.

About 2500 BCE, the builders raised the big stones. The large blocks are a hard local rock called sarsen. The tallest stand about 7 m above the ground, and the heaviest weighs more than 30 tonnes, as much as five adult elephants. Smaller "bluestones" were set up among them and rearranged several times over the following centuries.

Where the stones came from

In 2020, scientists compared the chemistry of the sarsens with rocks across southern England. Most matched an area called West Woods, about 25 km north. The bluestones came from much further away, the Preseli Hills of Wales, about 225 km off.

In 2024, researchers found that the flat Altar Stone at the centre came from north-east Scotland, about 750 km away. Nobody yet knows whether it travelled overland or by boat along the coast. Either way, it shows that people across Britain were connected 5,000 years ago.

How did they move them?

No one knows for certain, and that is why the question is still debated. The builders had no metal tools, no wheels and no horses for pulling. The most likely method is that teams of people dragged stones on wooden sledges, possibly along timber rails or greased tracks. Experiments have shown that a few dozen people can pull a large stone this way.

To raise a stone, workers probably dug a pit with one sloping side, slid the stone in, and hauled it upright with ropes, wooden frames and levers. The lintels on top were fixed with joints copied from woodwork, so each stone fitted snugly onto the next.

Lined up with the Sun

Stonehenge is arranged so that on midsummer morning, the Sun rises close to a stone outside the circle, called the Heel Stone. On midwinter evening, it sets in line with the monument from the other side.

Many experts think the midwinter sunset was the most important moment. Pig bones found at a nearby settlement suggest big feasts were held there in winter.

Who built it?

The builders were Neolithic farmers, people who kept cattle and pigs and grew crops. Some archaeologists think the workers lived at a big village called Durrington Walls, about 3 km away.

Today thousands of people still gather at Stonehenge for sunrise at the summer solstice. The monument is cared for by English Heritage, and since 1986 it has been a World Heritage Site.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Stonehenge was built by the Druids.

Truth: Druids were priests in Iron Age Britain, about 2,000 years ago. Stonehenge was built more than 2,000 years before that. The link was made by writers in the 1600s and 1700s, and it stuck.

Try this

On a sunny day, push a stick upright into the ground or into a pot of soil. Mark the tip of its shadow with a pebble every hour. Watch how the shadow moves and shrinks around midday. Stonehenge's builders lined up their stones with the Sun's movements in a similar way.
