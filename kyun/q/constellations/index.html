What are constellations?

The stars in Orion look like neighbours, but some of them are hundreds of light-years farther from us than others.

The short answer

A constellation is a group of stars that people have joined into a picture, and the patch of sky around that picture. Orion the hunter, Scorpius the scorpion and Leo the lion are famous examples.

Long ago, people all over the world grouped the stars into shapes to help them remember the sky, tell stories and keep track of the seasons. Today astronomers use 88 official constellations. They divide up the whole sky the way states divide up a country, so any star or galaxy can be given an address, such as "in the constellation Andromeda".

Surprising but true

The seven bright stars that Americans call the Big Dipper are called the Saptarishi, or Seven Sages, in India. It is not an official constellation at all, just part of a much bigger one.

Patterns, not neighbours

The stars in a constellation look like they belong together, but that is a trick of perspective. They simply lie in roughly the same direction from Earth.

Take Orion. Its bright stars are scattered at very different distances, some hundreds of light-years farther away than others. If you could fly far across the galaxy and look back, the hunter would fall apart into a jumble of unrelated stars.

It's a bit like standing in a stadium. A player on the field and a fan in the top row might line up perfectly from your seat. From any other seat, they don't.

Different skies, different stories

Every culture has made its own pictures. The bright stars Americans call the Big Dipper are the Plough in Britain and the Saptarishi, or Seven Sages, in India. In many Hindu households the faint star Alcor, beside the middle star of the handle, is linked to Arundhati, the wife of the sage Vasishtha.

Many of the official names come from Greek myths, passed on through Arabic and Latin books. In the 2nd century CE, the astronomer Ptolemy, working in Alexandria, listed 48 constellations. Most are still in use.

Who were the Greek gods?The ancient Greeks believed their gods lived on a real mountain, argued like a big family and sometimes turned people into spiders.

Indian astronomers tracked 27 nakshatras, groups of stars along the path the Moon follows through the sky. The Moon spends roughly one day in each. Nakshatras are still used in Indian calendars to choose dates for festivals and family events.

Filling in the southern sky

Ptolemy couldn't see the far southern sky from Alexandria, so his list had a big hole. European sailors heading south in the 1500s noticed unfamiliar stars and began naming them. In the 1750s the French astronomer Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille mapped the southern sky from the Cape of Good Hope. He named his new constellations after modern instruments, like the Telescope, the Microscope and the Air Pump.

By the early 1900s, different star maps used different patterns and borders. In 1922 the International Astronomical Union settled on 88 constellations, and its members later drew straight-edged borders between them. The biggest is Hydra, a long water snake. The smallest is Crux, the Southern Cross, which appears on the flags of Australia and New Zealand.

Slowly changing shapes

Stars are moving through space at great speed, but they are so far away that their positions barely change in a human lifetime. Over tens of thousands of years, though, the patterns slowly drift. The Big Dipper of 50,000 years from now will look noticeably bent out of shape.

So the constellations we know are a snapshot. People in the future will need new stories for new shapes, and if they live in another part of the galaxy, a completely new sky.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The stars in a constellation are close together in space.

Truth: They only look close because they lie in roughly the same direction from Earth. One star in a pattern can be many times farther away than its neighbour.

Try this

Scatter a small handful of dried rice or beads on a sheet of dark paper. Find a pattern you like, join the grains with a chalk or white pencil, and give it a name and a story. Then ask a friend to do the same with the same grains. You will probably see completely different pictures, just as different cultures did.
