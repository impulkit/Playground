Who was Chhatrapati Shivaji?

As a teenager he captured his first hill fort. By the end of his life he had built a kingdom and a navy, and stood up to the Mughal Empire.

The short answer

Shivaji Bhonsle was a Maratha leader who founded an independent kingdom in western India in the 17th century. He was born in 1630 at Shivneri fort, near Pune.

At the time, most of the Deccan was ruled by the Sultanate of Bijapur and the mighty Mughal Empire. Starting as a teenager with a small band of followers, Shivaji captured hill forts in the Sahyadri mountains, also called the Western Ghats. He used fast raids, local knowledge and surprise to beat much bigger armies. In 1674 he was crowned king at Raigad, taking the title Chhatrapati. He built a navy, ran an organised government and is a hero across Maharashtra and beyond.

Surprising but true

In 2025, UNESCO added twelve forts linked to the Maratha kingdom, most of them in Maharashtra, to its World Heritage list.

A boy and his mother

Shivaji's father, Shahaji, was a general who served the Bijapur sultans and spent long years away. Shivaji grew up in Pune with his mother, Jijabai. She told him stories from the Ramayana and Mahabharata and is remembered as the strongest influence on his life.

Around 1645, while still in his teens, Shivaji and his companions captured Torna fort. Soon he held a chain of forts across the hills. The local hillmen, called Mavalas, knew every path and became his soldiers.

Tiger claws and a surprise at night

In 1659, Bijapur sent a powerful general, Afzal Khan, to crush him. The two met for talks below Pratapgad fort. Each side later said the other struck first. Shivaji, who wore hidden armour and a weapon called bagh nakh, steel tiger claws, killed Afzal Khan, and his forces routed the enemy army.

In 1663, the Mughal general Shaista Khan had camped in Pune, in Shivaji's own childhood home. Shivaji and a small band slipped in at night, disguised as a wedding party according to some accounts. Shaista Khan escaped but lost fingers, and the Mughals were badly embarrassed.

The escape from Agra

In 1665, after a Mughal army besieged his forts, Shivaji signed a treaty and handed over many of them. The next year he visited the emperor Aurangzeb's court in Agra, where he felt insulted and was placed under guard.

According to the famous story, he escaped by hiding in large baskets of sweets being sent out of the house. However he did it, he got back to Maharashtra, won back his forts and grew stronger than before.

A king with a navy

In 1674, at Raigad, Shivaji was crowned in a grand ceremony. He set up a council of eight ministers, used Marathi in government and had a dictionary made to replace Persian words with Sanskrit and Marathi ones.

He also saw that power on the Konkan coast needed ships. He built a fleet and sea forts, including Sindhudurg on an island off the coast. Because of this, he is often called the father of the Indian Navy. In 2022 the Indian Navy adopted a new flag with a design inspired by his royal seal.

Forts in the clouds

Shivaji is said to have held or built more than 300 forts, many perched on cliffs that disappear into monsoon clouds for months. They had water cisterns cut into rock, winding gateways to slow attackers and hidden escape routes. Unlike the stone castles of Europe, which often stood on flat ground, these forts used the cliffs themselves as walls.

In 2025, UNESCO listed twelve forts as the Maratha Military Landscapes of India, including Shivneri, Raigad, Pratapgad and Sindhudurg, and Gingee in Tamil Nadu. Shivaji died at Raigad in 1680. The Maratha power he started kept growing and later stretched from Tamil Nadu to Punjab.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Shivaji fought the Mughals because he hated Muslims.

Truth: He fought the Mughal emperor and the sultans of Bijapur for land and power. Muslims served as officers in his army and navy, and even Mughal historians wrote that he ordered his soldiers not to harm mosques or holy books.
