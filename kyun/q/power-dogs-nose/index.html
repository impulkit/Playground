How good is a dog's nose?

A dog's nose has around 300 million smell receptors, compared with about 6 million in yours, so a dog might notice a teaspoon of sugar stirred into two Olympic swimming pools.

The short answer

A dog's nose is one of the best smell detectors on Earth. Many dogs have around 300 million smell receptors, special cells that catch scent molecules, compared with about 6 million in a human nose. The part of a dog's brain that handles smell is also much bigger, compared with its brain size, than ours.

Scientists usually say dogs smell 10,000 to 100,000 times better than people, though it is hard to measure exactly. One expert compared it to noticing a teaspoon of sugar stirred into two Olympic swimming pools. That is why dogs find bombs at airports, track missing people in forests and, in some studies, sniff out illnesses like diabetes or cancer.

Surprising but true

Dogs breathe out through slits at the sides of their nose, so the air going out doesn't push away the smells coming in.

A nose built for sniffing

When you breathe in, air and smells take the same path. A dog's nose is different. Inside, a fold of tissue splits the air in two. Most goes to the lungs. About an eighth goes to a special area at the back of the nose, packed with smell cells, where scent can build up.

When the dog breathes out, the air leaves through slits at the sides of the nose, swirling backwards. This lets the dog sniff in short bursts, up to about five times a second, without blowing away the smell it is following. A dog's wet nose also helps catch scent molecules.

Smelling in stereo

Each nostril can smell separately. Dogs can tell which side a smell is stronger on, and turn towards it. That helps them follow a trail, swinging their heads from side to side.

Dogs can even smell time, in a way. A trail left an hour ago smells stronger than one from yesterday. By sniffing a few footsteps, a dog can work out which direction a person went. Bloodhounds, bred for tracking, have followed trails more than a day old. In the USA, evidence from trained bloodhounds has been allowed in some court cases.

How did wolves become dogs?Every dog on Earth, from a Chihuahua to a Rajapalayam hound, comes from wolves that lived alongside Ice Age hunters.

Dogs with jobs

Dogs work in police forces, armies and airports around the world. India's armed forces and police use breeds such as Labradors, German shepherds and Belgian Malinois to find explosives and drugs. At airports, sniffer dogs can check hundreds of bags in the time a machine scans a few.

After earthquakes and building collapses, rescue dogs search for people trapped under rubble. Other dogs help farmers by sniffing out pests, or help scientists find animal droppings for wildlife studies.

Sniffing out disease

Some dogs have been trained to smell illnesses. Dogs living with people who have diabetes can learn to warn them when their blood sugar drops too low. In studies, trained dogs have picked out samples from people with some cancers and with malaria.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, dogs at airports in Finland and the United Arab Emirates were trained to detect the virus by smell. Scientists are still testing how reliable this is. Dogs get tired and distracted, so researchers are also trying to build electronic noses that copy them.

How your nose compares

Humans are not as bad at smelling as we think. We can tell apart thousands of different smells. In one experiment, people crawling on grass followed a chocolate scent trail by nose alone, a little like a dog, though much more slowly.

But a dog lives in a world of smell that we can barely picture. A walk around the block is like reading a newspaper full of messages left by other dogs. And every animal has its own superpower. For dogs it's smell. For the mantis shrimp, it's eyes that see colours no human ever has. Your own nose has its own story too, which starts with how smell works.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A dry nose means a dog is ill.

Truth: A dog's nose can be wet or dry for many reasons, such as sleeping, the weather or licking. A dry nose alone is not a reliable sign of illness. If a dog seems unwell in other ways, a vet can check it.

Try this

Play a sniffing game with a pet dog, if you have one and an adult says it's okay. Hide a small treat under one of three upturned cups while the dog watches, then shuffle them. Next time, hide it while the dog can't see. Time how quickly it finds the treat using only its nose.
