Can computers read our thoughts?

In 2023, a woman who had not spoken for 18 years after a stroke "talked" through a computer avatar at nearly 80 words a minute, using sensors on her brain.

The short answer

Not the way films show it. No machine can read your secret thoughts or dreams. But computers can now pick up certain brain signals and turn them into actions. People who cannot move have used brain implants to type, move a robotic arm, play video games and even speak through a computer voice.

These systems are called brain-computer interfaces. They work by listening to the tiny electrical pulses that brain cells use to talk to each other. A computer learns which patterns mean "move left" or "say hello", then carries them out.

Surprising but true

One of the first things an early brain-implant user did with his new mind-controlled cursor, in 2004, was play the video game Pong.

Your brain is electric

Your brain has around 86 billion neurons, cells that send messages using small electrical pulses. When you decide to move your hand, a pattern of pulses fires in an area near the top of your head. Those signals travel down your nerves to your muscles.

In 1924, German doctor Hans Berger placed electrodes on a person's head and recorded faint brain waves for the first time. This method, called EEG, is still used in hospitals today. It is a bit like listening to a cricket stadium from outside. You can hear the crowd roar, but not individual conversations.

Sensors inside the brain

To hear clearer signals, some devices are placed directly on or in the brain during surgery. In 1998, an American man who could not move used an implant to steer a computer cursor. In 2004, another man paralysed from the neck down used a tiny implant to move a computer cursor just by thinking. Soon he was playing the old video game Pong with it. Since then, people with implants have fed themselves with robot arms and sent text messages.

In 2023, two teams in the USA reported implants that helped people who had lost their voices to speak again. Sensors picked up signals for planned mouth movements, and a computer turned them into words at about 60 to 80 words a minute. Ordinary talking runs at about 150. One of the women had not spoken for 18 years after a stroke. Her words came out through a talking face on a screen. In January 2024, the company Neuralink put its first implant into a patient, who later played chess with his mind.

Can it guess what you are thinking?

In 2023, scientists at the University of Texas used a large brain scanner called fMRI and an AI system to guess what stories people were listening to. It got the gist, but not the exact words. It needed each person to lie in the scanner for about 16 hours to train it. It failed when people deliberately thought about something else.

That is the key point. Today's devices need training with a willing person, and they only decode certain kinds of signals. Thinking is spread across the whole brain in ways scientists still do not fully understand.

Who owns your brain data?

Even so, some people worry. Simple EEG headbands are already sold for gaming, meditation and focus. Companies could collect brain data the way apps collect your location. In 2021, Chile became the first country to protect "neural rights" in its constitution. In 2024, the US state of Colorado passed a law treating brain data as private, like fingerprints.

Indian scientists are working on brain-computer research too, at places like IIT Kanpur and the National Brain Research Centre near Delhi. Most of this work aims to help people with strokes or spinal injuries.

The big question is how to share the benefits while keeping minds private, a debate about privacy that is only beginning. Some engineers are already joining brain signals to robotic limbs that can send a sense of touch back to the user. And the AI systems that decode the signals are built from layers of maths loosely inspired by brain cells, called neural networks.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Brain implants can already read your secret thoughts.

Truth: Today's devices pick up signals from a small patch of the brain, usually those linked to planned movement or speech. They need long training with a willing person. They cannot pull out hidden memories or private thoughts.

Try this

Test your own brain-to-hand speed. Ask a friend to hold a 30 cm ruler just above your open fingers, then drop it without warning. See how many centimetres fall before you catch it. Those few hundredths of a second are your brain sending electrical signals down your nerves.
