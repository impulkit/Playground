How do people learn best?

Reading your notes again and again feels like studying, but testing yourself on them works far better.

The short answer

When you learn something, cells in your brain called neurons strengthen their connections with each other. Each time you use a fact or a skill, that pathway gets a little easier to travel. Learning, in a sense, is building roads in your head.

Scientists have tested many study methods. A few come out on top again and again: spreading practice over several days, testing yourself instead of rereading, mixing up different kinds of problems, and getting enough sleep. None of these needs expensive tools. They do take a little planning, and they often feel harder than the methods that don't work as well.

Surprising but true

In the 1880s, a German scientist named Hermann Ebbinghaus memorised lists made from more than 2,000 nonsense syllables, like "DAX" and "BOK", just to measure how fast he forgot them.

The curve of forgetting

In the 1880s, Hermann Ebbinghaus wanted to measure memory without the help of meaning. So he learned long lists of made-up syllables, then tested himself after minutes, hours and days. He found that forgetting is fast at first. Much of a new list was gone within a day, and then the loss slowed down.

He also found the cure. Each time he relearned a list, it took less effort, and it stayed longer afterwards. Spacing out review sessions flattened the curve. Teachers and app designers still use this idea, called spaced repetition, to plan practice for times tables, spelling and vocabulary.

Testing makes memories stick

Most students study by reading notes again. It feels comfortable because the words look familiar. But recognising something is not the same as remembering it.

Psychologists have run many experiments where one group rereads a passage and another group reads it once and then tries to write down everything they remember. A week later, the second group usually remembers much more. Pulling a fact out of your memory seems to strengthen it, a bit like a path that gets clearer the more you walk it. Flashcards, practice questions and explaining an idea to a friend all count.

Teaching works especially well. To explain photosynthesis to a younger cousin, you have to organise the idea, find simple words and notice the parts you don't really understand. Many teachers say they only really learned a subject once they had to teach it.

Why is it easier to learn languages when you're young?At six months old, a baby can hear the difference between speech sounds from almost any language. By their first birthday, much of that skill has faded.

Mix it up

When practising maths, it is tempting to do twenty of the same kind of problem in a row. Studies suggest it is better to mix different kinds together. This is called interleaving. It feels slower, because you must work out which method each problem needs. That extra thinking is exactly what helps on a real test, where questions come in any order.

The same idea works for sports and music. Many cricket coaches mix fast, slow and spinning balls in net practice, so batters learn to read the bowler instead of guessing what comes next.

Sleep is part of studying

While you sleep, your brain replays some of the day's experiences. This helps move new memories into longer-term storage. Students who stay up all night cramming often remember less than those who study and then get a full night of sleep.

Naps can help too, especially for young children. And exercise, by pumping more blood to the brain, seems to help people feel more alert and ready to learn.

The struggle is the point

Many good study methods feel uncomfortable. Testing yourself shows you what you don't know, which is unpleasant. Mixing problems feels messy. Psychologists call these "desirable difficulties". The effort is a sign that your brain is building something.

That is also why learning a new language or an instrument has slow patches. Getting stuck, making mistakes and trying again isn't a sign that you're bad at it. It's the way the brain's roads get built.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Everyone has a learning style, visual, auditory or hands-on, and learns best when lessons match it.

Truth: Many people have preferences, but careful studies have found no good evidence that matching lessons to a "style" helps people learn more. Most people learn best when ideas come in several forms, such as words, pictures and practice together.

Try this

Choose ten new words or facts. On day one, study them for five minutes. On days two, four and seven, cover the answers and try to recall them before checking. Make a note of how many you get right each time and watch the number climb.
