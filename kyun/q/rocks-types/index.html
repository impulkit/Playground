What are the three kinds of rock?

The marble of the Taj Mahal was once the muddy floor of an ancient sea.

The short answer

Geologists sort rocks into three big families by how they were made. Igneous rocks form when hot, molten rock cools and hardens. Sedimentary rocks form when sand, mud, shells and other bits settle in layers and slowly turn to stone. Metamorphic rocks are older rocks that have been changed by heat and pressure deep underground, without melting.

Rocks don't stay in one family forever. Over millions of years, any rock can be worn down, buried, squeezed or melted and turned into another kind. Geologists call this slow recycling the rock cycle.

Surprising but true

The Lalbagh Rock in the middle of Bengaluru is about 3,000 million years old, older than the first plants and animals on land by billions of years.

Born of fire

Igneous comes from the Latin word for fire. Deep underground, rock can be hot enough to melt into magma. When magma cools slowly, far below the surface, its crystals have time to grow big. That gives granite, the speckled stone used for kitchen counters and many old buildings.

When magma bursts out of a volcano as lava, it cools fast and the crystals stay tiny. That gives dark, fine-grained basalt. Much of the Deccan Plateau in western India is made of basalt, laid down in giant eruptions about 66 million years ago. Some lava is so full of gas bubbles that it hardens into pumice, a rock light enough to float on water.

Built in layers

Rain, wind, ice and rivers slowly break rocks into grains. Rivers carry the grains away and drop them as sediment in lakes and seas. Layer after layer piles up, and the weight squeezes the lower layers. Minerals in the water glue the grains together.

Sand becomes sandstone. Mud becomes shale. Shells and the skeletons of tiny sea creatures become limestone. The red sandstone of Delhi's Red Fort and the walls of the Grand Canyon are sedimentary rocks.

Because sediment settles gently, it can bury animals and plants whole. That is why nearly all fossils are found in sedimentary rock. Only a thin skin of Earth is made of these rocks, yet they cover about three quarters of the land surface.

Changed under pressure

Metamorphic means "changed in form". When rocks are buried deep, or caught where continents collide, heat and pressure change them. Their minerals rearrange into new crystals, while the rock stays solid, a bit like clay hardening in a kiln without melting.

Limestone becomes marble. The marble for the Taj Mahal came from Makrana in Rajasthan, and it began as sea-floor limestone. Shale becomes slate, which splits into flat sheets once used for school writing slates. Granite can become gneiss, a rock with wavy stripes.

How was the Taj Mahal built so perfectly?It took about 20 years, around 20,000 workers and white marble carried from Rajasthan.

Very old rocks

Much of southern India sits on some of the oldest rock on Earth. The Lalbagh Rock in Bengaluru is a mound of gneiss about 3,000 million years old. The Geological Survey of India has declared it a National Geological Monument.

The oldest known rocks, in northern Canada, are about 4 billion years old. Older rocks have been recycled away. Earth itself is about 4.5 billion years old, so the rock cycle has been turning for a very long time.

The cycle keeps turning

A grain of sand on a beach in Goa might once have been part of a granite mountain. In millions of years, it could be buried and turned into sandstone, squeezed into a metamorphic rock called quartzite, or carried down into Earth to melt.

Mountains rise as continents push together, and rain and rivers wear them down again. The Himalaya is still rising, while its rocks are already crumbling into the sand that will become new rock. Some of that crumbled rock ends up in fields as soil.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You can find fossils in any kind of rock.

Truth: Almost all fossils are found in sedimentary rocks, which form gently in layers of mud and sand. The heat of molten rock destroys remains, and the heat and squeezing that make metamorphic rock usually wipe fossils out.

Try this

Make a rock cycle with crayons. Snap a few old crayons into tiny crumbs with your fingers and press the crumbs into a loose layer, like sediment. Wrap the layer in foil, press hard under a heavy book, then hold it in your warm hands for a few minutes. The crumbs stick into a solid, stripy lump, a little like a metamorphic rock.
