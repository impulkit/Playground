What was the Persian Empire?

A line written about Persian royal messengers 2,400 years ago is carved over the main post office in New York City.

The short answer

The Persian Empire, also called the Achaemenid Empire, was founded by Cyrus the Great around 550 BCE in what is now Iran. Within a few decades it became the largest empire the world had yet seen. At its height under Darius I, it stretched from Egypt and the edge of Greece in the west to the Indus river in the east.

The Persians ruled dozens of different peoples by dividing the empire into provinces called satrapies, each under a governor. They built a long royal road and a fast postal service, and often let local people keep their own gods and customs. The empire lasted until Alexander the Great conquered it in 330 BCE.

Surprising but true

The Greek historian Herodotus said the Indian province of the Persian Empire paid the largest tribute of all, in gold dust.

Cyrus the founder

Cyrus began as the ruler of a small kingdom in southern Iran. He defeated the Medes, then the rich kingdom of Lydia in Turkey, and in 539 BCE he took Babylon, the great city of Mesopotamia.

A clay cylinder found in Babylon in 1879, now in the British Museum, records his conquest. It says he restored temples and let peoples who had been taken from their homes return. The Hebrew Bible praises Cyrus for allowing the Jews exiled in Babylon to go back to Jerusalem. A copy of the Cyrus Cylinder is displayed at the United Nations headquarters in New York.

Darius and the Royal Road

Darius I, who ruled from 522 to 486 BCE, organised the empire. He set taxes for each province, introduced gold coins and built Persepolis, a palace city with stone staircases carved with people from across the empire bringing gifts.

He also improved the Royal Road, which ran about 2,500 kilometres from Sardis, in Turkey, to Susa, in Iran. Stations along it kept fresh horses and riders. The Greek writer Herodotus admired these messengers, saying that neither snow, nor rain, nor heat, nor gloom of night stopped them. Those words, slightly adapted, are carved on the James A. Farley Post Office building in New York.

Why were Roman roads so straight?Some Roman roads run dead straight for kilometres, over hills and through forests, and cars still drive along a few of them 2,000 years later.

Persia and India

The empire reached into the Indus region, in today's Pakistan. Herodotus said this Indian province paid more tribute than any other, in gold dust. Indian soldiers served in Persian armies, and carvings at Persepolis show Indians bringing gifts.

Persian rule left traces in India. The Kharosthi script, used in the northwest for centuries, is thought to have grown out of Aramaic, the language of Persian government offices. Some historians also see Persian influence in the polished stone pillars of the Mauryan emperor Ashoka, built about two centuries later.

Water under the desert

Much of Iran is dry. Persians became experts at building qanats, underground tunnels that carry water from mountain springs to villages and fields far away, without losing it to the sun. Some qanats run for tens of kilometres, and many are still in use. UNESCO lists a group of Persian qanats as a World Heritage Site.

The Persians were also known for the religion of Zoroastrianism, which spoke of a struggle between truth and falsehood. Centuries later, Zoroastrians who left Iran settled in Gujarat. Their descendants are India's Parsi community.

Wars with Greece and the end

Darius and his son Xerxes tried to conquer Greece. The Greeks beat them at Marathon in 490 BCE, and again at Salamis and Plataea in 480 and 479 BCE. These wars made heroes of the Greek city-states in their own histories.

In 334 BCE, Alexander of Macedon invaded. He defeated the last Persian king, Darius III, and in 330 BCE Persepolis was burned. Its ruined columns still stand on a terrace in southern Iran, a World Heritage Site where visitors can see the carved gift-bearers climbing the stairs.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The ancient Persians were simply the villains of Greek history.

Truth: Most of the stories come from their Greek enemies. Persian records show a well-organised empire that built roads, ran a postal system, and often let conquered peoples keep their own customs and religions.
