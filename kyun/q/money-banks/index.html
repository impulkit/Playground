What does a bank do with your money?

When you put money in a bank, it does not sit in a box with your name on it. Most of it is lent to someone else almost straight away.

The short answer

A bank keeps your money safe and lets you take it out when you need it. But it does not just store it. A bank takes money deposited by many people and lends most of it to others: families buying houses, farmers buying seeds, businesses building factories.

Borrowers pay the bank interest, an extra charge for using the money. The bank pays a smaller amount of interest to people who deposited, and keeps the difference to cover its costs and make a profit. So when your savings grow in a bank account, it is partly because someone else is paying to borrow money.

Surprising but true

The word bank comes from the Italian banco, meaning bench, because early money-dealers in Italian cities did business at benches in the market.

Lending out most of it

On any normal day, only a few customers want their cash back. So a bank keeps a slice of its deposits as cash and reserves, and lends out the rest. In India, the Reserve Bank of India sets rules for how much banks must keep aside. This is called fractional reserve banking, because only a fraction is held back.

It works as long as people trust the bank. If everyone rushes to withdraw at once, a bank can run short of cash. That is called a bank run, and it has happened many times in history.

Keeping it safe

After many American banks failed during the Great Depression of the 1930s, the US government created the FDIC in 1933 to insure deposits. Today, if an insured US bank fails, customers get back up to 250,000 dollars. In India, the DICGC insures deposits up to ₹5 lakh per person in each bank.

Benches, temples and families

People have stored and lent valuables for thousands of years. In ancient Mesopotamia, temples and palaces stored grain and silver and made loans. In India, moneylenders called sahukars and banking families lent money to traders and kings for centuries.

Modern banking grew in Italian cities like Florence and Venice in the 1300s and 1400s. Moneychangers sat at benches, or banchi, in the market. The Medici family of Florence became rich bankers and spent huge sums on art and buildings during the Renaissance.

India's banks

The Reserve Bank of India began on 1 April 1935. It prints rupee notes, sets interest rates and watches over other banks. In 1969, the government nationalised 14 large banks, taking them into public ownership, partly to open more branches in villages.

For a long time, many Indians had no bank account at all. In 2014, the government launched the Jan Dhan Yojana to open free basic accounts, and more than 50 crore have been opened since. Today, many Indians move money in seconds using UPI, which links bank accounts to mobile phones.

Why borrowing matters

Banks also do many smaller jobs. They change foreign currency for travellers, keep valuables in lockers, send money abroad, and give out debit and credit cards. Many now offer most of these services through phone apps, so customers rarely need to visit a branch.

Without banks, a young couple might have to save for 30 years before buying a home, and a small business could not buy a new machine until it had saved every rupee. Loans let people do things now and pay later. That also carries risk. Borrowers who take on too much debt can struggle to pay it back, and the interest keeps adding up. Banks check whether a borrower can repay before lending. Understanding how interest works, which you can explore in money maths, is one of the most useful things anyone can learn.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The bank keeps your money locked in a vault with your name on it.

Truth: Your money is mixed with everyone else's and most of it is lent out. What the bank keeps is a record that it owes you that amount. Deposit insurance and strict rules make sure you can still get it back.

Try this

Set up a "family bank" with a jar and a notebook. When you deposit money, write it down. Agree with an adult on a small "interest" reward each month, like one extra rupee for every ten saved. Watch how your balance grows faster the longer you leave it.
