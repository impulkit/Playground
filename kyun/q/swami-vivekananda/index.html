Who was Swami Vivekananda?

In 1893 a young Indian monk walked onto a stage in Chicago, began with "Sisters and brothers of America", and the crowd of thousands rose to applaud.

The short answer

Swami Vivekananda was an Indian monk and teacher who lived from 1863 to 1902. He was a follower of the saint Sri Ramakrishna and helped bring the ideas of Vedanta, a school of Hindu philosophy, and of yoga to the West.

He became famous after a speech in Chicago in 1893, at a gathering of religious leaders from around the world. Back in India he founded the Ramakrishna Mission, which still runs schools, hospitals and relief work.

He urged Indians, especially young people, to be strong, confident and of service to the poor. His birthday, 12 January, is National Youth Day in India.

Surprising but true

Vivekananda died at just 39, yet his birthday, 12 January, is celebrated every year in India as National Youth Day.

A curious young man

Vivekananda was born Narendranath Datta in Calcutta, now Kolkata, in 1863. Friends called him Naren. He was clever, sporty and loved music, and he asked hard questions.

Bengal was full of new ideas at the time, a period called the Bengal Renaissance. Naren read Western philosophy at college. But one question bothered him: had anyone actually seen God?

He put it to many teachers. In 1881 he met Sri Ramakrishna, a priest at the Dakshineswar temple near Calcutta. Ramakrishna is said to have answered simply that yes, he had. Naren was puzzled, then drawn in, and became his student.

Wandering India

After Ramakrishna died in 1886, Naren and other young followers became monks. He later took the name Vivekananda.

For several years he travelled across India, often on foot, carrying little more than a staff and a bowl. He stayed with rajas and with the poor. The hunger and hardship he saw troubled him deeply.

In December 1892 he reached Kanyakumari, at India's southern tip. According to tradition he swam out to a rock in the sea and meditated there for three days. There he decided to go to the West, both to share India's ideas and to seek help for its people. The Vivekananda Rock Memorial now stands on that rock.

Chicago, 1893

In 1893 Chicago hosted a World's Fair, and alongside it a World's Parliament of Religions. Vivekananda arrived with little money and no official invitation, and friends he made in the USA helped him get a place.

On 11 September he stood up to speak. His first words, "Sisters and brothers of America", brought thousands of people to their feet in applause that went on for minutes. His speech was short. He spoke about tolerance, saying that different religions are like rivers with different sources that all reach the same sea.

American newspapers wrote about him. He spent the next few years lecturing in the USA and Britain and set up the Vedanta Society in New York in 1894.

Service as worship

Vivekananda came home in 1897 as a hero. That year he founded the Ramakrishna Mission. Its idea was that serving people, especially the poor and sick, is a way of serving God.

The Mission's headquarters is at Belur Math, on the Hooghly near Kolkata. Today it runs schools, colleges, hospitals and help for people hit by floods, cyclones and other disasters, in India and abroad.

He died at Belur Math on 4 July 1902, aged only 39.

His influence

Vivekananda's words gave confidence to many Indians under British rule. Leaders of the freedom struggle, including Subhas Chandra Bose, said his writings inspired them. Since 1985 India has marked his birthday as National Youth Day.

Not everyone agrees with all his views. Some historians point out that he spoke about Hinduism in ways that suited his time and audience, and people still debate how he should be remembered. But his call to young people, "Arise, awake, and stop not till the goal is reached", is still painted on school walls across India.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Vivekananda's Chicago speech was long and went on for hours.

Truth: His opening speech on 11 September 1893 was short, only a few minutes. He gave several more talks at the Parliament over the following days, but it was the brief welcome that people remember.
