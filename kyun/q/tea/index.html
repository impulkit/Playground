How did tea become India's favourite drink?

Most Indians drink chai every day, but a hundred years ago many had never tasted it, and advertisers worked hard to change that.

The short answer

Tea is now so Indian that it is hard to picture a railway station or a street corner without a chaiwala. Yet tea drinking became common across India only in the 20th century.

The story begins with the British. In the 1800s, Britain bought most of its tea from China, and the East India Company wanted a supply of its own. It set up tea gardens in Assam and later in Darjeeling and the Nilgiris, at first only to sell tea to Britain. Later, when sales abroad slowed, the tea industry ran big campaigns to get Indians drinking it. Indians took the drink and made it their own, boiled with milk, sugar and spices.

Surprising but true

Green tea, black tea and white tea all come from the leaves of the same plant. The difference is what happens after picking.

Wild tea in Assam

People in China have drunk tea for well over 2,000 years. The British long believed tea grew only there. But in the 1820s, a Scottish trader named Robert Bruce learned that the Singpho people of Assam drank a brew made from a local wild tea plant. An Assamese nobleman, Maniram Dewan, helped guide him to it.

The Assam plant was a larger-leaved kind of the same species. In 1839, the first Assam tea was auctioned in London. Soon forests in the Brahmaputra valley were being cleared for tea gardens. Northeast India still grows more than half of India's tea.

The hard side of the tea garden

Tea needed many workers to pick leaves by hand. In the 1800s, hundreds of thousands of people were brought to Assam from places such as Jharkhand and Odisha on harsh contracts. Pay was low, living conditions were poor, and many people fell ill.

Their descendants still make up much of the workforce in Assam's tea gardens. Many historians now tell the tea story with them at its centre.

Selling tea to India

In the early 1900s, most Indians did not drink tea. Then the tea industry, run mostly by British planters, began promoting it, especially from the 1930s. Its stalls handed out free cups at railway stations, fairs and factories, and showed people how to brew it.

The promoters wanted a light tea with a dash of milk. Indians had other ideas. They boiled the leaves with plenty of milk and sugar and often ginger, cardamom or cloves. That strong, sweet masala chai became the national drink. Tea stalls on railway platforms helped carry it to every corner of the country.

Tea in America

Tea has a famous place in American history. In 1773, colonists in Boston who were angry about a British tax tipped 342 chests of East India Company tea into the harbour. The Boston Tea Party was one of the events that led to the American Revolution.

Why did America fight for independence?The fight that created the United States began with a tax on tea, and that tea was shipped by the same company that ruled much of India.

Many Americans switched to coffee afterwards, and coffee is still more popular there. But Americans drink a lot of iced tea, especially in the southern states. And "chai latte" now appears on café menus across the country, though most Indians would hardly recognise it.

Leaves, dust and a clay cup

Most tea grown in India is turned into small, hard grains by a method called CTC, short for crush, tear, curl. It brews quickly and strongly, which is perfect for chai. Darjeeling tea, grown high in the hills, is usually made as whole leaves and is prized around the world for its delicate taste.

In many towns, chai is still poured into a kulhad, a little unglazed clay cup that gives the tea an earthy smell. After one use, it is thrown away and crumbles back into soil.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Green tea and black tea come from different plants.

Truth: Both come from Camellia sinensis. For black tea, the leaves are crushed and left to darken in the air before drying. For green tea, they are heated soon after picking, which stops the change and keeps them green.

Try this

Put a tea bag in a glass of cold water and another in a glass of warm tap water. Watch the colour spread for five minutes and compare. Warm water makes the colour and flavour leave the leaves much faster.
