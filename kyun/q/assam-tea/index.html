How did Assam become famous for tea?

The British spent years trying to grow Chinese tea in India, while a wild tea plant was already growing in the forests of Assam.

The short answer

Assam became famous for tea because a strong, native kind of tea plant grows there naturally, and the British turned that discovery into one of the world's biggest tea industries. The plant loves Assam's climate: hot, humid summers, very heavy monsoon rain and rich soil along the Brahmaputra river.

Today Assam grows about half of all India's tea. Its tea is dark and malty, strong enough to stand up to milk and sugar. That's why it goes into so many breakfast teas in Britain and the United States, and into countless cups of chai at Indian railway stations and roadside stalls.

Surprising but true

Assam grows about half of all the tea produced in India, more than any other state.

A plant hiding in plain sight

In the early 1800s, Britain drank huge amounts of tea, nearly all of it from China. China guarded its tea secrets closely. Paying for it in silver was costly, and that trade became part of the story of the Opium Wars.

In 1823, a Scottish trader named Robert Bruce was told about wild tea growing in Assam by a Singpho chief called Bessa Gam. The Singpho people had been brewing its leaves for generations. After Robert died, his brother Charles Bruce carried on the work, collecting plants and seeds and setting up the first experimental gardens.

British botanists at first doubted that this was real tea. Eventually they agreed it was a variety of the same plant, now called Camellia sinensis var. assamica. Its leaves are bigger than those of Chinese tea, and it grows into a small tree if left unpruned.

How did a trading company end up ruling India?A group of London merchants set out to buy pepper. Within 200 years, their company ran its own army and governed most of India.

From jungle to tea garden

In 1839, the first Assam tea was auctioned in London, and the Assam Company was formed to grow it. Forests were cleared, and neat rows of bushes spread across the valley. Gardens were laid out on gentle slopes and plains, with tall shade trees dotted between the bushes.

An Assamese nobleman, Maniram Dewan, worked with the early British companies and later set up gardens of his own. He is remembered as one of the first Indian tea planters. In 1858, the British executed him for supporting the revolt of 1857.

The workers behind every cup

Tea needs many hands. Leaves are plucked by hand, usually just "two leaves and a bud" from each shoot, every week or so. From the 1800s, British companies brought hundreds of thousands of workers, many of them Adivasi people from central and eastern India, to work in the gardens.

Conditions were often harsh: low pay, crowded housing and contracts that made it hard to leave. Their descendants still make up much of the workforce on Assam's tea estates. Pay and living conditions remain a big issue today. Their music and dances, such as the jhumur, have become part of Assam's culture too.

How Assam tea is made

After plucking, leaves are withered, rolled or crushed, left to darken in the air, and then dried. Most Assam tea is made by a method called CTC (crush, tear, curl), which turns leaves into tiny dark granules. These brew fast and strong, perfect for chai boiled with milk.

Some gardens make finer, whole-leaf teas with golden tips, which sell for high prices. Tea gardens border wildlife areas like Kaziranga, where one-horned rhinos and elephants roam. Sometimes elephants wander through the tea rows at night, and workers arrive in the morning to find their footprints between the bushes.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Tea was always grown in India, and Indians have always drunk it.

Truth: People in parts of the northeast, such as the Singpho, did drink tea from wild plants. But large tea gardens only began in the 1830s, and tea didn't become a common drink across India until the 20th century, helped by advertising campaigns.
