What will schools be like in the future?

In April 2020, schools closed for about 1.6 billion children around the world, and a giant accidental experiment in learning from home began.

The short answer

Nobody can say for sure, but some changes are already starting. Many students may get an AI tutor that explains things step by step, at their own speed. Virtual reality could take a class inside a beating heart or onto the surface of Mars. Lessons may mix time in a classroom with time online.

Still, most experts think the school building and the teacher will remain at the centre. During the pandemic, children everywhere learned from home, and many of them missed their friends and teachers more than they expected. School is also where kids learn to work together, argue, share and make friends.

Surprising but true

The idea of grouping children by age into "grades" is only about 200 years old. For most of history, children learned from family, tutors or in mixed-age groups.

The school you know is young

For most of history, there was no school as we know it. Children learned farming, crafts or trades from their families. Rich families hired tutors. In ancient India, students lived with a guru, and some travelled to great centres like Nalanda, which had thousands of students and teachers.

Classes by age, with bells, desks in rows and a set timetable, spread from Prussia, part of today's Germany, in the 1800s. Countries copied the model to teach millions of children to read, write and do sums. Many of today's schools still look a lot like that.

The accidental experiment

In April 2020, COVID-19 closed schools for about 1.6 billion learners. Overnight, kitchens became classrooms. Children joined lessons on phones and laptops, if their families had them.

Many didn't. In India, lessons went out on TV channels and radio, and teachers in some villages taught small groups in the open air or over phone calls. The closures showed that technology can help, but also that it can leave behind children without internet or devices.

Tutors made of code

In 2023, AI tutors began to appear in some classrooms. They are chatbots trained not to give answers straight away but to ask questions and offer hints, as a good tutor would. They can explain the same idea five different ways without losing patience.

They also make mistakes. An AI can state something wrong with total confidence. So experts say students need to learn how to check what AI tells them, and teachers need to guide how it's used. Some countries have tried AI textbooks and then pulled back after complaints from teachers and parents.

What is artificial intelligence?In 1997, a computer beat the world chess champion. Today AI can chat, draw and translate, yet it can still make mistakes a child wouldn't.

Field trips without the bus

With virtual reality headsets, a class in Lucknow could walk through ancient Rome or float inside the International Space Station. Some schools already use VR for science and history. A goggle-eyed field trip costs less than a real one, though headsets are still expensive and can give some people headaches.

Some scientists also think timetables may change. Research on sleep shows that teenagers' body clocks run later than adults', and some US school districts have pushed start times later so students can sleep more.

Augmented reality, which adds digital images on top of the real world through a screen, can make a textbook diagram of a volcano pop up and erupt on your desk.

India's plan

India's National Education Policy of 2020 aims to change what happens in class. It wants less learning by heart and more projects, coding from the middle school years, and teaching in a child's home language in the early grades. A government platform called DIKSHA, launched in 2017, puts textbooks and lessons online in many Indian languages for free.

The hardest part of the future may not be the gadgets. It may be making sure every child, in a city flat or a remote village, gets a good teacher and a fair chance. As machines take over more tasks, the jobs waiting for today's students may need skills no textbook has yet been written about.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In the future, robots will replace teachers.

Truth: Most experts expect AI to help teachers, for example by marking practice work or giving extra help, rather than replace them. Children learn a lot from people: being encouraged, asking questions and working with friends.
