How does compost turn waste into soil?

The middle of a well-made compost heap can get hotter than 60 °C, hot enough to steam on a cold morning, and the heat comes from billions of tiny living things.

The short answer

Compost is what you get when plant and food waste rots in a controlled way. Bacteria and fungi, too small to see, feed on peels, leaves and scraps. As they eat, they break them down into a dark, crumbly material that smells like a forest floor. Worms, beetles and other small creatures help by chewing and mixing.

The finished compost is full of humus, the rich part of soil that holds water and feeds plants. Gardeners and farmers mix it into the ground to grow healthier crops. Making compost also keeps waste out of rubbish dumps, where rotting food gives off a gas that warms the planet.

Surprising but true

One of the most famous composting methods was worked out in India. The British scientist Albert Howard developed it in Indore in the 1920s, and it is still called the Indore method.

Greens and browns

A compost heap needs two kinds of ingredients. "Greens" are wet, fresh things like vegetable peels, fruit skins, tea leaves and grass clippings. They are rich in nitrogen, which the microbes use to build their bodies. "Browns" are dry things like fallen leaves, straw, twigs and shredded paper. They are rich in carbon, which the microbes burn for energy.

Many gardeners use about two or three buckets of browns for every bucket of greens. Too many greens and the heap turns slimy and smelly. Too many browns and it hardly rots at all. Meat, dairy and oily food are usually kept out of home heaps, because they attract rats and flies.

The heat inside

When a big heap is built well, the microbes multiply so fast that they make heat, the same way your body warms up when you exercise. The centre can reach 55 to 65 °C within a few days. On a cold morning, you might see steam rising when you open it.

That heat is useful. It kills most weed seeds and harmful germs. Every week or two, gardeners turn the heap with a fork to bring air into the middle and mix the cooler outside into the hot centre. The heap should feel damp, like a sponge that has been squeezed out.

Worms do it too

Not all compost is hot. In vermicomposting, special worms called red wigglers do most of the work. They live in a box or bin, eat kitchen scraps and leave behind dark, rich droppings called castings.

Vermicomposting is popular in India, on farms and in city flats, because it works in a small space and doesn't smell when done right. Common garden earthworms do a similar job outdoors, pulling dead leaves underground and mixing them into the soil.

An Indian recipe

In the 1920s, a British plant scientist named Albert Howard was working at an agricultural institute in Indore, in central India. He watched how local farmers used animal dung and crop waste, and worked out a careful system of layering, watering and turning heaps. He called it the Indore method.

Howard wrote books about it, and his ideas spread to farms and gardens across Europe and America. Many gardeners who build compost heaps today are following steps first tested in Indian fields about a hundred years ago.

Why it matters

Food and garden waste make up a big share of what households throw away. In a landfill, buried under other rubbish without air, it rots slowly and gives off methane, a gas that traps far more heat than carbon dioxide. In a compost heap with plenty of air, the same waste turns into something useful instead.

Some cities collect wet waste separately and compost it in big plants. Others encourage families to compost at home. Leftovers can also be turned into cooking gas in a biogas plant. Either way, a banana peel that goes back into the soil helps grow the next banana, which is a neat way to cut down on food waste.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Compost always smells bad.

Truth: A healthy compost heap smells earthy, like soil after rain. A bad smell is a sign that something is off, usually too much wet food waste and too little air or dry material.

Try this

Make a mini compost jar. In a large clear plastic bottle with the top cut off, layer some soil, vegetable peels and dry leaves, and add a little water. Poke small air holes, cover the top with cloth and keep it in the shade. Mark the level and check it each week as the pile shrinks and darkens.
