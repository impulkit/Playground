Who is Banksy?

In 2018 a Banksy painting sold for over a million pounds, then shredded itself in front of the buyers.

The short answer

Banksy is an artist who paints on walls, mostly in Britain, and nobody outside a small circle knows who Banksy really is. The pictures are usually made with a stencil: a cut-out sheet held against a wall and sprayed with paint, so an image appears in seconds.

The art is often funny and sharp at the same time. A rat holds a sign. A girl reaches for a red heart-shaped balloon. Police officers have a pillow fight. Many pictures poke fun at war, money, or people in power. Banksy's work has also appeared far from Britain, on walls in New York, Paris and the West Bank, and on bomb-damaged buildings in Ukraine in 2022. Banksy began in the 1990s, probably in Bristol in the west of England, and by the 2000s was one of the best-known artists alive.

Surprising but true

The half-shredded painting sold again in 2021 for about £18.6 million, roughly 18 times its first price.

Why the mask?

Painting on a wall without permission is against the law in most places, so a street artist has good reason to stay hidden. Over time the secret also became part of the art. People talk about who Banksy might be almost as much as they talk about the pictures.

Some reporters have named people they believe are Banksy. None has been confirmed. Banksy speaks through a website and an office called Pest Control, which decides which works are real. That keeps the person and the art apart, a bit like a writer using a pen name. For an artist whose work questions power, privacy is also a kind of protection.

What is free speech?In 1965, a 13-year-old girl in Iowa wore a black armband to school to protest a war. Her case reached the US Supreme Court, and she won.

The painting that shredded itself

In October 2018, a framed copy of Girl with Balloon was sold at an auction house in London for just over £1 million. As the hammer came down, an alarm beeped and the canvas began sliding out of the bottom of its frame, cut into strips by a hidden shredder.

The room gasped. Banksy posted a video showing the shredder being built into the frame years before. It jammed halfway, leaving the girl's lower half hanging in ribbons. The buyer decided to keep it. Three years later the same half-shredded work sold again for about £18.6 million. A stunt meant to mock the art market had made the art worth far more.

Walls that suddenly become valuable

When a new Banksy appears, strange things happen. Owners have put clear plastic over walls to protect the paint. Some have cut whole sections of brick out and sold them. Councils that usually scrub away graffiti have argued about whether to keep it.

In 2009, Bristol's city museum hosted a surprise Banksy exhibition, set up in secret. More than 300,000 people visited in about three months, some queuing for hours outside.

In August 2024 a new animal appeared somewhere in London every day for nine days. There was a goat on a wall, monkeys on a bridge, a rhino climbing a car and a gorilla at London Zoo. Crowds gathered within hours. A few pieces were damaged or taken away almost as fast.

Art or vandalism?

People still disagree. Some see Banksy as a brave artist who brings art to ordinary streets, where anyone can see it for free. Others point out that most graffiti artists are fined for the same act, and that a famous name should not change the rules.

Banksy has also run bigger projects, such as Dismaland in 2015, a gloomy fake theme park on the English seaside that made fun of the happy-family kind. Like most of the work, it asked visitors to laugh first and think afterwards. Museums that collect famous paintings now argue over Banksy too, which may be the oddest twist for an artist who started on the street.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Banksy has been unmasked, and everyone knows who it is.

Truth: Journalists have named several people, and some guesses are popular. Banksy has never confirmed any of them, so the real name is still unknown to the public.

Try this

Make a stencil without scissors. Fold a sheet of scrap paper and tear a simple shape out of the fold, like a heart or a bird. Lay the paper flat on another sheet and dab poster paint through the hole with a sponge. Lift it off and see how cleanly the shape prints.
