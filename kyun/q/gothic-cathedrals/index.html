How were cathedrals built so tall?

Medieval builders, working without steel or engines, made stone churches taller than a 12-storey building, with walls that seem made more of glass than stone.

The short answer

Gothic cathedrals are the huge, pointed, window-filled churches built in Europe from the 1100s to the 1500s. Their builders used three clever tricks to go higher than anyone had before. Pointed arches sent the weight of the stone more steeply downwards. Ribbed vaults made the stone ceiling lighter. Flying buttresses, stone arms on the outside of the building, held the walls up.

Together these let builders make walls thin, tall and full of stained glass. The result was a building that felt full of light and seemed to reach for the sky, which was exactly what its builders wanted.

Surprising but true

Cologne Cathedral in Germany was started in 1248 and not finished until 1880, more than 600 years later.

The problem with stone

Stone is very strong when you squash it, but it cracks if it bends or gets pulled. A stone roof is heavy, and as gravity pulls it down, it also pushes the walls outwards.

Before Gothic, builders used round arches and very thick walls to hold that push. Churches in this older style, called Romanesque, were solid but dark inside, because big windows would weaken the walls.

Three clever tricks

The pointed arch was the first trick. Compared with a round arch, it sends more of the weight down and less of it sideways, so it pushes less on the walls. It could also be made taller or shorter while keeping the same width, which made planning easier.

Next came ribbed vaults: stone ceilings built on a frame of crossing stone ribs, with thinner stone filling the gaps. Last came the flying buttress, an arch outside the building leaning against the upper walls. It catches the outward push and carries it down to a heavy pillar on the ground, much as some bridges carry their load to the riverbank.

How do bridges hold up so much weight?Every bridge is a tug-of-war between two forces, a push and a pull. Good engineers decide which parts get which.

Walls of coloured glass

With the buttresses doing the work outside, walls no longer needed to be thick. Builders filled them with huge windows of stained glass, coloured with metals melted into the glass. Small pieces were held together with strips of lead to form pictures of saints and Bible stories.

Most people at the time could not read, so the windows taught stories in pictures. On a sunny day, coloured light spills across the stone floor. Chartres Cathedral in France still has most of its original glass from the 1200s.

Building for centuries

There were no cranes with engines. Workers lifted stones with wooden cranes, sometimes powered by people walking inside a giant wheel, like a hamster wheel. Masons learned by apprenticeship and used compasses, string and simple geometry rather than written calculations.

A cathedral often took generations. Many workers never saw the one they worked on finished. Cologne Cathedral was started in 1248, paused for centuries, and only completed in 1880. Its spires, about 157 metres tall, made it briefly the tallest building in the world.

When things went wrong

Builders sometimes pushed too far. At Beauvais in France, the stone vault over the choir reached about 48 metres, the highest ever built in the Gothic style. In 1284 part of it collapsed. It was rebuilt with extra supports, and the building was never completed as planned.

In 2019 a fire destroyed the roof and spire of Notre-Dame in Paris. Its stone vaults mostly held, protecting the inside. After five years of work by carpenters, stonemasons and glassmakers using many traditional methods, it reopened in December 2024.

Gothic far from Europe

The Gothic style spread across the world. In Mumbai, the Victoria Terminus railway station, now Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Terminus, mixes Gothic arches with Indian domes and carvings. In the United States, Washington National Cathedral was built in Gothic style over 83 years, finally completed in 1990. Even early skyscrapers, with their pointed tops, borrowed Gothic ideas.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Medieval builders were guessing, so their cathedrals often fell down.

Truth: Some did collapse, like part of Beauvais Cathedral in 1284. But builders learned from experience and used careful geometry, and many cathedrals have stood for 700 or 800 years.

Try this

Lean two books against each other to make a tent shape on a smooth table. Press down on the top and watch the bottoms slide apart. Now put a heavy object against the outside of each book and press again. Those objects are doing the job of a buttress.
