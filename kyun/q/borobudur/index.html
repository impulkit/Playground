What is Borobudur?

On the Indonesian island of Java stands a Buddhist monument built over a hill, with 2,672 carved stone panels that tell stories as you climb.

The short answer

Borobudur is a huge Buddhist monument in Central Java, Indonesia, built about 1,200 years ago. From the air it looks like a giant stone flower or a stepped cake. Each side of its square base is about 123 m long, more than a football field.

It isn't a temple you go inside. Instead you walk around it, level by level, climbing towards the top. The walls of the lower levels are covered in 2,672 carved panels showing stories from the life of the Buddha and from Buddhist teaching. Higher up, the carvings stop. You reach open round terraces with 72 bell-shaped stupas and a calm view of green rice fields and smoking volcanoes.

Surprising but true

Borobudur has no rooms or halls inside. Its builders covered a natural hill with stone, so the whole monument is solid.

A book you read with your feet

Pilgrims walk around each level clockwise, keeping the monument on their right. As they go, the panels unroll like a comic strip in stone. The lowest level shows people caught up in everyday wants and troubles. Further up come stories of the Buddha's past lives and his search for wisdom.

At the top there are no pictures at all. Each stupa is a stone lattice, like a bell made of trelliswork, with a seated Buddha statue inside. The climb is meant to feel like a journey from the busy world to a quiet, empty mind.

The panels are also a picture record of life in old Java. They show ships with outriggers, houses on stilts, musicians, markets and farmers with their animals. Historians use them to work out what people wore, ate and sailed in.

Who was the Buddha?A prince left his palace at 29 to find out why people suffer. What he taught now guides hundreds of millions of people.

Who built it

Borobudur was built between about 780 and 830 CE under the Sailendra dynasty, kings who ruled Central Java. The builders used grey andesite, a hard stone formed from cooled lava. They cut the blocks to lock together like pieces of a puzzle, without any mortar. Gutters and about a hundred spouts, many carved as monster heads, carried away the heavy tropical rain.

Java at this time was linked by sea to India, China and Sumatra. Buddhist monks, traders and ideas moved along these routes. The great sea kingdom of Srivijaya, based in Sumatra, had close family ties with the Sailendras. The style of the stupas grew from older Indian monuments like those at Sanchi.

Buried and found again

In the centuries after it was finished, Java's rulers moved east and Borobudur was used less. Eruptions of nearby Mount Merapi dropped ash over it, and plants crept across the stones. The villagers living around it never forgot it, though. They knew the hill of carved stones, even if few outsiders came to see it.

In 1814, Thomas Stamford Raffles, the British governor of Java, sent an engineer to clear it. Over the next century, Dutch teams repaired parts of it. Then, from 1975 to 1982, Indonesia and UNESCO took most of the monument apart, fixed its drainage and put more than a million stones back. In 1991 it became a World Heritage Site.

Looking after the stones

Borobudur still faces dangers. When Merapi erupted in 2010, a thick layer of volcano ash coated the monument, and workers spent months brushing it away by hand.

Millions of feet are a danger too. Steps had worn down, and some visitors touched or sat on the stupas. In 2023 Indonesia began limiting how many people may climb to the top each day, and climbers now wear special woven sandals that are gentle on the stone. Every year at Vesak, the festival of the Buddha's birth, monks walk in procession from nearby temples to Borobudur, as pilgrims did more than a thousand years ago.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Borobudur was lost in the jungle until a British governor discovered it in 1814.

Truth: Local Javanese people always knew it was there, even when it was half buried under ash and plants. In 1814 Thomas Stamford Raffles sent an engineer to clear and survey it, which made it famous in Europe.
