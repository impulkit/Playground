What are mud volcanoes?

On Baratang Island in the Andamans, small cones of grey mud bubble and gurgle, and in 2005 one burst into life after a huge undersea earthquake.

The short answer

A mud volcano is a hill or cone built from mud pushed up out of the ground by gas and water trapped deep below. It looks like a small volcano, with a crater on top, but instead of red-hot lava it oozes, bubbles or sometimes blasts out cool grey mud.

They form where layers of soft, wet sediment are buried and squeezed, often near oil and gas fields or along the edges of colliding tectonic plates. The pressure, plus gases such as methane, forces the mud up through cracks. Most are a few metres high, but some reach hundreds of metres.

Surprising but true

Azerbaijan has around 350 to 400 mud volcanoes, roughly half of all those known on land, and some occasionally shoot flames hundreds of metres into the air.

India's only mud volcanoes

The Andaman Islands, in the Bay of Bengal, sit near the boundary where the Indian plate dives under the Burma plate. On Baratang Island, visitors walk through forest to reach a clearing where small cones of grey mud bubble quietly.

After the giant earthquake and tsunami of December 2004, scientists noticed changes. In 2005, one of the Baratang mud volcanoes erupted strongly, building a new mound of mud and covering trees nearby. The Andaman Islands are the only place in India with known active mud volcanoes.

The fire-breathing mud of Azerbaijan

Azerbaijan, on the Caspian Sea, has more mud volcanoes than anywhere else, somewhere around 350 to 400 on land. That is roughly half of all the onshore mud volcanoes known.

Many of them leak methane, the same gas used in many kitchen stoves. Once in a while, a large eruption ignites the gas, and columns of fire shoot high into the sky, visible from kilometres away. In the same country, a hillside called Yanar Dag has burned for as long as anyone can remember, fed by gas seeping from the ground. Places like these are cousins of the burning gas crater in Turkmenistan, the "Door to Hell".

The mud that wouldn't stop

In May 2006, hot mud started pouring out of the ground near a gas-drilling well in Sidoarjo, on the island of Java, Indonesia. It became known as Lusi. Within months, a sea of mud had swallowed factories, schools, homes and rice fields.

About 40,000 people had to move. Years later, mud was still flowing, held back by huge walls. Scientists are still debating the cause. Many think the drilling triggered it by cracking rock layers. Others argue that an earthquake two days earlier, about 250 km away, set it off. The case went to courts and to scientific journals, with no final agreement.

The mud pot that walked

In southern California, near the Salton Sea, there are bubbling pools of mud called mud pots, driven by carbon dioxide gas rising from below. Most stay put. But one, near the town of Niland, started moving in 2016, creeping several metres a year.

By 2018 it was heading for a railway line and a highway. Engineers had to build barriers and change the tracks. It is one of very few known moving mud volcanoes in the world.

Mud volcanoes and oil

Geologists like mud volcanoes because they bring up material from deep underground. The mud and gas can show what lies buried kilometres below, including clues about oil and natural gas. In Azerbaijan, people have collected oil from seeps for centuries.

Scientists have also spotted features on Mars that look like mud volcanoes, suggesting there was once water underground there. Back on Earth, the bubbling mud of Baratang is a small window into the restless crust under India's island chain. Not far to the east sits Barren Island, India's only active volcano of the fiery, lava-spitting kind.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Mud volcanoes are small, harmless curiosities.

Truth: Most are small, but some grow hundreds of metres high, and a few burst violently or catch fire. Indonesia's Lusi eruption buried villages in mud and forced about 40,000 people to leave their homes.

Try this

Mix flour or cornflour with water in a cup until it is like thick runny mud. Push a straw to the bottom and blow gently. The air struggles up through the paste and bursts out in plops and splats, building tiny craters on the surface. That is how gas forces mud up through a mud volcano.
