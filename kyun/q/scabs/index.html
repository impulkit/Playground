How does a cut heal?

The moment you cut your finger, tiny pieces in your blood rush to the gap and start building a plug, like workers sealing a burst water pipe.

The short answer

When you cut yourself, your body runs a repair job in four overlapping stages.

First it stops the bleeding. Blood vessels narrow, and tiny cell pieces called platelets stick together over the gap, held by a mesh of protein threads. This forms a clot. When the clot dries on the surface, it becomes a scab.

Next, white blood cells clean out germs and dirt, which is why a cut can look red and puffy. Then new skin cells and fresh tissue grow in to fill the gap. Finally, over months, the repair is strengthened and tidied. A small cut is closed within days. A deeper one may leave a scar.

Surprising but true

Experiments in 1962 showed that cuts kept clean and covered heal faster than cuts left open to dry out, the opposite of what many people believed.

Stopping the leak

Your blood carries billions of platelets. Normally they float along smoothly. But when a vessel tears, they meet the rough tissue underneath, turn sticky and pile up. They also send out chemical signals calling for help.

Those signals start a chain of reactions that turns a protein in the blood into long threads called fibrin. The fibrin wraps around platelets and red blood cells like a net, and the plug becomes firm. Most small cuts stop bleeding within a few minutes.

The clean-up

Over the next few days, your immune system gets to work. Extra blood flows to the area, bringing white blood cells that swallow bacteria and dead cells. The area may feel warm, look pink and be a little sore.

This is normal. But sometimes a cut gets more painful, swells a lot, oozes pus or has red streaks spreading from it. Then it may be infected, and a trusted adult should get a doctor to look at it. People with a condition called haemophilia have blood that doesn't clot properly, so even small cuts need extra care.

Building new skin

Under the scab, cells at the edges of the wound start to multiply and crawl across the gap. Other cells lay down collagen, a tough protein that acts like scaffolding. Tiny new blood vessels grow into the area to feed it.

When the gap is covered with new skin, the scab has nothing left to do and falls off on its own. Picking it off early tears away new cells and can make a scar more likely.

Why moist is better

For a long time, many people believed cuts should be left open to dry. In 1962, a British scientist, George Winter, tested this on young pigs, whose skin is a lot like ours. Wounds that were covered healed about twice as fast as wounds left open to the air.

This led to the modern plasters and dressings that keep a wound clean and slightly moist. Good first aid for a small cut is simple: wash it with clean water, press to stop bleeding, and cover it.

Scars and stitches

A deep cut may need stitches, glue or strips that hold the edges together, so there is less gap to fill. Surgeons have been closing wounds this way for thousands of years. The ancient Indian surgeon Sushruta described stitching with thread.

Scar tissue is made of collagen laid down in a hurry, so it is less stretchy than normal skin. It has no hair or sweat glands. Scars often fade over a year or two, but rarely vanish completely. Some people's scars grow large and raised, which doctors can help with.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You should let a cut "breathe" in the open air so it dries out and heals faster.

Truth: Small wounds usually heal faster, and leave smaller scars, when they are kept clean and covered so they stay slightly moist. Ask a trusted adult to help clean and cover a cut.

Try this

With an adult's permission, look at an old scar on someone's knee or elbow with a magnifying glass. Compare it with the skin next to it. You may see that the scar is smoother and shinier, with no fine hairs and fewer lines, because scar tissue is built differently from ordinary skin.
