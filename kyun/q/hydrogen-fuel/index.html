Could hydrogen power cars and trains?

A hydrogen train in Germany carries passengers all day and puffs out nothing but water vapour.

The short answer

Yes, hydrogen can power vehicles, and some already run on it. Most use a fuel cell, a device that combines hydrogen with oxygen from the air to make electricity. That electricity drives a motor, just like in a battery electric car. The only waste is water and some heat.

The hard part is the hydrogen itself. Almost all of it today is made from natural gas, which releases carbon dioxide. Clean "green" hydrogen, made using solar or wind power, is still expensive. So whether hydrogen will fill the roads, or mostly power trucks, ships and factories, is a real debate.

Surprising but true

Hydrogen is the most common element in the universe, but on Earth there is almost no pure hydrogen gas lying around. It all has to be made.

How a fuel cell works

A fuel cell has two sides separated by a thin sheet. Hydrogen goes in one side. A catalyst, often platinum, splits the hydrogen into protons and electrons. The sheet lets only protons through. The electrons have to go the long way round, through a wire, and that flow is electricity.

On the other side, the protons and electrons meet oxygen from the air and join up to make water. Nothing burns. There's no combustion, just a steady chemical reaction, which is why fuel cells are quiet.

Trains that drip water

In September 2018, two hydrogen trains called the Coradia iLint began carrying passengers in Lower Saxony, Germany. They replaced diesel trains on a line without overhead electric wires. Since then, trains like these have run in a few other countries, though some German operators later chose battery trains instead because they cost less to run.

India has been working on its own. Indian Railways has built a hydrogen train meant for a route in Haryana, as a test for lines where putting up electric wires is hard, such as heritage hill railways. Most of India's railways are already electric, so hydrogen is aimed at the gaps.

Making hydrogen is the catch

On Earth, hydrogen is locked up in water and in fuels like natural gas. Pulling it out takes energy. Most is made by heating natural gas with steam, which gives off carbon dioxide. Engineers call that "grey" hydrogen.

"Green" hydrogen is made by passing electricity from renewable sources through water, splitting it into hydrogen and oxygen. In 2023, India launched its National Green Hydrogen Mission, aiming to produce 5 million tonnes a year by 2030, partly for fertiliser plants and refineries.

The Sun itself runs on hydrogen, though in a completely different way: its core crushes hydrogen atoms together in nuclear fusion.

Batteries versus hydrogen

Here's the numbers problem. Turning electricity into hydrogen, squeezing it into a tank, then turning it back into electricity in a fuel cell loses more than half the energy. Charging a battery and using it loses far less, often around a fifth. For a family car, that makes batteries cheaper to run.

Hydrogen fights back on weight and refuelling time. A hydrogen tank fills in about five minutes, and the gas stores lots of energy for its weight. Cars squeeze it to about 700 times normal air pressure so enough fits on board. That matters for a heavy truck crossing a continent, a ship, or a plane, where batteries would be enormous.

Hydrogen already goes to space

Hydrogen is old news to rocket scientists. Many rockets burn liquid hydrogen, chilled to minus 253°C, with liquid oxygen. ISRO's cryogenic engine, used on its GSLV and LVM3 rockets, works this way. Getting hydrogen into cars was always going to be harder than getting it into space, because a car needs a filling station on every highway. Only about a thousand hydrogen filling stations existed worldwide in 2024, most of them in Japan, South Korea, China and Germany. For now, the debate is as much about pumps as about engines.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Hydrogen cars are dangerous bombs on wheels, like the Hindenburg airship.

Truth: Hydrogen catches fire easily, but car tanks are thick carbon-fibre cylinders tested in crashes and fires. Hydrogen is very light, so if it leaks it rises and spreads away quickly instead of pooling under the car like petrol.
