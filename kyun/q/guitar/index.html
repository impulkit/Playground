How does a guitar work?

An electric guitar has no microphone inside. It "hears" its strings with magnets.

The short answer

A guitar makes sound when you pluck or strum its strings. Each string vibrates back and forth very fast. On an acoustic guitar, the strings pass their shaking to a thin wooden top, and the hollow body fills with vibrating air that comes out loud through the round sound hole.

To change the note, you press a string against a metal strip on the neck, called a fret. That shortens the part of the string that can vibrate. A shorter string vibrates faster and makes a higher note. An electric guitar works differently: it has a solid body and turns the strings' movement into an electric signal that an amplifier makes loud.

Surprising but true

An Indian musician, Vishwa Mohan Bhatt, rebuilt the guitar into a new instrument called the Mohan veena and won a Grammy award with it in 1994.

Six strings and a lot of frets

Most guitars have six strings, tuned from low to high as E, A, D, G, B and E. The thickest string gives the deepest note. The thinnest gives the highest.

Frets are spaced closer together as they go up the neck. Each one raises the note by a semitone, the smallest step on a piano. The twelfth fret sits exactly halfway along the string. Pressing there halves the vibrating length and gives the same note one octave higher, the same rule a sitar player uses, as explained in high and low notes.

From lutes to the classical guitar

Instruments with a neck, a hollow body and plucked strings have existed for thousands of years. The lute and the Arabic oud are close relatives of the guitar.

In Spain, during the 1800s, a craftsman named Antonio de Torres settled the shape of the modern classical guitar. He made the body larger and braced the wooden top with thin strips in a fan pattern, giving a richer sound. Classical guitars still use soft nylon strings. Guitars with steel strings, louder and brighter, became popular in the USA for folk, country and blues music.

Magnets that listen

Under the strings of an electric guitar sit pickups: small magnets wrapped in thousands of turns of very fine copper wire. When a steel string vibrates above them, it disturbs the magnetic field. That creates a tiny, fast-changing electric current in the wire, matching the string's movement exactly.

The signal travels down a cable to an amplifier, which makes it powerful enough to drive a loudspeaker. Guitarists can also pass the signal through effects boxes to make it echo, buzz or wail.

Louder than a band

In the 1920s and 1930s, guitarists in big dance bands had a problem. Trumpets and drums drowned them out. Inventors in the USA began fitting guitars with pickups. One of the first electric guitars sold, in the early 1930s, was a round, flat instrument nicknamed the "Frying Pan".

In the 1950s, solid-body electric guitars by companies such as Fender and Gibson became popular. They didn't howl with feedback as easily as hollow guitars, and they could be played very loud. They became the sound of rock and roll.

A Hawaiian twist and an Indian star

In Hawaii in the late 1800s, players began laying the guitar flat on their lap and sliding a smooth steel bar along the strings. Notes could glide smoothly instead of jumping. This Hawaiian steel guitar spread around the world, as you can read in Hawaiian culture.

Indian musicians loved it, because sliding notes suit Indian music well. Vishwa Mohan Bhatt added extra strings to create the Mohan veena, which he plays sitting cross-legged. In 1994, his album with the American guitarist Ry Cooder, A Meeting by the River, won a Grammy award. The guitar had come home in a new shape.

Myth or fact?

Myth: An electric guitar has a tiny microphone inside that picks up the sound.

Truth: The pickups don't listen to sound at all. They are magnets with coils of wire, and they sense the movement of the steel strings directly. That is why an unplugged electric guitar sounds very quiet.

Try this

Stretch a rubber band around an empty tissue box, across the hole. Slide a pencil under the band near one end to act as the bridge. Pluck the band, then press it down at different points with a finger and pluck again. Shorter lengths should give higher notes.
