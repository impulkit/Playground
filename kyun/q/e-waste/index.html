What happens to old phones and laptops?

The 5,000 medals at the Tokyo Olympics were made from gold, silver and bronze pulled out of old phones and gadgets donated by the Japanese public.

The short answer

Old phones, laptops, televisions, chargers and other gadgets become electronic waste, or e-waste. Some are repaired and sold again. Some are taken apart, and the valuable metals inside, such as gold, copper and silver, are recovered. But a lot is simply thrown away or left forgotten in drawers.

The world threw away about 62 million tonnes of e-waste in 2022, according to a United Nations report. That's enough to fill more than a million and a half large trucks. Only about 22% was properly collected and recycled. India is one of the three biggest producers, after China and the United States.

Surprising but true

A tonne of old mobile phones contains far more gold than a tonne of the rock dug out of a typical gold mine.

A gold mine in a drawer

A phone is packed with useful materials. Copper wires, gold on the circuit board connectors, silver, palladium, cobalt in the battery, and rare metals in the speakers and screen. A tonne of old phones holds much more gold than a tonne of ore from a typical gold mine, where miners might get just a few grams.

For the Tokyo 2020 Olympics, held in 2021, Japan asked people to donate old phones and electronics. Over about two years, the public handed in around 79,000 tonnes of devices, including about 6 million phones. All roughly 5,000 gold, silver and bronze medals were made from metal recovered from them.

Kabadiwalas and Seelampur

In India, most e-waste doesn't go to big factories. It goes to kabadiwalas, the scrap dealers who buy old newspapers and broken gadgets door to door. They sell to markets like Seelampur in Delhi, one of the country's biggest informal e-waste hubs, where thousands of workers take devices apart by hand.

These workers keep a lot of metal in use that would otherwise be thrown away. But the methods can be dangerous. Some burn plastic off wires to get the copper, or dip circuit boards in acid to pull out gold. The smoke and fumes contain lead, mercury and other poisons that harm the workers, often including children, and leak into soil and water.

Dangerous inside

E-waste contains harmful materials as well as valuable ones. Old TV screens had lead in their glass. Some switches and lamps contain mercury. Lithium batteries can catch fire if crushed in a rubbish truck. Plastics in cases and cables can give off toxic smoke when burned.

That's why e-waste shouldn't go in the normal bin. Dumped in a landfill, it can slowly leak these chemicals into the ground.

Rules and responsibility

India's E-Waste (Management) Rules, first made in 2011 and updated in 2016 and 2022, say that companies that make or sell electronics are responsible for collecting a share back for recycling. This idea is called extended producer responsibility. Authorised recyclers must handle the waste safely and keep records.

In the United States, rules differ from state to state. Some states require makers to pay for recycling, and many shops run take-back schemes for old phones and batteries.

Keeping gadgets longer

The best way to cut e-waste is to make it less often. Repairing a cracked screen or replacing a tired battery can keep a phone going for years. A "right to repair" movement, strong in the United States and Europe, is pushing companies to sell spare parts and share repair manuals. A smartphone handed down to a younger cousin is one less gadget in a drawer.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Most old electronics get recycled.

Truth: Worldwide, only about a fifth of e-waste is properly recycled. Much of it sits forgotten in drawers or ends up with informal recyclers who burn wires and use acids, which can harm workers and pollute soil and water.

Try this

Hunt around your home, with permission, for old phones, chargers, cables, remote controls and dead batteries. Make a list and guess what metals each might contain. Ask an adult to find the nearest authorised e-waste collection point, or a shop that takes old gadgets back.
