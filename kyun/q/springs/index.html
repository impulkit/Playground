How do springs store energy?

The Slinky was invented by accident when a spring fell off a shelf and seemed to walk across the floor.

The short answer

A spring is a coil of springy metal, usually steel. When you squash it or stretch it, you push the metal out of its natural shape. The metal pushes back, trying to return. While you hold it squashed or stretched, it is storing the energy you put in. Scientists call this elastic potential energy.

Let go, and the spring snaps back, releasing that energy. It might launch a toy, close a door, or push a pen nib back inside.

The spring doesn't create energy. It works like a battery for push and pull: you put energy in by bending it, and get it back later.

Surprising but true

In 1676, Robert Hooke first published his law of springs as a scrambled anagram, so rivals couldn't steal it before he was ready.

Hooke's law

In the 1600s, the English scientist Robert Hooke tested springs by hanging weights from them. He found a neat rule. If one weight stretches a spring by 2 cm, two weights stretch it by 4 cm, and three by 6 cm. The stretch grows in step with the force.

Hooke was worried rivals would steal his idea. In 1676 he published it as a jumbled set of letters, "ceiiinosssttuv". Two years later he revealed the answer, a Latin phrase ut tensio sic vis, meaning "as the stretch, so the force". This rule is now called Hooke's law, and it lets engineers design springs that push with exactly the force they need.

It only works up to a point. Pull too hard and the metal bends for good. That point is called the elastic limit.

Springs that keep time

Before springs, clocks were driven by heavy hanging weights, so they had to stand still. In the 1400s and 1500s, clockmakers began using a tightly wound coil called a mainspring. As it slowly unwound, it drove the gears. Clocks could finally be small enough to carry.

Around 1675, Hooke and the Dutch scientist Christiaan Huygens each worked on a tiny hairspring that made a watch's balance wheel swing back and forth at a steady beat. This made watches far more accurate. A wind-up watch today still uses a mainspring and a hairspring, with no battery at all, and clocks of this kind are still made by hand.

A toy that walks downstairs

In 1943, an American naval engineer named Richard James was working with springs meant to steady instruments on ships. One fell off a shelf and kept moving, flipping end over end across the floor. He and his wife Betty saw a toy in it, and Betty named it the Slinky.

In 1945, they demonstrated it at a department store in Philadelphia, and the first batch sold out quickly. Millions have been sold since. A Slinky walks downstairs because each coil passes its energy on to the next, and gravity keeps pulling it down.

Springs in your body

Living things use springs too. When you run, the long tendon at the back of your ankle, the Achilles tendon, stretches as your foot lands and springs back as you push off. It stores energy from one step and returns some of it in the next, like a pogo stick.

Kangaroos in Australia take this further. The huge tendons in their back legs act as springs, so hopping fast costs them surprisingly little extra energy. Trampolines, car suspensions, mattresses and the springy floors used in gymnastics all borrow the same trick: soak up energy on the way down, give it back on the way up.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A spring will always bounce back to its shape, however far you stretch it.

Truth: Every spring has an elastic limit. Stretch it beyond that, and the metal bends permanently and stays longer. That's why a toy spring pulled too far ends up looking tired and floppy.

Try this

Hang a rubber band or a spring from a pencil wedged between two books, and tie a small plastic bag to the bottom. Add coins one at a time and measure the stretch with a ruler after each. Draw a graph of coins against stretch. Does it make a straight line?
