Why do some people have dimples?

Cheek dimples are often caused by a smiling muscle that splits in two, tugging a tiny patch of skin inward every time you grin.

The short answer

Most cheek dimples come from a muscle. When you smile, a pair of muscles called the zygomaticus major pull the corners of your mouth up towards your cheekbones. In some people, one of these muscles splits into two bundles, or is attached a little differently.

When that split muscle pulls, a small patch of skin between the two bundles gets tugged inward. The result is a little dent that appears when you smile and fades when your face relaxes. That is a dimple.

Some people have one dimple, some have two, and most have none. Dimples are just a difference in how faces are built, a bit like ear shape. They do no harm and need no fixing.

Surprising but true

A golf ball has roughly 300 to 500 dimples, and they help it fly about twice as far as a smooth ball would.

The smiling muscle

Your face has more than 40 muscles, and unlike most muscles in your body, many are attached to skin rather than only to bone. That is why your face can make so many expressions. The zygomaticus major runs from your cheekbone down to the corner of your mouth, like a diagonal rope.

In a small study of faces, doctors found that people with cheek dimples often had a zygomaticus major with two separate bands. When it tightens, skin gets caught between the bands. Other people may have a short band of tissue linking the muscle to the skin, which also makes a dent.

The muscle pulls hardest during a broad grin, so dimples are deepest when you laugh hard.

Babies, chins and backs

Many babies have dimples that disappear as they grow. In young children, chubby cheeks can fold into dimples on their own. As the face slims and lengthens, those dents can fade. Now and then the opposite happens, and a dimple shows up in an older child.

A chin dimple, or cleft chin, is a different thing. It forms when the two halves of the lower jawbone don't fully join smoothly before birth, leaving a small groove that the skin follows.

There are even dimples low on the back. These two small dents sit just above the hips, where skin is tied by short ligaments to the pelvis bone underneath. They are sometimes called dimples of Venus.

Do dimples run in families?

Dimples often appear in parents and children. For years, school textbooks said they were controlled by a single dominant gene, like a simple switch. That story is too tidy.

Plenty of children of parents with dimples don't have them, and some children have them when neither parent does. A dimple can also show up on only one side. Scientists now think several genes may be involved, along with how the face develops before birth. Large, careful studies are still rare, which is why the question remains open.

How common dimples are depends on which group of people is studied. Surveys in different countries have come up with quite different numbers.

Dimples everywhere

People in many parts of the world find dimples charming, and they are often linked with friendliness, because they show up in smiles. In some places, people have surgery to create them, though doctors point out that a natural face is perfectly healthy without them.

Faces are full of small differences like these. Freckles, eye colour and dimples help make each face unique enough that even a phone can tell people apart.

And the strangest part? The word dimple is also used for the little dents on a golf ball, and those dents are a piece of clever science. They help the ball slip through the air and fly about twice as far, one of the tricks explained in how sports equipment works.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Dimples are controlled by one dominant gene, so a parent with dimples always passes them on.

Truth: Dimples do run in families, but many children of dimpled parents don't have them, and some appear or vanish as a child grows. Scientists now think inheritance is more complicated than a single gene.

Try this

Smile widely in front of a mirror and press gently on your cheeks with your fingertips. Feel the muscles bunch up as you smile and relax as you stop. If you have dimples, notice exactly where on the curve of the muscle they appear.
