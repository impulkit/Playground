How does a smartwatch count your steps?

A chip smaller than a grain of rice feels every jolt of your arm, and a few lines of maths decide which jolts were steps.

The short answer

Inside a smartwatch is a tiny sensor called an accelerometer. It measures how fast the watch speeds up or slows down, in three directions at once: up and down, side to side, and forwards and back. Every time your foot hits the ground, your whole body jolts, and your wrist jolts with it.

When you walk, those jolts come in a steady rhythm, about one or two every second. The watch runs a small set of rules, an algorithm, that looks for that rhythm. If a jolt is strong enough and arrives at about the right moment after the last one, the watch counts a step. Random shakes, like waving at a friend, mostly get ignored.

Surprising but true

The famous goal of 10,000 steps a day began as part of the name of a Japanese pedometer sold in the 1960s, not as a number from a medical study.

A weight on a spring, shrunk to a chip

The accelerometer works like a tiny weight held by springs. When the watch moves, the weight lags behind for a moment, the way your body tips back when a bus pulls away. The chip measures how far the weight shifts and turns that into a number.

All of this is carved into silicon only a few millimetres across, using the same methods that make computer chips. Many watches add a gyroscope, which senses twisting, so the watch can tell a step from a turn of the wrist. Some also carry GPS to measure how far you went outdoors.

Telling steps from everything else

The hard part is not sensing movement. It's deciding which movements are steps. Engineers record thousands of people walking, running, climbing stairs and doing other things, then tune the rules until the count matches what really happened.

Even so, no watch is perfect. Pushing a pram or a shopping trolley keeps your wrist still, so steps get missed. A bumpy ride or clapping along to music can add a few that never happened. Tests usually find good watches land within a few per cent of the real count when you walk normally, and drift further off during odd activities.

Why does your heart beat faster when you run?Sprint to the end of the road and your heart can more than double its speed within a minute, without you deciding anything.

The green light underneath

Turn many smartwatches over and you'll see small green lights flashing. Blood absorbs green light well. Each time your heart beats, a little more blood pushes into the tiny vessels in your wrist, and a little less light bounces back to a sensor beside the LEDs.

By timing those dips, the watch estimates your heart rate. It's a neat trick, but it struggles when the strap is loose, when you're moving very fast, or in cold weather when less blood flows to the skin. That's why the numbers can jump around during a sprint.

Where 10,000 came from

In 1965, a Japanese company sold a pedometer called manpo-kei, which means "10,000-step meter". The round number was catchy, and it stuck for decades.

Researchers have since tested it. A large review of studies, published in 2022, found that people who walk more do tend to live longer. But the extra benefit starts to flatten out somewhere between about 6,000 and 10,000 steps a day. Older adults reached that point sooner than younger ones. For a healthy body, the exact number matters less than moving often.

Fitbit's first tracker, which clipped onto clothing, went on sale in 2009. Today the same idea sits on millions of wrists, and inside most smartphones too, quietly counting while you get on with your day.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Scientists worked out that 10,000 steps a day is the right amount for health.

Truth: The number came from the name of a Japanese pedometer called manpo-kei, which means "10,000-step meter". Large studies since then suggest the benefits start to level off somewhere between about 6,000 and 10,000 steps a day, depending on age.

Try this

Wear a watch or carry a phone with a step counter. Walk exactly 50 steps, counting out loud, and check what the device says. Then try 50 steps while holding a bag in that hand, and compare the two results.
