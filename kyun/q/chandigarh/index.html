Why is Chandigarh a planned city?

When Partition left Indian Punjab without a capital, India built a whole new city on farmland at the foot of the Shivalik hills.

The short answer

Chandigarh is a planned city because it was designed all at once, on purpose, rather than growing slowly over centuries. In 1947, Partition split Punjab between India and Pakistan. Punjab's capital, Lahore, went to Pakistan, so Indian Punjab needed a new one.

Instead of choosing an old town, India's leaders decided to build a brand-new city. Prime Minister Nehru wanted it to stand for a free, modern India, not tied to the past. The city was named after Chandi Mandir, a temple to the goddess Chandi nearby. Its main plan was drawn by the architect Le Corbusier in the early 1950s.

Surprising but true

Chandigarh's famous Rock Garden began as one man's secret hobby. Road inspector Nek Chand spent years building sculptures from broken pots, plugs and bangles in a patch of forest before officials found it.

A city drawn on paper

The first plan was made by the American planner Albert Mayer and the architect Matthew Nowicki. When Nowicki died in a plane crash in 1950, the job passed to Le Corbusier, working with his cousin Pierre Jeanneret and the British architects Maxwell Fry and Jane Drew. Indian architects and engineers worked alongside them.

Le Corbusier kept the idea of a grid. He divided the land into rectangular sectors, each roughly 800 m by 1,200 m. Every sector was meant to be a small neighbourhood with its own homes, shops, school and green spaces, so that daily needs were within walking distance. Sectors are known by numbers, and there is famously no Sector 13.

Roads for every job

Le Corbusier gave Chandigarh a system of roads he called the "7 Vs". Fast roads ran between sectors, while slower streets wound inside them, and footpaths and cycle tracks had routes of their own. The idea was to keep fast traffic away from where children walked to school.

Long green belts run through the city, and thousands of trees were planted along its roads. Sukhna Lake, a reservoir made by damming a seasonal stream, gave people a place to walk and row.

Critics point out that the plan was not perfect. The city was designed for about 5 lakh people, but its population has grown well past that, and nearby towns such as Mohali and Panchkula have spread around it. Some people also find the long, wide roads hard to cross on foot. Planners still visit to study both what worked and what did not.

The Capitol Complex

At the head of the city, against the Shivalik hills, Le Corbusier designed the Capitol Complex. It has the High Court, the Legislative Assembly and the Secretariat, made of bold, rough concrete. Nearby stands the Open Hand Monument, a giant metal hand that turns in the wind. In 2016, the complex became part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site covering Le Corbusier's work in seven countries.

Chandigarh later took on a second role. When Haryana was created in 1966, the city became the capital of both Punjab and Haryana, and also a Union Territory run by the central government.

A garden of broken things

While the city was being built, a road inspector named Nek Chand began collecting waste: broken tiles, bottles, electrical fittings, bangles and toilet bowls. In a patch of forest, he secretly used them to make hundreds of sculptures of people and animals.

When officials found the garden in 1975, it was technically illegal. But people loved it, and the city decided to protect it. Today the Rock Garden is one of Chandigarh's most visited places, an artwork made almost entirely of things the planned city threw away. Urban planners still point to it: even the most carefully drawn city grows in ways nobody planned.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Le Corbusier designed every building in Chandigarh by himself.

Truth: He drew the master plan and the Capitol Complex, but a team built the city. Pierre Jeanneret, Maxwell Fry and Jane Drew designed many homes, schools and other buildings, working with Indian architects and engineers.
