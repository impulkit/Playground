How do thunderstorms form?

A lightning bolt heats the air around it to about 27,000 °C, roughly five times hotter than the surface of the Sun, in a fraction of a second.

The short answer

A thunderstorm needs three things: warm, damp air near the ground, something to push that air upwards, and an atmosphere where rising air keeps on rising.

On a hot afternoon, the Sun warms the ground, and the ground warms the air above it. Warm air is lighter, so it rises. As it climbs, it cools, and the water vapour in it turns into tiny droplets, forming a cloud. Turning vapour into water releases heat, which makes the air rise even faster.

Soon a small puffy cloud has grown into a towering cumulonimbus, often 12 km tall or more, with a flat top like an anvil. Inside, ice and water crash around, building up electric charge until lightning flashes and thunder booms.

Surprising but true

At any moment, there are roughly 2,000 thunderstorms happening somewhere on Earth.

A storm's life

Most thunderstorms last less than an hour and go through three stages.

In the growing stage, warm air shoots upward in a strong current called an updraft. The cloud builds higher and higher, but there's little or no rain yet.

In the mature stage, raindrops and hailstones get too heavy for the updraft to hold. They fall, dragging cold air down with them in a downdraft. This is when the storm is at its fiercest, with heavy rain, gusty winds, lightning and sometimes hail.

In the dying stage, the downdraft takes over and cuts off the supply of warm air. The rain eases, and the tall cloud slowly falls apart. Some storms join up into long lines, called squall lines, that can march across hundreds of kilometres.

Where the sparks come from

Inside the cloud, tiny ice crystals rise while heavier soft hail falls. As they knock together, the small crystals tend to pick up positive charge and the hail picks up negative charge. Positive charge gathers at the top of the cloud, and negative charge near the bottom.

When the difference grows big enough, electricity jumps, inside the cloud or down to the ground. The bolt heats the air around it to about 27,000 °C. The air expands so violently that it makes a shock wave, which we hear as thunder.

Storms in India and the USA

In India, thunderstorms are common in the hot months just before the monsoon. In Bengal, Odisha and Assam, sudden April and May storms called kalbaishakhi, or nor'westers, sweep in from the north-west in the late afternoon. They cool the air, bring rain and sometimes flatten crops.

In the USA, Florida has more thunderstorm days than any other state. On the Great Plains, special rotating thunderstorms called supercells can spin up tornadoes.

Lightning is one of the biggest weather dangers in India. The National Crime Records Bureau counted about 2,900 lightning deaths in 2022, many of them farmers working in open fields.

Staying safe

Weather experts have a simple rule: "When thunder roars, go indoors." If you can hear thunder, you're close enough to be struck. A solid building or a car with a metal roof is safe. Trees, open fields, beaches and water are not.

If you're caught outside, don't shelter under a lone tree. Stay away from metal fences and poles. Once indoors, avoid baths, showers and taps during the storm, since lightning can travel along pipes. Wait about 30 minutes after the last thunder before going out again.

Watching from space

Satellites now spot thunderstorms as they grow, and lightning sensors pick up flashes across whole countries. In India, the India Meteorological Department sends lightning warnings, and the government's Damini app shows strikes nearby. Forecasters can't say exactly where a bolt will land, but they can tell when the sky is getting ready to build one.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Lightning never strikes the same place twice.

Truth: It often does. Tall, pointed things are hit again and again. New York's Empire State Building is struck around 20 times a year.

Try this

During the next storm, watching safely from indoors, count the seconds between each flash and its thunder. Divide by three to get the distance in kilometres. Write down the numbers over ten minutes and you'll see whether the storm is coming closer or moving away.
