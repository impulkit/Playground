Did ancient India know Pythagoras's theorem first?

Vedic priests building fire altars with ropes and pegs wrote down the famous triangle rule, and worked out the square root of 2 correct to five decimal places.

The short answer

Ancient India knew the rule, and wrote it down early. A Sanskrit text called the Baudhayana Sulba Sutra states it clearly. It is usually dated somewhere between about 800 and 500 BCE, around the time of Pythagoras or before.

But "first" is tricky. Clay tablets from Babylon, from about 1800 BCE, already list number sets that fit the rule. And Baudhayana, like the Babylonians, gave the rule without the kind of step-by-step proof the Greeks later became known for. So the fair answer is that several ancient peoples knew it, and India's statement is one of the oldest and clearest.

Surprising but true

Baudhayana's rule for the square root of 2 gives 1.4142156. The true value is 1.4142135..., so he was right to five decimal places.

Altars made with rope

During the Vedic age, important ceremonies needed fire altars built in exact shapes and sizes. Some were squares or circles. Others were shaped like birds, such as a falcon with outspread wings, made of hundreds of carefully cut bricks.

Getting the shape right mattered. Priests believed the ritual only worked if the altar was precise. So they needed ways to draw right angles, make a square with twice the area of another, or turn a square into a circle of the same area. The tools were simple: ropes and pegs. The word sulba itself means cord.

What Baudhayana wrote

Baudhayana's Sulba Sutra is the oldest of several such texts. In one verse, it says roughly this: the rope stretched along the diagonal of a rectangle makes an area equal to the areas made by the two sides together. That is the same rule as a² + b² = c².

The text also gives triangles with whole-number sides that obey the rule, such as 3, 4 and 5, and 5, 12 and 13. Builders could use these to make perfect right angles with a knotted rope. Later Sulba Sutras by Apastamba and Katyayana repeat and extend these ideas.

The texts also explain how to turn one shape into another with the same area, for example a square into a rectangle, or two squares into one bigger square. Some even try to make a circle with the same area as a square. That cannot be done exactly, but their rope methods came close enough for a brick altar.

A very good square root

To make a square with double the area of another, you need a side that is the first side times the square root of 2. That number is about 1.41421356, and its digits never end or repeat.

Baudhayana's text gives a recipe: take 1, add a third, add a quarter of that third, then take away one thirty-fourth of that quarter. It comes to about 1.4142156. That is correct to five decimal places, close enough that a builder could never measure the difference with a rope.

Why the history matters

People sometimes argue about who discovered the rule first, and the debate can get heated. Historians try to stick to the evidence. Babylonian tablets prove the rule was used there very early. The Sulba Sutras show it was understood independently in India. Chinese texts show it in China too.

What the Sulba Sutras show most clearly is how practical needs pushed maths forward. Priests wanted perfect altars, so they found reliable geometry. Centuries later, Indian mathematicians such as Aryabhata built on this tradition, working on astronomy, the number pi and much more. The rope-stretchers of the Vedic age were among the first in a long line.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Pythagorean theorem was a Greek discovery that India learned later.

Truth: Baudhayana's text states the rule independently, probably around the time of Pythagoras or earlier. Babylonians knew number examples of it even before that. Many ancient cultures found the rule on their own.

Try this

Go outside with an adult, or use the floor indoors. Hammer or hold two pegs (or place two heavy books) a metre apart and stretch a string between them. Now try to make a perfect square using only string and pegs, without a ruler or protractor. Measure the diagonals at the end: if they are equal, your corners are square.
