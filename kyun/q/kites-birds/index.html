How do black kites rule India's city skies?

Delhi has one of the highest numbers of birds of prey of any city on Earth, and most of them are black kites.

The short answer

Black kites rule India's city skies because cities give them almost everything they need. Markets, slaughterhouses and rubbish heaps provide food. Tall trees and buildings give them nesting spots. Hot roofs and roads create rising air to soar on.

They are also bold, clever and quick. A black kite can swoop down and snatch a snack from a person's hand, then eat it in mid-air. In Hindi it is called the cheel. In many Indian cities, from Delhi to Kolkata, it is the large brown bird you see circling high overhead almost every day.

Surprising but true

The paper kite you fly was named after this bird, because both hang and drift on the wind.

Masters of soaring

A black kite has long wings, about 1.5 m from tip to tip, and a forked tail. Watch one closely and you'll see the tail twist and tilt like a rudder as it steers.

On sunny days, roads and roofs heat up and warm air rises from them in invisible columns called thermals. Kites circle inside them and rise for free, without flapping. Then they glide off, scanning the ground with sharp eyes. Some soar high above Delhi for hours. They lose height so slowly that they seem to hang on the wind, which is how the paper kite got its English name.

A city fed by people

Scientists from the Wildlife Institute of India and the University of Oxford have studied Delhi's kites for years. They found that the city has one of the highest densities of birds of prey anywhere in the world.

Part of the reason is people. In old parts of Delhi, many people, especially Muslim families, throw scraps of meat into the air for the kites. For some, it is a religious act of charity, believed to bring blessings or ward off troubles. The kites have learned exactly when and where this happens. This old tradition has quietly helped thousands of birds live in the middle of a huge city.

Visitors from the north

Not every kite you see in winter is a local. Each year, a larger kind, the black-eared kite, flies in from Central Asia and joins the city birds. In spring, it returns north to breed. So the same tree in Delhi may host local nesters in summer and migrant guests in winter.

A dangerous string

Kites have one big human-made danger: kite string. During festivals such as Makar Sankranti and on Independence Day, the sky fills with paper kites. Some strings, called manja, are coated with powdered glass so they can cut other kites' strings in fights.

Every year, many black kites, pigeons and other birds get tangled in these strings and badly cut. In 2017, India's National Green Tribunal banned glass-coated and synthetic manja across the country, though some is still sold. Volunteers at bird hospitals treat injured birds after every big kite-flying day. The film All That Breathes, about two brothers in Delhi who rescue injured kites, was nominated for an Oscar in 2023.

Firebirds of Australia

Black kites live across Asia, Africa, Europe and Australia, which makes them one of the most widespread birds of prey on Earth. In northern Australia, Aboriginal people have long told of kites and other hawks picking up burning sticks and dropping them to spread bushfires. The fire drives out small animals, which the birds then catch.

In 2017, scientists published reports from rangers and others who had seen this happen. It is one of very few cases of a wild animal using fire on purpose. City or bush, the black kite is very good at making the most of whatever people, or nature, throw its way. Its big scavenging neighbours, the vultures, have not been so lucky. And on festival days, the kites share the sky with thousands of paper rivals, as you can read in why India flies kites at Makar Sankranti.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Birds of prey only live in wild forests far away from people.

Truth: Black kites thrive in crowded cities. They feed on scraps and small animals around markets, rubbish heaps and rivers, and nest in city trees and on buildings.

Try this

On a sunny afternoon, watch the sky from a window or balcony for 10 minutes. When you see a big bird circling, count how many times it flaps in one minute. Notice whether it rises or sinks as it circles.
