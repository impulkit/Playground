What is ikat weaving?

In Patan, Gujarat, weavers dye a pattern onto thousands of threads before weaving, then line them up so perfectly that the design appears on both sides of the silk.

The short answer

Ikat is a way of making patterned cloth where the threads are dyed before they are woven. Weavers tie small bundles of thread tightly with string or strips of plastic in a planned pattern, then dip them in dye. The tied parts stay undyed. After many rounds of tying and dyeing, the threads carry a pattern of coloured sections. When they are woven on a loom, the pattern appears in the cloth.

The name comes from the Malay and Indonesian word mengikat, meaning to tie. Ikat is made across Asia, Africa and Latin America. India is famous for three styles: patola from Patan in Gujarat, ikat from Pochampally in Telangana, and Sambalpuri from Odisha.

Surprising but true

Ikat patterns are planned like pixel art. Each tiny block of colour on a thread has to land in exactly the right place when the cloth is woven.

Pattern before cloth

Cloth on a loom has two sets of threads. The warp threads run lengthwise and are stretched tight. The weft threads cross over and under them. In warp ikat, only the warp threads are dyed with the pattern. In weft ikat, only the weft threads are. In double ikat, both are, and both must line up exactly.

Planning is everything. A designer works out where every coloured section must fall on each thread. Then workers measure, tie and dye bundles of threads, much like bandhani tie-dye but on threads instead of finished cloth. A single design may need several dye baths, with knots added and removed between colours.

The feathered edge

When thousands of dyed threads are set up on a loom, each one moves slightly. The colours don't line up perfectly. So ikat patterns have soft, flickering edges, as if seen through heat haze.

Weavers adjust each thread by hand to bring the pattern into line. The blur is not a mistake. It is how people recognise real ikat. Many weavers plan designs on graph paper first, colouring in squares the way a screen lights up pixels.

What is a pixel?Every photo on your phone is really a grid of tiny coloured squares. Zoom in far enough and you can count them.

Patan patola, both sides the same

Patola from Patan, Gujarat, is a double ikat made in silk. It is among the hardest textiles in the world to weave. Because both warp and weft carry the pattern, the design is equally sharp on both sides of the cloth. A single sari can take a family of weavers many months.

Only a few families still make true Patan patola. Centuries ago, patola cloth was traded to Southeast Asia, where it was treasured by royal families in Indonesia and copied by local weavers. Patola designs were even used on ceremonial clothes there. A single Patan patola sari can cost lakhs of rupees, because so many months of skilled work go into it.

Pochampally and Sambalpuri

Pochampally, a cluster of villages near Hyderabad, makes bold geometric ikat in cotton and silk, often with diamonds and zigzags. In 2005, Pochampally ikat became one of the first handlooms in India to get a GI tag.

In Odisha, Sambalpuri ikat, called bandha, often shows conch shells, wheels and flowers. Some weavers even weave in pictures of animals and temples, which takes careful thread-by-thread planning. In 2021, Pochampally was named one of the best tourism villages in the world by the UN World Tourism Organization.

Ikat around the world

In Japan, ikat is called kasuri. In Uzbekistan, bright silk ikat with cloud-like patterns was worn by rich merchants along the Silk Road. Double ikat is also made in Tenganan, a village in Bali, Indonesia, where a cloth called geringsing is believed to protect the wearer.

Designers today use ikat in clothes, cushions and even sneakers. And the strangest part? Laying out an ikat pattern is like making a grid of repeating shapes, a kind of maths you can explore in tessellations.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ikat is printed onto cloth after weaving.

Truth: In real ikat, the pattern is dyed onto the threads first, before any weaving happens. The design only appears as the threads are woven together. Cheap printed fabrics that copy the look are often sold as "ikat" too.

Try this

Wrap a few rubber bands tightly around a long white string or strip of cotton cloth. With an adult, dip it in water coloured with turmeric or beetroot juice and leave it for an hour. Remove the bands and lay several dyed strips side by side to see a pattern form.
