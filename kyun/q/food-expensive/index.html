What are the most expensive foods?

In January 2026, a single tuna fish sold in Tokyo for about ₹29 crore, more than the price of a small aeroplane.

The short answer

The most expensive foods are usually rare, slow to produce or need lots of handwork. Saffron, picked flower by flower, can cost more per gram than silver. White truffles, smelly fungi found underground by trained dogs, sell for thousands of dollars a kilo. Bluefin tuna, beluga caviar and perfect Japanese melons can sell for astonishing prices at auctions.

Some prices are everyday expensive, and some are one-time records made for fun, luck or fame. In January 2026, at Tokyo's first fish auction of the year, a 243 kg bluefin tuna sold for ¥510.3 million, about $3.2 million or roughly ₹29 crore.

Surprising but true

It takes about 150 crocus flowers, each picked and pulled apart by hand, to make just one gram of saffron.

A crore-rupee fish

Every January, Tokyo's Toyosu fish market holds its New Year auction. The top tuna is a prize that sushi restaurant owners compete to win, partly for luck and partly for the news headlines. The 2026 fish beat the old record of ¥333.6 million, set in 2019.

Bluefin tuna can grow longer than a car and swim across whole oceans. Overfishing made them scarce, and their fatty belly meat, called otoro, is prized for sushi. Ordinary bluefin sells for far less than the record fish, but it's still costly.

Red gold from a purple flower

Saffron comes from the crocus flower. Each flower has just three red threads, the stigmas, which must be picked by hand, early in the morning. It takes about 150 flowers for a single gram.

Iran grows most of the world's saffron. India's comes mainly from Pampore in Kashmir, where fields turn purple each autumn. Kashmir saffron got a GI tag in 2020. Because it's so costly, saffron is often faked with dyed corn silk or flower petals. Real saffron colours milk slowly and evenly, while fakes bleed colour fast.

Why is saffron so expensive?Each saffron thread is a tiny part of a purple flower, picked by hand, and you need about 1.5 lakh flowers for one kilogram.

Fungi, eggs and melons

Truffles are fungi that grow underground among tree roots. The rare white truffle from Alba in Italy can't be farmed reliably, so trained dogs sniff them out in autumn forests. In 2007, a 1.5 kg white truffle sold for $330,000 at a charity auction.

Beluga caviar, salted eggs of the beluga sturgeon from the Caspian Sea, is one of the priciest foods by weight. Wild beluga are now critically endangered, so most caviar comes from farms.

In Japan, fruit is often given as a gift, and perfect fruit sells for huge sums. In 2019, a pair of Yubari King melons sold at auction for ¥5 million. Growers in the town of Yubari, on the island of Hokkaido, judge each melon on its shape, its netted skin and its sweetness. Only the best go to the first auction of the season.

Why are things so pricey?

Part of the answer is supply and demand: when something is rare and lots of people want it, the price climbs. Part is labour. Vanilla orchids, grown mostly in Madagascar, must each be pollinated by hand with a small stick, because the bees that pollinate it in the wild live only in Mexico and Central America. A worker can hand-pollinate around a thousand flowers a day, and each flower opens for only one morning.

And part is fame. An auction record, a famous chef or a royal story can make a food more desirable than its taste alone would. Spices have always worked this way. Five hundred years ago, black pepper from Kerala was so prized in Europe that sailors risked their lives to reach India for it, which is why it was nicknamed black gold.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Expensive foods cost a lot because they taste much better than ordinary ones.

Truth: Price mostly depends on how rare something is, how hard it is to grow or collect, and how much people want it. Taste matters, but many costly foods are pricey because of the labour, the luck, or the fame attached to them.
