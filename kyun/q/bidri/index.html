What is Bidri work?

Bidri craftsmen turn a dull grey metal jet black using a paste made with soil from an old fort, while the silver patterns stay shining.

The short answer

Bidri is a metal craft from Bidar, a town in northern Karnataka. A craftsman casts an object, such as a box or a vase, from an alloy that is mostly zinc with a little copper. He then carves fine grooves into it and hammers in silver wire or thin silver sheet.

The final step is the magic one. The piece is rubbed with a warm paste made from special soil and salts. The grey metal turns a deep, velvety black, but the silver does not. The result is a shining silver pattern on a black background, like moonlight on a dark night.

Surprising but true

The blackening paste traditionally uses soil from parts of Bidar Fort that have stayed out of the sunlight for a long time. Craftsmen say ordinary soil does not work as well.

Born in a sultan's capital

Bidri takes its name from Bidar. In the 1400s, Bidar became the capital of the Bahmani Sultanate, a kingdom in the Deccan with close links to Persia. Craftsmen, poets and architects came from Persia and Central Asia to work there.

Metalworkers brought skills for inlaying one metal into another. Over time they developed this local style. The flowers, vines and geometric shapes on many Bidri pieces still look a lot like patterns in Persian carpets and tiles. Later rulers in the Deccan, and then the Nizams of Hyderabad, kept the craft going.

From casting to carving

First, the zinc alloy is melted and poured into a mould made of clay or sand to form the basic shape. After cooling, the surface is filed smooth. It is then dipped briefly in a solution that darkens it a little, so the craftsman can see his design as he scratches it on with a sharp tool.

Next comes the patient part. Using small chisels and a hammer, he cuts the pattern into the surface. Silver wire or sheet is laid into the grooves and hammered until it locks into place. One vase can take days or even weeks.

There are several styles of inlay. In one, called tarkashi, fine silver wire is laid into narrow lines. In another, flat pieces of silver sheet are set into wider areas to form leaves and petals. Some pieces combine both, with raised silver that you can feel under your fingers. A few old pieces also used gold or brass.

Soil from the old fort

When the inlay is done, the piece is buffed and gently warmed. Then the craftsman rubs it with a paste of water, salt and soil. Traditionally, the soil comes from old parts of Bidar Fort that have been shaded from sunlight for a long time.

The salts in the paste react with the zinc in the alloy and form a thin black layer on its surface. Silver reacts differently, so it stays bright. This is a chemical change, rather like rusting on iron, but this time the result is beautiful and protective. Finally, the piece is rubbed with oil to deepen the black.

Hookahs, boxes and buttons

In the past, Bidri was used for hookah bases, betel-leaf boxes, washbasins and trays for wealthy households. Many fine old pieces are now in museums, including the Salar Jung Museum in Hyderabad and the Victoria and Albert Museum in London.

Today, craftsmen make jewellery boxes, vases, bangles, buttons and paperweights. The work is slow and the number of skilled families is small, so the craft has had government support. Bidriware has a GI tag, a label that protects the name for objects made in the traditional region.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Bidri objects are black because they are painted.

Truth: There is no paint. The black is a thin layer formed when the zinc alloy reacts with a paste of special soil and salts, so it is part of the metal's surface.

Try this

Draw a pattern on black paper with a silver gel pen or a white crayon. Notice how much brighter the lines look against black than against white paper. Bidri craftsmen use the same contrast, with real silver on blackened metal.
