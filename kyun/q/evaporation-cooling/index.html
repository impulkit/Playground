Why does a clay pot keep water cool?

A clay matka keeps water cool on a hot afternoon with no electricity at all. It works by sweating, a little like you do.

The short answer

A clay pot, such as a matka or a surahi, is full of tiny holes, too small to see. When you fill it with water, a little seeps slowly through the walls to the outside surface. There it evaporates, turning from liquid into invisible water vapour.

Evaporation needs energy. The water takes that energy as heat from whatever it's touching, which is the pot. So as water evaporates from the outside, the pot loses heat, and so does the water inside. The water can end up several degrees cooler than the air around it.

It's the same reason a wet T-shirt feels cold in a breeze, and why your body sweats on a hot day.

Surprising but true

After the 2001 earthquake in Gujarat, a potter named Mansukhbhai Prajapati designed a whole fridge made of clay that runs without electricity.

Why dry air matters

Evaporation happens fastest when the air is dry and moving. Dry air has plenty of room for more water vapour, so water escapes into it quickly. A breeze carries the damp air away and brings fresh, dry air.

That's why a matka works brilliantly in the dry heat of a Rajasthan summer and much less well in humid Kolkata or Mumbai during the monsoon. When the air is already full of moisture, evaporation slows down, and so does the cooling. People in many places keep their pots in a shady, airy corner, sometimes on a stand with a wet cloth around the base.

A fridge made of clay

In January 2001, a huge earthquake struck Gujarat. According to Prajapati, a newspaper photo afterwards showed broken clay pots described as the "poor man's fridge". A potter from Morbi named Mansukhbhai Prajapati took that idea literally.

After years of experiments, he designed a cupboard-shaped refrigerator made almost entirely of clay. Water poured into a chamber at the top seeps down through the walls and evaporates, cooling the shelves inside. It keeps vegetables, fruit and milk fresher for longer, with no electricity. He named it Mitticool, from the Hindi word mitti, meaning soil or clay. India's National Innovation Foundation helped to bring his invention to wider attention.

Pot inside a pot

In northern Nigeria in the 1990s, a teacher named Mohammed Bah Abba popularised a simple cooler. Put one clay pot inside a bigger one, fill the gap between them with wet sand, and cover the top with a damp cloth. As water evaporates from the sand and the outer pot, the inner pot stays cool.

Farmers used these pot-in-pot coolers to keep tomatoes, peppers and other vegetables fresh for days or weeks longer, instead of hurrying to sell them before they spoiled. He won an international award for the idea in 2000. Similar coolers, often called zeer pots, are used in dry regions of Africa and Asia.

Desert coolers and swamp coolers

The same trick cools whole rooms. In north India, many homes use a "desert cooler", a box with a fan behind pads of wet straw or wood shavings. Hot, dry air is pulled through the wet pads, and the evaporating water cools it before it blows into the room. In older times, people hung mats of fragrant khus grass in doorways and splashed them with water.

In the dry south-western USA, in states like Arizona, people use almost identical machines called swamp coolers. They use far less electricity than an air conditioner, but like the matka, they only work well when the air is dry. In a humid climate, they mostly make the room damp.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A clay pot keeps water cool because clay is a naturally cold material.

Truth: Clay isn't cold on its own. A dry clay pot warms up like anything else. The cooling comes from water seeping through tiny holes in the clay and evaporating from the outside, carrying heat away.

Try this

Wrap a wet handkerchief around one plastic bottle of water and leave a second bottle dry. Put both in a shady, breezy spot for an hour, then feel which is cooler. Try again with the wet cloth in a closed cupboard and notice the difference moving air makes.
