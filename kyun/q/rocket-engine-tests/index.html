How is a rocket engine tested before it flies?

Before a rocket engine ever leaves the ground, engineers bolt it down, set it roaring at full power, and try very hard to make it go wrong.

The short answer

A rocket engine is tested by firing it on the ground, bolted to a huge steel frame called a test stand. Engineers run it for as long as it will burn in flight, or longer, while hundreds of sensors measure its push, heat, pressure and shaking.

Only if the engine behaves exactly as predicted is it allowed onto a real rocket. A failure on the ground is cheap and safe. A failure in the sky can lose the rocket and its satellite.

Surprising but true

When liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen burn together in a rocket engine, the main thing that comes out of the nozzle is hot water vapour.

Many kinds of test

A new engine design is tested again and again, sometimes hundreds of times. Engineers push it harder and longer than any flight will, to find its limits.

Then each engine that will actually fly gets a final firing, called a flight acceptance test. It checks that this one engine, built from these exact parts, works perfectly. After that, it is carefully inspected and joined to its rocket.

Tests can also include shaking the engine to copy the vibration of launch, and firing it in a chamber with almost no air, to copy space.

How does a rocket work?A rocket doesn't push against the air. It throws hot gas out of the back so hard that the gas pushes it forward.

Super-cold fuel, extra care

Engines that burn liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen are called cryogenic. Hydrogen only becomes liquid at about minus 253 °C, and oxygen at about minus 183 °C. Your fridge uses a gentler version of the same science.

At these temperatures, metals can turn brittle and seals can leak. So engineers check that every pipe, valve and pump survives the shock of the cold, then the blast of heat when the fuel burns. That is why cryogenic engines, which give a lot of thrust from each kilogram of fuel, are among the hardest to build.

India's test site

ISRO tests its engines at the ISRO Propulsion Complex at Mahendragiri, in Tamil Nadu. Its first home-made cryogenic stage flew successfully on GSLV-D5 in January 2014, after years of tests there.

Today, GSLV and the bigger LVM3 both use cryogenic stages, and every engine is fired at Mahendragiri before it goes to the launch pad at Sriharikota. Many of these rockets carry satellites like those for NavIC, India's own navigation system.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The white clouds pouring off a rocket before launch are smoke from a fire.

Truth: They are mostly water vapour from the air, turning into mist as it touches the super-cold fuel tanks. It is the same thing you see around an ice cube on a hot day, only much bigger.

Try this

Blow up a balloon, pinch the neck, then tape the balloon to a toy car and let go. Now hold the car still with your hand while the balloon empties. You can feel the push. That is a tiny version of a rocket engine on a test stand.
