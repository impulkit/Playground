How did Chola artists cast bronze gods?

Every Chola bronze began as a figure of beeswax, and every mould was smashed to get the statue out, so no two are exactly alike.

The short answer

The Cholas were a dynasty of kings who ruled much of south India from about the 9th to the 13th century. Under their rule, artists in Tamil Nadu made some of the most graceful metal statues in the world. They used a method called lost-wax casting.

First, the artist sculpted a god or goddess in wax. The wax figure was covered in layers of clay and heated. The wax melted and drained away, leaving a hollow mould. Molten metal was poured in. When it cooled, the clay was broken off to reveal the statue, which was then filed, polished and carved with fine details.

Surprising but true

A two-metre statue of Shiva as Nataraja, the cosmic dancer, stands outside CERN, the giant physics laboratory in Switzerland, a gift from India in 2004.

Why bronze gods were needed

Chola kings built huge stone temples, like the Brihadeeswarar temple in Thanjavur, as described in How were India's great temples built?. The main stone image in a temple stays fixed in its inner shrine.

But at festivals, gods were taken out in processions through the streets, riding on chariots and carried on shoulders. For that, temples needed images that were beautiful, strong and light enough to carry. Metal was perfect. Many Chola bronzes have rings or holes at the base, where poles could be fitted for carrying.

Step by step

The wax was beeswax mixed with resin from a tree, which made it firm enough to carve fine fingers and jewellery. Sculptors followed old texts that set out the proportions of each god, measured in units based on the width of a finger or the length of a face.

Next came the clay. The finest clay, often from the banks of the Kaveri river, was smeared over the wax in thin layers, then thicker ones, with channels left for the metal to flow in. After drying, the mould was heated so the wax melted out. Then the hot metal, an alloy mostly of copper, was poured in.

Shiva the cosmic dancer

The most famous Chola bronze is Shiva as Nataraja, Lord of Dance. He dances inside a ring of flames, one foot raised, his hair flying out to the sides. With one hand he holds a small drum, whose beat stands for creation. In another he holds fire, which stands for destruction. Under his foot is a small figure representing ignorance.

The pose matches movements in classical dance, and Bharatanatyam dancers still use some of the same gestures. Chola sculptors also made gentle figures of Parvati, Vishnu, Lakshmi, saints and poets.

Swamimalai today

In Swamimalai, a town near Kumbakonam in Tamil Nadu, families of craftsmen still cast bronzes the old way. The clay still comes from local river beds, and the moulds are still broken by hand. "Swamimalai Bronze Icons" now has a geographical indication, a legal label that protects the name for statues made there in the traditional way.

A small statue takes days. A big one can take months, from the first wax figure to the final polish.

Stolen and returned

Because Chola bronzes are so beautiful and valuable, some have been stolen from temples and smuggled abroad. In recent years museums and collectors in countries including Australia and the United States have returned stolen statues to India after investigators proved where they came from.

For the villages that lost them, these are not just pieces of art. They are gods who once belonged to a temple and its people, carried through the streets at festivals, exactly as the Cholas intended a thousand years ago.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Chola bronzes were made in identical batches from a reusable mould.

Truth: Each mould was broken open to release the statue, and each wax model was shaped by hand. Every traditional Chola bronze is one of a kind.

Try this

Press a small toy or a shell into a ball of playdough to make a mould, then lift it out carefully. Fill the hollow with wet plaster of Paris or thick flour paste, with an adult's help, and leave it to set overnight. Peel the dough away and you have a cast copy of your object.
