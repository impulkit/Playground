Why can music make you cry?

In 1963 Lata Mangeshkar sang one patriotic song in Delhi, and the Prime Minister of India was reported to have wept in front of the crowd.

The short answer

Music can make you cry because it reaches the same parts of the brain that handle emotions, memories and rewards. A song can remind you of a person or a place. It can also build up tension and then release it, in a way your brain finds deeply satisfying.

When that happens, your brain releases dopamine, a chemical linked to pleasure and wanting. Your heart rate and breathing shift. Some people get goosebumps or shivers. Some get a lump in the throat and wet eyes. Scientists are still working out why a mix of notes can do all this, but they agree the effect is real and measurable.

Surprising but true

Your brain can release the feel-good chemical dopamine a few seconds before your favourite moment in a song, because it knows what is coming.

The song that moved a Prime Minister

On 27 January 1963, a few months after India's war with China, Lata Mangeshkar sang Ae Mere Watan Ke Logon at a ceremony in Delhi. The song remembered soldiers who had died. Jawaharlal Nehru was in the audience, and people who were there said he had tears in his eyes.

That song still makes listeners cry more than 60 years later. Its slow melody, the meaning of its words and the memory of a hard time all work together. Your brain doesn't separate them neatly. It feels them as one wave. Even listeners born decades later, who never knew that war, often say the last verse is the one that gets them.

Your brain loves a cliffhanger

In 2011, scientists at McGill University in Montreal scanned people listening to their favourite pieces. Each person had picked music that gave them chills. The scans showed dopamine being released at the peak moments.

The clever finding was the timing. One part of the brain released dopamine during the build-up, a few seconds before the best bit. Another part released it at the peak itself. Your brain enjoys guessing what comes next, and it enjoys being right, or being surprised in a good way. Film composers use this when a quiet scene swells into a huge chord.

Tears, notes and tension

Some musical tricks seem to bring tears more often than others. One study in the 1990s asked music lovers to mark the exact moments that made them cry. Many of them landed on an appoggiatura, a note that clashes with the harmony for a moment and then slides into place. Others came with sudden key changes or a voice rising to a high, straining note.

Indian music has its own ideas about mood. Old texts describe rasas, the feelings an art form can stir, including karuna, the feeling of compassion and sorrow. Certain ragas are traditionally linked to evening longing or quiet dawn.

Why do sad songs feel good?

Here scientists disagree. Some think sad music lets us feel sorrow safely, without anything bad actually happening. Others think our body responds to imagined sadness with calming chemicals. Some studies find that people who feel a lot of empathy enjoy sad music most.

What nobody disputes is that crying at music is normal. Plenty of people never do, and that is normal too. Tears themselves come from small glands above each eye, as the story of how tears work explains.

Older than words

Every human culture studied so far makes music. A big study published in 2019 looked at songs from hundreds of societies. Lullabies, love songs and dance songs turned up almost everywhere, and listeners could often guess a song's purpose even in a language they didn't know.

A grandmother in Kerala and a mother in Kansas both soothe babies with slow, gentle songs. Long before a child understands words, music is already speaking to the parts of the brain that handle emotions.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Only sad songs make people cry.

Truth: People also cry at music that is joyful, grand or full of meaning, such as an anthem or a wedding song. Many scientists describe these tears as "being moved", a feeling that mixes happiness, warmth and connection.

Try this

Make a playlist of five songs that move you, in any language. Listen with eyes closed and raise a hand each time you feel a shiver or a lump in your throat. Compare notes with a friend or parent and see whether the same moments move you both.
