What happens to your body deep underwater?

Dive just 10 metres under the sea and the water presses on you twice as hard as the air does at the surface.

The short answer

Water is heavy. As you go down, more and more of it presses on you from above. Every 10 metres of sea water adds about the same push as the whole atmosphere above us. So at 10 metres, the pressure is double; at 20 metres, triple.

Your body is mostly water, which hardly squashes. But air does. The air in your lungs, ears, sinuses and even a diving mask gets squeezed into a smaller space as you sink. That is why your ears hurt at the bottom of a deep pool. Divers must manage this squeeze, along with cold, darkness and the risk of bubbles forming in their blood on the way up.

Surprising but true

The Bajau people of Southeast Asia, who have dived for food for generations, tend to have spleens about 50% larger than their neighbours, which may help them stay under longer.

Popping ears

The first thing most swimmers notice is pain in their ears. Behind each eardrum is a small pocket of air. As water pressure rises, it pushes the eardrum inward. Divers fix this by "equalising": pinching their nose and gently blowing, or swallowing, to push air up a narrow tube from the throat into the middle ear.

It is the same trick that helps on a plane coming in to land. Divers equalise often on the way down, before it hurts. Forcing it or diving with a blocked nose from a cold can damage the ears.

Your body's diving switch

Humans have a built-in reaction called the mammalian dive reflex. When your face touches cold water and you hold your breath, your heart slows down. Blood vessels in your arms and legs narrow, keeping more blood and oxygen for the heart and brain.

Seals, whales and dolphins have a far stronger version. The blue whale can stay under for many minutes, while some beaked whales can dive for over two hours. In humans the reflex is gentler, but trained free divers, who dive on a single breath, can hold their breath for several minutes.

Divers without tanks

People have dived for food and treasure for thousands of years. In the Gulf of Mannar, between India and Sri Lanka, divers once collected pearls from oyster beds on a single breath. In Japan and Korea, women divers called ama and haenyeo still gather shellfish and seaweed, some well into their seventies.

The Bajau, a sea-going people in Indonesia, the Philippines and Malaysia, spend hours a day in the water. A 2018 study found that their spleens are much larger than those of nearby farming people. The spleen stores red blood cells and squeezes them into the blood during a dive, giving an extra boost of oxygen.

Bubbles and the bends

Breathing compressed air from a tank lets a scuba diver stay down much longer. But under pressure, extra nitrogen from the air dissolves into the blood and body tissues, like gas in a sealed bottle of soda. If the diver rises too fast, the pressure drops and the nitrogen forms bubbles, just as when the soda is opened.

Those bubbles can cause pain in the joints, dizziness and worse. This is decompression sickness, or the bends. It became famous in the 1870s among workers digging foundations for the Brooklyn Bridge in New York, who worked in pressurised chambers under the river and then came up too quickly. Today, divers follow tables or dive computers that tell them how slowly to rise, sometimes pausing on the way up.

Too deep for people

Below about 40 metres, breathing ordinary air can make divers feel drunk and confused, an effect called nitrogen narcosis. Deeper still, special gas mixtures are needed. At the bottom of the Mariana Trench, almost 11 km down, the pressure is more than 1,000 times that at the surface.

Only a few people have been there, sealed inside thick metal submarines built to resist the crush. The first were Jacques Piccard and Don Walsh in 1960. Their dive to the bottom took almost five hours.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Divers breathe pure oxygen from their tanks.

Truth: Most recreational scuba tanks hold ordinary compressed air, about 21% oxygen. Pure oxygen becomes poisonous at depth. Divers who go very deep breathe special mixtures that may include helium.

Try this

In a full bucket or bathtub, push an empty plastic bottle, cap tightly closed, down into the water. Feel how hard the water pushes it back up. Then squeeze a soft closed bottle at the surface and notice how the air inside resists. That push is the same pressure divers feel.
