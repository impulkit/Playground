Who were the women who did NASA's maths?

Before John Glenn would fly into orbit in 1962, he asked for one person to check the computer's numbers by hand. That person was Katherine Johnson.

The short answer

During and after the Second World War, an American aircraft lab called Langley, in Hampton, Virginia, hired hundreds of women to do maths. They worked out how air flowed over wings, how planes behaved and later how rockets should fly. Their job title was "computer".

Some of them were Black women. Because Virginia's laws kept Black and white people apart, they worked in a separate office, the West Area Computing unit, and had to use separate dining tables and toilets. Despite that, their work helped send Americans into orbit and to the Moon.

The 2016 book and film Hidden Figures told the story of three of them: Katherine Johnson, Dorothy Vaughan and Mary Jackson.

Surprising but true

In the 1940s and 1950s, "computer" was a job title. It meant a person, often a woman, who did difficult calculations by hand.

Doing maths by hand

In the 1940s, there were no laptops and few electronic computers. Engineers testing a new plane in a wind tunnel came back with pages of measurements. Turning them into useful answers took huge amounts of arithmetic.

The human computers did it with pencils, paper, slide rules and noisy desk calculating machines. One problem could take days. A single mistake could mean a plane design that did not fly, so the work had to be exact. Many of these women had trained as maths teachers, because few other jobs were open to them.

Dorothy Vaughan, the leader

Dorothy Vaughan joined Langley in 1943. In 1949, she became the head of the West Area Computers, one of the first Black supervisors there.

When electronic computers arrived in the late 1950s, she saw they would replace human calculation. Instead of waiting to lose her job, she taught herself and her team a programming language called FORTRAN. Many of her computers became some of NASA's early programmers. She is a good example of someone who watched the future coming and walked towards it.

Mary Jackson, the engineer

Mary Jackson started as a computer in 1951. She was invited to work in a wind tunnel team, and her boss encouraged her to train as an engineer. To do that, she needed courses held at a whites-only high school. She asked the City of Hampton for special permission to attend, and got it.

In 1958, she became NASA's first Black female engineer. Later in her career, she worked to help other women and minorities get hired and promoted. In 2020, NASA renamed its headquarters building in Washington, DC, after her.

Katherine Johnson and John Glenn

Katherine Johnson was so good at maths that she started high school at age 10. At Langley she worked on flight paths, calculating exactly where a spacecraft would go and land.

In 1962, the astronaut John Glenn was about to become the first American to orbit Earth. NASA had begun using new electronic computers to plan the flight. Glenn did not fully trust them. He asked for Johnson to check the numbers by hand. She did, matching the machine's results, and Glenn flew.

She later worked on calculations for the Apollo 11 Moon landing. In 2015, she received the Presidential Medal of Freedom, and she lived to 101.

Out of the shadows

For decades, these women were barely mentioned in histories of the space race. That changed when Margot Lee Shetterly, whose father worked at Langley, wrote Hidden Figures. In 2019, the street outside NASA headquarters was renamed Hidden Figures Way.

There are similar stories in India. When ISRO's Mars Orbiter Mission reached Mars in 2014, photos of women scientists celebrating in saris went round the world. Women have since led major Indian missions, including as project director of Chandrayaan-2. And the machines that replaced the human computers began as giant rooms full of glowing valves, as you can see in the story of the first computers.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The early space programme was the work of a few famous astronauts and engineers.

Truth: Thousands of people made it work, including hundreds of women "computers". Some of them, like Katherine Johnson, did calculations that astronauts' lives depended on, but their names were almost unknown for decades.

Try this

Time yourself adding up a column of ten three-digit numbers by hand, then check your answer with a calculator. Try a second column and see if you get faster. Langley's human computers did thousands of steps like this, and their answers had to be exactly right.
