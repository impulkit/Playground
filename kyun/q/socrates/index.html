Who was Socrates, the man who only asked questions?

Socrates never wrote a single book, yet 2,400 years after his death, teachers still name a way of thinking after him.

The short answer

Socrates was a philosopher who lived in Athens, in ancient Greece, about 2,400 years ago. He spent his days in the marketplace asking people questions about big ideas: What is justice? What is courage? How should a good person live?

He never wrote anything down and never ran a school. Instead, he talked. His questions often showed that important, confident people did not understand things as well as they thought. Many young Athenians loved him for it, and many powerful ones disliked him. In 399 BCE he was put on trial, found guilty and sentenced to death. His student Plato wrote down his conversations, and through them Socrates became one of the most famous thinkers in history.

Surprising but true

Socrates compared himself to a midwife, like his mother. He said he didn't give people ideas but helped them bring out ideas already inside them.

A stonemason's son

Socrates was born around 470 BCE. Ancient writers say his father was a stonemason and his mother a midwife. He fought as a foot soldier in Athens' wars and was said to be very brave and very tough, walking barefoot even in winter.

He was famous for not caring about money or fine clothes. He was also, according to his friends, rather ugly, with a snub nose and bulging eyes. That made his charm in conversation even more surprising.

The question game

Here is how a talk with Socrates might go. Someone says courage means never running away. Socrates asks whether a soldier who retreats to trick the enemy and then wins is a coward. The person admits he is not, and tries again. Socrates asks more questions, and the definition keeps breaking.

This back-and-forth is now called the Socratic method. Socrates said he was like his mother, a midwife: he didn't give people ideas, but helped them give birth to their own. Teachers and law schools still use the method today, and it is a big part of what philosophy means.

"The wisest man in Athens"

A friend of Socrates once asked the famous oracle at Delphi, a temple where people went to ask the gods questions, whether anyone was wiser than Socrates. The answer came back: no one.

Socrates was puzzled, because he didn't think he was wise. So he went around questioning politicians, poets and craftsmen. He found that they believed they knew things they didn't. He decided he was wiser only in one way: he knew how little he knew.

The trial

In 399 BCE, Socrates was accused of not respecting the city's gods and of corrupting the young. He was tried in front of a jury of about 500 ordinary citizens, as was normal in Athens. Some Athenians were angry because a few of his former followers had sided with the city's enemies or with cruel rulers.

In Plato's account, Socrates didn't beg for mercy. He said that the unexamined life is not worth living. A majority found him guilty and voted for death. His friends offered to help him escape, but he refused, saying he would obey the laws of the city.

A cup of hemlock

Socrates was given a drink made from a poisonous plant called hemlock. Plato's book Phaedo describes his last day, spent calmly talking with friends about whether the soul lives on after death.

In 1787, the French artist Jacques-Louis David painted the scene, with Socrates reaching for the cup while pointing upwards and still talking. The painting hangs in the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York. People still argue about the trial. Some see it as a warning about how even a democracy can punish someone for asking awkward questions, a debate that connects to free speech today.

Socrates and other great teachers

Socrates was born about ten years after Confucius died in China, and lived in roughly the same era as the Buddha in India. Like them, he taught by talking, and his students wrote his ideas down. Plato went on to teach Aristotle, who later taught Alexander the Great, so the questions asked in an Athenian marketplace travelled far.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Socrates said, "I know that I know nothing."

Truth: Those exact words don't appear in Plato's writings. In Plato's account of his trial, Socrates says something gentler, roughly that he doesn't think he knows what he doesn't know. The famous one-line version came later.
