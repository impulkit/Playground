Are emojis a language?

In 2015, the Word of the Year chosen by Oxford Dictionaries wasn't a word at all. It was a yellow face crying with laughter.

The short answer

Not quite. Emojis are a powerful way to add feeling to writing, but most linguists say they are not a language on their own. A language needs a grammar, a shared way to combine symbols into new meanings that everyone understands. Emojis don't have one. Try telling a friend exactly what you did last Tuesday using emojis alone, and you'll soon see the problem.

Emojis work more like the gestures, faces and tone of voice we use when we talk. A laughing face after a sentence tells the reader it's a joke, the way a laugh would in person. Text messages have no tone of voice, and emojis help fill that gap.

Surprising but true

The 176 emojis designed for Japanese phones in 1999 are now part of the collection of the Museum of Modern Art in New York.

Born in Japan

The word "emoji" is Japanese: e means picture and moji means character. In 1999, a designer named Shigetaka Kurita created a set of 176 simple pictures, each just 12 pixels square, for a Japanese mobile internet service. They included weather symbols, hearts and faces. A Japanese phone company had released an earlier set in 1997, but Kurita's became the most famous. In 2016, the Museum of Modern Art in New York added his 176 emojis to its collection.

Emojis went global in 2010, when they were added to Unicode, the system that lets computers everywhere show the same letters and symbols. The next year, Apple added an emoji keyboard to the iPhone, and soon emojis were on smartphones everywhere.

Who decides which emojis exist?

New emojis are approved by the Unicode Consortium, a non-profit group of technology companies and experts based in California. Anyone can send in a proposal, explaining why an emoji is needed and how often people would use it. Each year a few dozen new ones are accepted, and phone companies draw their own versions.

In 2019, the new emojis included a diya lamp, a sari, an auto rickshaw and a Hindu temple. People in India had long asked for symbols from their daily lives. Emojis have also become more inclusive over time, with different skin tones, wheelchairs, hearing aids and many kinds of families.

Pictures that need words

Writing has used pictures before. Egyptian hieroglyphs began as pictures, but scribes soon used many of them to stand for sounds, because pictures alone couldn't say everything. Chinese characters also began partly as pictures and became a full writing system. Emojis have never made that leap, and most people don't want them to.

Emojis can be misunderstood, too. Different phones draw the same emoji differently, so a face that looks cheerful on one screen can look nervous on another. Older and younger people may read the same symbol in different ways, just as they do with slang.

Grammar, almost

People do follow some patterns. Researchers who study emoji use have noticed that people often put them at the end of a sentence, like punctuation, and repeat them for emphasis. A string of emojis can tell a tiny story, but only if both people already share the context.

Some linguists compare emojis with sign language to show what they lack. Sign languages have a full grammar, a word order and the power to make endless new sentences. Others argue that emojis are the biggest change to everyday writing in years, because they let a two-word message carry a smile. Both points can be true at once.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Emoji comes from the English word "emotion".

Truth: Emoji is a Japanese word made of e (picture) and moji (character). The likeness to "emotion" is a happy accident.

Try this

Draw a famous short story, like the thirsty crow and the pot of water, using only small emoji-style pictures, with no words at all. Give it to a friend and see if they can tell the story back. Then try to describe what you did last Tuesday in pictures alone. Which was harder, and why?
