How many folds would reach the Moon?

Fold an ordinary sheet of paper in half 42 times, and, in theory, the stack would be thick enough to reach the Moon.

The short answer

Every time you fold paper in half, its thickness doubles. A sheet about 0.1 mm thick becomes 0.2 mm, then 0.4 mm, then 0.8 mm. At first nothing much seems to happen. But doubling speeds up like a rolling snowball.

After 42 folds, the stack would be about 440,000 km thick, more than the 384,400 km between Earth and the Moon. The catch is that you can't really do it. Paper gets too thick, too small and too stiff to fold long before then. It's a thought experiment, a way of showing just how powerful doubling is.

Surprising but true

Just 23 folds would make a stack about 840 m tall, taller than the Burj Khalifa, the world's tallest building.

The climb, fold by fold

Here is the journey, using 0.1 mm paper, with a few landmarks along the way.

After 7 folds, the stack is about 1.3 cm thick, as thick as a small notebook. After 10 folds, it is about 10 cm, the height of a coffee mug. At 17 folds, it reaches about 13 m, as tall as a four-storey building.

At 23 folds, it is about 840 m, taller than the Burj Khalifa in Dubai. At 27 folds, about 13 km, higher than planes fly and higher than Everest. At 30 folds, it passes 100 km and enters space. At 42, it reaches the Moon. And at 51 folds, it would pass the Sun, about 150 million km away.

Why doubling is so sneaky

Most things in daily life grow by adding. If you add 1 cm each time, 42 steps give you 42 cm. Doubling is different. Each step adds as much as all the steps before it put together. Mathematicians call this exponential growth, and they write it using exponents: 2 to the power 42 is about 4.4 trillion.

The same trick appears in the old legend of rice on a chessboard, where doubling one grain 63 times needs more rice than the world grows in centuries.

The girl who folded 12 times

For years, people said you could not fold paper more than seven or eight times. In 2002, Britney Gallivan, a high-school student in California, decided to test it. She worked out a mathematical formula showing how much paper each fold uses up, because the curved edge of each fold eats into the length.

Her answer: to fold 12 times in one direction, she needed a strip of paper more than a kilometre long. She got one, and walked up and down a shopping centre folding it, until she had 12 folds. In 2011, students at a school in Massachusetts used a giant roll of toilet paper and managed 13.

Why you can't keep going

Each fold halves the area of the paper as it doubles the thickness. After 10 folds, a sheet the size of a cricket field would be folded into a stack the size of a large rug, 1,024 layers thick. Long before 42 folds, the stack would be a tall, thin tower too stiff to bend. Each new fold also needs much more force, because you are bending twice as many layers as before.

You would also need a sheet of paper so big it is impossible. To reach 42 folds, the original sheet would need to be enormous, far larger than any country.

Folding for real

Folding matters outside puzzles. Engineers use origami, the art of paper folding, to pack solar panels, satellite antennae and even the mirror of the James Webb Space Telescope into rockets. Once in space, they unfold to full size. And the Moon you would reach with 42 folds has been visited for real: between 1969 and 1972, twelve astronauts walked on it during NASA's Apollo missions.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Nobody can fold a sheet of paper in half more than seven times.

Truth: With an ordinary sheet, seven is about the limit. But in 2002 a high-school student folded a very long strip 12 times, and in 2011 a school team managed 13 using a giant roll of toilet paper.

Try this

Take a sheet of A4 paper and fold it in half as many times as you can. Most people stop at 6 or 7. Then try a large sheet of thin newspaper or tissue paper. Count the layers after each fold, which go 2, 4, 8, 16 and so on.
