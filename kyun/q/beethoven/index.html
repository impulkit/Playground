How did Beethoven compose when he was deaf?

By the time he wrote his most famous symphony, Beethoven could hardly hear an orchestra at all. He heard it in his head instead.

The short answer

Beethoven composed when he was deaf by hearing the music in his mind. By the time his hearing failed, he had spent more than twenty years playing, studying and writing music. He knew exactly how each note and each instrument would sound, the way you can "hear" a familiar song in your head without any sound at all.

He worked out his ideas in sketchbooks, writing and rewriting themes until they were right. Some of his greatest works, including the Ninth Symphony, were written when he could hardly hear anything. He could not hear them played, but he knew how they would sound.

Surprising but true

Visitors talked to the deaf Beethoven by writing in notebooks, and about 140 of these conversation books survive.

A star pianist

Ludwig van Beethoven was born in December 1770 in Bonn, in today's Germany. His father, a singer, pushed him hard, hoping he would become a child star like Mozart.

In 1792, Beethoven moved to Vienna, the music capital of Europe, and studied for a while with the composer Joseph Haydn. He became famous as a dazzling pianist who could make up music on the spot. Rich music lovers paid him to compose. His music was often bold and stormy, and it pushed the pianos of the day to their limits.

The sounds fade

In his late twenties, Beethoven began to hear buzzing and whistling in his ears, and to lose his hearing. It got slowly worse for the rest of his life. Doctors tried many treatments, but none worked. Scientists still don't know what caused it.

For a musician, this was terrifying. In 1802, staying in the village of Heiligenstadt near Vienna, he wrote a letter to his brothers about his despair. But in the same letter, he said he could not leave the world until he had written all the music he felt inside him.

Ear trumpets and conversation books

For a time, Beethoven used ear trumpets, metal horns held to the ear to gather sound. As his hearing got worse, they stopped helping.

From 1818, friends and visitors talked to him by writing in notebooks, and he answered aloud. About 140 of these conversation books survive. They show him chatting about music, money, food and his nephew. A story says he also sawed the legs off a piano so he could feel its vibrations through the floor, but historians are unsure how true some of these tales are. The Ninth Symphony

On 7 May 1824, Beethoven's Ninth Symphony was first performed in Vienna. For the last part, he did something new: he added singers and a choir to a symphony, singing a poem about joy and friendship.

Beethoven stood on stage beside the conductor, beating time. People who were there later said that when the music ended, he was still facing the orchestra. One of the singers turned him round to see the crowd clapping and waving their hats.

A tune for a continent

The melody from that last part, known as "Ode to Joy", is now the anthem of the European Union. His Fifth Symphony starts with four famous notes, "da-da-da-DUM", which may be the best-known opening in classical music.

Beethoven died in Vienna in 1827. Thousands of people are said to have followed his funeral procession. His life is often held up as a story of courage. It is also a story about how much of music happens in the mind, where he kept hearing it long after his ears had stopped.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Beethoven was born deaf.

Truth: Beethoven could hear well as a child and young man, and became famous as a brilliant pianist. His hearing began to fail in his late twenties and got worse over many years.

Try this

Put your ear flat against a wooden table and gently cover your other ear. Ask a friend to scratch or tap softly on the far end of the table. You will hear it much more clearly through the wood than through the air. Sound can travel through solid things, including your own skull.
