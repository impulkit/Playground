What was the Cambrian explosion?

About 540 million years ago, the seas filled with animals that had eyes, legs, shells and claws, and one of them had five eyes and a nose like a vacuum hose.

The short answer

The Cambrian explosion was a burst of new animal life in the seas, starting about 539 million years ago. Over roughly 20 million years, many of the main animal groups we know today appear in the fossil record for the first time. Before it, most living things were small and soft. After it, the seas held animals with shells, legs, spines, claws, guts and, for the first time, sharp eyes.

The word "explosion" sounds sudden, but it was only quick by the standards of Earth's history. Twenty million years is about 60 times longer than our own species has existed. Scientists still argue about what triggered it.

Surprising but true

A Cambrian animal called Hallucigenia was first drawn upside down and back to front. Scientists mistook its spines for legs and its head for its tail.

Before the explosion

Life on Earth is far older than the Cambrian. For around 3 billion years, almost all living things were microscopic. Then, a little before the Cambrian, strange soft creatures appeared, called the Ediacaran organisms. Some looked like ribbed leaves, quilted mats or flat discs, and scientists still debate what many of them were.

In the Cambrian, things changed fast. Animals began making hard shells and skeletons. They burrowed into the sea floor, swam in open water and hunted each other. The burrows themselves are a clue. Rocks from before the Cambrian show mostly flat, simple trails on the surface of the mud. Cambrian rocks are churned with deep, twisting tunnels, a sign that animals had learned to dig for food and shelter.

A strange zoo

In 1909, the American scientist Charles Walcott, head of the Smithsonian Institution, found a layer of rock high in the Rocky Mountains of Canada. It was the Burgess Shale, and it had preserved soft-bodied animals in detail, down to guts and gills.

The animals were unlike anything alive today. Opabinia had five eyes on stalks and a long, flexible nose with a claw on the end. Anomalocaris, perhaps up to about half a metre long, had two spiny grabbing arms and a round mouth, and was one of the top hunters of its day. Pikaia, a small swimming worm-like animal, may be an early relative of all animals with backbones, including you.

In 1984, another rich site was found at Chengjiang in China, slightly older and just as detailed. It is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Why did it happen?

There is no single answer. Many scientists think rising oxygen in the seas allowed bigger, more active animals. Others point to changes in ocean chemistry that made it easier to build shells.

Another idea focuses on eyes. Once some animals could see, hunting became much more effective. Prey animals that grew armour, spines or speed survived better, and predators improved in turn. Early animals such as trilobites had well-developed eyes. Most likely, several of these causes worked together.

The Cambrian in India

Cambrian rocks are found in the Spiti valley in Himachal Pradesh, high in the Himalaya. Geologists have collected trilobites there, especially around the Parahio valley.

These rocks formed on an ancient sea floor, long before India collided with Asia and pushed the Himalaya skyward. That means fossils of sea creatures now sit at heights of several kilometres, in one of the coldest, driest places in India. Reaching them can mean days of trekking at heights of around 4,000 m, and fossil hunters have to work fast in the short summer before snow returns. Fossils from Spiti also help geologists work out where ancient India sat on the globe, and which other lands it was close to back then.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In the Cambrian explosion, animals appeared out of nowhere overnight.

Truth: It took tens of millions of years, and simpler animals already lived in the seas before it. What changed was that many animals grew hard parts, such as shells and spines, which fossilise more easily, so they suddenly show up much more in rocks.

Try this

Draw a made-up Cambrian animal using parts from the real ones, such as five eyes like Opabinia, a spiny back like Hallucigenia and grabbing arms like Anomalocaris. Then show it to a friend upside down and see if they can work out which way up it goes. It's harder than it sounds, which is exactly the problem scientists faced.
