Why does a cake rise?

A cake rises because millions of tiny gas bubbles swell in the oven, and then the batter sets around them like a sponge.

The short answer

A cake is mostly bubbles. When you stir batter, you trap air in it. Then a raising agent, usually baking powder or baking soda, makes extra bubbles of carbon dioxide gas.

In a hot oven, every bubble swells as the gas inside expands, and water turns into steam that pushes outwards too. The batter puffs up. Then the heat sets the eggs and flour into a firm, spongy wall around each bubble, so the cake stays tall after it cools. A good cake is really a frozen foam.

Surprising but true

The yeast that makes bread rise is a living fungus, a tiny cousin of the mushrooms you might see after the monsoon.

Where the bubbles come from

There are three main ways to get gas into dough or batter. The first is pure muscle. Beating butter and sugar together, or whisking eggs, folds in tiny air pockets.

The second is chemistry. Baking soda reacts with something sour, like buttermilk, curd or lemon, and fizzes out carbon dioxide. Baking powder mixes soda with a dry acid, so it fizzes as soon as it gets wet and again when it gets hot.

The third is biology. Yeast feeds on sugar in bread dough and breathes out carbon dioxide, slowly, over an hour or two. That slow rise is a kind of fermentation, and it adds flavour too.

The fungus in your bread

Yeast is a living single-celled fungus, so tiny that a teaspoon of dried yeast holds billions of cells. It is related to the mushrooms that pop up on lawns after rain. Bakers in ancient Egypt were already making raised bread thousands of years ago, long before anyone knew what yeast was.

Are mushrooms plants?Mushrooms grow out of the ground like plants, but their family tree puts them closer to you than to a mango tree.

In India, naan and pav are raised with yeast. Dhokla batter is often lifted with fruit salt, a packet of soda mixed with citric acid that fizzes the moment it meets water.

Building the walls

Gas alone would just escape. To hold it, a cake needs walls that can stretch and then set. Wheat flour forms gluten, a stretchy net of proteins, when it gets wet and mixed. Eggs add more protein, and flour starch swells and thickens.

As the cake bakes, the walls stiffen. That is why a cake that comes out too early can sink in the middle: the bubbles cool and shrink before the walls are strong enough to hold them up. Bread has much more gluten than cake, which is why it is chewy and a cake crumbles.

A powder in a tin

For most of history, bread rose only with yeast or with a bit of old, sour dough saved from the last batch. In 1843 an English chemist called Alfred Bird made an early baking powder so that his wife, who could not eat yeast, could still enjoy raised bread. Chemists in the United States improved the recipe in the following decades, and cheap tins of baking powder changed home baking on both sides of the Atlantic.

Baking up a mountain

Cakes behave differently at height. In Denver, a mile above the sea, or in Shimla, the air pressure is lower, so bubbles grow bigger and faster and can burst before the cake sets. Bakers in high places often use a little less baking powder and a slightly hotter oven. It is a neat reminder that a bubble is always fighting the air pressing on it from outside.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Baking soda and baking powder are the same thing.

Truth: Baking soda is pure sodium bicarbonate. It needs something sour, like curd or lemon juice, to make gas. Baking powder already contains a dry acid mixed in, so it only needs water and heat.

Try this

Put two spoons of baking soda inside a balloon. Pour a little vinegar into an empty plastic bottle, stretch the balloon over the neck, then lift it so the powder falls in. The gas that blows up the balloon is the same gas that lifts a cake.
