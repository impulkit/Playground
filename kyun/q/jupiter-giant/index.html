How big is Jupiter?

Jupiter is so big that all the other planets in the Solar System could fit inside it, with room to spare.

The short answer

Jupiter is the biggest planet by far. It is about 140,000 km wide, 11 times wider than Earth. About 1,300 Earths could fit inside it. It has more than twice the mass of all the other planets added together.

Jupiter is a gas giant. It has no solid ground to stand on. It is mostly hydrogen and helium, like the Sun, getting thicker and hotter the deeper you go. The coloured stripes we see are bands of clouds, made of ammonia and other chemicals, racing around the planet in opposite directions.

Surprising but true

Jupiter spins faster than any other planet. A whole day there lasts less than 10 hours, even though it is 11 times wider than Earth.

The size of it

Big numbers are easier with a model. If Earth were a pea, Jupiter would be a grapefruit and the Sun would be taller than a kitchen table. Scale models like this show why Jupiter dominates the planets.

Its gravity is strong too. If you could stand at the top of its clouds, you would weigh about two and a half times what you weigh on Earth. A child who weighs 30 kg at home would feel like 75 kg.

And yet it spins in under ten hours. That fast spin makes Jupiter bulge at its middle, so it is noticeably wider around its equator than from pole to pole.

The Great Red Spot

Jupiter's most famous feature is a giant storm called the Great Red Spot. It has been watched continuously since the 1830s and may be much older. Its winds blow at hundreds of kilometres an hour.

A hundred years ago, the spot was wide enough to fit about three Earths. It has been shrinking, and today it is only a little wider than one Earth. Nobody knows whether it will one day disappear.

A world of moons

In January 1610, Galileo pointed his small telescope at Jupiter and saw four tiny "stars" beside it. Night after night, they moved. He realised they were moons going around Jupiter. It was proof that not everything in the sky goes around Earth, an idea that got him into serious trouble.

Today more than 90 moons are known. The four big ones Galileo found are named Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto. Ganymede is bigger than the planet Mercury. Io is covered in erupting volcanoes, and icy Europa may hide a salty ocean under its crust.

Who was Galileo?In January 1610, an Italian professor pointed a home-made telescope at Jupiter and saw four tiny dots that changed how people saw the universe.

The comet that hit Jupiter

In July 1994, people around the world watched a comet crash into Jupiter. Comet Shoemaker-Levy 9 had been torn into more than 20 pieces by Jupiter's gravity. One after another, they slammed into the planet's clouds. The dark marks they left behind were bigger than Earth and lasted for weeks.

Jupiter's gravity pulls on passing comets and asteroids all the time. Some scientists think this protects Earth by sweeping them up. Others point out that Jupiter can also fling them towards the inner planets. The truth may be a bit of both.

Visiting the giant

Several spacecraft have flown past Jupiter, including the two Voyagers in 1979, which discovered its faint rings. NASA's Juno spacecraft arrived in 2016 and has been looping over its poles, seeing swirling storms that look like a painting. The European Space Agency's JUICE and NASA's Europa Clipper are on their way to study its icy moons.

In Hindi, Jupiter is Brihaspati or Guru, and Thursday is Guruvaar. It is often the brightest point in the night sky after the Moon and Venus. Through ordinary binoculars held steady, you can sometimes spot Galileo's four moons as tiny dots in a line.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Jupiter is a failed star that almost became a second Sun.

Truth: Jupiter is made of the same gases as stars, but it is nowhere near big enough. It would need about 80 times more mass before the squeezing in its core could start the nuclear fusion that makes a star shine.
