How does a touchscreen feel your finger?

Your phone screen doesn't feel the push of your finger. It feels the tiny electric charge your body carries.

The short answer

Most phones and tablets use capacitive touchscreens. Just under the glass is an invisible grid of very thin, see-through wires that carry a tiny electric charge, creating a weak electric field across the screen.

Your body conducts electricity. When your fingertip comes close to the glass, it draws away a little of that charge at the spot you touch. A small chip constantly measures the grid. It notices exactly where the charge has changed and works out the position of your finger, many times every second.

That's why a gentle tap works, and why a pencil or a gloved finger usually does nothing.

Surprising but true

One of the first touchscreens was invented in 1965 by E.A. Johnson in Britain, for air traffic controllers tracking planes.

An invisible grid

The wires in a touchscreen must carry electricity yet let light through, so you can still see the picture beneath. They're often made of indium tin oxide, a material that conducts electricity but is almost transparent when spread very thin.

The wires run in rows and columns, like a cricket scoreboard grid. Where a row crosses a column, they form a tiny sensor. The chip checks each crossing over and over. When your finger is near one, that crossing's electrical reading changes, and the chip knows which row and which column you're touching.

Because every crossing is checked separately, the screen can track several fingers at once. That's how you pinch to zoom.

Your body as part of the circuit

The trick relies on something called capacitance: the ability to store a little electric charge. Your body, mostly salty water, is good at this. It's related to the effect behind static electricity, when you get a small shock from a door handle.

The skin on your fingertip lets this effect pass through easily. Thick wool or leather gloves keep your finger too far away and don't conduct, so the screen ignores you. Touchscreen gloves have conductive threads woven into their tips.

Water confuses the screen too. A few raindrops can conduct electricity and act like extra fingers, which is why wet phones sometimes tap things by themselves.

The older kind

Before capacitive screens became common, many devices used resistive touchscreens. These have two flexible layers with a small gap between them. When you press, the top layer bends and touches the bottom one, completing a circuit at that spot.

Resistive screens work with any object, including a fingernail, a stylus or a gloved hand, but you have to press firmly. They were used on early handheld computers and still appear on some ticket machines, card machines and factory equipment. Some large screens instead use beams of invisible infrared light across their surface and sense where your finger breaks them.

Who invented them

In 1965, E.A. Johnson, an engineer at the Royal Radar Establishment in Malvern, England, described a capacitive touchscreen for air traffic controllers. It let them select planes on a radar screen with a finger.

In the early 1970s, engineers Frank Beck and Bent Stumpe at CERN, the European physics laboratory near Geneva, built capacitive touchscreens to control a particle accelerator. CERN is also where the World Wide Web was invented.

Touchscreens spread to ATMs, ticket machines and handheld organisers. The iPhone in 2007 made multi-touch screens part of everyday life.

Seeing and touching together

The touch layer is only part of the screen. Underneath, millions of tiny coloured dots called pixels make the image. The phone matches where you touch with what's displayed at that spot, so tapping an icon opens the right app.

Some screens can now sense how hard you press, or notice your finger hovering just above the glass. Many phones vibrate briefly when you tap, so the flat glass feels a little like a real button.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A touchscreen works by sensing how hard you press.

Truth: Most phone and tablet screens sense the electrical effect of your finger, not pressure. A light touch works fine. Older resistive screens, still found on some machines, do need a firm press.

Try this

With an adult's permission, open a drawing app on a phone or tablet and test different objects on the screen, such as your fingertip, your knuckle, a pencil, a spoon, a slice of fruit, a plastic ruler. Make a table of which work and which don't, and look for what the working ones have in common.
