How was the Sydney Opera House built?

The Sydney Opera House was meant to take four years and cost 7 million dollars. It took fourteen years and cost about 102 million.

The short answer

The Sydney Opera House sits on a point of land sticking into Sydney Harbour in Australia. In 1957, a young Danish architect named Jørn Utzon won a competition to design it. His drawings showed a cluster of white shells, but no one, including Utzon, knew how to build them.

Work began in 1959. It took years of experiments, early computers, a bitter quarrel and a change of architect before the building opened on 20 October 1973. Today its shells are as famous a symbol of Australia as the kangaroo, and it hosts concerts, opera, ballet and plays almost every day of the year.

Surprising but true

Every curved shell on the roof is cut from the surface of the same imaginary sphere, like pieces of peel from one giant orange.

A drawing with no instructions

The New South Wales government held an open competition in 1956, and more than 200 architects entered. Utzon's design was more a sketch of an idea than a plan. It had huge curved roofs rising over two halls, all resting on a heavy stone platform.

The builders started on the platform before the roof was designed. That was a mistake. When the roof was finally worked out, it was so heavy that parts of the platform had to be pulled down and rebuilt with stronger columns.

The orange peel idea

For four years, Utzon and the engineering firm Ove Arup & Partners tried shape after shape. The shells Utzon had drawn were free curves, each one different. That would have needed thousands of special moulds, and no one could calculate whether they would stand. The team used some of the first computers ever put to work on a building to test the sums.

In about 1961, Utzon found the answer. If every shell was cut from the surface of one sphere, all the curves would match. The shells could be made from ribs of concrete cast in a few repeated moulds on the ground, then lifted by crane and bolted together. A bit of geometry had saved the whole project.

A million tiles

The shells are covered in just over a million ceramic tiles, made in Sweden and fitted in big panels. There are two kinds: shiny white and matt cream. Together they make the roof glow in sunshine and look softer on cloudy days.

The tallest shell reaches about 67 m above the harbour, roughly as high as a 22-storey building. The building rests on hundreds of concrete piers sunk into the harbour floor.

Under the largest shells is the Concert Hall, which seats more than 2,500 people. Its grand organ has about 10,000 pipes, and it took ten years to build. The smaller hall next door is used for opera and ballet, and there are several smaller theatres tucked underneath.

The architect who never saw it

As costs rose, a new state government cut off Utzon's payments and argued over his plans for the inside. In 1966 he resigned and left Australia. He never returned. A team of Australian architects finished the interiors, which ended up quite different from his designs.

Most of the money came from a special state lottery, so taxpayers paid relatively little. The final bill was about 102 million Australian dollars, nearly fifteen times the first estimate.

Utzon lived to see his work honoured. In 2003 he won the Pritzker Prize, the top award in architecture, and in 2007 the Opera House became a World Heritage Site. He died the next year, in Denmark, having seen photographs of his finished building but never the real thing. Many visitors who come for an opera or a ballet now walk first to the edge of the harbour to look up at the shells he imagined.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Opera House roof is painted white.

Truth: The roof is covered in just over a million tiles made in Sweden, in two shades, glossy white and matt cream. They are arranged in a chevron pattern, and their smooth glaze lets the rain wash them clean.

Try this

Peel an orange as carefully as you can into a few large curved pieces. Lay them on the table point up and lean them against each other in a row. You have made a small Sydney Opera House roof, built the same way. Every piece comes from one sphere.
