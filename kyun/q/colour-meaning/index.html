Do colours mean the same everywhere?

In much of India a bride wears red. In much of America she wears white. Same happy day, very different colours.

The short answer

No. The colours themselves are the same everywhere, because light and eyes work the same way for everyone. What people feel about a colour is learned, and it changes from place to place.

In much of India, red is the colour of weddings, festivals and good fortune, while white is often worn in mourning. In much of the USA and Europe, brides wear white and mourners wear black. A colour that means joy in one city can mean grief in another.

A few reactions may be shared more widely. Red catches the eye almost everywhere, which may be one reason so many warning signs and traffic lights use it. Scientists are still arguing about how much of this is nature and how much is culture.

Surprising but true

About a hundred years ago, an American magazine for baby-clothes shops advised pink for boys and blue for girls.

Red for a bride, white for a bride

In many Hindu weddings, the bride wears a red or deep pink sari or lehenga, and red sindoor in the parting of her hair has long been a sign of marriage. Red is linked with good luck, energy and new beginnings. In China too, red is the colour of weddings, and New Year money is given in red envelopes.

White wedding dresses are newer than many people think. In 1840, Queen Victoria of Britain married in white, and wealthy brides in Britain and America copied her. Over the next hundred years, white became the standard in the West. Before that, many brides simply wore their best dress, whatever its colour, and wore it again afterwards.

Colours of grief and of prayer

In many Indian families, white is worn after a death, and widows in some communities traditionally dressed in plain white. In the USA, black is the usual colour at funerals, a custom that came from Europe.

Saffron carries deep meaning in India. Many Hindu and Buddhist monks wear saffron, ochre or maroon robes, linked with giving up worldly things. On India's national flag, the government describes the saffron band as standing for strength and courage. Green is closely linked with Islam and appears on the flags of many Muslim-majority countries. During Holi, people throw every colour at once, and nobody worries about what any single one means.

Pink for boys

Some colour rules that feel ancient are surprisingly young. In the early 1900s, American babies were mostly dressed in white, which could be boiled and bleached clean. When pastel colours became fashionable, there was no fixed rule. In 1918, a trade magazine for baby-clothes shops wrote that pink, "being a more decided and stronger color", suited boys, while blue, "more delicate and dainty", suited girls.

Advice went both ways for years. Only around the 1940s and 1950s did "pink for girls, blue for boys" settle in the USA, helped along by shops and advertisers. The historian Jo Paoletti traced this story through old catalogues, magazines and photographs.

Why is Holi played with colours?In the town of Barsana, women playfully beat men with long sticks during Holi, while the men hide under shields and try to splash them with colour.

Do words shape what we see?

Languages divide the rainbow differently. Russian has two everyday words for blue: goluboy for light blue and siniy for dark blue. Experiments suggest Russian speakers tell those shades apart slightly faster than English speakers do. Some languages use a single word for both green and blue. The ancient Greek poet Homer described the sea as "wine-dark", which has puzzled readers for centuries.

In 1969, two American researchers, Brent Berlin and Paul Kay, compared colour words in many languages. They argued that languages add colour words in a fairly regular order, starting with dark and light, then red, then green or yellow. Other researchers have challenged parts of their idea, and the argument still runs.

Whatever language you speak, your eyes receive the same light as anyone else's. Your culture decides whether that red means a wedding, a warning or a winning team.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pink has always been a girls' colour and blue a boys' colour.

Truth: In the USA, babies were mostly dressed in white until the early 1900s, and some advice even said pink for boys. "Pink for girls, blue for boys" only became the usual rule around the 1940s and 1950s.

Try this

Run a colour survey. Ask five people of different ages which colour they think of for "happy", "danger", "calm" and "sad". Write their answers in a table. Do older and younger people agree? Ask a friend in another city or country to try the same survey and compare.
