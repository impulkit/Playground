What are crown jewels?

Britain's crown jewels contain more than 23,000 gems, but almost all of them are newer than they look, because in 1649 the old royal crowns were melted down for coins.

The short answer

Crown jewels are the crowns, sceptres, orbs, swords and other precious objects used by kings and queens, especially at coronations, when a new monarch is crowned. They are meant to show power, wealth and history, and each object has a meaning. An orb stands for the world, a sceptre for the power to rule.

Many countries have, or once had, crown jewels. Britain's are the most famous, kept in the Tower of London and seen by millions of visitors. Iran, Denmark, Sweden and others also keep royal treasures. In India, the jewels of the Nizams of Hyderabad and the treasures of other princely states tell a similar story of royal splendour.

Surprising but true

In 1671, a man named Thomas Blood tried to steal Britain's crown jewels by flattening a crown with a mallet and stuffing it under his cloak.

Britain's sparkling collection

Britain's crown jewels include about 100 objects set with more than 23,000 gems. The heaviest crown, St Edward's Crown, weighs about 2.2 kg, as much as two big bags of sugar. It is used only at the moment of crowning, because it is so heavy.

The Sovereign's Sceptre holds Cullinan I, about 530 carats, one of the largest cut diamonds in the world. It was cut from the Cullinan diamond, found in South Africa in 1905, which weighed about 3,106 carats, roughly the size of a fist. The Imperial State Crown includes nearly 3,000 diamonds.

The crown made for Queen Elizabeth, the Queen Mother, holds the Kohinoor, taken from Punjab in 1849.

Melted and remade

The collection looks ancient, but most of it is from after 1660. In 1649, during the English Civil War, King Charles I was executed. Parliament, led by Oliver Cromwell, ordered the royal regalia destroyed. The gold was melted down into coins. Many gems were sold.

When the monarchy returned in 1660, King Charles II needed new jewels for his coronation. Craftsmen made new crowns, sceptres and orbs, some copying the old ones. Only a few older pieces survived, including a 12th-century anointing spoon.

What is the Tower of London?For centuries the Tower of London kept lions, an elephant and even a polar bear, which was allowed to fish in the River Thames on a long chain.

The great jewel robbery

In 1671, Thomas Blood, an Irish adventurer, tried to steal the jewels. Dressed as a clergyman, he befriended the elderly keeper of the jewels, then attacked him. Blood flattened the crown with a mallet to hide it under his cloak, while his friends grabbed the orb and sceptre.

He was caught near the Tower gate. Oddly, King Charles II pardoned him and even gave him land. Nobody knows exactly why. Since then, the jewels have been guarded far more closely, behind thick glass and steel in the Tower of London.

Jewels of India

India's princely states had dazzling collections. The Nizams of Hyderabad were among the richest rulers in the world. Their jewels included the Jacob Diamond, about 184 carats, which the last Nizam reportedly found in his father's slipper and used as a paperweight.

In 1995, the Government of India bought 173 pieces of the Nizams' jewellery. They are kept in the vaults of the Reserve Bank of India and shown at the National Museum in New Delhi only occasionally. The Mughal emperors also had legendary treasures, including the Peacock Throne, which was carried off to Persia in 1739.

Jewels still disappearing

On 19 October 2025, thieves broke into the Louvre museum in Paris. In less than eight minutes, they stole eight pieces of jewellery linked to French queens and empresses, valued at about 100 million US dollars. They also dropped a crown that belonged to Empress Eugénie, which was found broken outside. Several suspects were arrested, but by mid-2026 the stolen jewels themselves had not been found.

Crown jewels are made to last forever, but history keeps proving how fragile they are. Wars, revolutions and thieves have broken up many collections. Some of those lost treasures are still being hunted today.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The British crown jewels are owned by the King personally, and he could sell them.

Truth: They belong to the Crown, meaning the monarchy as an institution, and are held in trust for the nation. The monarch can't sell them, and they are kept on display for the public in the Tower of London.
