What is it like at the top of the Eiffel Tower?

The Eiffel Tower's highest viewing deck is 276 metres up, roughly as high as a 90-storey building, and on a hot summer day the whole tower grows a few centimetres taller.

The short answer

At the top of the Eiffel Tower, you stand on a small, windy platform about 276 metres above Paris. The city spreads out below like a map. You can follow the River Seine as it curves past bridges and spot the white domes of Sacré-Cœur on its hill. On a clear day you can see for tens of kilometres. Below, the boats on the river look like toys and the trees of the Champ de Mars park form neat green rows.

Getting there takes two lifts. The first climbs one of the tower's four slanting legs to the second floor. A second lift goes straight up the narrow middle to the top. The ride is short but stomach-turning, with iron beams flashing past the glass. Up top, part of the deck is enclosed by windows, and part is open to the wind behind a safety mesh.

Surprising but true

Gustave Eiffel built a small private apartment near the top of his tower, where he entertained guests including the inventor Thomas Edison.

The climb

Visitors who prefer stairs can walk up 674 steps to the second floor, about 115 m up. Many families take the stairs to the first floor, where a section of glass floor lets you look straight down at people the size of ants, 57 m below.

The ride to the top uses lifts designed for an unusual problem. The tower's legs are curved, so the lower lifts must travel along a slope that changes angle as they rise. Engineers in 1889 had to invent new ways to do it, and the lifts have been rebuilt several times since.

The full climb from the ground to the top is about 1,665 steps, but the stairs above the second floor are closed to the public. Hungry visitors can stop at restaurants on the first and second floors. At the very top there is a small champagne bar, probably the highest place to buy a drink in Paris.

How does a lift go up and down?A lift is really a giant see-saw. A heavy block on the other end of the cables does most of the work.

Eiffel's hideaway

Gustave Eiffel, whose company built the tower, kept a small apartment near the top. He used it for scientific experiments on weather and air resistance, and for entertaining important guests. In 1889, the American inventor Thomas Edison visited and gave Eiffel one of his new phonographs.

Today the room is open to view through windows. Wax figures of Eiffel, Edison and Eiffel's daughter sit inside, as if chatting in their Sunday best. Eiffel's company also designed the iron skeleton inside the Statue of Liberty in New York.

Wind, heat and paint

The tower went up in just over two years, built from about 18,000 iron parts held together by roughly 2.5 million rivets. It sways only slightly in strong winds, usually by a few centimetres, because its open lattice of iron lets the air pass through. Heat has more effect. On hot days, the iron expands and the tower grows by several centimetres.

Keeping it from rusting is a never-ending job. About every seven years, painters cover all 18,000 iron parts by hand, using around 60 tonnes of paint. The latest repainting, begun in 2019, brought back a yellow-brown that Gustave Eiffel chose in 1907. It is darker at the bottom and lighter at the top, so it looks even against the sky.

Night lights

When it opened for the World's Fair in 1889, the tower was the tallest structure in the world, until New York's Chrysler Building passed it in 1930. Many people in Paris first hated it. It survived partly because it was useful for radio, and antennas still stand on top.

Every evening after dark, the tower glitters for five minutes at the start of each hour, as 20,000 small lights flash at random. At the 2024 Paris Olympics, each medal held a small piece of iron taken from the tower during repairs, so athletes carried a little bit of it home.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Parisians loved the Eiffel Tower from the start.

Truth: Many famous artists and writers signed a protest against it in 1887, calling it ugly. It was only meant to stand for 20 years and was saved partly because it made a useful radio antenna.

Try this

Look at a ruler-straight metal railing or gate on a hot afternoon and again on a cool morning. You won't see the change, but builders leave small gaps in bridges and rail tracks for exactly this reason. Look for those gaps on a bridge or a flyover when you cross one with an adult.
