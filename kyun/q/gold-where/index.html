Where does gold come from?

The gold in a wedding necklace is older than Earth. It was made in a violent crash between dead stars.

The short answer

Gold atoms are not made on Earth. Ordinary stars, like the Sun, can build light elements such as carbon and oxygen, but not heavy ones like gold. That takes something far more violent.

Scientists think most gold was forged when two neutron stars, the super-dense leftovers of dead stars, crashed into each other. The crash sprayed gold and other heavy metals into space. Some of that dust ended up in the cloud that formed our Solar System. Inside Earth, hot water and moving rock later gathered the gold into veins where miners can find it.

Surprising but true

All the gold ever mined in human history would fit in a cube about 22 m on each side, roughly as long as a cricket pitch.

Made in a star crash

In August 2017, detectors in the USA and Italy picked up ripples in space itself, called gravitational waves. They came from two neutron stars colliding about 130 million light-years away. Telescopes around the world, including NASA spacecraft, then turned to look. They saw the glow of freshly made heavy elements.

That one crash was thought to have made gold weighing several times as much as Earth. Some scientists think certain rare exploding stars, a kind of supernova, make gold too. The debate is about how much each kind of event contributes.

Why gold isn't deep down

When Earth was young and partly molten, heavy metals sank towards the centre. Most of Earth's gold probably went with the iron into the core, far beyond reach. Many geologists think the gold we mine today arrived a little later, delivered by meteorites that hit Earth after the core had formed.

Near the surface, hot water flowing through cracks dissolves tiny amounts of gold and leaves it behind in veins, often inside white quartz. When those rocks wear away, rivers carry the heavy gold flakes and drop them in gravel. That is where most gold rushes began.

Rushes and mines

In January 1848, a carpenter named James Marshall spotted shiny flakes in a stream at Sutter's Mill, in California. Within a year, tens of thousands of people were heading west. They were called the forty-niners, after the year 1849. The small port of San Francisco grew into a city almost overnight.

India has deep history with gold mining too. The Kolar Gold Fields in Karnataka, about 100 km from Bengaluru, had shafts more than 3 km deep, among the deepest in the world. It closed in 2001. The Hutti mine in Karnataka still produces gold today.

Rare, by the numbers

Gold is about 19 times heavier than the same amount of water. So the 200,000 tonnes or so mined in all of history, divided by 19, fills only about 11,000 cubic metres. That makes a cube roughly 22 m on each side. It would stand about as tall as a seven-storey building.

Miners work hard for it. In a typical mine today, a tonne of rock may hold only a few grams of gold, about the weight of a single ring.

Why people love it

Gold almost never rusts or tarnishes. A gold coin from ancient Rome still shines. It is also very soft. Goldsmiths can beat it into sheets so thin that light shines through, and Indian sweet makers sometimes use edible gold and silver leaf, called vark.

Because gold is rare, heavy and long-lasting, people have used it as money and savings for thousands of years. Indian families together hold a huge share of the world's gold, much of it as jewellery passed down at weddings and festivals.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Gold was made inside the Earth.

Truth: Earth only gathered and moved gold around. The gold atoms themselves were forged in space before our planet existed, then mixed into the cloud of dust and gas that formed the Sun and planets.

Try this

Be a gold panner. Mix a handful of sand with a few small heavy metal objects, such as washers or coins, in a wide bowl of water. Swirl the bowl gently in circles and tip a little water and sand over the edge each time. The heavy metal stays at the bottom, just as gold does in a miner's pan.
