What is a palindrome?

The name of a language spoken by more than 3 crore people in Kerala reads the same backwards as forwards. Try spelling Malayalam backwards.

The short answer

A palindrome is a word, number or sentence that reads the same backwards as forwards. "Level", "radar" and "madam" are palindromes. So is the number 12321, and the date 22/02/2022.

For longer phrases, people usually ignore spaces, capital letters and punctuation. The most famous English one is "A man, a plan, a canal: Panama". The word palindrome comes from Greek words meaning "running back again".

Surprising but true

Your DNA is full of palindromes. Certain short stretches read the same on one strand as on the other strand read backwards, and some proteins use them as landmarks for cutting.

Palindromes in many languages

English is full of short ones: noon, kayak, racecar, refer. The longest common English palindromes are only about seven to nine letters, like "rotator" and "rotavator". The Irish writer James Joyce invented a longer one in his 1922 novel Ulysses: "tattarrattat", the sound of someone knocking at a door. It has 12 letters, and the Oxford English Dictionary lists it.

One of the best-known palindromes in India is the name of a language: Malayalam, spoken mostly in Kerala. In Hindi, you can find short palindromes such as jalaj, meaning lotus, and nayan, meaning eye. They read the same both ways in Devanagari letters as well as in English spelling.

Sanskrit poets went much further. In a style called viloma kavya, verses can be read forwards and backwards. One famous Sanskrit poem, the Ramakrishna Viloma Kavyam, tells the story of Rama when read one way and of Krishna when read the other way.

The square at Pompeii

One of the oldest famous word puzzles is the Sator square. It is made of five Latin words: SATOR, AREPO, TENET, OPERA, ROTAS. Written as a grid, it reads the same forwards, backwards, up and down.

An example was found scratched into a column at Pompeii, the Roman town buried by a volcano in 79 CE. Others have turned up across the old Roman world. Nobody is completely sure what the square was for. It may have been a charm, a word game or a secret sign.

Number palindromes

In maths, palindromic numbers have a kind of symmetry. 121, 1331 and 14641 are palindromes, and they are also powers of 11. Squaring numbers made only of 1s gives a beautiful pattern: 1, 121, 12321, 1234321, all the way up to 12345678987654321.

There is a game you can play with almost any number. Reverse it and add. If the answer isn't a palindrome, do it again. For 57, it takes two steps. For 89, it takes 24 steps and ends with a 13-digit number.

For 196, nobody has ever reached a palindrome, even using computers for years. Mathematicians are still unsure whether it ever will. Some people use computers to test palindromes in other number systems, like binary, where the patterns change completely.

Palindromes in nature

Palindromes even appear in living things. DNA is made of two strands with four chemical letters: A, T, G and C. A always pairs with T, and G with C. Some short stretches read the same on one strand as on the other strand going the opposite way.

For example, GAATTC on one strand sits opposite CTTAAG, which reversed is GAATTC again. Bacteria make proteins that recognise palindromes like this and cut DNA there. Scientists borrowed those proteins as tools, and they helped make modern genetics possible.

Palindromes are a bit like word riddles. Writers have spent years building long palindrome sentences and even whole palindrome poems, just to show it can be done.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Every number becomes a palindrome if you keep reversing it and adding.

Truth: Most numbers do, but nobody has ever turned 196 into a palindrome this way, even after computers carried on for millions of steps. Nobody has proved it never will, either.

Try this

Pick any two-digit number, like 57. Reverse it and add, so 57 + 75 = 132. Do it again, and 132 + 231 = 363, a palindrome. Try other numbers and count how many steps each takes. Some, like 89, take a surprisingly long time.
