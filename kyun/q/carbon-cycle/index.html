What is the carbon cycle?

Some of the carbon in your body was once inside a dinosaur, a volcano or the bottom of the sea.

The short answer

Carbon is one of the main building blocks of life. It's in your muscles, in trees, in the air, in seawater and in rocks. The carbon cycle is the way carbon keeps moving between all these places.

Plants take carbon dioxide out of the air and use sunlight to turn it into sugar. Animals eat plants. When living things breathe, die and rot, the carbon goes back into the air. The oceans soak up carbon dioxide and give it off again. Over millions of years, some carbon gets locked into rocks, coal and oil, and volcanoes slowly release it. Today humans are moving carbon much faster than nature does, by burning fuels.

Surprising but true

Since 1958, a weather station on a Hawaiian volcano has measured the carbon dioxide in the air. The line goes up and down every single year, as plants in the north grow and lose their leaves.

The fast cycle: air, plants and animals

The quickest part of the cycle happens around us every day. Through photosynthesis, plants and algae take in carbon dioxide and water and, using sunlight, make sugar. They use that sugar to grow leaves, wood and roots.

When an animal eats a plant, the carbon passes into its body. Every time you breathe out, you return some of it to the air as carbon dioxide. When plants and animals die, fungi and bacteria break them down and release their carbon too. In a tropical forest, a fallen leaf can disappear in a few weeks.

The ocean's share

The oceans hold about 50 times more carbon than the air. Carbon dioxide dissolves into seawater at the surface, especially in cold water. Tiny ocean plants, called phytoplankton, use it for photosynthesis, just as land plants do.

When sea creatures with shells die, some sink to the seabed. Over millions of years their shells pile up and turn into limestone and chalk. The white cliffs of Dover in England are made mostly from the shells of tiny ocean algae. The oceans have also taken up about a quarter of the carbon dioxide people have released. It has made seawater slightly more acidic, which harms corals.

The slow cycle: rocks and volcanoes

Some carbon stays put for a very long time. Swamp plants buried hundreds of millions of years ago were squeezed and heated into coal and oil. Limestone can hold carbon for tens of millions of years.

Slowly, it comes back. Rain wears down rocks and carries carbon to the sea. Moving plates of the Earth's crust push rocks deep underground, and volcanoes breathe some of that carbon out again as gas. This slow cycle takes millions of years and has helped keep Earth's climate steady over long stretches of time.

A curve on a mountain

In 1958, an American scientist named Charles David Keeling began measuring carbon dioxide on Mauna Loa, a volcano in Hawaii. It was far from cities and factories, so the air was well mixed.

His measurements, continued to this day, are called the Keeling Curve. They zigzag every year as northern forests grow and lose leaves. But they also climb steadily. Before about 1750, the air held about 280 parts per million of carbon dioxide. In May 2025, Mauna Loa measured about 430 parts per million.

Why it matters now

Burning coal, oil and gas takes carbon that was locked away for millions of years and puts it into the air in just a couple of centuries. Cutting down forests adds more. Carbon dioxide is a greenhouse gas, which traps heat, so more of it is warming the planet.

Forests, soils and the sea absorb some of the extra, and that helps. Coastal places like the mangroves of the Sundarbans store large amounts of carbon in their muddy soils. Protecting them, planting trees and burning less fuel all help keep more carbon out of the air.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Amazon rainforest makes 20 per cent of the oxygen we breathe.

Truth: The Amazon's plants do make huge amounts of oxygen, but the forest's animals, fungi and microbes use up almost all of it again. Most of the oxygen in the air built up over millions of years, much of it from tiny ocean plankton.

Try this

Put a fresh leaf from a water plant, or a leafy sprig, in a clear glass of water and leave it in bright sunlight for an hour or two. Look closely for tiny bubbles on the leaf. They are oxygen, made as the leaf takes in carbon dioxide from the water and turns it into sugar.
