Why do so many cultures have flood stories?

In an Indian story, a tiny fish warns a king about a great flood. In a Mesopotamian one, a god whispers the warning through a reed wall. The stories are thousands of kilometres apart.

The short answer

Flood stories appear in Mesopotamia, India, Greece, China, the Americas, Australia and elsewhere. In many, a god or wise being warns one good person, who builds a boat or finds a high place, survives with family and animals, and starts the world again.

Scholars think there are several reasons. People almost everywhere lived beside rivers and coasts, where terrible floods really happened. At the end of the last ice age, rising seas drowned land that people knew. And some stories travelled from one people to another, changing as they went. Each culture also used the story to say something about gods, goodness and new beginnings.

Surprising but true

Some Aboriginal Australian stories about the sea swallowing land may preserve memories of real sea-level rise from more than 7,000 years ago.

The oldest written flood

The earliest written flood stories come from Mesopotamia, in what is now Iraq, about 4,000 years ago. In the Epic of Gilgamesh, a man named Utnapishtim is warned by the god Ea, who whispers through the reed wall of his house. He builds a huge boat, loads his family and animals, and survives a storm of six days and seven nights. Then he sends out birds to look for land.

In 1872, a self-taught scholar named George Smith, working at the British Museum, read this tablet for the first time in thousands of years. It caused a sensation, because it was so similar to the story of Noah in the Bible.

Manu and the fish

In the Shatapatha Brahmana, an ancient Sanskrit text, Manu finds a tiny fish while washing his hands. It begs him to protect it from bigger fish. He keeps it in a pot, then a pond, then the sea, as it grows enormous. The fish warns him of a flood and tells him to build a boat.

When the waters rise, Manu ties the boat to the fish's horn, and it pulls him to a northern mountain. In later Puranas, the fish is Matsya, the first avatar of Vishnu. Manu becomes the ancestor of humanity, which is why one Sanskrit word for human, manava, comes from his name.

What was Mesopotamia?Every time you check a clock, you use an idea from Mesopotamia, where people counted in sixties more than 4,000 years ago.

Greece, China and beyond

In Greek myths, Zeus floods the world, and Deucalion and his wife Pyrrha survive in a chest that floats to a mountain. Afterwards, they throw stones over their shoulders, and the stones become new people.

China's story is different. Floods along the Yellow River last for years. A hero named Yu the Great tames them without any boat, by digging channels to lead the water to the sea. He works so hard that he passes his own house three times without going in. The Yellow River really has flooded catastrophically again and again in Chinese history.

Memories of rising seas

About 20,000 years ago, at the height of the last ice age, sea level was about 120 m lower than today. As the ice melted, coasts moved inland, sometimes by many kilometres. Land bridges disappeared. People living on low coasts would have watched the sea creep closer over generations.

In 2016, two researchers studied 21 Aboriginal stories from around Australia's coast describing the sea drowning land. Matching them with sea-level records, they argued some may preserve memories more than 7,000 years old, passed down by word of mouth. Not everyone agrees, but it shows how long a story can survive.

Why they still matter

For Jews, Christians and Muslims, the story of Noah (Nuh in the Quran) carries religious meaning, and for Hindus the story of Manu and Matsya does too. People read them as faith, history, lessons or all three.

Floods are not just stories of the past. Today, sea level is rising again by a few millimetres every year, and low-lying places from the Sundarbans to Miami are planning for higher water.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Scientists have found evidence of one flood that covered the whole Earth at the same time.

Truth: Geologists have found evidence of many large floods in different places and times, but no single flood covering every continent in human history. For believers, flood stories carry religious and moral meaning beyond geology.

Try this

Ask a grandparent or older neighbour about the biggest flood or storm they remember. Write down the story in their words. Then tell it to a friend and ask them to retell it to someone else. See which details change and which stay the same.
