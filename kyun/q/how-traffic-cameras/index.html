How do speed cameras catch cars?

A speed camera can measure a car's speed in a fraction of a second by bouncing radio waves or laser pulses off it, often without any flash you can see.

The short answer

Speed cameras measure how fast a car is moving, then take a photo of its number plate if it is going too fast. They do this in a few different ways.

Some send out radio waves and listen to the echo. Some fire quick pulses of laser light. Some use sensors buried in the road. Others photograph a car at two points far apart and time how long it took to travel between them.

The fastest of these measurements take less than a second. The camera then records the plate, the time, the place and the speed. In many countries, including India, a fine can then be sent straight to the owner's phone.

Surprising but true

One of the first speed camera companies was started by a Dutch racing driver, Maurice Gatsonides, who first built a device to measure his own speed on corners.

Radar and the Doppler effect

Listen to an ambulance siren as it races past. As it comes towards you the sound is higher. As it moves away, it drops lower. That change is called the Doppler effect, after the Austrian scientist Christian Doppler, who described it in 1842.

Radio waves do the same thing. A radar speed camera sends out a beam of radio waves. When they hit a moving car, they bounce back slightly squashed if it's coming closer, or slightly stretched if it's going away. The camera's computer measures exactly how much the waves have changed. From that, it works out the car's speed.

Radar uses the same idea as the giant dishes that track aircraft and storms. Here it is shrunk into a box on a pole.

Lasers that count in billionths

Laser speed guns, also called lidar, work a little like a bat's echoes. The gun fires short pulses of invisible infrared laser light at a car and times how long each one takes to bounce back. Light travels about 30 cm in a billionth of a second, so the gun needs an extremely precise clock.

If each pulse takes a tiny bit less time to return than the last, the car is coming closer. The gun fires many pulses in a fraction of a second and works out how fast the distance is shrinking. Because the beam is narrow, an officer can aim it at one vehicle in heavy traffic.

Sensors under the road

Some cameras don't measure speed by themselves at all. Instead, two sensors are set into the road a few metres apart. They might be loops of wire that notice the metal of a car passing over them, or strips that feel the pressure of tyres.

The system times how long the car takes to go from one sensor to the next. If the gap is 3 metres and the car crosses it in a tenth of a second, it is going 30 metres a second. That is 108 km/h. This is speed and distance maths, done in an instant.

Average-speed cameras

Some drivers slow down just before a camera, then speed up again. Average-speed cameras stop that trick. One camera reads a car's number plate. Another camera, perhaps many kilometres further on, reads it again. A computer divides the distance by the time taken.

If the average comes out too high, the car must have broken the limit somewhere in between. Cameras that read number plates are also used at toll plazas and in cities to spot stolen vehicles. The tag-reading system in FASTag tolls is a close cousin.

Who invented them?

In the 1950s, Maurice Gatsonides was a Dutch rally driver. He wanted to know how fast he took corners, so he helped design a device to measure speed. His company, Gatso, was founded in 1958 and went on to make speed cameras used in many countries.

There is some irony in that. A man who loved driving fast ended up giving police one of their best tools against speeding. And the strangest part? Some police radar guns can track several cars at once, using the same wave tricks that let scientists measure how fast distant galaxies are rushing away.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If a speed camera doesn't flash, it hasn't caught you.

Truth: Many modern cameras use infrared light that human eyes can't see, and in daylight some don't need a flash at all. The picture can be taken without the driver noticing anything.

Try this

Stand by a quiet road with an adult and listen to a car or motorbike go past. Notice how the engine sounds higher as it comes towards you and lower as it moves away. That change in pitch is the Doppler effect, the same idea a radar speed camera uses.
