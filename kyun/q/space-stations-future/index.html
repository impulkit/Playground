What will space stations look like in the future?

The International Space Station is due to be steered into the Pacific Ocean around 2030, and companies and countries are racing to build what comes next.

The short answer

The International Space Station has been home to astronauts since 2000, but it is getting old. NASA and its partners plan to retire it around 2030. After that, low Earth orbit will probably have several smaller stations instead of one giant one.

China already has its own station, Tiangong. India plans the Bharatiya Antariksh Station, with a first module targeted for 2028. American companies are building private stations that could host scientists, tourists and astronauts from many countries. Further ahead, engineers dream of spinning stations with artificial gravity, and outposts near the Moon.

Surprising but true

When the ISS is retired, a special SpaceX spacecraft will push it down so that its leftover pieces fall into a remote part of the Pacific nicknamed the spacecraft cemetery.

The ISS, a hard act to follow

The ISS is about as long as a football field and weighs over 400 tonnes. It was built from more than 40 launches by the USA, Russia, Europe, Japan and Canada. People have lived aboard without a break since November 2000.

But its oldest parts have been in space since 1998, and some are cracking and leaking. Maintenance costs NASA billions of dollars a year. When its time comes, a SpaceX vehicle will dock and fire its engines to slow it down. Most of the station will burn up, and the remaining pieces will fall into a remote stretch of the South Pacific far from any land.

China's Tiangong

Tiangong, meaning "heavenly palace", was completed in 2022. It is T-shaped, with three main modules, and much smaller and lighter than the ISS. Chinese astronauts, called taikonauts, live there in crews of three, swapping every six months or so. China has invited scientists from other countries to fly experiments aboard. If the ISS retires on schedule, Tiangong may for a while be the only government-run station in orbit.

India's station

India wants its own station too. The Bharatiya Antariksh Station is planned to start with one module launched around 2028, growing to five modules by about 2035. It will build on Gaganyaan, India's programme to launch astronauts on Indian rockets.

In 2025, Group Captain Shubhanshu Shukla of the Indian Air Force spent about 18 days on the ISS during a private mission called Axiom-4. He was the first Indian to visit the station, and only the second Indian citizen in space, after Rakesh Sharma in 1984. Experience like his will help plan India's station.

Private stations

NASA no longer wants to own a station. Instead, it plans to rent space on stations built by companies. Several are in the works. Vast, a Californian company, plans a small one-module station called Haven-1, now expected in 2027. Axiom Space wants to attach modules to the ISS and later fly them free. Others, such as Starlab and Orbital Reef, are on drawing boards.

These stations will be cheaper partly because of reusable rockets, which bring launch costs down. They might host film shoots, factories making special medicines or crystals, and paying visitors.

Spinning wheels and bigger dreams

Long stays in weightlessness weaken muscles and bones, so astronauts exercise for about two hours a day. One fix is artificial gravity. In the 1950s, the rocket engineer Wernher von Braun drew a wheel-shaped station that spun to press astronauts gently against its outer rim. The 1968 film 2001: A Space Odyssey made the idea famous.

A spinning wheel needs to be large, perhaps hundreds of metres wide, or the spin makes people dizzy. Nobody has built one yet. But as rockets get cheaper and bigger, engineers are again sketching wheels, inflatable modules and even stations made from material dug up on the Moon. Future stations may also have to dodge more and more space junk, another problem left for the next generation of builders.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Astronauts float on the space station because there is no gravity up there.

Truth: Gravity at the ISS's height is about 90% as strong as on the ground. Astronauts float because the station and everyone in it are falling around Earth together, so nothing presses them against the floor.

Try this

Put a small ball or marble in a bucket and, holding the handle, swing it slowly in a circle beside you, outdoors and with plenty of space. The ball stays pressed to the bottom even when the bucket is sideways. A spinning space station would use the same effect.
