What are rogue planets?

Somewhere in the dark between the stars, there may be more lonely planets drifting with no sun than there are stars in our galaxy.

The short answer

A rogue planet is a planet that doesn't orbit a star. It drifts alone through the galaxy, in darkness and bitter cold. Most were probably born around a star like any other planet, then flung out into space by the gravity of bigger neighbours. Some may have formed on their own from a collapsing cloud of gas.

They are very hard to see, because they give off almost no light. Astronomers find them by watching for a rare trick of gravity, when a rogue planet passes in front of a far-off star and makes it briefly brighter. From the few they've found, scientists guess there could be billions or even trillions in the Milky Way.

Surprising but true

A rogue planet with a thick atmosphere could, in theory, keep an ocean of liquid water warm under its ice for billions of years, heated from inside.

Kicked out of home

Young solar systems are messy. Planets form in a spinning disc of gas and dust. Some grow big, like Jupiter, and their gravity tugs hard on the smaller ones. A small planet that wanders too close to a giant can be slingshot right out of the system, like a ball whacked out of a cricket ground.

Computer models suggest our own solar system may have lost a planet or two this way, billions of years ago. If so, it is still out there somewhere, frozen and drifting. Flung planets don't slow down much, because space has almost nothing to rub against. A planet thrown out at a few kilometres per second would keep that speed for billions of years, crossing the gaps between stars one slow step at a time.

Finding a planet you can't see

In 1915, Einstein showed that heavy objects bend space, and light travels along those bends. So a planet passing in front of a distant star acts like a magnifying glass. The star's light bends around the planet and looks brighter for a while.

For a big planet the brightening lasts a day or two. For one the size of Earth, it can be just a few hours. Telescopes have to stare at millions of stars at once to catch these flickers. Surveys from New Zealand, Chile and elsewhere have found dozens of possible rogue planets so far.

Are there planets around other stars?Thirty-five years ago, nobody had found a single planet around another star. Now we know of more than 6,000.

A new hunter in space

On 30 August 2026, NASA launched the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope on a Falcon Heavy rocket from Florida. Roman can see an area of sky about 100 times bigger than Hubble can in one shot. Astronomers expect it to find hundreds of rogue planets, some as small as Mars.

That will give the first proper count. It will help answer a big question: are lonely planets rare oddballs, or are they the most common kind of planet in the galaxy?

Strange pairs in Orion

In 2023, astronomers using the Webb telescope spotted dozens of objects about the mass of Jupiter floating free in the Orion Nebula. Some seemed to come in pairs, orbiting each other with no star. They were nicknamed JuMBOs, for Jupiter-mass binary objects.

Not everyone is convinced. Some astronomers think many could be faint background stars or galaxies. Further study is under way, and it shows how tricky it is to spot dark objects in space.

Could anything live there?

A rogue planet's surface would be colder than −200°C, with no sunrise, ever. But a planet keeps some heat inside from when it formed, and radioactive rocks keep warming it. Under a thick layer of ice or a heavy atmosphere, that heat might keep an ocean liquid for billions of years.

It's only an idea for now. But Jupiter's moon Europa already has an ocean under its ice, warmed from within, far from the Sun's heat. Scientists wonder whether life needs a sun at all. And if our own Sun vanished tomorrow, Earth would sail off in a straight line and become a rogue planet too.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A rogue planet is on its way to crash into Earth.

Truth: Stories about a hidden planet called Nibiru are not true. Astronomers would easily see any planet close enough to threaten us. Space is so empty that a rogue planet passing near the Sun is extremely unlikely.

Try this

Look at a lamp across the room through the bottom of a round glass, or a wine glass stem. Move the glass slowly from side to side. Watch the light stretch and brighten. A planet's gravity bends starlight in a similar way, though for a very different reason.
