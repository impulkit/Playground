What are Kondapalli toys?

In one Andhra village, carvers turn a wood so light it almost floats into elephants, kings and whole little village scenes.

The short answer

Kondapalli toys are bright, hand-carved wooden figures from Kondapalli, a village about 16 km from Vijayawada in Andhra Pradesh. Carvers make elephants with royal seats on their backs, palm-tree climbers, women grinding grain, bullock carts, and whole sets of gods from Hindu stories.

What makes them special is the wood. It comes from a tree called tella poniki, which is soft enough to carve with simple tools and so light that big toys feel almost empty in your hand. In 2007 the toys received a GI tag, a legal label that says only toys made in this region may use the name.

Surprising but true

Kondapalli toys are carved from tella poniki, a soft wood so light that a toy elephant the size of a cricket ball weighs less than an apple.

Wood that almost floats

Tella poniki grows on the hills around the village. Carvers cut it, let it dry and then shape each part separately. A body here, a trunk there, four legs in a row. Once the parts are ready, they are joined with makku, a paste made from ground tamarind seeds and sawdust.

Then comes the smoothing, a coat of chalk-like base and the paint. Older toys used colours made from plants and minerals. Many today use enamel paint for a glossy shine. The finished pieces are so light that a child can hold a whole elephant in one palm. Drop one into a bucket of water and it would bob like a cork.

A whole village in miniature

The most famous Kondapalli design is the ambari elephant, carrying a fancy canopy on its back like the elephants in royal processions. Another favourite is the toddy tapper climbing a palm tree, with a rope around his waist. There are farmers with ploughs, women carrying pots and bullocks pulling carts.

Together they work like a tiny museum of everyday life in old Andhra villages. Many show jobs that machines now do. A child playing with them is holding a picture of how great-grandparents lived. Indian artists have long loved this kind of scene, and the same patient hand skills appear in wood carving across the country.

Dolls for festival steps

Kondapalli toys come alive during festivals. At Sankranti and Dasara, many families in Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Tamil Nadu set up bommala koluvu, a display of dolls on wooden steps. Gods sit on the top steps, then kings and saints, then ordinary people and animals lower down.

Neighbours visit to admire each other's steps, children sing, and someone hands out sweets. Some families keep the same dolls for 50 years or more and add one new piece every year, so the display grows along with the family.

Many families follow an old custom of arranging the dolls in an odd number of steps, often three, five, seven or nine. Kondapalli sets are made for this. A full Dashavatara set shows all ten forms of Vishnu in a row, from a fish to a warrior king, each the height of a pencil. Because the wood is so light, even a tall stack of steps does not sag under a crowd of gods.

Carvers from far away

Many Kondapalli carvers belong to a community known as the Aryakshatriyas. Family stories say their ancestors came from Rajasthan several hundred years ago, perhaps in the time of the great Vijayanagara kings. Historians think this is likely, since some designs echo Rajasthani art, but nobody has found a written record that settles it.

Today the craft is under pressure. Cheap plastic toys cost less, and the special tree is harder to find. Some families have left carving for other work. Others now make key chains and fridge magnets beside the big pieces, and sell online to buyers in the USA and Europe.

Kondapalli is not India's only toy village. About 650 km away in Karnataka, the lacquered wooden toys of Channapatna are made on spinning lathes. Their makers colour each toy by pressing a stick of hard lac against wood spinning so fast that the heat from rubbing melts the colour on.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Kondapalli toys are painted plastic toys made in factories.

Truth: Each one is carved by hand from soft wood, joined with tamarind paste, smoothed and hand-painted. A single family may spend days on one large set.

Try this

Make a mini Kondapalli elephant from a ball of atta dough. Roll a fat body, four stubby legs and a trunk, and press them together with a little water. Once it dries, paint it with poster colours or turmeric water and add a tiny paper howdah on top.
