What is Chhau dance?

In Purulia, West Bengal, Chhau dancers leap and somersault in huge painted masks crowned with peacock feathers, acting out battles between gods and demons.

The short answer

Chhau is a dramatic dance from eastern India that mixes storytelling with martial moves. Dancers leap, spin, crouch and strike as if in battle. Many wear painted masks, and the music is loud and thrilling, led by booming drums and a reedy wind instrument.

There are three main styles, named after the places they come from: Purulia in West Bengal, Seraikela in Jharkhand and Mayurbhanj in Odisha. They tell stories from the Ramayana, the Mahabharata, old Puranic tales and local legends.

In 2010, UNESCO added Chhau to its list of the world's Intangible Cultural Heritage, meaning living traditions worth protecting.

Surprising but true

Many Purulia Chhau masks are made in a single village, Charida, where families have shaped them from clay, paper and cloth for generations.

Moves from the battlefield

Chhau movements grew out of mock fighting. Dancers practise with swords and shields, or with sticks, and learn to kick, jump and swivel like warriors. Many experts think the dance grew from the exercises soldiers and villagers once used to stay fit.

That connects Chhau with other Indian traditions where fighting and dance meet, like Kalaripayattu in Kerala. In Chhau, even a calm god moves with a warrior's balance.

The name is a puzzle. Some say it comes from a Sanskrit word for shadow or mask. Others link it to "chhauni", meaning an army camp. Scholars still disagree.

Three styles, three looks

Purulia Chhau is the boldest. Dancers wear huge masks with bulging eyes, painted in bright colours and topped with beads, tinsel and peacock feathers. The headdresses can be taller than the dancer's own head. Since the mask can't change expression, dancers act with their whole bodies, including big jumps and somersaults.

Seraikela Chhau, from Jharkhand, was supported for centuries by the local royal family. Its masks are smaller, smoother and more delicate. The movements are graceful and controlled. Some dances show a single character, such as a peacock, a deer or a boatman, rather than a whole battle.

Mayurbhanj Chhau, from Odisha, uses no masks at all. Dancers keep their faces calm and still, and let their bodies tell the story.

What is the Mahabharata?It has about 100,000 verses, making it one of the longest poems ever composed. Hidden inside it is the Bhagavad Gita.

The mask makers of Charida

Many Purulia masks come from Charida, a village in West Bengal where generations of families work as mask makers. They build each mask over a clay mould, layering it with paper and cloth soaked in glue. When it dries, the shell is lifted off, painted and decorated.

A big demon mask can take days to finish. The painting comes last: white or pink for gods, fierce reds, greens and blacks for demons, with eyebrows that sweep up like flames. Charida's artists now also make smaller masks for homes and gift shops. In 2018, the Purulia Chhau mask received a Geographical Indication tag, which protects the name for craftspeople from that area.

Purulia's favourite story is the goddess Durga defeating the buffalo demon Mahishasura. The same tale lies at the heart of Durga Puja, celebrated across Bengal each autumn.

Drums in the night

Chhau is traditionally danced around Chaitra Parva, a spring festival around March and April. In villages, performances can go on late into the night in open grounds lit by lamps and floodlights.

The music sets the pace. A huge kettle drum called the dhamsa booms like thunder, while dhols keep fast rhythms and a shehnai-like instrument called the mohuri weaves the tune. Dancers react to every change in beat. Of all instruments, drums may be the oldest that humans have used to move together.

Chhau was once danced only by men. Today more women perform it, and troupes have toured the world. And the strangest part? Its masks belong to a global family that stretches from Japan to Venice to West Africa, explored in masks of the world.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Chhau is one single dance performed the same way everywhere.

Truth: There are three main styles, from three different places. They share martial roots and festival seasons, but differ in masks, music and movement.

Try this

Make a simple mask from a paper plate. Ask an adult to cut two eye holes, then paint a fierce face for a demon or a calm one for a god. Put it on and try telling a story using only your body, since the mask can't change expression.
