Who were the Satavahanas?

The Satavahana kings of the Deccan named themselves after their mothers, and some of their coins carry pictures of ships.

The short answer

The Satavahanas were a dynasty that ruled much of the Deccan plateau, in today's Maharashtra, Telangana and Andhra Pradesh, for roughly 300 years. They rose after the Mauryan Empire broke up, and were most powerful from about the 1st century BCE to the early 3rd century CE.

Their capital was Pratishthana, today's Paithan on the Godavari river, and later they moved east towards Amaravati. They grew rich from farming and from trade, including trade with the Roman world by sea. They also gave land and money to Buddhist monks, and some of India's oldest cave monasteries were carved in their time.

Surprising but true

Satavahana kings were often known by their mother's name, such as Gautamiputra, meaning "son of Gautami".

Kings named after mothers

Many Satavahana kings were known by their mother's name. Gautamiputra Satakarni means "Satakarni, son of Gautami". His son was Vasishthiputra Pulumavi, "son of Vasishthi".

This does not mean women ruled, since the throne passed from father to son. Historians think it may show how important a mother's family was in Deccan society. Queens were certainly powerful. Gautamiputra's mother, Gautami Balashri, had a long inscription carved at the Buddhist caves near Nashik praising her son. An earlier queen, Naganika, recorded grand sacrifices made by her husband in an inscription at Naneghat, a mountain pass in the Western Ghats.

The great comeback

Around the 1st century CE, the Satavahanas lost much of their western land to the Shakas, rulers whose ancestors came from Central Asia. Under Gautamiputra Satakarni, in the early 2nd century CE, they struck back.

He defeated the Shaka ruler Nahapana, and in a satisfying show of victory he had Nahapana's silver coins stamped again with his own name and symbols. Hoards of these over-struck coins have been found in Maharashtra. The Nashik inscription calls Gautamiputra a king whose horses drank the water of three oceans.

Ships, coins and Rome

The Satavahanas controlled roads linking inland cities to ports on the Arabian Sea and the Bay of Bengal. A Greek sailors' guide from the 1st century CE, the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, names Paithan and the busy port of Barygaza, today's Bharuch.

Traders sent pepper, cotton cloth, beads and spices to the Roman Empire. Roman gold and silver coins, wine jars and pottery have been dug up across south India. Some Satavahana coins even show a two-masted ship, a sign that sea trade mattered to the kings. Their coins are mostly made of lead, copper and a mix called potin, small change for busy markets.

Caves, stupas and poems

The Satavahana period saw Buddhist monks and merchants pay for magnificent rock-cut caves. The great prayer hall at Karla, near Lonavala, has a high curved ceiling and rows of carved pillars. The oldest caves at Ajanta, caves 9 and 10, date from around this time too. In the east, the great stupa at Amaravati was decorated with fine carved panels.

The kings wrote their inscriptions in Prakrit, an everyday language rather than Sanskrit. A collection of 700 short Prakrit love poems, the Gatha Saptashati, is traditionally credited to a Satavahana king called Hala. Some of its verses describe village life, rain and farm work in ways that still feel fresh.

The end of the dynasty

By the early 3rd century CE the kingdom broke into smaller pieces ruled by local families, such as the Ikshvakus in Andhra. The trade routes and Buddhist sites they protected lived on, and the Prakrit of their inscriptions is an ancestor of the Marathi spoken around Paithan today.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ancient India's trade with Rome all went through the north.

Truth: Much of it went by sea from western and southern ports. Roman coins, pottery and wine jars have been found at sites across the Deccan and south India, in lands ruled by the Satavahanas and their neighbours.
