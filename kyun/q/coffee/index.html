Where did coffee come from?

Coffee beans aren't beans at all. They are the seeds of a small red fruit that first grew wild in the forests of Ethiopia.

The short answer

Coffee comes from a shrub whose wild home is the highland forests of Ethiopia, in East Africa. Its red fruits, called cherries, each hold two seeds. Those seeds, dried, roasted and ground, become coffee.

The first clear records of people drinking coffee come from Yemen, across the Red Sea, in the 1400s. Sufi monks drank it to stay awake for night-time prayers. From there it spread to Mecca, Cairo and Istanbul, then to Europe, India and the Americas. Today coffee is grown in more than 50 countries and drunk all over the world.

Surprising but true

According to a much-loved legend, all of India's early coffee grew from seven seeds smuggled out of Yemen by a Sufi pilgrim.

The dancing goats

A famous story tells of an Ethiopian goatherd named Kaldi who noticed his goats leaping about after eating red berries from a certain bush. He tried them himself and felt full of energy.

It is a lovely tale, but the first written version appeared in 1671, centuries after coffee was already popular. Historians treat it as a legend. What botanists do know is that arabica, the most widely drunk kind of coffee, comes from Ethiopia, and wild coffee still grows in its forests.

Coffee houses everywhere

The port of Mocha in Yemen shipped coffee across the Arab world, which is why "mocha" is still a coffee word. By the 1550s, Istanbul had coffee houses where people met to talk, play games and argue about the news.

In the 1600s, coffee houses spread to Venice, London and Paris. In London, some became so linked to particular businesses that they turned into famous companies. Coffee travelled along the same sea trade routes that carried spices and cloth.

Seven seeds to Karnataka

For a long time, Yemen guarded its coffee, selling only roasted or boiled beans that could not sprout. According to a well-known legend, a Sufi saint named Baba Budan, returning from pilgrimage in the 1600s, hid seven seeds and planted them in the hills of Chikmagalur in Karnataka. Those hills are now called Baba Budangiri.

Karnataka today grows about 70% of India's coffee, with Kerala and Tamil Nadu growing much of the rest. Much of it grows in the shade of taller trees, alongside pepper vines and cardamom. Not far away, the cafés of Bengaluru keep the city's programmers awake.

Filter coffee and a steel tumbler

In South India, coffee is made in a two-part metal filter. Ground coffee sits in the top, hot water drips slowly through it, and a thick dark brew, called decoction, collects below. It is mixed with boiled milk and sugar.

It is served in a steel tumbler set in a wide bowl called a dabara. The coffee is poured back and forth between the two, from a height, to cool it and make it frothy. In many Tamil and Kannada homes, the day doesn't begin until that pouring starts.

Why coffee keeps you awake

Coffee's caffeine works by blocking a chemical in the brain called adenosine, which builds up during the day and makes you feel sleepy.

The world's biggest coffee pot

Brazil has been the biggest coffee grower in the world since the 1800s, followed by Vietnam. The USA grows hardly any, except in Hawaii and Puerto Rico, but Americans drink a great deal of it. Many say coffee became the patriotic drink after the Boston Tea Party, when tea was linked with the British.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Drinking coffee stunts your growth.

Truth: There is no good evidence for this. But caffeine can disturb sleep and make people jittery, and children feel it more strongly. That's why doctors say children should have little or none.
