Why is the Gupta age called a golden age?

An iron pillar from this age has stood outdoors in Delhi for about 1,600 years without rusting away.

The short answer

The Gupta dynasty ruled much of north India from about 320 to 550 CE. In this time, Indian mathematicians, astronomers, poets, sculptors and builders produced work that people still use or admire today.

Aryabhata calculated pi very closely and said the Earth spins on its axis. Kalidasa wrote plays and poems that are classics of Sanskrit. Sculptors carved calm, graceful images of the Buddha and of Hindu gods. Trade brought wealth, and the kings issued lots of gold coins. In the 19th and 20th centuries, historians looked back and called it a golden age. Not everyone agrees the label fits.

Surprising but true

Aryabhata finished his famous book on mathematics and astronomy in 499 CE, when he was only 23 years old.

Kings who wrote their own praise

The founder, Chandragupta I, came to power around 320 CE. His son Samudragupta fought campaigns across India. A long poem praising his victories was carved onto an old Ashokan pillar now standing in Prayagraj.

Some of Samudragupta's coins show him playing a stringed instrument like a veena. Others show him hunting a tiger or riding a horse. Gold coins worked like tiny posters, carrying the king's image to every market where they were spent. His son, Chandragupta II, extended the empire west to Gujarat. He is often linked to the legendary King Vikramaditya, famous in stories for his wisdom and his court of nine gems.

Numbers and stars

In 499 CE, Aryabhata wrote the Aryabhatiya, a short book in verse. It gives pi as about 3.1416, explains eclipses as shadows rather than demons, and suggests that the stars only seem to move because the Earth turns.

Indian mathematicians of this era also used a place-value system with nine digits, and in the following centuries a symbol for nothing became part of it. That system later spread through the Arab world to Europe and became the numbers you use today, including zero.

A pillar that won't rust

In the Qutb complex in Delhi stands an iron pillar about 7 metres tall, as high as two storeys of a house. Its inscription praises a king called Chandra, usually identified as Chandragupta II.

Ordinary iron left outdoors goes orange and flaky within a few years. This pillar has stood for about 1,600 years with only light rust. Scientists found that its high phosphorus content helps a thin protective film form on its surface. Forging a single piece of iron this big was a huge feat for its time.

Stories, temples and travellers

Kalidasa's play Abhijnanashakuntalam tells of a king who forgets the woman he loves because of a curse. Translated into English in 1789, it later delighted the German poet Goethe.

Some of the earliest stone temples for Hindu gods were built in this period, like the Dashavatara temple at Deogarh. A Chinese monk named Faxian travelled through India around 400 CE and wrote that the land was peaceful and people could travel freely. The great monastery of Nalanda was also founded under the Guptas.

Golden for whom?

Historians today ask harder questions. Most people were farmers who paid taxes and rarely saw a gold coin. Caste rules became stricter, and some groups were treated as outcastes.

From the late 400s, attacks by Central Asian groups known as the Hunas weakened the empire, and it broke up by around 550 CE. So the age shines brightly in art and science, while daily life for many was far less golden.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In the Gupta golden age, life was good for everyone in India.

Truth: The rich and the learned did well, but most people were farmers living simple lives. The caste system also grew stricter, so life was much harder for those at the bottom.
