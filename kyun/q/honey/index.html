How do bees make honey?

A worker honeybee makes only about a twelfth of a teaspoon of honey in her whole life.

The short answer

Honey starts as nectar, a thin, sugary liquid that flowers make to attract insects. A honeybee sucks it up with her tongue and stores it in a special stomach called a honey crop. Back at the hive, she passes it mouth to mouth to younger bees.

Along the way, an enzyme the bees add splits the nectar's sugar into two simpler sugars, glucose and fructose. The bees spread the liquid into wax cells and fan it with their wings until most of the water has evaporated. When it is thick and sticky, they cap the cells with wax. That stored food is honey, and it feeds the colony through cold or rainy seasons when no flowers bloom.

Surprising but true

Honey can last for thousands of years without spoiling. Archaeologists have found pots of it in ancient Egyptian tombs.

A lot of work for a little honey

A single bee makes only a tiny amount of honey in her life, about a twelfth of a teaspoon. A strong hive can still make many kilograms a year, because it may have tens of thousands of workers.

To make one jar, bees must visit hundreds of thousands of flowers, often flying several kilometres from the hive. They find the best patches using a waggle dance that tells their sisters the direction and distance, explained in how bees dance. The cells they fill are near-perfect hexagons, a shape that holds the most honey for the least wax, as you can see in the maths of the honeycomb.

Why are honeycomb cells hexagons?Bees don't know geometry. But they build the best shape anyway.

Honey that never goes bad

Honey keeps for a very long time. It has so little water and so much sugar that bacteria and moulds can't grow in it. It is also slightly acidic, and an enzyme from the bees slowly produces tiny amounts of hydrogen peroxide, which fights germs.

Archaeologists have found pots of honey in ancient Egyptian tombs, thousands of years old. Egyptians used honey in food, in medicine and as offerings to their gods.

Honey hunters

People have collected honey from wild bees for at least 8,000 years. A rock painting in a cave near Valencia in Spain shows a person climbing ropes to reach a bees' nest, with bees buzzing around. It is among the oldest cave paintings of people gathering food.

In India, honey hunting is still practised. In the Sundarbans mangrove forest of West Bengal, collectors called mouals go into the forest each spring to gather wild honey, facing danger from tigers. In the Nilgiri hills, people of several Indigenous communities climb cliffs on rope ladders to reach the giant nests of rock bees.

India's honeybees

India has several kinds of honeybees. The giant rock bee builds huge single combs hanging from cliffs, trees and even water tanks in cities. The smaller Indian honeybee nests in hollows and is kept by farmers in wooden boxes. The European honeybee, brought to India in the 20th century, is now widely kept too.

The colour and taste of honey depend on the flowers. Honey from litchi orchards in Bihar is pale and fragrant. Honey from the forests of the Western Ghats can be dark and strong. In the USA, clover honey is common, and orange blossom honey comes from Florida and California.

Honey and people

Honey was the main sweetener in many parts of the world long before sugar became cheap. It sweetens tea and lemon water, and is a favourite home remedy for a sore throat.

There is one important rule. Babies under one year old should never be given honey, because it can carry spores of a germ that their young stomachs can't yet handle. For older children and adults, it is a safe and very old treat.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Honey that has gone solid and grainy has spoiled.

Truth: That's crystallisation, a natural process where glucose forms tiny crystals. The honey is still good. Standing the jar in a bowl of warm water will make it runny again.

Try this

On a sunny day, sit quietly near a flowering plant, at a safe distance, and watch the bees for ten minutes. Count how many flowers one bee visits before it flies away. Notice the yellow lumps of pollen on some bees' back legs.
