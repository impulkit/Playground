What happens at the Modhera Sun Temple?

On the equinox mornings in March and September, the first rays of sunrise at Modhera reach right into the temple's inner shrine.

The short answer

The Modhera Sun Temple in Gujarat was built about a thousand years ago for Surya, the Hindu Sun god. Its builders lined it up so that on the equinoxes, in March and September, the rising sun shines straight through the doorway and into the inner shrine, where Surya's image once stood.

Today no regular worship happens there. Instead, visitors come to see its carvings, its huge stepped tank and, each January, a dance festival. At night, a light show plays across the stone. And the village around it now runs on sunlight in a very modern way.

Surprising but true

The village beside the temple, Modhera, was declared India's first village to run on solar power around the clock in 2022, a thousand years after its temple to the Sun was built.

Built for the sun

The temple stands on the bank of the small Pushpavati river in Mehsana district. It was built under Bhima I, a king of the Chaulukya dynasty, also called the Solankis. An inscription on a wall gives a date of 1026–27 CE.

That is just after the Ghaznavid ruler Mahmud of Ghazni raided Gujarat in 1024–25. Some historians think the temple may honour the people who fought him. Others think it was simply a royal gift to the Sun.

The temple has three parts lined up east to west. First comes the Surya Kund, the stepped tank. Then a dance hall with carved pillars. Then the shrine hall, where the god lived. On the summer solstice, the noon Sun stands almost straight overhead here, because Modhera lies very close to the Tropic of Cancer.

A tank like an upside-down pyramid

The Surya Kund is about 50 metres long. Its sides are a maze of steps forming small triangles and diamonds, a bit like a stepwell turned into a giant puzzle. Little shrines are tucked among the steps, often counted as 108, to gods such as Ganesha, Shiva and Vishnu.

Worshippers once bathed in the tank before climbing up to the temple. In Gujarat's hot, dry summers, water was precious, and a tank like this also stored rain for the town. A great carved gateway called a torana once stood between the tank and the dance hall. Its tall pillars still frame the view for anyone climbing up from the water. From the bottom, the steps rise towards the dance hall like seats in a stadium.

Stone that tells stories

Almost every surface is carved. Pillars show dancers, musicians, animals and scenes from the Ramayana and Mahabharata. On the shrine walls, 12 forms of Surya appear, one for each month of the year. Many wear tall boots, a sign of styles that came from Central Asia and Persia.

Parts of the temple were damaged in later wars, and the main image of Surya has long been lost. Even so, the carvings that survive are so crisp that you can count the beads on a dancer's necklace.

Dance and light

Every January, just after the kite festival of Uttarayan, Gujarat Tourism holds the Modhera Dance Festival on the temple's steps. Dancers perform classical styles like Bharatanatyam, Kathak and Odissi with the lit-up temple behind them. It has been held since 1992.

Since 2022, a 3D projection show lights up the temple at night, telling its history in colour on the old stone.

A Sun village, again

In October 2022, Modhera village was declared India's first village powered by the Sun around the clock. More than a thousand homes got solar panels on their roofs, and a solar farm with large batteries keeps power flowing after dark.

Some families now sell spare electricity back to the grid. A thousand years ago, people here prayed to the Sun. Now they also pay their bills with it. Another great Sun temple stands at Konark in Odisha. It is carved as a stone chariot, and some of its 24 giant wheels work as sundials.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Modhera is still a working temple where daily worship takes place.

Truth: The temple has not been used for regular worship for centuries. The image of Surya is gone, and the site is a protected monument looked after by the Archaeological Survey of India.

Try this

Find a window that faces east. On a sunny morning, put a strip of tape where the sun's first patch of light lands on the floor or wall. Do it again a month later. The patch moves, because the Sun rises at a different spot as the seasons change.
