How does a flask keep tea hot?

A good vacuum flask can keep tea hot from breakfast until the evening, because between its two walls there is almost nothing at all.

The short answer

Heat always tries to move from hot things to cooler things. A flask slows that down. It has two walls, one inside the other, and the air between them has been pumped out to leave a vacuum. Heat finds it very hard to cross empty space.

The inner walls are also shiny, so they bounce heat back towards the tea. A tight stopper stops warm steam escaping from the top. Together these tricks can keep a drink hot for hours.

Surprising but true

The vacuum flask was invented in 1892 to keep things extremely cold. Its inventor, James Dewar, needed it to store gases he had turned into liquids at about minus 200 °C.

Three ways heat escapes

Heat leaves a cup of tea in three ways. In conduction, heat passes from one particle to the next by touch, the way a steel spoon in tea gets hot at the handle. In convection, warm air or water rises and carries heat away, like steam curling off a cup. And in radiation, heat travels as invisible infrared rays, the warmth you feel on your face from a fire across the room.

A good flask blocks all three. Most flasks also have a narrow neck, which leaves less room for heat to sneak out at the top. That is also why the stopper matters so much. Leave the lid off, and steam carries heat out of the opening far faster than it can leak through the walls. Every time you pour a cup, a little of that warmth escapes too.

Nothing is a great blanket

Conduction and convection both need particles, such as air, water or metal, to carry the heat. A vacuum has almost none. So heat from the inner wall has very little to pass through on its way to the outer wall.

That leaves radiation, which can cross empty space. It is how sunlight reaches Earth. To stop it, flask makers coat the walls with a shiny layer, like a mirror for heat. Infrared rays hit it and bounce back into the tea.

A scientist with a cold problem

James Dewar was a Scottish scientist working at the Royal Institution in London. In the 1890s he was turning gases like oxygen into liquids, which meant cooling them to about minus 200 °C. Ordinary containers let them boil away in minutes.

In 1892 he made a double-walled glass vessel with a vacuum between the walls, silvered to reflect heat. It kept his liquids cold for much longer. Dewar never patented it. In 1904 two German glassblowers started a company in Berlin to sell flasks to the public. They held a contest to name it, and the winner was Thermos.

Glass or steel

Early flasks were made of thin glass, which was easy to seal but broke if you dropped it. Many people in India remember carrying a flask with a fragile glass bottle inside a metal case on train journeys. Most flasks today are stainless steel, which is tougher, though slightly worse at holding heat.

Scientists still use large Dewar flasks to store liquid nitrogen. The same idea keeps vaccines cold on long journeys to villages and helps hospitals carry blood and organs.

Hot and cold, the same flask

A flask has no idea what is inside it. The vacuum slows heat flowing in as well as out. So on a summer day in Chennai or Phoenix, a flask of iced water stays chilled for hours.

A refrigerator works in the opposite way, actively pumping heat out using electricity. A flask does nothing at all. It just makes heat take the slow road, and it never needs plugging in. Your body uses a similar trick when it keeps its temperature steady, with fat and hair helping to hold warmth in.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Wrapping something in a blanket warms it up.

Truth: A blanket makes no heat of its own. It slows heat escaping, which keeps a warm body warm. Wrap an ice cube in a blanket and it will melt more slowly, not faster.

Try this

Fill two cups with the same warm (not hot) water. Wrap one in a thick towel or sock and leave the other bare. After 20 minutes, dip a finger in each and feel which has stayed warmer.
