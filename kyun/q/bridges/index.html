How do bridges hold up so much weight?

Every bridge is a tug-of-war between two forces, a push and a pull. Good engineers decide which parts get which.

The short answer

When a truck drives onto a bridge, its weight pushes down. The bridge has to carry that weight to the ground at each end, or to pillars in between, without bending too much or breaking.

Engineers think about two forces. Compression squeezes things together, like stacking heavy books on a box. Tension stretches things apart, like a rope in a tug-of-war. Stone and concrete are good at being squeezed but crack when pulled. Steel cables are superb at being pulled but would crumple if squashed. Every bridge design is a way of sending the load through the parts that can handle it.

Lay a ruler across two cups and press the middle. The top surface gets squeezed and the bottom gets stretched. That is a beam bridge in miniature.

Surprising but true

Kolkata's Howrah Bridge, opened in 1943, was put together without a single nut or bolt. Its steel parts are held by rivets.

Arches, beams and triangles

The simplest bridge is a beam, a log across a stream. It works for short gaps but sags if made too long.

Arches are much stronger. An arch turns the weight pressing down into a sideways push that travels around the curve into the ground. Roman engineers built stone arch bridges and aqueducts, some still standing after 2,000 years, as you can see alongside Roman roads.

Truss bridges use frames of triangles. A triangle can't change shape unless one of its sides breaks, which makes it the stiffest simple shape there is. Many old railway bridges across Indian rivers are steel trusses, criss-crossed with diagonal beams. As a train rolls over one, some beams are squeezed and others are stretched, and the triangles share the load between them.

Hanging from cables

For very long gaps, engineers hang the road from cables. In a suspension bridge, two thick main cables are draped over tall towers and anchored at each end. Thinner cables drop down to hold the road. The Golden Gate Bridge in San Francisco, opened in 1937, has a main span of 1,280 metres, longer than a dozen football fields laid end to end.

Cable-stayed bridges run straight cables from the towers directly to the road, in a fan or harp pattern. Mumbai's Bandra–Worli Sea Link, opened in 2009, is one, stretching out over the Arabian Sea.

Why do a triangle's angles always add up to 180 degrees?Tear the three corners off any paper triangle, line them up, and they always make a perfectly straight line.

Famous bridges of India

The Howrah Bridge over the Hooghly in Kolkata is a cantilever bridge. It is built from two huge steel arms reaching out from each bank and joined in the middle. It opened in 1943 and is held together with rivets, not nuts and bolts. Tens of thousands of vehicles and many more people on foot cross it every day.

In Jammu and Kashmir, the Chenab Rail Bridge is a steel arch about 359 metres above the river, higher than the Eiffel Tower is tall. It opened to trains in 2025, after nearly two decades of work in the mountains.

Bridges that grow

In Meghalaya, in northeast India, Khasi and Jaintia communities have trained the roots of rubber fig trees across streams for generations. Over many years, the roots thicken and twist into living bridges. Some are strong enough to carry dozens of people at once, and they get stronger as the trees grow.

Bridges can fail when engineers misjudge a force. In 1940, the Tacoma Narrows Bridge in the US state of Washington twisted wildly in a steady wind and collapsed four months after opening. Nobody died, and engineers have tested bridge designs in wind tunnels ever since.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Bridges are solid and don't move at all.

Truth: Bridges are designed to flex. Steel grows longer on hot days and shorter on cold ones, so bridges have expansion joints, the metal teeth you feel as a bump when you drive over them. Long suspension bridges can also sway several metres sideways in strong winds.

Try this

Lay a sheet of A4 paper as a bridge between two books placed about 15 cm apart. Put coins on it one at a time and count how many it holds. Now fold another sheet into a zigzag, like a paper fan, and try again. The folds make the paper far stiffer, the same idea engineers use in steel beams.
