How does a country of 140 crore people vote?

It's the biggest election on Earth. And the rule is: every voter counts.

The short answer

In a democracy, people choose their leaders by voting. In India, every citizen aged 18 or over can vote, and each vote counts the same, whether the voter is a farmer or a film star.

In 2024, nearly 97 crore people were registered to vote, more than the whole population of Europe. About 64 crore actually voted, over 44 days in seven phases. The Election Commission tries to make sure nobody has to travel more than about 2 km to vote, so it set up over 10 lakh polling stations.

Your vote is secret. Nobody else needs to know who you chose.

Surprising but true

One of the world's highest polling stations is at Tashigang in Himachal Pradesh, over 4,600 m up.

Who runs elections

The Election Commission of India organises elections and makes sure they're free and fair. The Constitution set it up in 1950, and it is independent of the government.

Before an election, it updates the voter list, announces the dates and sets rules, called the Model Code of Conduct, that all parties must follow. Crores of teachers, officials and police officers are drafted in as election staff. They carry voting machines by boat, on foot, on elephants and by helicopter.

The first great election

India's first general election, in 1951–52, was one of the biggest experiments in democracy ever tried. About 17 crore people could vote, and most of them could not read or write.

Every adult could vote from the very start, men and women alike, rich or poor. In many older democracies, women had waited decades for the vote, as you can read in women's vote. To help voters who couldn't read, each party was given a picture symbol. The voting age was 21 until 1989, when it was lowered to 18.

How did women win the right to vote?In some countries women campaigned for more than 70 years to vote. In India, they could vote in the very first national election.

Machines and ink

Voters press a button next to a candidate's name and symbol on an electronic voting machine, used across India since 2004. A printer beside it shows a paper slip for a few seconds, so voters can check their choice. These printers have been used at every booth since 2019.

A mark of special ink on the left forefinger shows you've voted, so nobody votes twice. It contains silver nitrate, which darkens in light and lasts for days. It is made by a company in Mysuru, Karnataka. Since 2013, voters can also choose NOTA, "none of the above".

Booths in surprising places

The rule about reaching voters is taken seriously. Tashigang in Himachal Pradesh, over 4,600 m up, has one of the highest polling stations in the world.

For years, officials walked into Gir forest in Gujarat to set up a booth for a single voter, a priest at a temple deep in lion country. In the Andaman Islands and the north-east, booths are reached by boat and long treks.

Counting day

All the votes from every phase are counted on a single day, weeks after the first ones were cast. Machines are kept sealed and guarded in strong rooms until then. Counting starts in the morning, and by evening the country usually knows the result, which TV channels follow seat by seat.

Different elections

People elect Members of Parliament to the Lok Sabha, members of state assemblies, and local panchayats and municipal councils. A third of seats in panchayats and municipalities are reserved for women.

Why every vote matters

Some elections have been won by just a handful of votes. In 2008, a candidate in Rajasthan lost an assembly seat by one vote. That's why making voting easy for everyone matters, from a voter in a big city to one in a forest.

Myth or fact?

Myth: One vote never makes a difference.

Truth: In the 2008 Rajasthan assembly election, C.P. Joshi lost his seat in Nathdwara by a single vote. Close results happen more often than people think, especially in local elections.

Try this

Hold a secret ballot in your class on something simple, like the next class story. Everyone writes a choice on a slip, folds it and drops it in a box, and two people count the slips in front of everyone. Then try the same vote by show of hands. Talk about whether anyone voted differently when others could see.
