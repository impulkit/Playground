Why do sunflowers have spiral patterns?

Count the spirals in a sunflower. You'll often get 34 one way and 55 the other.

The short answer

The pattern 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13... is famous as the Fibonacci sequence. Each number is the two before it added together.

A sunflower grows its seeds from the centre outwards, one at a time. Each new seed appears at a turn of about 137.5° from the last, an angle called the golden angle. That angle packs seeds tightly with no gaps. A side effect is that the seeds line up in spirals, and the number of spirals is usually a Fibonacci number.

Indian poets and mathematicians found the same numbers much earlier while counting the ways to arrange long and short beats in poetry.

Surprising but true

Indian poets discovered the ‘Fibonacci’ numbers centuries before Fibonacci, by counting rhythms.

Why that angle works

Suppose the plant turned by a simple fraction of a circle, like a half or a third. The seeds would line up in straight spokes, leaving big empty wedges between them. The golden angle is special because it never quite repeats. Each new seed lands in a gap left by the others.

Botanists call the arrangement of leaves and seeds phyllotaxis. Pinecones, pineapples and cauliflowers often show the same spiral numbers. A pineapple usually has 8 spirals one way and 13 the other. The same packing rule shows up in plants as different as daisies and the leaves around a stem.

Poets got there first

In Sanskrit poetry, syllables are short or long, and a long one takes the time of two short ones. Poets wanted to know how many rhythms could fill a line of a given length.

Count them. A line one beat long has just 1 pattern. Two beats give 2 patterns: short-short or long. Three beats give 3, four beats give 5, five beats give 8. Each total is the sum of the two before, because every pattern ends in either a short or a long syllable.

Pingala's work on rhythm, more than 2,000 years ago, hints at this. The scholar Virahanka spelled it out clearly, probably between the 6th and 8th centuries CE. Gopala and Hemachandra described it again in the 1100s.

Numbers that keep growing

Divide each Fibonacci number by the one before it and something odd happens. 8 ÷ 5 is 1.6. 13 ÷ 8 is 1.625. 55 ÷ 34 is about 1.618. The answers creep closer and closer to a single number, about 1.618, known as the golden ratio.

That link is why the golden angle and the Fibonacci numbers belong together. The golden angle is the part of a full circle you get by using the golden ratio to split it. So a plant that turns by that angle ends up with Fibonacci spirals almost for free, without ever counting.

How Fibonacci got the name

Leonardo of Pisa, known later as Fibonacci, put the sequence in his maths book Liber Abaci in 1202. His version was a puzzle about how fast rabbits breed. He had learned Indian numerals while travelling in North Africa, and his book helped spread them across Europe.

The name "Fibonacci sequence" came only in the 1800s, from the French mathematician Édouard Lucas. By then, the Indian poets' work had been forgotten in Europe. Historians now point out that the numbers had an older Indian history.

Not every sunflower obeys

In 2012, hundreds of people in Britain grew sunflowers to mark the centenary of Alan Turing, the computer pioneer, who had studied plant spirals. Scientists counted the seed spirals. Most heads showed Fibonacci numbers, but a good number did not, with near misses or other patterns.

Nature follows rules, but growing things are messy. The spiral is a strong habit, not a law. Similar ideas link these numbers to the golden ratio.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The golden ratio and Fibonacci numbers are hidden in almost everything, from the Mona Lisa to the shape of your body.

Truth: The numbers really do appear in many plant spirals, for a good reason. But many famous claims about buildings, paintings and faces fall apart when people measure carefully.

Try this

Find a pinecone, a pineapple or a cauliflower. Mark one spiral with a dab of chalk or a sticky note, then count all the spirals running that way. Count again in the other direction. Are both numbers in the Fibonacci list?
