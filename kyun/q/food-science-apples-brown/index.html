Why do cut apples turn brown?

The same chemical reaction that turns a sliced apple brown in your lunchbox turns green tea leaves into the black tea your family drinks.

The short answer

When you cut or bite an apple, you break open millions of tiny cells. Inside some of those cells are natural chemicals called polyphenols. Elsewhere in the cell sits an enzyme, a kind of chemical helper, called polyphenol oxidase. In a whole apple they're kept apart.

The knife mixes them together and lets in oxygen from the air. The enzyme then helps oxygen react with the polyphenols, a process called oxidation. That makes new chemicals, which link up into brown pigments, related to the melanin that colours human skin and hair. Within minutes, the white flesh starts to look tanned.

Surprising but true

Scientists have bred apples that hardly brown at all by switching off the genes for the browning enzyme. They went on sale in the USA in 2017.

Why fruit bothers to do this

Browning isn't a mistake. It's part of the plant's defence. When an insect chews into an apple or a potato, the brown chemicals that form can be bitter or harmful to germs and pests. They may help seal the wound, a bit like a scab.

Many fruits and vegetables do it. Bananas go brown after peeling, and their skins darken in the fridge. Avocados, potatoes, pears, mushrooms and brinjal all brown when cut. Some apple varieties brown slowly because they have less of the enzyme or fewer polyphenols.

Tricks to keep slices pale

There are a few ways to stop browning, and each blocks a different part of the reaction.

Lemon juice works twice over. Its acid makes the enzyme sluggish, and its vitamin C reacts with oxygen first, protecting the fruit. Pineapple and orange juice work for the same reason. Cold slows the enzyme down, so slices in the fridge brown more slowly. Covering slices or dropping them in water keeps oxygen away. A pinch of salt in water also slows the enzyme.

Heat destroys the enzyme completely. That's why cooks briefly boil some vegetables before freezing them, and why apple pie filling stays golden instead of turning dark brown.

Why does iron rust?In Delhi stands an iron pillar about 1,600 years old that has barely rusted. Scientists spent decades working out why.

How browning makes your tea

Every leaf of tea starts out green. To make green tea, workers heat the leaves soon after picking, which kills the enzyme and keeps them green. To make black tea, they do the opposite.

In Assam and other tea regions, the fresh leaves are wilted, then rolled or crushed to break their cells, just like cutting an apple. They're spread out in the air, where the enzyme turns them coppery brown. Tea makers often call this step fermentation, though it's really oxidation. It gives black tea its colour, strength and flavour. Raisins, dates and cocoa beans also get some of their brown colour from similar reactions.

Browning you want

Sometimes cooks want food to turn brown. When bread toasts, onions fry or paneer sizzles on a grill, a different reaction makes the colour. Sugars and proteins react together in the heat, a process called the Maillard reaction. It needs no enzyme at all, just high temperature, and it gives toast, roasted coffee and biscuits much of their flavour.

The apple that doesn't blush

In the 2000s, a company in Canada developed a new kind of apple that barely browns when cut. Scientists used genetic engineering to quieten the genes that tell the fruit to make polyphenol oxidase. The USA approved the first of these apples in 2015, and they went on sale as packaged slices in 2017.

Some people welcome them, saying they could cut food waste, since bruised and browned fruit often gets thrown away. Others prefer fruit bred in traditional ways. Either way, it shows how a single enzyme, controlled by a few genes, decides how your fruit looks.

And the strangest part? Browning is a slow-motion cousin of another oxygen reaction you see on old bicycles and gates. Iron meeting oxygen and water makes the orange crust called rust.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A cut apple that has turned brown has gone bad and isn't safe to eat.

Truth: Browning is a harmless chemical change. The apple may taste a little different and look less tempting, but it's perfectly safe. It's rotting, with mould or a bad smell, that means you should throw it out.

Try this

Cut an apple into five slices with an adult's help. Leave one on a plate, dip one in lemon juice, one in salty water, one in plain water and wrap one tightly in cling film. Check every 15 minutes for an hour and line them up from palest to brownest. Which trick won?
