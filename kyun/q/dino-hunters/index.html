What is it like to be a dinosaur hunter?

Sue, one of the biggest and most complete T. rex skeletons ever found, was spotted in 1990 by a fossil hunter who walked over to a cliff while her team's truck had a flat tyre.

The short answer

Being a dinosaur hunter, or palaeontologist, means a mix of long walks in hot, dusty places, careful digging with tiny tools and years of work in labs and museums. Most of the time is spent looking, not digging. Fossil hunters walk slowly over rocky hillsides, scanning the ground for bits of bone that have weathered out of the rock.

When they find something, the real work begins. They clear the rock around it, map and photograph every piece, wrap the bones in plaster and carry them out, sometimes by helicopter. Then preparators spend months or years freeing the bones from rock in a lab. Only after that can scientists study the animal and, if it is new, give it a name.

Surprising but true

Digging up a dinosaur can take a few weeks, but cleaning the bones out of the rock in a laboratory can take years.

Where to look

Dinosaur fossils are found in sedimentary rocks, layers of mud and sand that built up while dinosaurs were alive, between about 230 and 66 million years ago. The best hunting grounds are badlands: dry, rocky places with little soil or plants, where wind and rain keep wearing away the rock and exposing new fossils.

Famous dinosaur country includes the Hell Creek area of Montana and the Dakotas, the Gobi Desert in Mongolia, Alberta in Canada and Patagonia in Argentina. In India, dinosaur fossils come from rocks along the Narmada valley in Gujarat and Madhya Pradesh, and from the Godavari valley.

A day in the field

Field seasons often happen in summer, when the weather is dry. Days start early to beat the heat. Teams carry water, hats, sunscreen, rock hammers, chisels, brushes, dental picks and glue to hold crumbly bones together.

Once a bone is found, the team digs a trench around it and builds a plaster jacket, like a cast on a broken arm. The jacket protects it on the bumpy ride back. Every bone's position is recorded, because how the skeleton lies in the rock tells a story about how the animal died and was buried.

The story of Sue

In August 1990, a team was digging near Faith, South Dakota, when their truck got a flat tyre. While others drove to town for repairs, fossil hunter Sue Hendrickson walked to a cliff she had wanted to check. She spotted bones sticking out of the rock above her.

They belonged to a Tyrannosaurus rex, about 90% complete, one of the most complete ever found. It was named Sue after her. There was a long legal fight over who owned it. In 1997 the skeleton was sold for over 8 million dollars to the Field Museum in Chicago, where visitors still line up to see it.

Dinosaur hunting in India

India's fossil story began in the 1800s, when British officers found dinosaur bones near Jabalpur. In the early 1980s, geologists from the Geological Survey of India found bones of a large meat-eating dinosaur at Rahioli in Gujarat. Scientists from India and the US later studied them, and in 2003 named the animal Rajasaurus, the "royal lizard".

Gujarat's Balasinor area has a dinosaur fossil park, and huge nesting grounds with fossil eggs have been found in Madhya Pradesh's Dhar district. Many Indian fossils come to light because farmers, road workers and schoolteachers notice something odd and report it.

Becoming a palaeontologist

Palaeontologists usually study geology or biology at university, then specialise. Many begin as children who collect rocks, visit museums and read all they can about the dinosaurs of India or wherever they grew up. Some of the most important early finds were made by amateurs, like Mary Anning in England in the 1800s.

Fossils are protected by law in many places, so never dig on your own. Joining a museum programme or a guided fossil trip is the safe way to start, and you might just spot the next great find.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Dinosaur fossils are the dinosaurs' actual bones, just very old.

Truth: Most dinosaur fossils are no longer bone. Over millions of years, minerals from groundwater slowly replaced the bone, turning it into rock with the same shape. That's why fossils are heavy and stony.

Try this

Make a "fossil dig" at home. Bury a few clean chicken bones or small toys in a tray of damp sand, and let it dry. Then dig them out using only a spoon, an old toothbrush and a paintbrush, without breaking anything. Draw a quick map of where each item was found before you lift it, just as real diggers do.
