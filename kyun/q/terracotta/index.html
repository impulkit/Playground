What is terracotta art?

In 1974, farmers digging a well in China struck a clay head. It belonged to an army of thousands of life-sized soldiers buried for over 2,000 years.

The short answer

Terracotta means "baked earth" in Italian. It is clay that has been shaped and then fired in a kiln, a very hot oven, until it turns hard and reddish-brown. The same clay is used for flowerpots and roof tiles. When artists shape it into figures, tiles or decorations, we call it terracotta art.

People have made terracotta for thousands of years, because clay is cheap and found almost everywhere. It can be pressed into moulds, rolled, pinched or carved. After firing, it lasts a very long time, even buried underground.

Some of the most famous artworks in the world are terracotta, from toy carts in the Indus cities to an entire buried army in China.

Surprising but true

The Terracotta Army of China was once brightly painted, with pink faces and red, green and purple clothes. Most of the paint flaked off soon after the figures were dug up.

Clay and fire

Raw clay is soft because it's full of water. As it dries in the sun, it stiffens, but it will turn back to mud if it gets wet. Firing changes it for good. In a kiln at around 900 to 1,000 °C, the tiny grains of clay fuse together.

The iron naturally found in most clay gives terracotta its warm red or orange colour. That's the same shade as many bricks, which are really terracotta blocks.

Unglazed terracotta stays slightly porous, meaning it has tiny holes that let water through. That's why water kept in an earthen matka stays cool. A little seeps out and evaporates from the outside. Glazed terracotta, covered with a glassy coating, holds water instead and can come in bright colours.

Toys from the Indus cities

Some of the oldest terracotta in South Asia comes from the Indus cities, such as Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, more than 4,000 years ago. Archaeologists have found clay animals, little carts with wheels that turned, whistles, figurines and seals.

Many of these look very like toys. A clay bullock cart from Harappa is not so different from carts still seen in Indian villages. The pottery traditions that began there have carried on, with changes, ever since.

An army for an emperor

In 1974, farmers near Xi'an in China were digging a well when they found pieces of a clay figure. Archaeologists soon uncovered pits full of life-sized soldiers, horses and chariots. They were made for the tomb of Qin Shi Huang, the first emperor of a united China, who died in 210 BCE.

Experts estimate there are about 8,000 figures, though not all have been dug up yet. Workers made bodies in moulds, then added faces, hair and armour by hand, so the soldiers look like individuals. Each carried real bronze weapons.

What did ancient China invent?The oldest printed book with a date on it was made in China in 868 CE, almost 600 years before Gutenberg's printing press.

Bengal's clay temples

In Bishnupur, West Bengal, the Malla kings built temples almost entirely of brick and terracotta, mostly in the 17th and 18th centuries. The walls are covered with thousands of carved clay tiles showing scenes from the Ramayana and Mahabharata, Krishna's life, hunting parties, soldiers and ships.

The Jor Bangla temple even copies the curved thatched roof of a Bengali village hut, in clay. Nearby, potters in Panchmura village still make the long-necked Bankura horse, now a symbol of Indian folk craft.

Village guardians

In Tamil Nadu, villages honour the guardian god Aiyanar with huge terracotta horses, some taller than a person, placed at the edge of fields. They are made in pieces, fired in open fires and assembled on the spot. Over the years rain and sun wear them down, and new ones are made.

Across the world, gardeners still use terracotta pots because they let roots breathe. The same baked earth holds a geranium on a windowsill in Ohio and a tulsi plant in a courtyard in Lucknow.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Terracotta Army soldiers were always the reddish-brown colour you see in photos.

Truth: They were painted in bright colours over a coating of lacquer. When the figures were dug up and met dry air, the lacquer curled and the paint flaked off, often within minutes.

Try this

Make a small tile out of air-drying clay or thick dough. While it's soft, press patterns into it with a fork, a pencil end or a leaf. Let it dry for a couple of days. Real terracotta is then fired in a kiln, which only an adult or a potter should do.
