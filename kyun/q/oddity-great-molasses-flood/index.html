What was the Great Molasses Flood?

In January 1919 a giant tank burst in Boston, and a wave of sticky brown syrup taller than a house rushed down the street.

The short answer

On 15 January 1919, a huge steel tank full of molasses burst open in the North End of Boston, in the USA. About 8.7 million litres of the thick, sweet syrup poured out at once. It formed a brown wave up to about 7.5 m high, taller than a two-storey house.

The wave raced through the streets at about 56 km/h. It knocked buildings off their foundations and bent the steel supports of an elevated railway. Twenty-one people died and about 150 were injured. It remains one of the strangest disasters in American history, and it changed how buildings are checked for safety.

Surprising but true

The molasses wave moved at about 56 km/h, faster than a person can run, even though molasses is famous for being slow.

Why was there so much molasses?

Molasses is the dark, sticky syrup left over when sugar is made from sugar cane. It is used in cooking, but in 1919 most of it in Boston was going to be turned into industrial alcohol. That alcohol was used in factories and to make explosives during the First World War.

A company stored it in a giant tank near the harbour, about 15 m tall and 27 m wide. Full, it held roughly 12,000 tonnes of molasses, as heavy as about 2,000 elephants. People who lived nearby said the tank groaned and leaked. The company reportedly painted it brown so the leaks wouldn't show.

The day the tank broke

The few days before had been bitterly cold, but on 15 January the temperature climbed above freezing. Just after noon, people heard a deep rumble and a sound like machine-gun fire. Those were the metal rivets popping out of the tank.

The walls split open. The wave swept through a busy neighbourhood of homes, stables and workshops. It shoved a fire station almost into the harbour and lifted a railway carriage off its tracks. Horses and people were caught in the flood.

Thick as glue

Rescuers waded in as quickly as they could, but the molasses fought back. As the syrup spread out and cooled in the winter air, it became thicker and thicker.

In 2016, scientists at Harvard University tested this with corn syrup in a cold room. They found that the spreading molasses would have cooled fast at its edges, turning almost like glue. That helps explain why people trapped in it couldn't escape. Some liquids behave in even stranger ways, like the ones described in non-Newtonian fluids.

Cleaning up a sticky city

Firefighters sprayed salt water from the harbour onto the streets, which helped to thin the molasses. Even so, the clean-up took weeks. Molasses got tracked across the city on boots, horses and tram seats. Boston Harbour stayed brown for months.

For decades afterwards, some people in the North End claimed that on hot summer days they could still smell molasses. Nobody can prove it, but the story is still told. A small plaque near the spot, in a park beside the harbour, now marks what happened. Many people walk past it every day without knowing that the ground under their feet was once buried in syrup.

Who was to blame?

Families sued the company that owned the tank. After years of hearings, a court decided in 1925 that the tank had been badly built and never properly tested. The company had to pay large sums to the victims' families.

The disaster changed the rules. Massachusetts and other states began demanding that engineers check and sign off on the plans for big structures. Today, molasses is still made wherever sugar cane is crushed. In India, much of it is turned into ethanol, a fuel blended into petrol, and the same sugar cane gives us jaggery and white sugar.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Molasses is so slow it could never be dangerous.

Truth: Molasses flows slowly when poured from a spoon. But millions of litres falling from a tall tank have huge weight behind them. In Boston that weight pushed the wave along faster than people could run.

Try this

With an adult, put two spoonfuls of honey or golden syrup on two plates, one warmed in the sun or on a warm plate, one cooled in the fridge for an hour. Tilt both plates and race them. Cold syrup is far slower, just like the molasses that trapped people in Boston.
