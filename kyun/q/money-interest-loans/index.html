How do loans work?

Nearly 4,000 years ago, a king of Babylon carved limits on interest rates into stone, because borrowing money has always needed rules.

The short answer

A loan is money you borrow now and pay back later. The money you borrow is called the principal. On top of it, you usually pay interest, a kind of rent for using someone else's money. Interest is usually given as a percentage per year.

If you borrow ₹1,00,000 at 10% a year, you pay ₹10,000 a year in interest on top of returning the original amount. Families borrow to buy homes, students to pay for college, farmers to buy seeds, and businesses to grow. Loans make big things possible sooner, but they cost extra, and they must be paid back.

Surprising but true

The Code of Hammurabi, from ancient Babylon, set maximum interest rates for loans of grain and silver. It is one of the oldest known laws about borrowing.

Why lenders charge interest

Someone who lends money gives up the chance to use it themselves. There is also a risk that the borrower won't pay it back. And prices usually rise over time, because of inflation, so money paid back later buys a little less. Interest makes up for all three.

That is how a bank works. You keep savings in the bank, and the bank pays you interest, say 3% to 7% a year. The bank lends that money to others at a higher rate, perhaps 8% to 11% for a home loan in India. The difference pays for staff, buildings and losses on loans that go bad, and leaves some profit.

Simple and compound interest

With simple interest, you pay interest only on the original amount. Borrow ₹1,00,000 at 10% for three years and you pay ₹10,000 each year, ₹30,000 in total.

With compound interest, unpaid interest is added to the debt, and next year you pay interest on that too. After year one you owe ₹1,10,000. After year two, ₹1,21,000. After year three, ₹1,33,100. It grows faster each year, like a snowball rolling downhill. This is the same doubling pattern described by exponents.

A handy shortcut called the Rule of 72 estimates how long money takes to double. Divide 72 by the interest rate. At 8% a year, savings double in about 9 years. At 36% a year, which some credit cards charge on unpaid bills, a debt doubles in about 2 years.

What does a bank do with your money?When you put money in a bank, it does not sit in a box with your name on it. Most of it is lent to someone else almost straight away.

EMIs and credit cards

Most home and car loans in India are repaid through EMIs, or equated monthly instalments. Each month you pay the same amount. Early on, most of it is interest. Later, more of it pays off the principal. A 20-year home loan can end up costing well over the price of the house once all the interest is added.

Credit cards are short-term loans. If you pay the full bill on time, there is usually no interest. If you don't, the interest rate is often very high, frequently above 30% a year in India, and around 20% or more in the United States.

Lenders through history

Lending is ancient. The Code of Hammurabi, carved in Babylon around 1750 BCE, set the highest interest a lender could charge on grain and on silver. Many religions have also had rules about charging interest.

In Indian villages, farmers have often borrowed from local moneylenders at very high rates, sometimes losing their land when they could not repay. The writer Premchand described this hardship in his novel Godaan. In Bangladesh, Muhammad Yunus started Grameen Bank, which gave very small loans to poor villagers, mostly women, who could not get bank loans. He and the bank shared the Nobel Peace Prize in 2006.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Interest is a fee that only borrowers pay.

Truth: Interest works both ways. When you put money in a savings account, the bank borrows it and pays you interest. The bank then lends money to others at a higher rate, and the difference helps pay its costs.

Try this

Lend a friend or family member 10 pretend coins (buttons work well), with a rule that they must pay back 1 extra coin for every 10 they owe at the end of each "year". Play for five rounds without any repayments, adding the interest each round. How many do they owe at the end?
