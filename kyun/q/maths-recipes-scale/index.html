How do you make a recipe for 100 people?

Multiply a recipe for 4 by 25 and you might get 100 plates of food that is far too salty, still raw in the middle and burnt on the bottom.

The short answer

Start with simple maths. If a recipe feeds 4 and you need to feed 100, divide 100 by 4. That gives a scale factor of 25. Multiply every ingredient by 25, so 2 cups of rice becomes 50 cups and 1 onion becomes 25.

That gets you close, and for many dishes it is enough. But big cooks know three traps. Strong flavours like salt and chilli can overpower a big pot, so they are added slowly and tasted. Big pots and tins heat in strange ways, because of how area and volume grow. And a kitchen needs equipment that is big enough. Scaling a recipe is really two jobs: maths for the shopping list and cooking sense for everything else.

Surprising but true

A cake tin twice as wide as yours holds four times as much batter, not twice as much.

The kitchen that feeds a city

At the Golden Temple in Amritsar, the free kitchen, or langar, often serves 50,000 or more people a day, and well over a lakh on festival days. Volunteers sit in rows rolling rotis, and machines can turn out thousands an hour.

Nobody there multiplies a family recipe by 25,000. Langar cooks work from experience, in units of whole sacks of flour and giant cauldrons, called degs, that can each hold enough dal for thousands. They taste, adjust and taste again.

What is the Golden Temple?The holiest place of the Sikhs has four doors, one on each side, so that anyone from any direction can walk in.

India's school lunch scheme is even bigger. Under PM POSHAN, the mid-day meal programme, more than ten crore children are given lunch on school days. Some central kitchens cook more than a lakh meals before the bell rings for lunch.

Why bigger isn't simple

Here is the trick that catches people out. Take a square tin 20 cm on each side. Double the side to 40 cm and the floor of the tin is 2 × 2 = 4 times bigger. Keep the batter the same depth, and you need four times as much.

Now double a ball of dough in every direction. It grows 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 times in volume. This is the square-cube rule, and it explains why big things behave differently from small ones. A giant cake has much more inside compared with its surface. Heat comes in through the surface, so the middle cooks last. Big bakers often lower the oven temperature and bake for longer, so the outside doesn't burn while the centre is still gooey. The same rule explains why an elephant needs thick legs, and why area and volume puzzles show up everywhere.

Salt, spice and the taste test

Cooks often say: scale the rice, scale the vegetables, but go slowly on salt and spice. In a huge pot, less liquid boils away for each litre of food, so flavours don't concentrate as much. Some spices also seem to get stronger in large batches.

The safe method is to add about three quarters of the full amount, taste, then add more if needed. You can always add salt. You can't take it out.

Fractions in the kitchen

Scaling down is a maths puzzle too. Halving a recipe that uses 3 eggs gives you one and a half. Cooks either beat 2 eggs and use three quarters, or round up and accept a slightly richer cake. Turning 2⅔ cups into a recipe for one person means dividing fractions, which is good practice for a test, and even better practice for dessert.

Cooking for crowds at home

At an Indian wedding, a halwai might cook for 500 guests over two days. In the USA, a Thanksgiving dinner for 20 means a turkey that needs hours in the oven. In both kitchens the rule is the same: write the scaled list down, buy a little extra and taste constantly. The best large-scale cooks treat recipes as a ratio, not a list of fixed amounts, which is exactly how a cup of chai stays the same whether you make one or fifty.

Myth or fact?

Myth: To feed ten times as many people, you just multiply every ingredient and cooking time by ten.

Truth: Ingredient amounts mostly scale up, but salt and spices usually need less than the full increase, and cooking times don't scale in a straight line at all. A huge pot takes much longer to heat, but nowhere near ten times as long.

Try this

Take a recipe from a book at home and work out the amounts for your whole class. First find the scale factor by dividing the class size by the number of people the recipe serves. Multiply every ingredient by it. Then ask an adult cook which numbers they would change, and why.
