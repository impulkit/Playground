What can dinosaur footprints tell us?

A bone tells you a dinosaur died. A footprint tells you what it was doing on one ordinary afternoon.

The short answer

Dinosaur footprints are fossils of behaviour. A single print shows the shape of a foot. A line of prints, called a trackway, shows how the animal walked, how fast it was going and whether it was alone or in a group.

Footprints can also show things bones cannot. They prove that dinosaurs walked with their legs under their bodies and carried their tails off the ground. Footprints are far more common than skeletons, and they record animals in the place where they were alive, not where they died.

Surprising but true

The first dinosaur footprints studied in America, found in 1802, were thought to have been made by Noah's raven.

Noah's raven

In 1802, a farm boy named Pliny Moody was ploughing a field in Massachusetts when he turned up a stone slab with three-toed prints. People at the time thought they had been made by giant birds, perhaps Noah's raven from the story of the flood.

In the 1830s, the geologist Edward Hitchcock collected thousands of these tracks in the Connecticut River valley. He too thought they were bird tracks. He was not as wrong as it sounds. The prints were left by meat-eating dinosaurs, the group that later gave rise to birds. That is why many dinosaur tracks look like a chicken's footprints, only much bigger.

A chase in Texas

In 1938, the fossil hunter Roland T. Bird studied tracks along the Paluxy River near Glen Rose, Texas. One trackway showed a giant long-necked dinosaur walking along, with a large meat-eater following in its steps. Some scientists think it was a hunt. Others say the two may have passed at different times.

Bird dug up part of the trackway, and it is now on display at the American Museum of Natural History in New York. Elsewhere in Texas, he found tracks of about 20 long-necked dinosaurs travelling together, among the first evidence that some dinosaurs lived in herds.

Reading the clues

Each footprint is a little like a fingerprint, with its own shape. Scientists look at the number of toes, the claws and the width of the foot. Three slim toes with sharp tips usually mean a meat-eater. Wide, round prints mean a long-necked plant-eater. Leg length can be worked out from foot size, and the gap between prints gives the speed.

Matching a print to a species is hard, though. Many dinosaurs had similar feet, and the same foot left different prints in soft or firm mud. That is why tracks usually get their own names, separate from the animals that made them. A print called Eubrontes, for example, describes a shape of footprint, not a particular dinosaur. Scientists who study tracks, called ichnologists, also look at how the mud squeezed up around a foot. That can show whether the animal was slipping, turning or pushing off hard.

Footprints in India

India has its share of tracks. Near Jaisalmer in Rajasthan, scientists have found three-toed footprints about 200 million years old, from the early Jurassic, pressed into rock that was once a beach. Footprints have also been reported from Kutch in Gujarat, along with eggs and bones in other parts of the country.

Records around the world

At Cal Orck'o in Bolivia, a quarry wall the size of several football fields holds thousands of footprints. The rock was once flat lakeshore mud, later tilted almost upright, so the tracks now seem to climb a cliff. Near Broome, in Western Australia, sauropod prints about 1.7 m long are among the biggest ever found, long enough for a child to lie down in.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Giant human footprints were found beside dinosaur tracks in a Texas riverbed.

Truth: Scientists studied the claimed "human" prints at the Paluxy River in Glen Rose. They turned out to be worn or mud-filled dinosaur tracks, and some were carvings. No human tracks have ever been found with dinosaur tracks.

Try this

Spread a thin layer of damp sand or flour on a tray. Walk two fingers across it slowly, then quickly, then make a "herd" with several fingers at once. Look at the gaps and the depth of each print. Can a friend tell which trail was made fast, without watching?
