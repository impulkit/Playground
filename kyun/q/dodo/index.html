What happened to the dodo?

Less than a hundred years after sailors first saw a dodo, there were none left anywhere on Earth.

The short answer

The dodo was a big, flightless bird that lived only on Mauritius, an island in the Indian Ocean. It was a kind of giant pigeon, about 1 m tall. Sailors first saw it in 1598. By the end of the 1600s, it was gone.

People did hunt dodos, but the bigger problem was what they brought with them. Pigs, rats, monkeys and dogs ate dodo eggs and chicks, and settlers cut down the forests. A bird that nested on the ground and had never met a predator could not cope.

Surprising but true

One of the most accurate paintings of a living dodo was made in India, at the court of the Mughal emperor Jahangir.

An island with no enemies

Mauritius lies about 2,000 km off the coast of Africa, far from any big land. Before people arrived, there were no ground-dwelling mammals that hunted birds. The dodo's ancestors were pigeons that flew there long ago.

With plenty of fallen fruit and no predators, flying became unnecessary. Over many generations, the birds grew larger and their wings shrank. This happens again and again on islands. New Zealand had giant flightless moas, and Madagascar had elephant birds.

Sailors and settlers

Dutch sailors landed on Mauritius in 1598. They wrote that dodos were tough to chew. They also noted that the birds were unafraid and easy to catch. Ships began stopping there for fresh water and food.

The Dutch set up a settlement in 1638. They brought pigs, goats and dogs, and rats came ashore from the ships. Crab-eating monkeys were also brought to the island. Dodos laid a single egg on the ground, which made them easy targets. The last widely accepted sighting was in 1662. Some scientists think a few may have survived until around 1690.

A dodo in a Mughal painting

A few live dodos were taken away by ship. At least one seems to have reached India. Around 1625, the Mughal court artist Ustad Mansur, famous for his pictures of animals, painted a dodo among other birds for the emperor Jahangir. The painting shows grey feathers, a big hooked beak and yellow legs.

Scientists value it as one of the most realistic pictures of a living dodo. Many European paintings show fat, comical birds. Some of those may have been overfed captives, or copies of copies. Mughal painters worked from life.

What is left

For a long time, almost nothing remained. The Oxford University Museum of Natural History has the only dodo with surviving soft tissue: a dried head and a foot. In 1865, a schoolteacher on Mauritius found hundreds of dodo bones in a marsh called the Mare aux Songes. Most dodo skeletons in museums today are built from those marsh bones.

DNA from dodo bones confirmed in 2002 that it was a pigeon. Its closest living relative is the Nicobar pigeon, a shimmering green bird found on islands in the Bay of Bengal, including India's Nicobar Islands. The dodo was a pigeon and therefore, like all birds, a kind of dinosaur.

A famous symbol

In 1865, the same year as the marsh discovery, Lewis Carroll put a Dodo in Alice's Adventures in Wonderland. His real name was Charles Dodgson, and some people think he chose the bird as a joke about his own name. The book made the dodo famous.

Today the dodo appears on the coat of arms of Mauritius. The phrase "dead as a dodo" became a way of saying something is gone for good. It is also a warning that extinctions are still happening.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The dodo died out because it was fat, slow and stupid.

Truth: The dodo was well suited to its island, which had no ground predators. It had no reason to fear people or animals. It died out because humans and the animals they brought arrived suddenly, not because it was foolish.
