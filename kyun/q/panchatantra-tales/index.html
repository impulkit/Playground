Why do Panchatantra stories use animals?

A clever hare tricks a lion into jumping down a well. Stories like this were first told to teach princes how to rule.

The short answer

The Panchatantra uses animals because animals make lessons easier to swallow. A story about a foolish lion or a sly jackal can teach a hard truth about power, trust and greed without insulting any real person. Readers recognise human types in the animals, laugh at them and remember the lesson.

The book's own opening tells why it was written. A king named Amarashakti despaired of his three dull sons, who hated their lessons. A wise old scholar named Vishnu Sharma promised to teach them the art of wise living, called niti, in six months. He did it with stories, and the princes learned.

Surprising but true

By some counts, there are more than 200 versions of the Panchatantra in over 50 languages.

A book for princes

Pancha means five and tantra can mean a treatise or book, so Panchatantra means "five books". Each book has a main story with many smaller fables tucked inside, as characters tell each other tales to prove a point. The five books deal with losing friends, winning friends, war and peace, losing what you have gained, and acting without thinking.

The first book follows two jackals, Karataka and Damanaka, at a lion's court. The scheming Damanaka breaks up the lion's friendship with a bull, with sad results. It's a lesson for a ruler about trusting flattering advisers.

Why animals work

Scholars offer several reasons. Animals come with ready-made personalities. Most listeners already think of a jackal as cunning and a lion as proud, which saves the storyteller time. Animals also make stories safe. A minister could tell a king a story about a stupid lion without being thrown into prison.

Animal stories are memorable and funny too, which matters for children and for princes who would rather not study. In a world where most people lived close to forests and fields, the animals were familiar neighbours. The fables of Aesop, from ancient Greece, use the same trick, and so do India's own Jataka tales.

Famous tales

In "The Lion and the Hare", a greedy lion demands one animal a day for his dinner. When it's the hare's turn, he arrives late and tells the lion that another lion stopped him. He leads the angry lion to a well, where it sees its own reflection, leaps in to attack its "rival", and never climbs out.

In "The Monkey and the Crocodile", a crocodile invites his monkey friend home on his back, then admits halfway across the river that his wife wants to eat the monkey's heart. Thinking fast, the monkey says he left his heart up in the fruit tree and must fetch it. Once safely back in the branches, he stays there. In "The Tortoise and the Geese", a tortoise carried through the sky on a stick opens its mouth to chatter, and falls.

A book that travelled the world

In the 500s CE, a Persian doctor named Burzoe is said to have carried the Panchatantra from India to the court of King Khosrow I and translated it into Middle Persian. In the 700s, the writer Ibn al-Muqaffa turned it into Arabic as Kalila wa Dimna, named after the two jackals. From Arabic it passed into Hebrew, Greek, Latin, Spanish and German.

In the 1600s, the French poet Jean de La Fontaine said some of his famous fables were inspired by the Indian sage "Pilpay". The name probably comes from Bidpai, the storyteller of the Arabic version. Today the stories reach children through books, cartoons, Amar Chitra Katha comics and hundreds of translations.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Panchatantra is just a children's book.

Truth: It was written as a guide for princes about politics, friendship and good judgement. Children love the stories, but adults have studied them for centuries.

Try this

Write your own Panchatantra-style story. Choose two animals whose natures fit your idea, such as a boastful peacock and a patient tortoise. Give the story a problem, a clever twist and a one-line lesson at the end. For an extra challenge, have one character tell a shorter story inside your story.
