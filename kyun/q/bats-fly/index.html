Are bats birds?

They fly, they have wings... but look closely at those wings.

The short answer

No. Bats are mammals, like cats, cows and people. They have fur, and mothers feed their babies milk. Birds have feathers and lay eggs. Bats and birds learned to fly separately, millions of years apart.

Bats are the only mammals that can flap and fly, rather than just glide. They are also very common. There are more than 1,400 kinds, about one in every five kinds of mammal on Earth. In India, big fruit bats called flying foxes hang in noisy groups from tall trees in many towns. At dusk they fly off to feed on fruit and flowers.

Surprising but true

A bat's wing is a hand. The skin stretches between four very long finger bones, and the short thumb sticks out as a hook for climbing.

A wing made from a hand

Look at your hand and spread your fingers. Now picture each finger many times longer, with thin skin stretched between them, down to your ankle. That is a bat's wing. The thumb stays short and free, with a claw for clinging and climbing.

Because the wing has so many bones and joints, a bat can change its shape with every beat. A bird's wing is stiffer. This is one reason bats can twist and turn so sharply after insects. Long before bats, flying reptiles called pterosaurs also flew on wings of skin, but theirs hung mainly from one enormous finger. A bat's wing skin is thin but tough, and it heals quickly if torn. It is dotted with tiny hairs that seem to help the bat feel how the air is flowing over it.

Hunting by sound

Most small bats hunt insects at night. They shout high-pitched calls, too high for our ears, and listen to the echoes that bounce back. This is called echolocation. It lets a bat find a moth in complete darkness and dodge thin branches.

Many big fruit bats do not echolocate at all. They find food with sharp eyes and a strong sense of smell. When a bat rests, it hangs upside down. Its toe tendons lock into place, so hanging takes almost no effort, even while it sleeps.

Long lives and real dangers

For their size, bats live a very long time. A mouse may live two years, but a small Brandt's bat in Siberia was found to be at least 41 years old. Most bats have just one baby, called a pup, each year. In crowded caves, mothers find their own pup among thousands by its call and smell.

Bats face serious threats. In North America, a fungus disease called white-nose syndrome has killed millions of hibernating bats since it was first found in New York State in 2006. In India, old roosting trees are often cut down. Fruit bats can also carry some viruses, such as Nipah, which has caused outbreaks in Kerala. That is a good reason never to touch a bat, and to leave out fruit that an animal has nibbled.

Gardeners of the night

Bats are good for people. Insect-eating bats gobble up huge numbers of mosquitoes and crop pests. Fruit bats drop seeds far from the parent tree, which helps forests spread. Bats pollinate the flowers of many plants, including wild bananas, and the agave plants of Mexico. In Southeast Asia, the smelly durian fruit depends largely on bats to carry its pollen.

In Texas, Bracken Cave is home to millions of Mexican free-tailed bats every summer. When they pour out at sunset, the swirling cloud can be picked up on weather radar. In Austin, people gather on a city bridge on summer evenings just to watch more than a million bats leave for the night. To see how differently birds solve the same problem, read how birds fly.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Bats are blind.

Truth: Every bat can see. Many fruit bats have large eyes and see well at dusk, and the bats that use echoes to hunt still use their eyes too.

Try this

Spread your hand and stretch a sheet of kitchen cling film or a thin plastic bag across your fingers, with a grown-up's help. Now curl and spread your fingers. Watch how the "wing" changes shape. Bats do this all the time in flight, which makes them very quick at turning.
