How do joints work?

One doctor cracked the knuckles of only his left hand for more than 60 years to test an old warning. Neither hand got arthritis.

The short answer

A joint is a place where bones meet. Without joints, your skeleton would be one stiff piece, and you couldn't walk, write or wave.

Most of the joints that move are built the same way. The ends of the bones are covered with smooth, slippery cartilage. The joint is wrapped in a capsule filled with a thick, clear liquid called synovial fluid, which works like oil in a machine. Tough straps called ligaments hold the bones together, while muscles attached by tendons pull on the bones to make them move.

Surprising but true

The shoulder can move in more directions than any other joint in your body, which is also why it is the joint most often dislocated.

Hinges, balls and pivots

Different joints move in different ways, and engineers recognise many of them from machines.

Your knees and elbows are hinge joints. Like a door hinge, they mostly bend and straighten in one direction. Your hips and shoulders are ball-and-socket joints: a rounded bone end sits in a cup, letting the arm or leg swing round in almost any direction.

When you shake your head to say no, you use a pivot joint between the top two bones of your neck. The joint at the base of your thumb is a saddle joint, which lets the thumb swing across to touch each fingertip. Small gliding joints in your wrists and ankles let bones slide over each other.

Some joints barely move at all. The bones of your skull are joined by wiggly seams that lock them in place to protect your brain.

Slippery by design

Healthy cartilage soaked in synovial fluid is extremely slippery, with less friction than most man-made bearings. That's how a knee can bend millions of times over a lifetime.

Cartilage has no blood supply, though, so it heals slowly if damaged. Over many years, especially after injuries or with heavy wear, it can thin. That is one cause of arthritis, which makes joints stiff and sore. Doctors can replace a badly worn hip or knee with a metal and plastic one. The British surgeon John Charnley developed a successful hip replacement in the 1960s, and today it is one of the most common operations in the world.

The knuckle-cracking question

Many children are told that cracking their knuckles will give them arthritis. An American doctor named Donald Unger decided to test it on himself. For more than 60 years, he cracked the knuckles of his left hand but not his right. In 1998 he reported that neither hand had arthritis. Larger studies have also found no link. His patient experiment earned him an Ig Nobel Prize in 2009, a jokey award for research that makes people laugh and then think.

The pop itself comes from the synovial fluid. When you pull the joint apart, a gas-filled space suddenly opens up in the fluid. Researchers in Canada filmed it inside a finger with an MRI scanner in 2015.

Keeping joints happy

Joints like to move. Activity keeps the muscles around them strong, and strong muscles take strain off the joints. Sports like swimming, cycling and gymnastics help keep joints flexible.

Sprains happen when a ligament is stretched too far, often at the ankle. Warming up before sport, wearing proper shoes and not playing through sharp pain all help. If a joint swells up badly or won't move after a fall, tell an adult so a doctor can take a look.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Cracking your knuckles gives you arthritis.

Truth: Studies have found no link between knuckle cracking and arthritis. It may annoy people around you, but it doesn't seem to harm the joints.

Try this

Go on a joint hunt with your own body. Bend each finger at every knuckle, then try to wiggle the same knuckles from side to side. Do the same with your elbow, wrist, shoulder, neck and ankle. Make a table of which joints work like a hinge, bending one way only, and which can swing round in a circle.
