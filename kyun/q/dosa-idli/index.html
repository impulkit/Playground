Why are dosas and idlis fermented?

Every idli you have ever eaten was puffed up overnight by billions of invisible microbes working in a steel pot.

The short answer

Idli and dosa batter is made by soaking rice and urad dal, grinding them into a thick paste, and leaving it overnight. While the kitchen sleeps, microbes in the batter wake up and feed.

Lactic acid bacteria turn some of the sugars into acid, which gives the batter its gentle sour taste. Bacteria and yeasts also give off bubbles of carbon dioxide gas, so by morning the batter has risen and is full of tiny holes. Steam it and you get soft, spongy idlis. Spread it thin on a hot griddle and you get a crisp dosa. Without the overnight wait, both would come out flat and heavy.

Surprising but true

The same batter can make a fat, spongy idli or a thin, crisp dosa. The difference is mostly how it is cooked.

Who is doing the work

No one adds yeast to idli batter. The microbes are already there, on the rice and dal, on the grinder and on the cook's hands. Scientists who studied idli batter found that one group of lactic acid bacteria grows fastest and makes most of the gas.

As the bacteria multiply, the batter becomes more acidic. Many harmful germs cannot live in acid, so the fermentation also helps keep the batter safe. It works best between about 25 and 35°C, which is why idli comes so easily in a hot South Indian kitchen.

Are all germs bad?Right now, about as many bacteria live in and on you as there are cells in your body, and most of them are on your side.

Why urad dal matters

Rice alone would not rise well. Urad dal, a small black lentil that is white once its skin is removed, contains a slippery, gummy substance. When it is ground, this goo forms a sticky web in the batter.

That web traps the gas bubbles the way gluten does in wheat bread. Cooks say the dal should be ground until it is fluffy and light, almost like whipped cream, and many grind it separately from the rice for this reason. Dal also adds protein, so a plate of idli with sambar is a well-balanced meal.

Good for your stomach

Fermentation partly breaks down the starch and proteins before you eat them. That is one reason idli is a common first food for babies and a gentle meal for people who are unwell. Studies have found that fermentation can raise the amounts of some B vitamins in the batter.

Steaming adds no oil at all. A typical idli is light, which is why it is a favourite breakfast in hospitals and homes from Bengaluru to New Jersey.

Where idli came from

Dosai, an early form of dosa, appears in Tamil writing from well over a thousand years ago, according to the food historian K.T. Achaya. The first clear mention of idli, called iddalige, is in a Kannada text written in 920 CE. But that early idli seems to have been made from dal alone and not fermented.

Achaya suggested that the fermented, steamed idli may have come from Indonesia, where similar steamed cakes were made, perhaps carried by traders or cooks between the 8th and 12th centuries. Other historians are not convinced. What is clear is that by a few hundred years ago, the rice-and-dal idli had become South India's own.

A breakfast that went everywhere

Udupi in coastal Karnataka is famous for its vegetarian restaurants, and cooks from there opened eateries across India in the 20th century. Masala dosa, a crisp dosa wrapped around spiced potato, became a favourite in Chennai, Mumbai and far beyond.

Today you can buy ready-made batter in packets in Indian shops in Singapore, London and Texas. Even so, the morning check of the rising batter, with a lifted lid and a quick sniff, is a ritual in millions of homes.

Quick facts

Main ingredients
rice and urad dal
Ratio
often about 3 or 4 parts rice to 1 part dal
Fermenting time
about 8–12 hours, longer in cold weather
Who does the work
lactic acid bacteria and yeasts
Idli is
steamed; dosa is fried on a griddle

Myth or fact?

Myth: Fermented food is just food that has started to go bad.

Truth: Spoiling is caused by unwanted microbes. Fermentation is controlled growth of helpful ones, which make the food sour, which in turn keeps many harmful germs away. Curd, bread, cheese and idli all depend on it.

Try this

Next time someone at home grinds idli or dosa batter, stick a strip of tape on the outside of the container to mark the level. Check again the next morning and measure how far it rose. Smell it too. The sour smell comes from acid made by the microbes.
