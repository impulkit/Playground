How are banknotes made?

A banknote isn't really paper. Most are made of cotton, which is why a forgotten note can survive a trip through the washing machine.

The short answer

Banknotes are printed on special paper made mostly from cotton, sometimes mixed with linen. In some countries they're printed on thin plastic, called polymer. The paper is made in secure mills, with watermarks and security threads built in while it's still wet pulp.

Then printing presses add layer after layer: background patterns, colour-changing ink, tiny writing, a portrait and serial numbers. Large sheets with dozens of notes each are checked by machines and people, cut into single notes, counted and sent to the central bank. In India, the Reserve Bank of India's notes come from four presses: in Nashik, Dewas, Mysuru and Salboni.

Surprising but true

The US government says its banknotes can be folded back and forth about 4,000 times before they tear.

Cotton, not trees

Ordinary paper is made from wood pulp, which turns soft and falls apart when wet. Banknote paper is made from cotton fibres, often from leftovers of cotton mills. Cotton is long and strong, so notes survive pockets, wallets and the occasional wash. The US government says its notes can be folded back and forth about 4,000 times before they tear.

US notes are 75% cotton and 25% linen. India's notes use cotton too, with paper made at mills in Narmadapuram (once called Hoshangabad) and Mysuru. During papermaking, the pulp is pressed thinner in some places and thicker in others, creating a watermark, a picture you can see when you hold the note up to the light. A security thread is woven into the paper at the same time.

Printing that fights forgers

Forgers try to copy money, so banknotes are packed with tricks. Intaglio printing presses ink so hard that it stands up from the paper. Colour-shifting ink changes from green to blue as you tilt the note. Micro-lettering is so small it looks like a line until you use a magnifying glass.

Some features glow under ultraviolet light. Others are holograms or shiny strips. Indian notes have a see-through register, where parts of a design on the front and back fit together perfectly when held up to light. They also carry raised marks so that people who are blind can tell values apart.

A thousand years of paper money

The first government paper money was used in China during the Song dynasty, about 1,000 years ago. Merchants in Sichuan had been using paper receipts instead of carrying heavy iron coins, and the government took over printing them. When the traveller Marco Polo visited China in the 1200s, he was astonished that people traded paper for real goods.

India's first paper notes were issued by private banks in the late 1700s. The Reserve Bank of India, set up in 1935, began printing its own notes in 1938.

Plastic money

In 1988, Australia introduced the first polymer notes in wide use, made of a tough plastic film. They're harder to forge and last much longer than cotton notes. Canada, the UK, Singapore and many other countries now use them too.

How long does a note last? The US Federal Reserve estimates a $1 note lasts about 7 years, while a $100 note, used less often, lasts about 23 years. Worn-out notes are shredded. In India, old notes are shredded and sometimes turned into bricks or pressed boards.

Fewer notes in your pocket?

In India, many people now pay with phones using UPI, and in some cities, shopkeepers prefer a QR code to cash. Yet India still prints billions of notes each year, and cash remains important where internet is patchy.

The value of a note doesn't come from the cotton or ink. It comes from trust that others will accept it. If governments print too much, that trust can wobble, and prices climb, a problem economists call inflation.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A country can get rich just by printing lots more money.

Truth: If a country prints much more money without making more goods and services, prices rise and each note buys less. This is called inflation, and in extreme cases it has made money nearly worthless.

Try this

With an adult, look at a banknote closely. Hold it up to the light to find the watermark and the thread. Tilt it to see whether any colour shifts. Run a finger over the portrait and the corner marks to feel the raised ink. Count how many security features you can find.
