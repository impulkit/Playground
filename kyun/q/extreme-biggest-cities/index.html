Which cities have the most people?

In 2025 the UN counted again and found that Tokyo, the world's biggest city for decades, had slipped to third place.

The short answer

By the United Nations' newest count, published in November 2025, the city with the most people is Jakarta in Indonesia, with about 42 million. That's more people than live in all of Canada. Second is Dhaka in Bangladesh, with about 37 million, and third is Tokyo, with about 33 million.

India has two cities in the top ten. Delhi is fourth with about 30 million, more than the whole of Australia, and Kolkata is ninth with about 22 million. Cairo, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Manila and Seoul fill the other spots.

Surprising but true

Jakarta, the world's biggest city by the UN's 2025 count, has more people than the whole of Canada.

Where does a city end?

Counting a city sounds simple until you try to find its edge. Delhi's official limits hold far fewer people than the giant urban sprawl that spills into Haryana and Uttar Pradesh. New York City has about 8 million people, but nearly 20 million live in the wider region around it.

For its 2025 report, the UN tried to be fair to everyone. It chopped the whole planet into squares one kilometre wide. Any square with at least 1,500 people became "city", and squares that touched were joined together. That single change pushed Jakarta and Dhaka above Tokyo, whose old figure had included quieter towns further out.

Why do cities keep growing?In 1837, Chicago had about 4,000 people. Just over 50 years later, it had more than a million.

Why some cities explode

Dhaka is one of the most crowded places on Earth. Every year, families arrive from villages hit by floods and river erosion, looking for work in the garment factories. Lagos, Kinshasa and Karachi are also growing very fast.

Tokyo is going the other way. Japan has more deaths than births each year, so the country is shrinking, and its capital is now growing only slowly. Cities rise and fall with the people who move to them.

A short history of big

About 2,000 years ago, ancient Rome may have held around a million people, an enormous number for its time. After Rome shrank, no city in Europe got that big again for well over a thousand years. Around 1800, Beijing was probably the largest city anywhere. London took over during the 1800s, pulled up by factories and trade.

In 1950, only two cities on Earth had 10 million people or more: New York and Tokyo. These giants are called megacities. By 2025 the UN counted 33 of them, and most are in Asia. The UN also found that about 45 percent of all people now live in cities, with many more in towns.

India's giants

Delhi has grown from under 20 lakh people around 1950 into one of the world's biggest urban areas, drawing people from across the country for work and study. Kolkata, once the capital of British India, has been a giant city for well over a century.

Mumbai isn't in the UN's new top ten, but it's still one of the most crowded cities on Earth, squeezed onto a narrow peninsula with the sea on three sides. Bengaluru, Hyderabad and Chennai are among India's fastest-growing cities.

Counting all these people is a huge job. India is supposed to hold a census every ten years. The last one took place in 2011, and the next is planned for 2027.

Too big for comfort

Huge size brings huge problems. Jakarta is sinking. People pumped so much water from under the ground that parts of the city drop several centimetres every year, and some areas sit below sea level. Indonesia is building a new capital, Nusantara, on the island of Borneo, partly because of this.

Big cities also need water, buses, schools, toilets and clean air for millions at once, which is why urban planning matters so much. And the strangest part? Jakarta isn't alone, because several other giant cities are slowly sinking into the ground as well.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Everyone agrees on a single list of the world's biggest cities.

Truth: Lists disagree a lot, because some count only the area inside official city limits, some count the whole region people commute from, and the UN now counts joined-up crowded land. Tokyo can be first, third or lower depending on the method.

Try this

Look at a map of your own town or city and try to draw its edge with a pencil. Is the edge where the buildings stop, where the city council's area ends, or where people still travel in every day for work? Ask two family members and compare. Each line gives a different population, which is exactly the argument experts have.
