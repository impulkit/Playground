How does your body know it's thirsty?

A few gulps of water make you feel less thirsty within seconds, long before the water reaches your blood. Your brain is predicting what's coming.

The short answer

Your body is more than half water, and it needs to keep that water in balance. When you lose water by sweating, breathing and going to the toilet, your blood becomes slightly saltier and its volume drops a little.

Special sensor cells in the hypothalamus, a small area at the base of the brain, notice the change. Even a rise of just one or two per cent in saltiness is enough. The brain then makes you feel thirsty, so you drink. At the same time it releases a hormone that tells your kidneys to save water, which is why your pee gets darker when you haven't drunk enough.

Surprising but true

In 1971, an Indian doctor named Dilip Mahalanabis used a simple drink of water, salt and sugar to save thousands of refugees from cholera, at a time when many doctors thought only drips could work.

Where the water goes

An adult loses around 2 to 3 litres of water a day, more on hot days or during sport. Some leaves as sweat, some as water vapour in your breath (you can see it fog up a cold window), and a lot as urine.

You replace it by drinking, and by eating. Fruit like watermelon and oranges, dal, rice and curd all contain water. Your body even makes a little water itself when it breaks down food.

The quick switch-off

A few sips can make thirst fade almost at once. It takes 10 minutes or more for water to be absorbed from your gut into your blood. So your blood is still salty when the thirst disappears.

Scientists, including Zachary Knight's team at the University of California, San Francisco, found that sensors in the mouth and throat track how much you're swallowing. They send signals that quickly calm the thirst neurons in the brain, as if the brain is predicting that water is on its way. This stops you from drinking far more than you need.

How do camels survive the desert?A thirsty camel can drink about 100 litres of water in a quarter of an hour, about five big buckets' worth.

A life-saving drink

Severe diarrhoea, from illnesses like cholera, can drain water and salts from the body dangerously fast, especially in small children. For a long time, the main treatment was a drip into a vein, which needs trained staff and supplies.

In the 1960s, scientists working in Dhaka and Kolkata showed that a drink of water with the right amounts of salt and sugar could be absorbed by the gut even during cholera. In 1971, during the Bangladesh Liberation War, the Indian doctor Dilip Mahalanabis ran a refugee camp near the border where cholera broke out. Drips ran short, so his team gave patients this oral rehydration solution, or ORS. Far fewer died than usual. ORS is now one of the cheapest and most important medicines in the world, recommended by the WHO.

How much should you drink?

The old rule of "eight glasses a day" isn't based on strong evidence. Needs vary with your size, how active you are and the weather. A child playing cricket in a Chennai summer needs much more than one reading indoors in winter.

For most healthy children, drinking when thirsty, having water with meals and drinking extra in hot weather or during sport works well. Drinking water beats sugary drinks. Feeling dizzy, very tired or having a headache on a hot day can be signs of not drinking enough, so tell an adult.

Thirst in desert animals

Some animals survive with very little to drink. Camels can lose a quarter of their body water and then drink over 100 litres in a few minutes. Their humps store fat, not water. Kangaroo rats in American deserts may never drink at all, getting the water they need from their food.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Everyone must drink exactly eight glasses of water a day.

Truth: There's no single right amount. How much you need depends on your size, the weather and how active you are, and food supplies a good share of your water. For most healthy people, drinking when thirsty works well.

Try this

Check the colour chart method many doctors suggest. Through a day, note whether your pee is pale yellow (well watered) or dark yellow (drink more). Also note how much you drank and how active you were. Notice how the colour changes after sport or on hot days.
