How does a paper kite stay up?

A kite is a wing on a leash. The string that holds it down is exactly what keeps it up.

The short answer

A kite stays up because it is tilted against the wind, like a wing. As the wind hits the tilted kite, the air is pushed downwards and backwards. The air pushes back on the kite, upwards and backwards. The upward part of that push is called lift.

The string is the secret. It holds the kite at just the right angle and stops it simply blowing away. Without the string, the wind would carry the kite along like a leaf. With it, the kite climbs into the wind instead. When lift and the pull of the string balance the kite's weight and the wind's push, it hangs steady in the sky.

Surprising but true

In 1901, Guglielmo Marconi used a kite to hold up the aerial that picked up the first radio signal sent across the Atlantic Ocean.

The four forces

Four forces act on a flying kite. Weight pulls it down. Wind pushes it back, a force called drag. Lift pushes it up. The string pulls it down and towards the flyer.

If the kite is flat to the ground, it gets almost no lift. If it faces the wind straight on, it gets lots of drag and little lift, so it just gets shoved backwards. The best angle is in between. That's why kites have a bridle, the short strings that attach to the main line. Moving where it's tied changes the angle.

Wind matters too. Too little, and the kite sinks. Too much, and the string may snap. Higher up, winds are usually steadier and stronger, which is why kites often fly better once they get above the treetops.

Tails and fighters

A tail keeps a kite from twisting and diving. It adds weight and drag at the bottom, so the kite points into the wind like a weathervane.

India's fighter kites, the patang, usually have no tail at all. That makes them unstable, but also quick to steer. A skilled flyer can make one dart, loop and dive with tiny tugs on the string, to cut down rival kites. The cutting strings, called manja, used to be coated with glass, but that harms birds, and glass-coated manja is now banned in India.

From China to the world

Kites were invented in China more than 2,000 years ago, possibly first made of silk and bamboo. Paper kites came later. They spread across Asia, including India, and much later to Europe.

The bird and the toy share a name in English. A kite hangs on the wind with hardly a flap, just like the black kite that circles over Indian cities.

Kites that changed history

Before they built their aeroplane, the Wright brothers flew a large kite in 1899 to test their idea of twisting wings to steer. In 1901, the Italian inventor Guglielmo Marconi used a kite in Newfoundland, now part of Canada, to lift a long wire aerial high into the air. With it, he picked up a faint radio signal sent from England, across the Atlantic Ocean.

Benjamin Franklin's famous kite experiment of 1752 showed that lightning is electricity. Some historians are not sure it happened exactly as described. Either way, it was extremely dangerous. Don't try it.

A sky full of kites

Each January, around Makar Sankranti, rooftops across Gujarat and Rajasthan fill with families flying kites. In Ahmedabad, the International Kite Festival draws flyers from many countries. In Delhi, Independence Day on 15 August is a big kite-flying day.

In the USA, Washington, D.C., holds a kite festival near the Washington Monument each spring. Different places, same idea: a scrap of paper, a stick or two, a string, and the wind.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Benjamin Franklin's kite was struck by lightning.

Truth: Franklin described flying a kite in a storm to show that lightning is electricity, by collecting a small electric charge from the wet string. A direct lightning strike would very probably have killed him. Never fly a kite near a storm or power lines.

Try this

With an adult, make a mini kite from a sheet of paper, two straws or ice-cream sticks crossed and taped on, a length of thread and a paper tail. Fly it in an open park, far from roads, trees and power lines. Try it with a short tail, then a long one, and see which flies steadier.
