What's at the bottom of the deepest ocean?

Drop Mount Everest in, and it would vanish under 2 km of water.

The short answer

The deepest known point in the ocean is the Challenger Deep, at the southern end of the Mariana Trench in the Pacific Ocean. It lies about 11 km below the waves. If you dropped Mount Everest in, its peak would still be about 2 km underwater.

At the bottom it is pitch dark and close to freezing. The water pressure is over 1,000 times what you feel at the beach. That is like having about a tonne pressing on every patch of skin the size of your fingernail.

And yet things live there. Pale, shrimp-like creatures scuttle over the mud, eating anything that drifts down from the sunlit sea far above.

Surprising but true

Only a few dozen people have ever visited the Challenger Deep. It is still one of the least explored places on Earth.

A crack in the seabed

The Mariana Trench is a long, curved valley in the ocean floor, about 2,500 km long. It formed where one huge slab of Earth's crust, the Pacific plate, slides down underneath another. This kind of grinding edge also causes earthquakes and builds chains of volcanic islands.

The Challenger Deep is named after a British ship, HMS Challenger, which measured the trench in 1875 by lowering a weighted rope. Later ships used echoes of sound, and found an even deeper spot. Scientists still argue about the exact depth by a few metres, because measuring through 11 km of water is hard.

The first trip down

On 23 January 1960, two explorers, Jacques Piccard from Switzerland and Don Walsh from the US Navy, squeezed into a steel ball under a craft called the Trieste. Partway down, they heard a loud crack. One of the outer windows had split, but they kept going.

After nearly five hours they touched the bottom. Their landing stirred up a cloud of silt, so they could see very little. They stayed about 20 minutes, then rose back up.

Nobody returned for 52 years. In 2012 the film-maker James Cameron dived alone. Since 2019, a new submersible has made many trips. In 2020 it carried Kathryn Sullivan, the first American woman to walk in space, making her the first person to do both.

Life under pressure

Deep-sea creatures have soft bodies with no air spaces to crush. Amphipods, cousins of the sandhoppers on a beach, are the most common visitors to bait left at the bottom. Some can even digest bits of sunken wood.

Fish can't quite reach the very bottom. Scientists think that below about 8,200 to 8,400 m, the chemistry inside a fish's body stops working properly. In 2023, a team filmed a small snailfish at about 8,300 m in a trench off Japan, the deepest fish ever seen.

Even here, people have left a mark. A plastic bag was spotted at almost 11 km deep, and ocean plastic has been found in the guts of animals living in the trench.

India goes deep

The Indian Ocean's deepest point is in the Java Trench, south of Indonesia, at about 7 km. India is building its own crewed submersible, Matsya-6000, as part of its Deep Ocean Mission. It is designed to carry three people to 6 km below the surface.

Scientists at the National Institute of Ocean Technology in Chennai have been testing it. Its job will be to study the deep seabed and its life. If it succeeds, India will join the small group of countries that can send people into the deepest zones of the sea. The bottom of the ocean is closer to us than the Moon, yet we have still seen only a sliver of it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Nothing can live at the bottom of the deepest ocean.

Truth: Every visit to the Challenger Deep has found life. Shrimp-like amphipods, sea cucumbers, giant single-celled creatures and many kinds of microbes all live there.

Try this

Push an empty plastic bottle with its cap screwed on down to the bottom of a bucket of water. Feel how hard the water pushes back. Water pushes harder the deeper you go, and at the bottom of the Challenger Deep it would squash the bottle flat.
