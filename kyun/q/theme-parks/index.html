How do roller coasters work?

Most roller coasters have no engine on the train at all. After the first hill, gravity does nearly all the work.

The short answer

A roller coaster is a lesson in energy. A chain or cable drags the train slowly up the first hill. At the top, the train has stored energy simply because it is high up. When it tips over the edge, gravity pulls it down, and that stored energy turns into speed.

At the bottom, the speed carries the train up the next hill, where it slows and stores energy again. Back and forth, the energy swaps between height and speed until the ride ends. A little is lost all the time to friction and air resistance, which is why the brakes at the end only have to stop a train that is already slowing.

Surprising but true

Loops on modern roller coasters are shaped like an upside-down teardrop, not a circle. A perfectly round loop would squash riders far harder at the bottom.

Ice slides and switchbacks

Coasters began with sleds. In Russia, by the 1600s, people built wooden ramps covered in ice and slid down them in winter. Later, wheeled carts on tracks let the fun continue in summer.

In 1884, LaMarcus Adna Thompson opened the Switchback Railway at Coney Island in New York. Riders paid a few cents to roll down a gentle wooden track at about the speed of a bicycle. Within a few decades, America had hundreds of wooden coasters, and the race to build higher and faster rides was on.

India's first big amusement parks came later. EsselWorld in Mumbai opened in 1989, and parks such as Wonderla in Bengaluru followed.

Why you feel weightless

Over the top of a hill, the track curves away beneath you. Your body wants to keep going in a straight line, so for a moment you float slightly off the seat. Riders call this "airtime". At the bottom of a dip, the opposite happens and you feel pressed down, heavier than usual.

Engineers measure these pushes in "g". One g is what you feel sitting still. Many coasters reach about 4 g at the bottom of a drop, so for a second you feel four times heavier. Your inner ear, which helps with balance, notices every change. That is part of why your stomach seems to flip.

Keeping the train on the track

Each car has three sets of wheels. Running wheels sit on top of the rail and carry the weight. Side wheels stop it sliding left or right. Upstop wheels sit under the rail, so the car cannot lift off over a hill or hang loose upside down.

Loops use a teardrop shape so that the curve is tight at the top, where the train is slow, and gentle at the bottom, where it is fast. This keeps the forces on riders within safe limits. The first modern coaster with a teardrop loop, Revolution, opened in California in 1976. Before that, a few loop coasters in the early 1900s used round loops, and riders complained of sore necks.

Launching with magnets

Some coasters skip the chain lift and fire the train forward instead. Some use powerful hydraulic motors. Others use linear motors, rows of electromagnets along the track that push and pull on metal fins under the car, a little like magnets chasing each other in a line.

Formula Rossa at Ferrari World in Abu Dhabi uses a hydraulic launch. It reaches about 240 km/h in under five seconds, faster than most cars on a motorway. For years it was the fastest coaster in the world, until Falcons Flight in Saudi Arabia opened at the end of 2025 with a top speed of about 250 km/h. Riders wear goggles, because at that speed even a small insect hitting your eye would hurt.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Roller coaster loops are perfect circles.

Truth: Most modern loops are teardrop-shaped, tighter at the top and wider at the bottom. A circular loop would need the train to enter it much faster, which would push riders hard into their seats at the bottom.

Try this

Make a marble roller coaster from foam pipe insulation cut in half lengthways, or from folded card, taped to a wall or a stack of books. Start the marble at different heights. Find the lowest starting point that still gets the marble over your second hill.
