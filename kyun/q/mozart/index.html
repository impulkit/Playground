Who was Mozart?

He wrote his first symphony at about eight years old, in London, and died at 35 having written more than 600 pieces of music.

The short answer

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart was an Austrian composer who lived from 1756 to 1791. He was one of the most famous child performers in history, touring Europe from the age of six. As an adult, he wrote music of almost every kind: symphonies, operas, piano concertos, church music and pieces for small groups.

He wrote more than 600 works in just 35 years. Many are still among the most performed pieces of classical music. People love his music for its clear tunes, its playfulness and the way it can turn suddenly serious or sad. His operas, like The Magic Flute, are staged all over the world.

Surprising but true

According to his father's letters, the teenage Mozart heard a secret piece of church music in Rome once and wrote the whole thing down from memory.

A child on tour

Mozart was born in Salzburg, in today's Austria. His father, Leopold, was a musician and teacher. Wolfgang and his older sister, Nannerl, were both brilliant at the keyboard. Wolfgang was writing short pieces by the age of five.

Leopold took his children on long tours to show them off at royal courts. Between 1763 and 1766, the family travelled through Germany, Paris and London, where Wolfgang met the composer Johann Christian Bach. In London, aged about eight, he wrote his first symphony. Travel in coaches over bumpy roads was slow and tiring, and the children were often ill.

What makes London famous?London has the world's oldest underground railway, a castle where ravens are kept by royal tradition, and a clock bell called Big Ben that most people mistake for a tower.

The famous memory

In 1770, the Mozarts visited Rome. In the Sistine Chapel, they heard the Miserere by Gregorio Allegri, a piece the Vatican's choir sang only during Holy Week. Copies weren't supposed to leave the chapel.

According to his father's letters, Wolfgang, aged 14, wrote down the whole piece from memory after hearing it, then went back to check. Stories like this made him seem almost magical. Scientists today say such skills come from huge amounts of practice from a very young age. A brain trained this way gets very good at music, as you can read in how the brain works.

Freelance in Vienna

As a young man, Mozart worked for the Archbishop of Salzburg, but he hated being treated like a servant. In 1781, he left and moved to Vienna to earn his living by teaching, performing and selling his music. This was unusual and risky.

In Vienna, he wrote many of his greatest works, including piano concertos he played himself on the piano of his day. His operas The Marriage of Figaro and Don Giovanni followed. He earned good money at times, but spent freely and often had to borrow.

The last year

In 1791, his opera The Magic Flute was a success in Vienna. It includes the Queen of the Night's fiery song, one of the hardest pieces for a soprano, with notes far above most voices, as explained in singing.

He was also writing a Requiem, music for the dead, when he fell ill. He died on 5 December 1791, aged 35. The Requiem was finished by one of his students.

Myths and legends

The 1984 film Amadeus showed a rival composer, Antonio Salieri, plotting against Mozart. It is a great story, but historians have found no evidence that Salieri harmed him. The two men knew each other and seem to have been polite rivals.

Another myth says that playing Mozart to babies makes them cleverer. A small study in 1993 sparked the idea, but later research did not find any lasting effect. The young composer Beethoven may have visited Vienna hoping to meet Mozart in 1787. What happened, if they met, nobody knows for sure.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Mozart wrote "Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star".

Truth: The tune is an older French folk song. Mozart wrote a set of variations on it, and the English words were added from a poem by Jane Taylor, published in 1806.

Try this

Take Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star and make your own variations. Hum it slowly, then quickly, then bouncy, then sad and low. Clap a new rhythm while you hum. Mozart turned this one simple tune into twelve different pieces.
