Who was Nandalal Bose?

If you have seen the hand-painted pages of India's original Constitution, you have seen the work of Nandalal Bose and his students.

The short answer

Nandalal Bose (1882–1966) was one of India's most important modern artists and art teachers. A student of Abanindranath Tagore in the Bengal School, he went on to lead Kala Bhavana, the art school at Santiniketan, for decades.

He painted gods and epics, village life and nature. He made posters for the freedom movement at Gandhi's request. And he and his students decorated the pages of the original Constitution of India. For him, art wasn't only for galleries. It belonged in homes, walls, festivals and daily life.

Surprising but true

Nandalal Bose designed the emblems for two of India's highest civilian awards, the Bharat Ratna and the Padma Shri.

Learning from the past

Nandalal was born in 1882 in Bihar and studied in Kolkata, where he joined Abanindranath Tagore's classes in 1905. He learned to paint in the soft, Indian-inspired style of the Bengal School.

He also loved the folk arts of Bengal: the painted scrolls of storytellers, clay dolls and the patterns women drew on floors for festivals. He believed these were as much a part of Indian art as temple sculpture.

In 1909, he travelled to the Ajanta caves with an English artist, Christiana Herringham, to copy the ancient murals. Sitting for weeks in the dim caves, studying paintings more than 1,500 years old, changed how he saw line and colour. Later he studied the frescoes at Bagh, in Madhya Pradesh, too. These visits gave his work its strong, flowing outlines.

The teacher at Santiniketan

In 1921, Rabindranath Tagore founded Visva-Bharati at Santiniketan, a university where classes were often held outdoors under trees. Nandalal joined its art school, Kala Bhavana, and soon became its head.

He taught students to draw from nature, to watch birds, trees and people closely, and to try many crafts: murals, woodcuts, textiles, pottery and festival decorations. He travelled with Tagore to China and Japan in 1924. His students included K. G. Subramanyan, Ramkinkar Baij and Benode Behari Mukherjee, who became great artists themselves.

Art for the freedom movement

Nandalal admired Gandhi. In 1930, during the Salt March, he made a black-and-white linocut of Gandhi striding along with a walking stick. It is known simply as Bapuji, and it became one of the most recognised images of Gandhi.

In 1938, Gandhi asked him to decorate the Indian National Congress session at Haripura, a village in Gujarat. Bose painted dozens of bright posters of everyday people: a farmer ploughing, a woman grinding grain, a drummer, a mother and child. He used simple shapes and earthy colours so villagers would feel the art was theirs. The Haripura posters are now treasured in museums.

Painting the Constitution

When India's Constitution was ready, it was written out by hand in beautiful calligraphy. Nandalal Bose and his students were asked to decorate it. They painted borders and pictures for each part, telling India's long story. There is a seal from Mohenjo-daro, scenes from the Ramayana and Mahabharata, the Buddha and Emperor Ashoka. Later pages show Akbar's court, Shivaji, Gandhi's Dandi March and more.

One of his students, Beohar Rammanohar Sinha, painted several pages, including the decorated border around the Preamble. The original volumes are kept in special sealed cases in the library of Parliament in New Delhi.

Honours and a lasting mark

After independence, Nandalal Bose designed the emblems for the Bharat Ratna and the Padma Shri awards. He received the Padma Vibhushan in 1954. The Government of India later declared his works "national art treasures", meaning they cannot be taken out of the country.

He died at Santiniketan in 1966. Many of his paintings are at the National Gallery of Modern Art in New Delhi. His students carried his habits all over India: drawing outdoors, respecting village crafts, and treating a festival decoration as seriously as a painting in a frame.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The original Constitution of India was typed or printed.

Truth: The original copies, in Hindi and English, were handwritten in flowing calligraphy. Nandalal Bose and his students at Santiniketan decorated the pages with paintings and borders.
