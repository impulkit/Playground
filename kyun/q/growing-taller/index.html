Why do children grow taller?

Children grow at the ends of their bones, in thin slices of rubbery cartilage that slowly turn to bone and then switch off for good.

The short answer

You grow taller because your long bones, like those in your legs and spine, get longer. Near each end of these bones is a thin layer of softer, rubbery tissue called cartilage. This is the growth plate.

Cells in the growth plate keep dividing and piling up, pushing the ends of the bone further apart. Behind them, the older cartilage slowly hardens into bone. The whole process is driven by growth hormone, a chemical messenger from a pea-sized gland at the base of your brain. In your teens, other hormones speed things up, then tell the growth plates to finish. Once they turn to solid bone, you stop getting taller.

Surprising but true

You are about a centimetre taller when you wake up than when you go to bed, because the cushions between your spine's bones squash during the day.

Fast, slow, fast, stop

Growing isn't steady. Babies grow fastest of all. A newborn is about 50 cm long and grows around 25 cm in the first year. That's almost the length of a school ruler in twelve months, though it slows quickly after that.

Through middle childhood, most children grow about 5 or 6 cm a year. Then comes the growth spurt of puberty, when a child can shoot up 8 to 10 cm in a single year. Girls usually start this spurt earlier, around 10 to 12, and boys a couple of years later. That's why, for a while, many girls in a class are taller than the boys. By the late teens, the growth plates close and height settles.

What decides how tall you'll be

Much of your height comes from your genes, the instructions you inherited from your parents. Tall parents tend to have tall children, though not always. Scientists have found hundreds of genes that each nudge height a tiny bit.

But genes aren't everything. Children need enough food, especially protein, and enough sleep, and they need to stay mostly free of long illnesses. Across the last hundred years, average heights have gone up in many countries as diets and health care improved. Children today in India, the USA and most of the world are, on average, taller than their great-grandparents were at the same age.

Taller in space

On Earth, gravity squashes your spine a little all day. Between each of the small bones in your back sit soft discs, like jam-filled cushions. As you stand and walk, they flatten slightly. At night, lying down, they spring back. That's why you're about a centimetre taller in the morning.

Astronauts on the International Space Station can become up to 3% taller, because there's no gravity pressing on their spines. That can mean a few centimetres. NASA designs spacesuits with room for this. Once astronauts come back to Earth, their spines settle again within a few months.

How tall can people get?

The tallest person ever reliably measured was Robert Wadlow, from Illinois in the USA. He reached 2.72 metres (8 feet 11 inches) before he died in 1940. His body made far too much growth hormone, and his growth plates never closed.

That's very rare. A doctor checks a child's growth over time by plotting height on a growth chart. A child growing much faster or slower than usual may need tests, and treatment exists for some growth problems. Most differences in height, though, are simply normal. Everyone's bones follow their own timetable, and children who are small at ten are often not small at sixteen.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Drinking coffee or tea stunts your growth.

Truth: There's no good evidence that caffeine makes children shorter. But caffeine can disturb sleep, and sleep is when most growth hormone is released, so it's still sensible for children to go easy on it.

Try this

Ask an adult to mark your height on a door frame first thing in the morning, then again just before bed, using a book flat on your head. Compare the two marks. Keep marking your height every few months on the same frame and watch the line climb.
