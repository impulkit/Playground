Why do airports check your bags?

An airport X-ray machine paints your bag in colours. Orange means something like food or clothes, and blue usually means metal.

The short answer

Airports check bags and passengers to stop anything dangerous getting onto a plane. A plane is a sealed metal tube flying kilometres above the ground, full of people and fuel. A weapon or a small fire on board is far harder to deal with there than on a train or a bus.

So before you board, your bags go through an X-ray machine and you walk through a metal detector or body scanner. Officers look for weapons, explosives and things that could start a fire, like some batteries, lighters and fuel. Most of the time they find nothing worrying, just too much toothpaste. It is one of the last steps in the long chain that makes an airport work.

Surprising but true

In India, dry coconut is not allowed in checked bags, because the oil in it can catch fire.

Why it started

In the 1960s and early 1970s, there were many hijackings, when people took over planes by force. Passengers were rarely searched. In 1973 the USA began screening every airline passenger with metal detectors and bag X-rays.

After the attacks of 11 September 2001, when hijacked planes were flown into buildings in New York and near Washington, checks became much stricter. The USA set up the Transportation Security Administration, the TSA, that November. In India, airport security is overseen by the Bureau of Civil Aviation Security, and since 2000 the Central Industrial Security Force has guarded most big airports.

What the machines see

An X-ray scanner shines a beam through your bag. Dense things block more X-rays than light ones. The computer colours the picture: orange for things made from plants and animals, like food, leather or clothes, green for some plastics, and blue for metal. An officer watches a screen and stops the belt if a shape looks odd. Checked suitcases go through their own scanners, out of sight, on conveyor belts beneath the terminal.

How do X-rays see inside you?The first X-ray picture of a person showed a woman's hand: her bones, and her wedding ring.

Many airports now use CT scanners, which take hundreds of pictures from different angles and build a 3D image. Officers can spin it round on screen. At some of these airports, you can leave your laptop and liquids inside your bag.

Body scanners use harmless radio waves to find hidden objects under clothes. They show a cartoon outline of a person, not a real picture. Officers may also wipe a small cloth over a bag or your hands and test it in a machine that can detect tiny traces of explosives.

Rules that puzzle travellers

In 2006, police in Britain stopped a plot to blow up planes with liquid explosives hidden in drink bottles. Since then, many countries have limited liquids in cabin bags to containers of 100 ml or less. Some airports with newer scanners have begun to relax the rule.

Other rules have less to do with attackers and more with fire. Power banks must travel in your cabin bag, where the crew can reach them. In India, dry coconut and some kinds of matches are banned from checked bags because they can catch fire.

Faster and faster lines

Airports keep trying to move people faster. Indian airports introduced DigiYatra in December 2022, letting travellers who sign up walk through gates that use face recognition instead of an ID card. Some people welcome the speed. Others worry about privacy and who stores the pictures.

In 2025 the TSA stopped asking most passengers to take off their shoes, a rule it had kept since 2006. On 30 November 2025, the Sunday after Thanksgiving, it screened about 3.13 million people in one day, a record. Trained dogs still patrol many terminals too. A trained sniffer dog can pick out the smell of explosives on a suitcase as it rolls past.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Airport X-ray machines can damage your phone, laptop or the food in your bag.

Truth: The X-ray dose in a bag scanner is tiny and does no harm to electronics, food or medicines. The only thing that can sometimes be affected is old-style camera film, which is why some photographers ask for it to be checked by hand.

Try this

Pack a "mystery bag" for a friend with five everyday objects, like a spoon, an apple, a pencil, keys and a sock. Let them feel the bag from the outside only and guess what's inside. Then talk about why airports use X-ray machines instead of guessing.
