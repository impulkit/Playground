Why is Iceland called the land of fire and ice?

Iceland sits on a crack in the Earth that is pulling apart, so it has glaciers sitting on top of volcanoes.

The short answer

Iceland is an island in the North Atlantic, just south of the Arctic Circle. It gets its nickname because it has two very different forces side by side. Huge glaciers cover about a tenth of the land. Underneath and around them are dozens of active volcanoes, hot springs and steaming cracks in the ground.

This happens because Iceland sits right on the line where two of Earth's great plates are pulling apart, and over an unusually hot spot deep in the Earth. Molten rock keeps rising to fill the gap. So in Iceland, you can stand on a glacier and see steam rising from the ground a short drive away.

Surprising but true

The English word "geyser" comes from Geysir, a hot spring in Iceland that shoots boiling water high into the air.

A country splitting in two

Iceland sits on top of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, a long underwater mountain range that runs down the middle of the Atlantic Ocean. On one side is the North American plate, and on the other, the Eurasian plate. They move apart by about 2 cm a year, roughly as fast as your fingernails grow.

At Þingvellir National Park, you can walk through a rocky valley between the two plates. In a crack called Silfra, filled with glacier water so clear you can see 100 m, divers swim between continents. Iceland gets small earthquakes almost every day as the land stretches.

Volcanoes under ice

When a volcano erupts under a glacier, the heat melts the ice very fast, and floods of meltwater and rock can burst out. In 2010, an eruption at Eyjafjallajökull threw up a cloud of ash that drifted over Europe. For days, many thousands of flights were cancelled because ash can damage jet engines.

New land is still being made. In 1963, an eruption under the sea built a new island, Surtsey, off Iceland's south coast. Scientists have watched since then as plants, insects and birds arrived on the bare rock. Since 2021, a string of eruptions on the Reykjanes peninsula near Reykjavík has sent lava flowing towards roads and a fishing town.

Why does Old Faithful shoot water into the sky?Every hour or so, a fountain of boiling water blasts up to 55 m high. The heat comes from magma hidden under the ground.

Free heat from below

Iceland uses its volcanoes as a giant heater. Hot water from deep underground is piped into about nine in every ten homes, warming radiators and taps. This geothermal heat also warms pavements in Reykjavík to melt snow, and greenhouses where tomatoes and even bananas grow.

Almost all of Iceland's electricity comes from rushing rivers and underground heat. The Blue Lagoon, a milky-blue pool near the airport, is filled with warm water left over from a geothermal power station. It is one of the most popular sights in the country.

Vikings and an old parliament

Iceland was settled by Norse people, often called Vikings, from Norway, along with people from Britain and Ireland, from the 870s onwards. In 930 CE, chieftains began meeting each summer at Þingvellir to settle disputes and make laws. That gathering, the Althing, is one of the oldest parliaments in the world still running.

Icelanders kept long written stories, called sagas, about their families and feuds. Modern Icelandic is close enough to old Norse that children can still read those sagas.

Names and lights

Most Icelanders don't have family surnames. Instead, a child's last name is usually made from a parent's first name. If a man called Jón has a son named Ólafur and a daughter named Anna, they would be Ólafur Jónsson and Anna Jónsdóttir. So even the phone book was arranged by first names.

In winter, when nights last almost 20 hours, the aurora often dances over the island. In summer the Sun barely sets, and people play football at midnight in daylight.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Iceland is covered in ice and Greenland is green.

Truth: It is roughly the other way round. Only about 11% of Iceland is under glaciers, and much of it is green in summer. Around 80% of Greenland is buried under a giant ice sheet.

Try this

Ask an adult to help. Put a few ice cubes in a bowl, then gently pour a little warm tap water next to them. Watch how the hot and cold meet. In Iceland, melted glacier water and hot underground water meet in the same way, making warm rivers and steaming pools.
