What is your body clock?

Deep in your brain, a cluster of about 20,000 nerve cells keeps time like a clock, telling your body when to feel sleepy, hungry and alert.

The short answer

Your body clock is an internal timer that runs on a cycle of about 24 hours. Scientists call it the circadian rhythm, from Latin words meaning "about a day". It tells your body when to feel sleepy and when to wake, when to feel hungry, and even when your temperature should rise and fall.

The master clock sits in a tiny part of the brain called the suprachiasmatic nucleus. It is about the size of a grain of rice and is made of around 20,000 nerve cells. Every morning, light entering your eyes helps reset it so that it stays in step with the Sun.

Surprising but true

In 1729, a French scientist shut a mimosa plant in a dark cupboard and found its leaves still opened and closed on a daily rhythm, with no sunlight at all.

Light sets the time

Your body clock does not run perfectly on its own. In most people, it runs a little slow, with a natural cycle slightly longer than 24 hours. Light fixes that. Special cells in the retina at the back of your eye detect brightness and send signals straight to the master clock.

Morning light tells your brain the day has begun. In the evening, as light fades, a gland in your brain starts releasing melatonin, a hormone that makes you feel sleepy. Bright screens late at night can delay this, which is one reason doctors suggest switching them off before bed, as explained in what screens do to sleep.

A day in your body

Your body clock affects much more than sleep. Your body temperature is usually lowest in the early hours before dawn and highest in the late afternoon or evening. Many people feel a dip in energy in the early afternoon, the time when a short nap feels most tempting. Your digestion, blood pressure and alertness all follow daily patterns.

This is why the time of day matters. Many athletes perform best in the late afternoon, and doctors are studying whether some medicines work better at certain hours.

Can plants move?Touch a chhui-mui leaf and it folds shut in seconds. Plants move all the time; most are just too slow for us to notice.

Living in a cave

How do we know the clock runs on its own? The first clue came from plants. In 1729, the French scientist Jean-Jacques d'Ortous de Mairan put a mimosa plant in a dark cupboard. Its leaves kept opening and closing each day as if the Sun were still rising.

In 1938, two American scientists, Nathaniel Kleitman and Bruce Richardson, spent 32 days deep in Mammoth Cave in Kentucky, trying to live on a 28-hour day. Richardson's body adjusted somewhat, but Kleitman's stubbornly stuck to about 24 hours.

In 1962, a young French cave explorer named Michel Siffre lived alone in an underground cave for about two months, with no clock. He kept a natural sleep-wake rhythm, but when he came out, he thought far fewer days had passed than really had.

The genes behind the clock

How do cells keep time? Scientists studying fruit flies found genes that make a protein which builds up in cells during the night and breaks down during the day, in a loop that takes about 24 hours. Jeffrey Hall, Michael Rosbash and Michael Young won the 2017 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for working this out.

Similar clocks tick in almost every cell in your body. The master clock in the brain keeps them all in time, like a conductor leading an orchestra.

When the clock gets confused

If you fly from Mumbai to New York, the Sun rises at a time your body thinks is evening. Your clock takes several days to catch up, which is called jet lag. People who work night shifts, like nurses and pilots, have to fight their body clocks too.

Children aged 6 to 12 need about 9 to 12 hours of sleep a night, according to sleep experts. Going to bed and getting up at roughly the same time each day, and getting daylight in the morning, keeps your clock running smoothly.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Early risers are just more disciplined than people who like to stay up late.

Truth: Whether you are a morning or evening person is partly set by your genes and your age. Teenagers' body clocks naturally shift later, which is why many teens find early mornings hard.

Try this

For one week, write down the time you feel sleepy at night and the time you wake up without an alarm on weekends or holidays. Ask a parent or grandparent to do the same. Compare your notes. Are you an early bird, a night owl, or somewhere in between?
