How was Petra carved into rock?

The builders of Petra didn't stack stones to make buildings. They started at the top of a cliff and cut their way down.

The short answer

Petra is an ancient city in southern Jordan, carved straight into cliffs of reddish sandstone. It was built by the Nabataeans, a wealthy Arab trading people, who made it their capital more than 2,000 years ago.

The Nabataeans did not build most of Petra's grand buildings out of blocks. They cut them out of the solid rock face, like sculptors carving a statue. Using iron chisels and hammers, stonecutters started at the top of a cliff and worked down, shaping columns, doorways and decorations as they went. Behind each grand front, they hollowed out rooms. Hundreds of tombs, temples and halls fill the valley, glowing pink, orange and red in the sunlight.

Surprising but true

Petra was a desert city, yet its people built dams, cisterns and clay pipes so clever that they could store water for dry months and stop flash floods from wrecking the town.

Rich from camel trains

Petra stood at a crossroads of desert trade routes. Camel caravans carried frankincense and myrrh from southern Arabia, along with spices and silk from further east, towards the Mediterranean. The Nabataeans controlled the water and the paths, and they grew rich by charging and supplying the traders.

With that wealth, they built a city that may have held around 20,000 people at its peak. Their buildings mix styles from Egypt, Greece and Mesopotamia, because traders brought ideas as well as goods.

Carving a cliff

Sandstone is soft enough to cut with iron tools, but strong enough to hold its shape for thousands of years. Workers likely began by cutting steps up the side of the cliff. Then they flattened the top of the area they wanted to carve and worked down, layer by layer.

The proof is in unfinished tombs. Some have neatly carved tops and rough rock at the bottom where work stopped. The most famous building, the Treasury, has a front about 40 m tall, roughly as high as a 13-storey building. Its columns, statues and great stone urn were all carved from one rock face.

Water in the desert

Petra gets little rain, but when storms come, water rushes down the rocky gorges as flash floods. The Nabataeans turned this danger into a supply. They built dams to slow and redirect floods, channels cut into rock along the walls of the gorges, clay pipes and big underground cisterns to store water.

Some experts think this clever water system mattered as much as trade to Petra's success. It let the city grow gardens and fountains in a dry valley, much as stepwells in Rajasthan and Gujarat kept water through the dry season.

The Siq and the Treasury

The main way into Petra is the Siq, a narrow, winding gorge about 1.2 km long. Its walls rise up to about 80 m, and in places it is only a few metres wide. As you walk, the gorge curves and darkens, then suddenly opens to reveal the Treasury, glowing in the light.

That view appeared in the 1989 film Indiana Jones and the Last Crusade, which made Petra famous around the world.

After the Nabataeans

In 106 CE, the Roman Empire took over the kingdom, as told in ancient Rome. Trade routes shifted to the sea, and an earthquake in 363 CE damaged much of the city. Over time, most people left.

Local Bedouin families never forgot Petra and lived among its caves. But to Europeans it was a legend until 1812, when a Swiss traveller, Johann Ludwig Burckhardt, visited in disguise. In 2007 it was voted one of the new Seven Wonders, and today archaeologists say most of the city still lies unexcavated.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Treasury at Petra was built to hold treasure.

Truth: It got its name from a later Bedouin legend that treasure was hidden in the stone urn at the top. People even shot at the urn to try to break it open. Archaeologists think the building was most likely a royal tomb.

Try this

Take a bar of soap and, with an adult's help, use a blunt lolly stick or plastic spoon to carve a simple doorway shape into one side. Start at the top and work down. Notice how the uncut part below gives you something solid to lean on.
