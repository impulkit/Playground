What makes the Brahmaputra special?

The Brahmaputra goes by at least four different names on its way from Tibet to the sea, and in Assam it can be so wide you can't see the far bank.

The short answer

The Brahmaputra is one of Asia's great rivers. It rises in Tibet, near Mount Kailash, flows east across the high Tibetan plateau for over 1,000 km, then makes a sharp turn and cuts south through the Himalaya into India. It crosses Arunachal Pradesh and Assam, enters Bangladesh, and joins the Ganga before reaching the Bay of Bengal.

It is special for its size, its power and its moods. In Assam it can be several kilometres wide, braided into many channels around shifting sandbanks. It carries huge amounts of water and silt, floods most years, and has shaped the life, culture and wildlife of the whole valley.

Surprising but true

Majuli, in the middle of the Brahmaputra in Assam, is often called the world's largest river island, yet it has shrunk by more than half in about a hundred years as the river eats its banks.

A river with many names

In Tibet the river is called the Yarlung Tsangpo. It runs at more than 3,000 metres above sea level, among the highest major rivers on Earth. Near a mountain called Namcha Barwa it bends in a giant U-turn and plunges through the Yarlung Tsangpo Grand Canyon, one of the deepest gorges in the world.

When it enters Arunachal Pradesh, it is called the Siang. Joined by the Dibang and the Lohit, it becomes the Brahmaputra in Assam. In Bangladesh it takes the name Jamuna, and after meeting the Ganga (there called the Padma), it flows on to the sea. It ends in the same delta as the Ganga, with its huge mangrove forests.

Wide, wild and always moving

The Brahmaputra drains young, steep mountains that are still rising and crumbling. Heavy monsoon rains wash rock and soil into it, so it carries one of the biggest loads of silt of any river. Where the land flattens in Assam, the river slows and drops sand, building bars and islands. Then it flows around them in many channels.

The river keeps changing course. Villages that stood on a bank one year can be in the water the next. That makes the river hard to bridge. The Bogibeel Bridge near Dibrugarh, opened in 2018, carries both a road and a railway about 4.9 km across it. Floods come almost every monsoon, damaging homes and farms. But they also spread fresh silt over the fields, making them fertile, and feed the wetlands and grasslands along the river.

Majuli and the river islands

Hundreds of sandy islands, called chars or chaporis, dot the river. The biggest is Majuli, home to about 1.7 lakh people (2011 Census). It is famous for its satras, monasteries started in the tradition of the saint Srimanta Sankardeva in the 1500s, where monks keep alive dance, drama and mask-making.

Majuli is in danger. As the river wears away its banks, the island has shrunk. It covered roughly 1,250 square km in the early 1900s, and between about 350 and 500 today, depending on how it is measured. Walls and other protection works are trying to slow the loss.

Rhinos, tea and silk

Along the south bank lies Kaziranga National Park, home to about two-thirds of the world's greater one-horned rhinos. Each monsoon, floods cover much of the park. Animals move to higher ground, some crossing the national highway, where traffic is slowed to let them pass. The floods are hard on wildlife, but they also renew the tall grasslands that rhinos feed on.

How did Kaziranga save the one-horned rhino?A hundred years ago there may have been only a few dozen rhinos in Kaziranga. Now about two out of every three on Earth live there.

The fertile valley also grows much of India's tea, on estates that stretch over low hills on both sides of the river. At Sualkuchi, on the north bank near Guwahati, families weave golden muga silk, made by silkworms found almost only in Assam.

Songs of the river

For people in Assam, the Brahmaputra is more than water. The singer Bhupen Hazarika grew up near its banks. In his song "Bistirno Parore", he asks the great river why it flows on silently while people suffer on its shores. A Hindi version, "Ganga Behti Ho Kyun", asks the same of the Ganga. In 2017, India opened a 9 km bridge from Dhola to Sadiya, over a tributary of the Brahmaputra. It was then the country's longest river bridge, and it was named after him.

Myth or fact?

Myth: All of India's big rivers are named after goddesses.

Truth: The Brahmaputra's name means "son of Brahma", one of very few big Indian rivers given a male name. In Assam it is also called the Luit. The Son, in central India, is another river traditionally seen as male.
