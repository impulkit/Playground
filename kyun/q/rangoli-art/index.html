How is rangoli made?

Before sunrise, in thousands of Tamil Nadu streets, women draw a fresh pattern of rice flour at the doorstep, knowing that by evening feet, wind and ants will have taken it away.

The short answer

Rangoli is a pattern drawn on the floor, usually at the entrance of a home, to welcome guests and good fortune. It is made by letting powder trickle from between your thumb and forefinger, as if you were sprinkling salt very slowly. Artists use rice flour, chalk powder, crushed stone, coloured powders or flower petals.

Designs range from a simple row of lotuses to huge, detailed circles of peacocks and lamps. Rangoli is drawn for festivals such as Diwali, Onam and Pongal, for weddings and new homes, and in many households every morning. Then it is swept away, and a new one is drawn.

Surprising but true

Some kolam patterns are drawn as a single unbroken line looping around a grid of dots, and computer scientists have studied them as a kind of picture language.

Many names, many styles

Almost every part of India has its own floor art. In Tamil Nadu it is the kolam, in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana muggulu, and in Karnataka rangavalli. In Bengal, women paint alpana with a paste of rice and water, using a finger or a twist of cloth. In Rajasthan, mandana designs are drawn in white on floors and walls coated with red earth. In Kerala, the pookalam at Onam is made entirely of flower petals.

The styles differ too. Some use only white lines. Others are filled with rich colours. Some show gods and animals, like the wall paintings of Madhubani. Others are pure geometry.

Dots and lines

Many kolams start with a grid of dots, called pulli. The artist then draws lines that loop and curl around the dots. In one style, the sikku kolam, a single continuous line weaves around every dot and ends where it began, without ever crossing a dot.

Kolams use mirror and rotating symmetry, and many repeat in tidy patterns. In the 1970s and 1980s, the computer scientist Gift Siromoney and his colleagues at Madras Christian College in Chennai studied kolams as "picture languages", working out rules that could generate them. Kolam artists, most of them women with no formal maths training, had been following such rules by memory for generations.

Art that feeds the ants

In Tamil Nadu, a kolam drawn in rice flour is said to be a small act of kindness: it feeds ants, birds and other small creatures that pass by. The doorstep is first swept and sprinkled with water, which keeps dust down and helps the flour stick.

During Margazhi, the cold month from mid-December to mid-January, kolams become bigger and more elaborate. Streets in Chennai and other towns fill with designs that spread across the road, and some neighbourhoods hold competitions. On Pongal, kolams show pots of rice boiling over, a sign of plenty.

How to make one

Experienced artists draw freehand, but beginners can use tricks. Draw a chalk outline first, or use a grid of dots as a guide. Some people use stencils, or plastic shakers with holes. For bright colours, powders are often made from coloured rock dust, or from rice flour mixed with natural dyes like turmeric.

The trick is control. Pinch the powder, keep your hand close to the floor and move steadily. Lines come out even only after a lot of practice, which is why many grandmothers can draw a perfect kolam in minutes while beginners struggle. Many learners practise on paper or a slate first, before trying the doorstep.

Rangoli around the world

Indian families have carried rangoli wherever they settled. In the United States, it is drawn at Diwali on doorsteps, in temples and at school celebrations, sometimes with sidewalk chalk. In Malaysia, Singapore and Mauritius, malls and town squares display giant festive rangolis. Wherever it is made, the idea stays the same. A doorstep should look welcoming, even if the pattern lasts only a day.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Rangoli is only a Diwali decoration.

Truth: Many families draw a kolam or rangoli every single day, and the tradition is tied to harvest festivals, weddings, Onam, Pongal and the cold month of Margazhi, as well as Diwali. Each region has its own name and style.

Try this

On a piece of dark paper, draw a grid of dots, five across and five down. Using chalk or white crayon, try to draw lines that curve around the dots without touching them, and see if you can make a pattern with one continuous line. Then try a design with flowers or leaves, filling them with coloured chalk.
