How did Wangari Maathai plant millions of trees?

She didn't plant them all herself. She paid village women a few coins per tree, and together they planted more than 5 crore.

The short answer

Wangari Maathai was a Kenyan scientist who started the Green Belt Movement in 1977. She did not plant millions of trees herself. She got thousands of women in villages across Kenya to do it, and paid them a little for every seedling that survived.

The trees gave the women firewood, fruit and shade, and held the soil in place so streams kept flowing. The work also gave women money and confidence.

The movement has planted more than 5 crore (50 million) trees. In 2004, Maathai won the Nobel Peace Prize, the first African woman to do so.

Surprising but true

In 2004 Wangari Maathai became the first African woman to win the Nobel Peace Prize. The committee said planting trees was a way of building peace.

A girl from the hills

Maathai was born in 1940 in Nyeri, in the green highlands of central Kenya. As a child she fetched water from a stream near a fig tree, and watched tadpoles swim in it. Years later, that stream had dried up, and she never forgot it.

She was a brilliant student. In 1960 she won a scholarship to study in the United States, one of hundreds of young Kenyans flown there in a programme called the Kennedy Airlift. Back home, in 1971, she became the first woman in East and Central Africa to earn a PhD, in veterinary anatomy.

Why trees?

In the 1970s, rural women told her their streams were drying up, food was scarce, and they had to walk further each day for firewood. Forests had been cut for farms and plantations of tea and coffee.

Maathai saw that one action could tackle all these problems. Trees protect the soil from rain and wind. They help the ground hold water, and they make food and fuel, using sunlight through photosynthesis. So she began planting.

How the Green Belt Movement worked

Women set up tree nurseries, collecting seeds from local trees and raising them in small pots. When the seedlings were ready, they were planted on farms, at schools and along roads. Foresters had said only experts could grow trees. Maathai said women had been growing things for thousands of years.

Each woman was paid a small sum for every tree that was still alive after a few months. That rule meant people cared for the trees long after planting.

Standing up to power

Maathai also fought the government when it wanted to build on public green spaces. In 1989 she campaigned against a 60-storey tower planned for Uhuru Park in Nairobi. The tower was never built.

Protecting forests and speaking up for democracy got her arrested and beaten more than once. She kept going, and in 2002 she was elected to Kenya's parliament.

Trees and peace

Some people were puzzled when Maathai won the Nobel Peace Prize. What did trees have to do with peace?

The Nobel Committee explained that when land is stripped bare, people compete for scarce water, firewood and farmland, and that competition can turn into conflict. Caring for the land, it said, helps prevent fights before they start. Maathai agreed. She also saw that women planting trees together learned to organise, speak up and demand fair treatment, which is how democracy grows.

The Green Belt Movement spread beyond Kenya to other African countries. In 2006 she helped launch a UN campaign that set out to plant a billion trees around the world in a single year, and it passed that goal.

The hummingbird

Maathai liked to tell a story. A forest is on fire, and the big animals flee. A tiny hummingbird flies back and forth, dropping water on the flames one beakful at a time. When the others laugh, it says, "I am doing the best I can." She died in 2011, and the trees her movement planted are still growing in Kenya.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Wangari Maathai planted millions of trees with her own hands.

Truth: She organised the planting. The Green Belt Movement trained thousands of women to grow seedlings in nurseries and plant them, paying a small amount for each tree that survived.

Try this

Grow a tree from a seed. Wash a lemon seed or an orange pip, push it about 1 cm into a pot of damp soil and keep it on a sunny windowsill. Water it when the top of the soil feels dry. Measure and draw it every week, and see how long it takes to sprout.
