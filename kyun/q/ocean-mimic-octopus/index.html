How does the mimic octopus pretend to be other animals?

Divers in Indonesia in 1998 watched an octopus turn into a stripy fish, then a flat fish, then a venomous sea snake, all in a few minutes.

The short answer

The mimic octopus pretends to be other animals by changing three things at once: its colour, its shape and the way it moves. In seconds, its brown skin can flash into bold brown-and-white stripes. Then it arranges its arms and body to look like a dangerous animal.

Its best-known acts are a venomous lionfish, a flat sole fish skimming over the sand and a poisonous banded sea snake. Predators that have learned to avoid those animals think twice. Many animals can camouflage themselves to blend in. The mimic octopus does the opposite. It stands out, while dressed as something you shouldn't eat.

Surprising but true

When a damselfish attacks it, the mimic octopus has been seen hiding six arms in a burrow and waving the other two in opposite directions, looking just like a banded sea snake, which hunts damselfish.

Found in 1998

Divers had glimpsed this octopus for years, but scientists first recorded it properly in 1998, at the mouth of a river in Sulawesi, Indonesia. They were astonished. Other octopuses copy the colour of rocks and seaweed. This one copied animals.

It got its scientific name, Thaumoctopus mimicus, in 2005. Thauma means "wonder" in Greek.

A wardrobe of disguises

As a flatfish, the octopus pulls its arms together behind it and flattens its body into a leaf shape. It then swims fast just above the sand, rippling like a sole. Some soles in these waters are poisonous to eat, so a hungry fish has good reason to leave it alone.

As a lionfish, it spreads its striped arms all around its body like long, spiky fins. As a sea snake, it hides most of its body in a hole and stretches out two banded arms in opposite directions. With its head hidden, they look like the black-and-white bands of a sea snake.

Some divers say they have also seen it imitate jellyfish, stingrays or mantis shrimp. These are harder to prove.

How the colour trick works

An octopus's skin is packed with thousands of tiny colour sacs called chromatophores. Each one holds yellow, red, brown or black pigment. Muscles around each sac stretch it open or squeeze it shut. The octopus's brain controls them directly, so patterns can change in less than a second.

Below the colour sacs are cells that reflect light and make shimmering whites and blues. Together they work like a living screen with millions of pixels, the same system that lets octopuses change colour to match rocks and weed.

Choosing the right costume

The most puzzling part is that the octopus seems to pick its disguise to suit the threat. Researchers watched one hide as a sea snake when damselfish pestered it. Banded sea snakes eat damselfish, so that disguise would scare them most.

Octopuses have about 500 million nerve cells, roughly as many as the thinking layer of a dog's brain. Two-thirds of them are in the arms, not the head. That might help each arm play its part in a disguise. Scientists still argue about how much the octopus plans its acts and how much is instinct. Either way, it shows how clever octopuses can be.

Life on the mud

Mimic octopuses live on flat, muddy or sandy seabeds in the warm waters around Indonesia, the Philippines, northern Australia and nearby seas. There is nowhere to hide, no coral and no rocks. Pretending to be something dangerous may be the best defence on such open ground.

They hunt small fish, crabs and worms, often poking their arms into burrows. When they are not acting, they look plain and brownish, almost boring. The stripes appear only when they move into the open. Like other octopuses, they have three hearts and blue blood.

A close cousin, the wonderpus, lives in the same areas and also has bold stripes. Copying another animal's warning colours isn't rare in nature, and some of the best tricks on land belong to the colour-changing chameleon.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The mimic octopus copies other animals by changing its body into theirs.

Truth: It doesn't grow new parts. It uses colour changes, body shape, the way it holds its arms and the way it swims. It's like a very good actor using costume, posture and movement rather than becoming someone else.

Try this

Play mimic octopus. With a friend, choose three animals and agree on a body shape for each using only your arms, like flat and wide for a flatfish. Take turns acting them out silently while the other guesses. Then try changing between them as fast as you can.
