Who was Georgia O'Keeffe?

Georgia O'Keeffe painted flowers so big that a single petunia could fill a canvas, because she wanted busy people to stop and really look.

The short answer

Georgia O'Keeffe was an American painter who lived from 1887 to 1986. She is famous for enormous close-up paintings of flowers, for glowing pictures of the New Mexico desert, and for bleached animal skulls floating in the sky. She is often called one of the founders of modern art in America.

She painted in a clean, simple way, with smooth colours and crisp edges. Many of her pictures look partly real and partly abstract, meaning built from shapes and colours rather than exact details. She worked for more than seven decades and kept making art even after her eyesight began to fail.

Surprising but true

In 2014, her painting Jimson Weed/White Flower No. 1 sold for 44.4 million dollars, a record at the time for any artwork by a woman sold at auction.

A farm girl who wanted to be an artist

O'Keeffe grew up on a dairy farm in Wisconsin. By the age of ten, she had decided to be an artist, which was a bold plan for a girl at the time. She studied in Chicago and New York, then worked as an art teacher, including in the dusty plains of Texas, where the big skies stayed with her for life.

In 1915 she made a set of charcoal drawings that looked like nothing else: swirling lines and shapes, with no people or objects. A friend showed them to Alfred Stieglitz, a famous photographer who ran a gallery in New York. He exhibited them in 1916. The two later married, and Stieglitz took hundreds of photographs of her.

Flowers the size of a window

In the 1920s O'Keeffe began painting single flowers, so close and so large that they filled the whole canvas. A petunia, an iris or a calla lily became as big as a window. She explained that people rush past flowers without seeing them, so she would paint them too big to ignore.

Critics wrote a lot about what the flowers "meant". O'Keeffe often grew tired of this. She said she was simply painting what she saw. In 2014, one of these pictures, Jimson Weed/White Flower No. 1, sold for 44.4 million dollars, setting a record at the time for a work by a woman at auction.

City of towers

Living in New York in the late 1920s, she painted the city too. From a high apartment, she looked down at skyscrapers rising around her and painted them at night, glowing with lights, or with the sun flaring behind them. She later said that some male artists told her only men should paint the city. She painted it anyway.

The faraway

In 1929 O'Keeffe first spent a summer in New Mexico, and the landscape changed her life. She called it "the faraway". There were red and yellow cliffs, a flat-topped mountain called the Pedernal and a sky that seemed to go on forever.

For years she spent summers there and winters in New York. After Stieglitz died, she moved to New Mexico for good in 1949, living in two houses at Abiquiú and Ghost Ranch. She walked the deserts collecting bones and stones and painted them against the hills.

Painting from the sky

O'Keeffe began flying around the world in her seventies, visiting places from Peru to India and Japan. Looking down from aeroplane windows, she saw rivers winding like ribbons and clouds lying below her like a floor. These became new paintings. Sky Above Clouds IV, finished in 1965, is more than 7 metres wide and now hangs at the Art Institute of Chicago.

In her eighties her eyesight weakened, and she worked on pottery and drawings with help from an assistant. She died in Santa Fe in 1986, aged 98. The Georgia O'Keeffe Museum there opened in 1997, one of the first museums in the USA devoted to a single woman artist. Like Frida Kahlo in Mexico, she showed a whole generation that a woman could be a great modern painter.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Georgia O'Keeffe only painted flowers.

Truth: Flowers made her famous, but they are only about 200 of her more than 2,000 works. She also painted city skyscrapers, desert hills, bones, rivers seen from planes and huge skies full of clouds.
