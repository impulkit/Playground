How do habits form, and why are they hard to break?

You probably brushed your teeth this morning without deciding to. Your brain has handed the job to an autopilot.

The short answer

A habit is something you do automatically when you're in a familiar situation. You walk in the door and drop your bag in the same spot. You sit at the table and reach for the same chair. You didn't decide. Your brain just did it.

Habits form through repetition. Each time you do something in the same place or at the same time, and it ends well, the link between the situation and the action gets stronger. After enough repeats, the situation alone triggers the action. Habits live in a different kind of memory from facts, the kind that also holds skills like riding a bicycle.

That's why habits are hard to break. The trigger is still there, and the brain still reaches for the old routine, whether or not you want it to.

Surprising but true

In a 2009 study in London, building a new daily habit took 66 days on average, and for some people it took more than 250.

The habit loop

Scientists often describe a habit as a loop with three parts. First there's a cue, the trigger: a time, a place, a feeling or a sound. Then the routine, the thing you do. Last comes the reward, a good feeling that tells your brain the routine was worth remembering.

The brain chemical dopamine helps with this learning. When a routine pays off, dopamine helps strengthen the connection. Over time the brain starts expecting the reward as soon as it spots the cue. A phone buzz is a cue. Checking it is the routine. A new message is the reward. Designers of social media apps understand this loop very well.

Autopilot in the brain

In the 1990s, scientist Ann Graybiel and her team at MIT in the USA trained rats to run through a simple maze for chocolate. At first, a deep part of the rats' brains called the basal ganglia was busy all the way through the maze. As the rats learned, that activity bunched up at the start and the end. In the middle, the brain could relax.

That's the point of habits. They save effort. Your thinking brain is free to plan your day while your body brushes your teeth on autopilot. One study by psychologist Wendy Wood found that about 40 per cent of what people did each day was done out of habit, usually while thinking about something else.

How long does it take?

In 2009, researchers at University College London asked 96 volunteers to pick a new daily habit and track it for 12 weeks. Some chose to drink a glass of water at breakfast. Others chose to run before dinner.

On average, it took 66 days for the action to feel automatic. But one person got there in 18 days, and for some it would have taken longer than eight months. Easy habits formed faster. Missing the odd day didn't ruin anything, which is good news for anyone who slips up.

Changing a habit

Willpower alone is a weak tool against a habit, because the habit runs faster than your decisions. Researchers suggest changing the situation instead. If the cue never appears, the routine doesn't start. Leaving a phone in another room at bedtime works better than promising yourself not to check it.

Another trick is to keep the cue but swap the routine. If you snack when you're bored after school, keep a fruit bowl where the biscuit tin used to be. The brain still gets a reward, just a different one, and the new loop slowly takes over the old one.

Myth or fact?

Myth: It takes exactly 21 days to form a new habit.

Truth: The 21-day idea comes from a 1960 self-help book by a surgeon, Maxwell Maltz, who said people took a minimum of about 21 days to adjust to changes. When scientists measured habit-building, it took about 66 days on average, with huge differences between people.

Try this

Pick one tiny habit, such as filling your water bottle before school, and tie it to something you already do every day, like putting on your shoes. Tick a calendar each day you manage it. After a few weeks, notice whether you've started doing it without thinking.
