What will clothes of the future do?

Scientists have already made fabrics that cool you down in the sun, shirts that track your heartbeat and jackets you can tap to change a song.

The short answer

Future clothes may be able to sense, cool, heat, heal and even talk to your phone. Scientists are building fabrics with sensors woven in to measure heartbeat and breathing. Others are working on cloth that keeps you cool on a hot day without any electricity, coats that warm up on cold mornings, and fibres that clean themselves.

Some of these already exist. You can buy shirts that track your heart rate and running socks that measure your steps. Others are still in labs. The big challenges are making them comfortable, washable and cheap, and finding a way to recycle them when they wear out. Electronics hate water, and clothes go through the wash dozens of times a year.

Surprising but true

Some cooling fabrics are designed to let your body's heat escape as invisible infrared light, which ordinary cotton traps.

Clothes that sense

In 2017, Google and Levi's sold a denim jacket with a touch-sensitive patch on its sleeve. Swipe it, and your phone skipped a song or read out directions. The threads in the patch were made of fine metal wrapped around cotton.

Doctors are interested in shirts that measure heartbeat, breathing and temperature all day. They could help people with heart problems, or alert parents if a baby has trouble breathing. Today, a smartwatch does some of this on your wrist. A shirt touches far more of your skin, so it might measure more accurately.

Clothes that cool you down

On a hot day in Delhi or Phoenix, cotton traps a lot of body heat. Your body releases heat partly as infrared light. Researchers at Stanford University and other labs have made fabrics that let infrared light pass through but still look like normal cloth.

Other fabrics reflect sunlight. Some are inspired by the shiny hairs of the Saharan silver ant, which reflect sunlight and help it survive scorching sand. Engineers think such clothes could help outdoor workers in India's heatwaves, where cooling can protect health.

Clothes from space and spiders

Many clothing ideas come from space. NASA developed materials that store and release heat, called phase-change materials, to keep astronauts comfortable. Some of these now appear in jackets, gloves and mattresses. Memory foam and some sportswear fibres also have roots in space research.

Spider silk is famous for being strong and stretchy. Spiders are hard to farm, because they eat each other. So companies are using yeast and bacteria to brew silk proteins in tanks, then spinning them into thread. A few products, including a jacket and a tie, have been made this way in small numbers.

Clothes that change colour

Some fabrics change colour with heat or light, like a mood ring. Others use tiny electronics to switch patterns. Scientists study animals such as the chameleon and the cuttlefish, which change colour using special skin cells.

Military researchers have tried fabrics that blend into their surroundings. Fashion designers have made dresses that glow with LEDs or shift colour as you move. At a 2016 gala in New York, one actress wore a gown woven with optical fibres that glowed when the lights went down. For now, most of these are one-off designs, not everyday wear.

The problem of waste

Clothes already create huge amounts of waste. Every year, millions of tonnes of old clothes are thrown away. Smart clothes, with wires and chips, could make this worse, adding to the growing pile of e-waste.

So some researchers are working on clothes that can be taken apart, or made from materials that break down safely. India has an old answer too. Khadi, hand-spun and hand-woven cotton, is simple, breathable and long-lasting. The future of clothes may mix space-age fibres with very old ideas: wear things longer, repair them and choose fabrics that suit the climate.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Smart clothes will be full of hard wires and batteries, so they will never be comfortable.

Truth: Researchers are making soft, washable fibres that work as sensors, wires and even batteries inside ordinary-looking cloth. Some are already bendable enough to go through a washing machine, though most are not yet cheap.

Try this

Test which fabric keeps you coolest. Wrap three identical bottles of water in different cloths, such as white cotton, black cotton and a shiny sports shirt. Leave them in the sun for 30 minutes, then feel or measure how warm each one is. Note which fabric worked best and guess why.
