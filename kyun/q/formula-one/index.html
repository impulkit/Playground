How does Formula One work?

A Formula One pit crew can change all four tyres in about two seconds, less time than it takes to read this sentence.

The short answer

Formula One is the top level of single-seater motor racing, using some of the most advanced car engines ever built. Eleven teams each run two cars, and the 22 drivers race at about 24 Grands Prix a season on tracks around the world. Drivers earn points for finishing in the top ten: 25 for a win, 18 for second, and so on. The driver with the most points at the end of the season is world champion. There is also a title for the best team.

The "formula" is the set of rules every car must follow, from its weight to its engine size. Teams build their own cars within those rules, so engineering is as important as driving.

Surprising but true

A Formula One car's wings press it onto the track so hard that, at high speed, it makes more downward force than the car's own weight.

Cars that stick to the road

A Formula One car can reach about 350 km/h on a long straight. Its secret is in the corners. The wings at the front and back work like aeroplane wings turned upside down. Instead of lift, they create downforce, pushing the car onto the track.

At high speeds this force is greater than the weight of the car, which lets drivers take corners much faster than a normal car could. Engineers often say a Formula One car could, in theory, drive upside down on a ceiling at high enough speed, though nobody has tried.

Engines and brakes

Since 2014 the cars have used a 1.6-litre turbocharged V6 engine joined to electric motors. Energy from braking is stored in a battery and released for extra power. From 2014 to 2025, energy from hot exhaust gases was collected too, but new rules in 2026 dropped that part and made the electric motor much stronger. Together they produce roughly 1,000 horsepower.

The brakes are just as dramatic. They can slow a car from over 300 km/h to under 100 km/h in a few seconds. Carbon brake discs glow orange and can reach about 1,000°C.

Why do things slow down?A football rolling across grass stops by itself. Voyager 1, launched in 1977, is still coasting through space. The difference is friction.

Two-second pit stops

Tyres wear out during a race, and the rules make drivers use at least two different types of dry tyre. So at least once, drivers stop in the pit lane. About 20 crew members change all four wheels, often in around two seconds. The fastest stops have been under 2 seconds.

When to stop is a big strategy decision. Stopping early can help a driver jump ahead, but the new tyres must last to the end. Rain changes everything. Teams must guess when to switch to grooved wet tyres, and one good guess can win a race.

Safety has improved hugely. Since 2018 every car has had a halo, a curved titanium bar over the cockpit that protects the driver's head. Many drivers disliked its look at first, but it has since been credited with saving lives in crashes.

The drivers

Drivers need strength as well as skill. In fast corners they feel forces up to about five times their body weight, and their necks must hold up a heavy helmet. In hot races they can lose 2 to 3 kilograms in sweat.

Michael Schumacher of Germany and Lewis Hamilton of Britain have each won seven world titles. Hamilton is the only Black driver to have raced in Formula One. The first Indian driver was Narain Karthikeyan from Coimbatore, who raced in 2005.

Formula One in India and the USA

India hosted the Indian Grand Prix at the Buddh International Circuit near Greater Noida from 2011 to 2013. Sebastian Vettel won all three races. The race then stopped, partly over tax and cost disagreements.

In the USA, Formula One was once less popular than NASCAR. A Netflix series, Drive to Survive, brought in many new fans. By 2023 the USA had three races a year, in Austin, Miami and Las Vegas.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Formula One is just about who has the fastest car.

Truth: The car matters a lot, but races are also won by tyre choices, pit stop timing, strategy in the rain and the driver's skill at overtaking. Teammates with the same car often finish far apart.

Try this

Hold a sheet of paper just below your lips and blow over the top of it. The paper rises, because faster-moving air above it has lower pressure. Formula One wings use the same idea upside down, so the air pushes the car down instead of lifting it.
