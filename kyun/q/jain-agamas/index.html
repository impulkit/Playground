What are the Jain scriptures?

For about a thousand years, Jain monks carried their holy teachings only in memory, until a great council in Gujarat finally wrote them down.

The short answer

The Jain scriptures are called the Agamas. They record the teachings of Mahavira, the 24th and last Tirthankara, or great spiritual teacher, of Jainism, who lived in eastern India about 2,500 years ago. His closest followers memorised his words, and monks passed them on by heart for centuries.

The two main Jain traditions see the Agamas differently. Svetambara Jains, whose monks and nuns wear white, believe a large part of the Agamas survives, in around 32 to 45 texts depending on the group. Digambara Jains believe the original Agamas were lost, and they treat other ancient works as their scriptures. Both honour a text called the Tattvartha Sutra, which sums up Jain teaching.

Surprising but true

Digambara Jains believe the original scriptures were lost long ago. Svetambara Jains believe much of them survived and were written down at Valabhi, in Gujarat, in the 5th century CE.

Teachings carried by memory

Jainism is one of India's oldest religions. It teaches ahimsa, not harming any living being, along with truthfulness, not stealing, self-control and not clinging to possessions. Jains believe every living thing, even a tiny insect, has a soul. The 2011 census counted about 45 lakh Jains in India. Large communities live in Gujarat, Maharashtra, Rajasthan and Karnataka, and others in the USA, the UK and East Africa.

According to tradition, Mahavira's chief disciples, called ganadharas, arranged his teachings into twelve groups called Angas, or limbs. Monks learned them by heart in Ardhamagadhi, an everyday language of the time, rather than in Sanskrit.

Remembering huge texts word for word is hard. During famines, when monks scattered, parts were forgotten. Jain tradition says monks met several times to recite what they remembered and fix the texts.

Written down at Valabhi

The Svetambara tradition says the most important meeting took place at Valabhi, in Gujarat, around 453 or 466 CE. Led by a teacher named Devarddhigani Kshamashramana, the monks wrote the surviving teachings down for the first time.

The collection includes eleven of the twelve Angas. The twelfth was already lost. The first Anga, the Acharanga Sutra, explains how monks and nuns should live, walk, eat and speak without harming living things. Other texts tell stories, discuss the soul and describe how the universe is arranged.

Digambara Jains don't accept these texts as the original Agamas. Instead they revere works such as the Shatkhandagama, composed in the early centuries CE, and the writings of the teacher Kundakunda, such as the Samayasara. Very old palm-leaf manuscripts of the Shatkhandagama's commentaries are kept at Moodabidri in Karnataka.

A book everyone shares

One text bridges the two traditions. The Tattvartha Sutra, by a scholar called Umaswati, was written in Sanskrit, probably between the 2nd and 5th centuries CE. In short lines, it explains the soul, karma and the path to freedom from rebirth.

One of its lines, Parasparopagraho jivanam, means "souls render service to one another". It appears on the official Jain emblem and is often quoted as the heart of Jain ethics. The same care for all living things is why nearly all Jains are vegetarian, and many avoid root vegetables as well.

Gold, blue and the Kalpa Sutra

The Kalpa Sutra tells the life of Mahavira and other Tirthankaras. Every year, during the festival of Paryushana, monks read it aloud to gatherings of Jains.

From about the 14th century, wealthy Jain families in Gujarat and Rajasthan paid for beautiful copies of it, painted with gold and deep blue. Giving a holy book to a temple was considered an act of great merit. Jain communities kept these manuscripts in special storerooms called gyan bhandars, "treasuries of knowledge". Some, like those in Jaisalmer and Patan, have protected palm-leaf and paper manuscripts for many centuries, making them among India's oldest working libraries.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Jainism is just a branch of Hinduism or Buddhism.

Truth: Jainism is a separate religion with its own teachers, beliefs and scriptures. It grew up in the same part of India at a similar time to Buddhism, and the three traditions share some ideas, but Jains have their own path.

Try this

Jain monks walk slowly and look at the ground to avoid stepping on tiny creatures. For five minutes, walk around your home or garden very slowly, watching where your feet land. Count how many ants, insects or other small living things you notice that you usually miss.
