Why does India get hailstorms?

On a hot April afternoon, balls of ice can crash onto Indian fields. The heat is exactly what makes them.

The short answer

Hail needs a very tall storm cloud with powerful winds rushing upwards inside it. India makes those clouds best in March, April and May, just before the monsoon. The ground gets baking hot, while the air several kilometres up is still cold and dry.

Hot air near the ground rises fast, like a bubble in a pot of boiling dal. It carries water droplets high into the cloud, where it is well below freezing. The droplets freeze onto tiny bits of ice and grow. Strong upward winds keep tossing them back up, so they keep growing. When a stone gets too heavy for the wind to hold, it falls.

Surprising but true

The deadliest hailstorm on record hit Moradabad, in what is now Uttar Pradesh, on 30 April 1888. About 246 people died.

How a hailstone grows

The clouds behind hail are cumulonimbus clouds, the giants that also make thunderstorms. Some reach more than 10 km up, as high as passenger jets fly. Inside, the rising air can move faster than a car on a highway.

A hailstone starts as a small seed, maybe a frozen raindrop or a grain of dust. Super-cooled water, which is liquid even below 0 °C, freezes onto it the moment they touch. Each trip up and down adds another coat. That is why a cut hailstone often shows rings, like the inside of an onion.

Most hail is the size of a pea or a marble. The heaviest ever weighed, about 1 kg, fell on Gopalganj in Bangladesh in 1986. The largest in the United States was 20 cm across, as wide as a dinner plate, in Vivian, South Dakota, in 2010.

Where hail hits in India

Hail falls most often in central and northern India, in states such as Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh, Punjab and Haryana, and in the Northeast. In eastern India and Bangladesh, violent spring storms called Nor'westers, or kalbaisakhi in Bengali, often bring hail along with strong winds.

Hills and high plateaus get extra hail. Up there, a falling stone has less warm air to pass through, so it reaches the ground before it melts. During the monsoon itself, hail is rare. The air is so warm and moist that the freezing level sits very high, and most ice melts on the way down.

Ice on the harvest

The timing is cruel for farmers. March and April are when wheat stands ripe in north Indian fields, mangoes hang on trees and grapes are ready in Nashik. Ten minutes of hail can flatten a crop that took months to grow. In early 2014, hailstorms across Maharashtra and nearby states damaged crops on a huge scale.

Hail can be dangerous for people and animals too. On 30 April 1888, a storm in Moradabad killed about 246 people. It is still listed as the deadliest hailstorm ever recorded.

Seeing it coming

Hailstorms are small and short, which makes them hard to forecast. A storm can grow from a few puffy clouds to a hail-maker in less than an hour. The India Meteorological Department watches for them with Doppler radar, which can spot rain and ice moving inside a cloud.

When the signs look strong, it sends out "nowcasts", warnings for the next few hours. Farmers can't move a field of wheat indoors. They can, though, bring animals under cover, pick ripe fruit early and park tractors in a shed.

In the United States, the worst hail falls on the Great Plains, in states such as Texas, Colorado and Nebraska. Hail there damages billions of dollars' worth of cars, roofs and crops in a bad year. The cause is the same as in India: hot ground below, cold dry air above, and a giant cloud in between.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Hail is a winter thing, so it only falls when the weather is cold.

Truth: Hail is most common in warm months. It needs hot ground to send air rushing upwards into very tall clouds, where the top is far below freezing even in summer.

Try this

With an adult's help, half-fill a small plastic cup with water and freeze it. Add a thin layer of water with a drop of milk in it and freeze again, then a layer of plain water. Pop it out and look at your stripes. Real hailstones build their layers the same way, one coat at a time.
