How was Bangladesh born?

On 16 December 1971, about 93,000 Pakistani soldiers surrendered in Dhaka, and a new country called Bangladesh was on the map.

The short answer

When British India was divided in 1947, Pakistan was made from two Muslim-majority regions far apart: West Pakistan, and East Pakistan, which was Bengal's eastern half. More people lived in the East, but power, money and the army were mostly in the West.

After years of unfair treatment, East Pakistan's people demanded self-rule. In March 1971 the army cracked down, and Bengali leaders declared independence. Nine months of fighting followed. In December, India entered the war. On 16 December, Pakistan's forces in the East surrendered, and Bangladesh became a free country.

Surprising but true

Before 1971, Pakistan was one country in two pieces, East and West, separated by about 1,600 km of Indian land.

One country, two pieces

At Partition, the new Pakistan had two wings with about 1,600 km of India between them. They shared a religion, but not much else. The people of the East spoke Bengali, ate rice and fish, and had their own literature and music. Most leaders in the West spoke Urdu or Punjabi.

In 1948, the government declared that Urdu alone would be the national language. Students in Dhaka protested. On 21 February 1952, police fired on a protest and several students died. Bangladesh remembers them every year, and UNESCO later made 21 February International Mother Language Day, which is why the Bengali language holds a special place in the story.

The election and the crackdown

In November 1970, a terrible cyclone hit East Pakistan and killed hundreds of thousands of people. Many felt the government in the West was slow to help.

A month later, Pakistan held a national election. The Awami League, led by Sheikh Mujibur Rahman, won almost every seat in the East, enough for a majority in the whole parliament. But the leaders in the West would not hand over power. Talks failed.

On the night of 25 March 1971, the Pakistani army began a crackdown in Dhaka. Students, teachers and ordinary people were killed. Bengali leaders declared independence the next day.

Nine months of war

Bengali soldiers, students and villagers formed the Mukti Bahini, the "freedom force", and fought a guerrilla war. Many terrible things happened that year, and people in Bangladesh remember them as a genocide. How many died is still argued over.

About 10 million refugees crossed into India, mostly into West Bengal, Tripura and Assam. It was one of the biggest refugee movements of the 20th century. India housed and fed them in camps, at a huge cost. Prime Minister Indira Gandhi travelled abroad to ask other countries for help, with mixed success.

Thirteen days in December

On 3 December 1971, Pakistan's air force struck Indian airfields. India declared war. Indian forces and the Mukti Bahini moved fast towards Dhaka, while India's navy blocked the ports.

The war pulled in the big powers of the Cold War. India had signed a friendship treaty with the Soviet Union that August. The United States backed Pakistan and sent a group of warships, led by the aircraft carrier USS Enterprise, into the Bay of Bengal. By the time it arrived, the fighting was nearly over.

The war lasted 13 days. On 16 December, Lieutenant General A. A. K. Niazi of Pakistan signed the surrender in Dhaka before Lieutenant General Jagjit Singh Aurora of India. About 93,000 soldiers and officials became prisoners of war. Most were returned to Pakistan after the Simla Agreement of 1972 between India and Pakistan.

A new country

Sheikh Mujibur Rahman returned home in January 1972 and became Bangladesh's leader. The country began with a ruined economy and millions of people coming back to burned villages. Bengali became the official language.

Bangladesh has changed a great deal since then. Today it has about 17 crore people (2024) and is one of the biggest makers of clothes in the world. Its people still mark 16 December as Victory Day, and 26 March as Independence Day.

Myth or fact?

Myth: India created Bangladesh in a two-week war.

Truth: The fight for Bangladesh began in March 1971, led by Bengalis themselves, including the Mukti Bahini freedom fighters. India's army joined in December and the war ended quickly, but the struggle had gone on for nine months.
