Is there a city under the sea near Dwarka?

The Mahabharata says the sea swallowed Krishna's city of Dwaraka. Off the Gujarat coast, divers have found stone walls and anchors on the seabed.

The short answer

Yes, there are stone remains under the sea near Dwarka in Gujarat. Divers have found large worked blocks, lines of stones that look like walls, and dozens of stone anchors. What nobody agrees on is how old they are.

The archaeologist S. R. Rao, who led many dives in the 1980s and 1990s, believed they belonged to a port city about 3,500 years old, and linked it with Krishna's Dwaraka. Many other archaeologists think at least some of the finds are much younger, perhaps from the last 2,000 years. The question is still open.

Surprising but true

Since the end of the last ice age, sea level around the world has risen by about 120 metres, drowning old coastlines everywhere, including India's.

The city in the story

In the Mahabharata, Krishna builds a splendid city called Dwaraka on the western coast. Near the end of the epic, after Krishna leaves the world, the sea rises and swallows the city. Arjuna watches the waves cover the streets.

The modern town of Dwarka is one of Hinduism's holiest places. Its Dwarkadhish temple draws huge crowds, especially at Janmashtami. For millions of believers, the drowned city is a matter of faith.

Divers on the seabed

In the early 1980s, S. R. Rao and the National Institute of Oceanography began searching the waters off Dwarka and nearby Bet Dwarka island. The work was hard. Strong currents stir up mud, and divers often could see only a metre or two in front of them.

They mapped blocks of stone, some dressed into shapes, lying in rough lines several metres under water. Around Bet Dwarka, they found pottery and a seal carved with animal heads. Rao argued the finds matched a late Harappan-age port.

Why experts disagree

The biggest problem is dating. Stone cannot be carbon dated. Carbon dating only works on things that were once alive, like wood, bones or seeds.

The many stone anchors are a good example. Triangular and three-holed stone anchors were used by sailors across the Indian Ocean for a very long time, into medieval centuries. Some researchers who studied the Dwarka anchors compared them to anchors from the last 1,000 to 2,000 years. Others noted that blocks may have fallen from later buildings on the shore and been rolled about by waves.

Rao's supporters reply that the pottery and other objects point to an early date. Without a sealed layer of datable material, it is very hard to settle.

There have been bolder claims too. In 2001, news reports described a "lost city" more than 9,000 years old in the Gulf of Khambhat, further down the Gujarat coast. Most archaeologists did not accept it, because the objects were dredged up from the seabed, not dug from layers that could be studied.

Drowned coasts are real

It is not strange to find buildings under the sea. At the peak of the last ice age, about 20,000 years ago, so much water was locked up in ice sheets that sea level was about 120 m lower than today. As the ice melted, the sea rose and drowned old shores worldwide.

Most of that rise had finished several thousand years ago. Smaller local changes continue, as land sinks or rises and storms eat away coasts. Ancient ports in Gujarat, like the Harappan dockyard at Lothal, show that this coast had busy sailors and traders more than 4,000 years ago.

Diving again

In February 2025, the Archaeological Survey of India sent its revived underwater archaeology team to explore off Dwarka and Bet Dwarka again, with modern equipment and several women divers among the archaeologists. Their aim is to record the remains carefully and collect objects that can be dated.

Whatever they find, the sea is still changing. Today, sea level is rising again, by a few millimetres every year, and coastal cities from Mumbai to Miami are watching it closely.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Scientists have proved that Krishna's Dwaraka lies under the sea.

Truth: Real stone structures and anchors have been found underwater. But their age is disputed, and no find so far clearly links them to the city in the Mahabharata.

In a tray, build a little coastline from clay or play dough and put tiny model houses near the edge. Add a little water for the sea, then place ice cubes on the "land" as glaciers. As they melt, watch the water creep up and cover the houses nearest the shore.
