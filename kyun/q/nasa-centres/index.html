What happens at NASA's space centres?

NASA's astronauts launch from Florida, but seconds after the rocket clears the tower, the mission is run from a room in Houston, Texas, about 1,400 km away.

The short answer

NASA, the US space agency, does its work at ten main centres spread across the country, plus its headquarters in Washington, DC. Each has a specialty. Some build rockets, some test engines, some train astronauts, some study Earth's climate, and one drives robots on Mars.

The two most famous are Kennedy Space Center in Florida, where astronauts blast off, and Johnson Space Center in Houston, Texas, where they train and where Mission Control watches over them. In April 2026 both were busy with Artemis II, which carried four astronauts around the Moon and back in about ten days, the first crew to go that far since 1972.

Surprising but true

Astronauts practise spacewalks underwater, in a pool at Johnson Space Center deep enough to hold full-size parts of the International Space Station.

Kennedy: the launch pad

Kennedy sits on the Atlantic coast, next to the US Space Force base at Cape Canaveral. Launching east over the ocean is safer, because falling rocket parts land in water, and Earth's spin gives a free push. That's why most launch sites are near a coast.

Its giant Vehicle Assembly Building was built for the Saturn V Moon rockets. It is so tall that its doors, about 139 m high, are among the largest in the world. Launch Complex 39A, where Apollo 11 lifted off, is still used today. Visitors can tour the site and stand under a real Saturn V lying on its side.

Johnson: Mission Control

In Texas, Johnson Space Center is home to NASA's astronauts. They learn to fly jets, fix space station parts and survive in rough places. One of its key tools is a huge indoor pool where astronauts in spacesuits practise spacewalks underwater, which feels a bit like floating in space. Much of the astronaut training happens here. Next door, the visitor centre, Space Center Houston, lets people walk inside a full-size model of a space shuttle sitting on top of a real jumbo jet.

Mission Control is here too. Flight controllers sit at rows of screens, watching every detail of a mission. When Apollo 13's crew radioed "Houston, we've had a problem" in 1970, it was these engineers who worked out how to get them home.

JPL: driving robots far away

In Pasadena, California, the Jet Propulsion Laboratory builds and drives robotic spacecraft. It is run for NASA by the Caltech university. Engineers there sent the Voyager probes past the outer planets and now operate the Mars rovers, including Curiosity and Perseverance.

Because radio signals take several minutes to reach Mars, nobody can steer a rover with a joystick. Drivers plan each day's moves in advance and send them up as a list of commands.

NASA also works with private companies and other countries. SpaceX rockets carry astronauts to the space station from Launch Complex 39A, which NASA leases to the company. In 2025 a radar satellite called NISAR, built jointly by NASA and India's space agency, launched from Sriharikota to map changes on Earth's surface. It was one of the biggest joint projects in NASA's links with ISRO.

The rest of the team

Marshall Space Flight Center in Alabama designed the Saturn V and now the Space Launch System rocket used for Artemis. Stennis in Mississippi fires rocket engines on giant test stands that shake the ground for kilometres. Goddard in Maryland builds Earth-watching satellites and helps run the Hubble and Webb space telescopes.

Langley in Virginia, the oldest centre, began in 1917 as an aircraft lab. In the 1950s and 1960s, women mathematicians there, including Katherine Johnson, worked out spacecraft paths by hand. Glenn in Ohio works on engines and power. Ames and Armstrong, both in California, study aircraft and supercomputing. Together they show that sending one person to space takes thousands of people on the ground, most of whom never leave Earth.

Myth or fact?

Myth: NASA does all its work at one big space centre.

Truth: NASA is spread across ten main centres in different states, each with its own job, from testing rocket engines in Mississippi to studying aircraft in California.
