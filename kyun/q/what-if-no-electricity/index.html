What if the power went out everywhere for a week?

In July 2012, over 600 million people in India lost power at once. Now stretch that to the whole world, for seven days.

The short answer

Electricity itself can't stop. Your heart beats because of electrical signals, and your nerves send messages the same way. So this is really a question about the power grid, the giant web of wires and power stations that keeps lights, phones and pumps running.

If that grid shut down everywhere for a week, the first hour would feel like a normal power cut. By the end of day one, phones, cash machines and water pumps would be failing. By day seven, the biggest problems would be clean water, cold food and medicine.

Surprising but true

Electricity can never really switch off, because your heartbeat and every thought in your brain run on tiny electrical signals.

The first few hours

When the grid goes down, homes with a battery inverter or a diesel generator keep a few lights on. Hospitals and airports switch to backup generators within seconds.

Mobile towers have batteries too, but many last only a few hours. Then the network starts to fade. A fridge stays cold for about four hours if nobody opens the door. A full freezer can hold out for about two days.

Trains powered from overhead wires stop where they are. That includes metros in Delhi, Mumbai locals and New York's subway, though diesel trains could keep moving.

Day one to day three

In big cities, electric pumps move water through pipes and up into rooftop tanks. As tanks empty, taps in high-rise flats run dry. Petrol pumps also need electricity to pump fuel out of underground tanks, so cars and generators soon run low.

Money gets strange. Card machines, UPI payments and bank computers all need power and network. Shops that still sell anything take only cash.

Traffic lights go dark too, so every big crossing turns into a slow, honking puzzle. In 2003, ordinary New Yorkers stepped into the road to wave cars through. Lifts stop between floors, which is why firefighters spend the first hours of any big blackout rescuing people stuck inside them.

What real blackouts teach us

On 30 and 31 July 2012, parts of India's northern and eastern grids collapsed. Over 600 million people lost power, the largest blackout ever recorded. Trains stopped and some miners were trapped underground until power came back. Most areas were back within a day or two.

On 14 August 2003, about 55 million people in the north-eastern USA and Canada lost power. Many New Yorkers walked home across the bridges. Without city lights, people saw a sky full of stars that most had never seen from their own street.

How could it really happen?

Storms on the Sun can do it. In September 1859, a huge solar storm hit Earth. Sparks flew from telegraph equipment, and some operators found they could send messages with their batteries unplugged. This is called the Carrington Event.

In March 1989, a smaller solar storm knocked out power across Quebec in Canada for about nine hours. A storm the size of 1859's today could damage big transformers, which take months to replace. That is why scientists watch the Sun closely.

By the end of the week

The hardest part would be water, because pumping and cleaning it both need power. Food in cold stores would spoil, and medicines like insulin that must stay cool would be at risk.

But people are good at this. Villages with hand pumps and wells would cope better than tall city towers. Neighbours would share, cook together and pass news by word of mouth. And when the power returned, it would likely come back in patches, one area at a time.

Then the night sky would brighten into its usual orange glow. The stars would vanish again, which is exactly why astronomers worry about skies that are never dark.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If the power goes out, the water in the taps keeps flowing as normal.

Truth: In many cities, electric pumps push water to homes and lift it into the tanks on top of buildings. Once the tank is empty, the taps in tall buildings can run dry.

Try this

Spend one evening hour at home with the lights and screens off (with an adult around and a torch handy). Make a list of every single thing you wanted to do that needed power. Most families are surprised how long the list gets.
