What does an entrepreneur do?

In 1981, seven young engineers in Pune started a software company with ₹10,000, money Narayana Murthy borrowed from his wife, Sudha. They called it Infosys.

The short answer

An entrepreneur is someone who starts a new business, taking a risk in the hope that it will succeed. The word comes from the French entreprendre, meaning "to undertake". Entrepreneurs spot a problem or a need, come up with a way to solve it, and gather the people, money and tools to make it happen.

A startup is a young company trying to grow fast, often using new technology. But entrepreneurs are everywhere: the woman who opens a tiffin service from her kitchen, the farmer who starts selling honey online, the teenager who fixes phones. What they share is a willingness to try something without knowing whether it will work.

Surprising but true

The garage in Palo Alto, California, where Bill Hewlett and David Packard started their company in 1939, is now a California historical landmark, often called the birthplace of Silicon Valley.

Garages and small rooms

Many famous companies began in tiny spaces. In 1939, Bill Hewlett and David Packard started making electronic equipment in a rented garage in Palo Alto, California, with about $538. That garage is now called the birthplace of Silicon Valley. Decades later, Apple's first computers were put together in the Jobs family home.

In 1981, Narayana Murthy and six colleagues started Infosys in Pune. Murthy borrowed ₹10,000 from his wife, Sudha Murty, to get going. For years, the company struggled. It later became one of India's biggest software firms and helped make Bengaluru a centre of technology.

What the job looks like

Running a startup is less about one brilliant idea and more about solving hundreds of small problems. Founders talk to customers, build a first simple version, see what goes wrong and improve it. They hire people, manage money and keep going when things fail.

Many startups need more money than the founders have. They may borrow, or they may sell part of their company to investors in return for cash. Some investors, called venture capitalists, put money into many young firms, knowing most will fail but hoping one or two will grow huge. Later, a successful company may sell shares on the stock market.

India's startup boom

India now has one of the largest startup scenes in the world. The government launched its Startup India programme in January 2016, offering tax breaks and simpler rules. By 2025, more than 1.5 lakh startups had been officially recognised.

Take Flipkart. In 2007, two former engineers, Sachin Bansal and Binny Bansal (who are not related), began selling books online from a small flat in Bengaluru. They sometimes delivered parcels themselves. Within about a decade it had grown into one of India's biggest online shops, selling everything from phones to fridges.

Indian startups have changed how people pay, travel, shop and order food. Paying a shopkeeper with a phone, or booking an autorickshaw from an app, would have seemed odd to most people in 2010. Many new firms also come from smaller cities, not just Bengaluru, Delhi and Mumbai.

Failure as part of the story

Most new businesses don't last. Estimates vary, but a large share of startups close within their first few years. Many entrepreneurs fail at least once before something works.

That isn't always a disaster. A failed startup teaches lessons and often leaves behind skilled people who start again. Some entrepreneurs aren't after money at all. Social entrepreneurs try to solve problems like clean water or education, sometimes as cooperatives or charities. What counts, in the end, is whether people's lives get easier.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Successful entrepreneurs are lone geniuses who get it right first time.

Truth: Most businesses are built by teams, and most founders fail or change direction several times. Learning quickly from mistakes matters more than having a perfect first idea.

Try this

Think of a small problem at home or school, like lost pencils or a messy shoe rack. Sketch three possible fixes, then ask five people which one they would use and why. Change your best idea using what they said. That loop of trying, asking and improving is what entrepreneurs do every day.
