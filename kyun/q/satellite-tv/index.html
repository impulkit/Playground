How does satellite TV work?

The dish on your roof is pointing at a machine about 36,000 km away that seems to hang still in the sky, even though it is racing along at about 11,000 km an hour.

The short answer

A TV station sends its programmes up to a satellite in space using a big dish on the ground. The satellite catches the signal, boosts it and beams it back down over a huge area, sometimes a whole country or more. The dish on a roof or balcony catches that faint signal and passes it to a box that turns it into pictures and sound.

The clever part is where the satellite sits. TV satellites orbit about 35,786 km above the equator, where one trip round the Earth takes almost exactly a day. So from the ground, the satellite never seems to move, and the dish can stay fixed.

Surprising but true

The idea of TV satellites parked in one spot above the equator was described in 1945 by the science fiction writer Arthur C. Clarke, nearly 20 years before the first one worked.

The parking spot in the sky

Most satellites go round the Earth much faster than once a day. The International Space Station, for example, circles every 90 minutes or so. The higher a satellite goes, the slower it moves, because gravity is weaker further away.

At about 35,786 km, one orbit lasts 24 hours. This is called a geostationary orbit, meaning "staying still over the Earth". The writer Arthur C. Clarke described it in a magazine article in 1945. He suggested that three satellites spaced around the equator could send radio and TV to almost the whole planet. Because of him, this orbit is sometimes called the Clarke orbit.

Why the dish is curved

By the time a signal has travelled 36,000 km, it is very weak. The dish works like a cupped hand behind your ear. Its curved shape reflects the signal and focuses it on one small point, where a part called the LNB sits on an arm in front of the dish.

The LNB boosts the signal and passes it down a cable to the set-top box. The box decodes the channels and checks whether you have paid for them. Bigger dishes catch more signal, which is why the dishes of the 1980s were often wider than a person was tall.

The first TV from space

The first satellite to relay live TV across the Atlantic was Telstar, in July 1962. It wasn't geostationary, so each broadcast lasted only as long as Telstar was passing overhead, about 20 minutes at a time. In 1964, a geostationary satellite called Syncom 3 carried pictures of the Tokyo Olympics to the United States.

India's own story began with the SITE experiment of 1975, which beamed lessons to villages using a borrowed NASA satellite. Today, India's GSAT and INSAT satellites, built by ISRO, carry many of the country's TV channels.

Dishes on every rooftop

In India, private direct-to-home, or DTH, services started in 2003. Before that, most homes got satellite channels through a local cable operator. The operator had a large dish and ran wires along lanes and up the walls of buildings. DTH gave each home its own small dish, usually about 60 centimetres across.

Doordarshan launched its free DTH service, now called DD Free Dish, in 2004. Families buy the dish and box once and then watch dozens of channels without a monthly bill. It is popular in villages and hill areas that are hard to reach by cable.

When the rain comes

Satellite TV has one weakness. Heavy rain can freeze or blank the picture for a few minutes. Raindrops absorb and scatter the high-frequency radio waves that carry the signal, an effect engineers call rain fade. During a strong monsoon downpour, many Indian families know to wait it out.

There is a stranger glitch too. Twice a year, around the equinoxes, the Sun passes directly behind the satellite as seen from a dish. For a few minutes each day, the Sun's radio noise drowns out the signal.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Satellites stay up because there is no gravity in space.

Truth: Gravity at a TV satellite's height is still there, just weaker. The satellite is falling towards Earth all the time, but it moves sideways so fast that it keeps missing.

Try this

Stand in the middle of a room while a friend walks in a circle around you, always facing you. If you turn on the spot at the same speed, your friend stays in front of your nose. That is how a TV satellite stays over the same patch of Earth.
