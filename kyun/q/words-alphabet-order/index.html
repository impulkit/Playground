Why is the alphabet in that order?

A clay tablet dug up in Syria shows people reciting almost the same letter order as our ABC about 3,400 years ago, and nobody knows why they chose it.

The short answer

Nobody planned the order for English. We inherited it, almost letter by letter, from a chain of older alphabets: Latin from Rome, Greek before that, and before the Greeks, the Phoenicians and their neighbours in the eastern Mediterranean.

Clay tablets from the ancient city of Ugarit, in today's Syria, list letters in nearly the same sequence about 3,400 years ago. Their list began aleph, beth, gimel, daleth, which became our A, B, C, D. Why those early writers lined them up that way is a genuine mystery. Some scholars think it helped children memorise them, maybe as a chant. Others think the order is simply an accident that stuck.

Surprising but true

Z is at the end because the Romans threw it out of their alphabet, then later added it back as an afterthought.

Letters that were pictures

The first alphabet grew out of Egyptian hieroglyphs, used by Semitic-speaking workers about 3,800 years ago. They took small pictures and used each for the first sound of its name. Aleph meant ox, and its letter was an ox head. Turn our capital A upside down and you can still see the horns.

Beth meant house, which gave us B. Gimel may have been a throwing stick or a camel, and it became the Greek gamma and our C and G. The Greeks kept the order but renamed the letters alpha, beta, gamma, delta. Put the first two together and you get the word "alphabet".

The strange case of Z

The early Latin alphabet had Z in seventh place, where Greek had zeta. Around 300 BCE the Romans decided they didn't need it, because the sound had vanished from Latin, and dropped it.

That left a gap. Romans had been using C for both the "k" and "g" sounds, which caused confusion. So they made a new letter by adding a little bar to C, creating G, and slotted it into Z's old seat. Two centuries later, when educated Romans wanted to write Greek words, they brought Z back. Its old place was taken, so it went to the end of the line, along with Y.

The newest letters

For centuries, I and J were the same letter, and so were U and V. Printers in the 1500s and 1600s slowly started treating them as separate letters. W began as two Vs or two Us written together, which is why we still call it "double U". In French it is "double V".

English children in the 1800s even recited "&" after Z as if it were a letter. They ended with "and per se and", which slurred over time into the word "ampersand".

A different ancient order

There was once a rival alphabet order, beginning h, l, ḥ, m. It was used in ancient South Arabia, and it survives today in Ethiopia. Ethiopia's Ge'ez script, used for Amharic, still starts with ha, la, ḥa, ma. A child in Addis Ababa learns a completely different sequence from a child in Chicago, both descended from the same early experiments.

India did it by science

Indian scripts took a very different path. Over 2,000 years ago, Sanskrit grammarians studied exactly how the mouth makes each sound, and arranged the alphabet to match. Vowels come first. Then consonants march from the throat (ka, kha, ga, gha, ṅa) to the palate, the roof of the mouth, the teeth and finally the lips (pa, pha, ba, bha, ma).

Each row follows the same pattern of plain, breathy, voiced, breathy-voiced and nasal sounds. Devanagari, Bengali, Tamil, Kannada and other Indian scripts all use versions of this order. It is one of the most logical alphabets ever arranged, built by the careful thinkers behind Sanskrit.

Looking up a word in an English dictionary means following a sequence that is about 3,400 years old. Putting words in that order was a later invention, and the people who first tried it have a story of their own in dictionaries.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The ABC order was designed so that the most useful or common letters come first.

Truth: E, T and S are the most common letters in English, but they are scattered through the alphabet. The order is very old and was inherited, not designed for English at all.

Try this

Say "ka, cha, ṭa, ta, pa" slowly, one after another, and notice where your tongue or lips touch each time. You should feel the sound moving from the back of your mouth to the front. Now try "a, b, c, d" and see if you can find any pattern at all.
