How did Indian boxers become champions?

A town in Haryana nicknamed "Mini Cuba" and a small state in the Northeast have produced many of India's best boxers, and most of the recent world champions are women.

The short answer

For a long time, India won very little in boxing. That changed in the 2000s. In 2008, Vijender Singh won India's first Olympic boxing medal, a bronze in Beijing. Four years later, Mary Kom won bronze at London 2012, the first Games to include women's boxing.

Since then, Indian women in particular have become a force. Nikhat Zareen, Lovlina Borgohain, Nitu Ghanghas, Saweety Boora, Jaismine Lamboria and Minakshi Hooda have all won world titles. Much of this talent comes from two places: the small town of Bhiwani in Haryana, and Manipur in the Northeast.

Surprising but true

Mary Kom won six world amateur titles over 16 years, from 2002 to 2018, and became a mother along the way.

The town called Mini Cuba

Cuba, a small island nation, has won more Olympic boxing golds than almost anyone. So when Bhiwani started producing boxer after boxer, people called it "Mini Cuba". Vijender Singh trained there, and so did many others on India's national team.

Part of the story begins with Hawa Singh, a soldier who won heavyweight gold at the Asian Games in 1966 and 1970. He later coached in Bhiwani and helped build a boxing culture there. Haryana already loved wrestling and kabaddi. Families saw that sport could lead to government jobs, often in the army or police, and young people lined up at the boxing clubs.

Manipur's champions

Over in Manipur, the hero was Ngangom Dingko Singh, who won gold at the 1998 Asian Games in Bangkok. A teenage girl from a farming family, Mangte Chungneijang Mary Kom, was inspired by him and started training soon after.

Mary Kom became one of the most successful amateur boxers ever. She won six world titles between 2002 and 2018, an Olympic bronze, and a Commonwealth Games gold in 2018. She fought in lighter weight classes, often against taller opponents, and won with speed and clever footwork. A Hindi film about her life came out in 2014.

A golden decade for women

Lovlina Borgohain, from a village in Assam, won bronze at the Tokyo Olympics, held in 2021. Nikhat Zareen, from Nizamabad in Telangana, won world titles in 2022 and 2023. At the 2023 World Championships in New Delhi, Indian women won four gold medals.

In September 2025, at the World Boxing Championships in Liverpool, Jaismine Lamboria and Minakshi Hooda both became world champions. Many of these boxers had to argue with relatives or neighbours who thought boxing was no sport for girls. Their medals have changed a lot of minds, and many villages now hold proud welcomes for returning champions.

What happens in the ring

An Olympic bout lasts three rounds of three minutes each, with a minute's rest between them. Five judges score each round. They reward clean punches that land with the front of the glove, as well as good defence and control of the fight. Boxers are grouped by weight, so a 48 kg flyweight never has to fight someone twice her size. Elite men stopped wearing padded headguards at major amateur events in 2013, after officials argued that the guards gave a false sense of safety.

Boxers need fast reflexes to slip a punch and fire one back. They skip, run and spar for hours. Boxing can cause injuries, especially to the head, which is why rules, gloves, doctors and referees are so strict.

Highs and lows

At Paris 2024, India's boxers came home without a medal, a reminder of how hard the Olympics are. But boxing will be at the 2028 Games in Los Angeles, and India now has more young boxers, coaches and training centres than ever. Many of those young boxers are girls who grew up watching Mary Kom on television, the way she once watched Dingko Singh.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Boxing is mostly about who is strongest and hits hardest.

Truth: In Olympic-style boxing, points come from clean punches, and speed, timing and defence often beat raw power. Many champions, including Mary Kom, were smaller than their opponents.

Try this

Boxers train their footwork with a skipping rope. Count how many skips you can do in 30 seconds, rest for a minute, and try again. Then try skipping on the balls of your feet without letting your heels touch the ground, the way boxers move in the ring.
