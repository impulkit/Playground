How does water reach the top floor?

New York still has thousands of wooden water tanks perched on its rooftops, because pumping water up and letting it fall is a cheap, reliable trick.

The short answer

Water reaches the top floor in one of two ways: something pushes it up, or it falls down from somewhere even higher. Most buildings use both. Pumps push water up to a tank on the roof or a high reservoir. Then gravity does the rest, pulling water down through pipes to every tap.

The deeper water is, the harder it presses. Every 10 metres of water adds about one atmosphere of pressure, roughly the weight of the whole sky above you pressing on each square centimetre. So a tank on the roof of a five-storey building gives the ground-floor taps a strong push, while the top floor gets only a gentle flow.

Surprising but true

No suction pump, however strong, can pull water up a pipe more than about 10 metres. To go higher, water has to be pushed from below.

The trick pump-makers couldn't beat

About 400 years ago, well-diggers in Italy noticed something odd. Their suction pumps, which worked like giant drinking straws, could not lift water more than about 10 metres, however hard they pumped. Scientists, including Galileo's student Evangelista Torricelli, worked out why.

A suction pump doesn't really pull water. It removes air from the pipe, and the weight of the air outside pushes the water up. That push is only strong enough to hold up about 10 metres of water. To lift water any higher, you have to push it from below, with a pump at the bottom.

The Indian rooftop tank

In many Indian towns, city water arrives only for a few hours a day. So homes store it. Water flows into an underground tank called a sump. An electric motor then pumps it up to a plastic or concrete tank on the roof, often a black one you can see from the street.

From the roof tank, water flows down whenever you open a tap. This setup works even when the city supply stops or the power goes out, as long as the tank has water. The catch is pressure. A shower on the top floor, just a few metres below the tank, may only dribble.

Water towers and high reservoirs

Cities use the same idea on a bigger scale. In the United States, many towns have a water tower: a big tank on tall legs, often painted with the town's name. Pumps fill it at night, when fewer people need power, and gravity supplies homes all day.

In Mumbai, a big reservoir sits on top of Malabar Hill, and the Hanging Gardens were laid out over it in the 1880s. In New York, thousands of wooden tanks sit on roofs. Wood swells when wet and seals itself, keeps water cool and is cheap to replace.

Skyscrapers and booster pumps

A very tall building has a problem. If one tank at the top fed every floor, the pressure at the bottom would be strong enough to burst pipes. If one pump at the bottom pushed water to the top, it would need enormous force.

So skyscrapers split the job. Water is pumped up in stages, to tanks on middle floors, then higher again. Each stage feeds the floors just below it. Pressure-reducing valves keep the force at each tap gentle. In the tallest towers, such as the Burj Khalifa in Dubai, water climbs through several of these stages before it reaches the top.

Old ideas, still working

The ancient Romans moved water into cities with aqueducts, long channels sloping gently downhill for many kilometres, sometimes carried across valleys on stone arches. Some Roman aqueducts still stand, and one in Rome still feeds famous fountains.

Your own body solves the same problem. Your heart is a pump that pushes blood up to your brain, against gravity, about once every second. A giraffe's heart has to push blood about two metres up its neck, so its blood pressure is roughly twice ours.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Water comes out of taps because the pipe sucks it in from the river or reservoir.

Truth: Water is pushed, not sucked. Either pumps push it, or it flows down from a higher tank or reservoir, and the weight of water above gives it pressure. Suction alone can't lift water more than about 10 metres.

Try this

Take an empty plastic bottle and, with an adult's help, poke three small holes down one side, one near the top, one in the middle and one near the bottom. Cover them with tape, fill the bottle with water and stand it in a sink. Pull off the tape and watch which stream shoots out farthest.
