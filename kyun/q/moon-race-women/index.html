Who were the women who did the maths for the space race?

Before John Glenn would fly around the Earth in 1962, he asked for one person to check the computer's numbers by hand. She was Katherine Johnson.

The short answer

When the USA raced the Soviet Union in the space race, it needed huge amounts of maths. How fast must a rocket go? At what angle should a capsule come back so it neither bounces off the air nor burns up? Much of this was worked out by hand, with pencils, slide rules and desk calculators.

Many of the people doing those sums were women. Some of the best were Black women at NASA's Langley centre in Virginia, who worked in a separate unit because of segregation laws. Katherine Johnson, Dorothy Vaughan and Mary Jackson were three of them. Their story became widely known through the 2016 book and film Hidden Figures.

Surprising but true

At NASA in the 1950s and 1960s, the word "computer" often meant a person, usually a woman, who did calculations by hand.

When computers were people

Before electronic machines took over, "computer" was a job title. From 1935, the aircraft research agency NACA, which later became NASA, hired women to check data from wind tunnels and test flights. The work was careful and repetitive, and it needed real skill.

During the Second World War, more workers were needed, and Langley began hiring Black women with maths degrees. Virginia's laws kept them apart. They worked in the "West Area" unit, ate at a separate table, and used separate bathrooms. Their work, though, went into the same aircraft and rockets as everyone else's.

Dorothy Vaughan, the leader

Dorothy Vaughan was a maths teacher before she joined Langley in 1943. In 1949 she became head of the West Area unit, the first Black supervisor at the agency.

She saw the future coming. When IBM's electronic computers arrived, she taught herself and her team the programming language FORTRAN. When the segregated unit was closed in 1958, many of her staff moved straight into the new computing work.

Katherine Johnson, the trajectory expert

Katherine Johnson loved numbers from childhood and finished high school at 14. At Langley she worked on the maths of flight paths, called trajectories. She calculated the path for Alan Shepard, the first American in space, in 1961.

The next year, John Glenn prepared to become the first American to orbit the Earth. NASA's electronic computers had worked out his route, but Glenn wanted it checked. According to NASA's history, he asked for Johnson by name. She checked the numbers by hand, and they agreed. Later she helped with calculations for the Apollo Moon landings. In 2015 she was awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom.

Mary Jackson, the engineer

Mary Jackson began as a computer in the West Area. An engineer noticed her talent and encouraged her to train as an engineer herself. The classes she needed were held at an all-white school, so she had to get special permission from the city to attend. In 1958 she became NASA's first Black woman engineer. Later she worked to help other women and minorities get hired and promoted.

Code for the Moon

At the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Margaret Hamilton led the team that wrote software for the Apollo guidance computer. During the first Moon landing in 1969, alarms flashed as the computer was flooded with tasks. Her team's software was designed to drop less important jobs and keep going, and the landing went ahead.

India has its own story. Women scientists and engineers helped lead ISRO's Mars Orbiter Mission in 2014 and the Chandrayaan missions to the Moon, doing the same kind of careful calculation, now with far faster machines.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The first American astronauts flew with computers doing all the calculations.

Truth: Electronic computers helped, but for years much of the work was done or checked by hand. Teams of human computers worked out flight paths, and astronauts trusted their results.
