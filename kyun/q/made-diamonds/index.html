How are diamonds formed and cut?

The diamond in a ring was probably born more than a billion years ago, about 150 kilometres underground, before there were any animals on Earth.

The short answer

A diamond is carbon, the same stuff as the graphite in your pencil. The difference is how the atoms are packed. In graphite, carbon atoms sit in flat sheets that slide apart. In diamond, each atom locks onto four others in a tight 3D frame, which makes it the hardest natural material known.

Natural diamonds form about 150 to 200 kilometres underground, in the mantle, where it is hotter than 1,000 °C and the pressure is crushing. Rare, deep volcanic eruptions carried them up to the surface. Then miners dig them out, and cutters grind them into shapes that throw light back like fire.

Surprising but true

For about 2,000 years, almost every diamond known to the world came from India, mostly from riverbeds along the Krishna River, and was traded at Golconda.

Born deep, delivered by volcanoes

Most natural diamonds are between 1 and 3.5 billion years old. They formed long before dinosaurs, before plants grew on land. Down in the mantle, the pressure is roughly 50,000 times that of the air around you, like balancing a loaded lorry on a fingernail.

Diamonds would stay there forever without a ride. Long ago, unusual eruptions blasted up from deep below and through the crust, probably rising tens of kilometres an hour. They left carrot-shaped pipes of a rock called kimberlite, named after Kimberley in South Africa. If the trip had been slow, the diamonds would have turned into graphite on the way.

When India was the world's only source

For about 2,000 years, nearly all the world's diamonds came from India. They were washed out of riverbeds along the Krishna River and traded at Golconda, near today's Hyderabad. The Koh-i-Noor and the famous blue Hope Diamond, now in the Smithsonian in Washington DC, both came from Indian mines.

In the 1720s, diamonds were found in Brazil. Then in 1867, a child in South Africa picked up a shiny stone near a riverbank, which turned out to be a big diamond. Within a few years, huge mines opened there. The biggest gem diamond ever found, the Cullinan, came from a South African mine in 1905. It weighed 3,106 carats, about 620 grams, as heavy as a small papaya.

Cutting a stone into fire

A rough diamond looks like cloudy glass. Cutters plan every cut carefully, often with computer scans, to get the biggest, brightest stone with the fewest flaws. Only diamond can scratch diamond, so stones are sawn with lasers and ground on spinning wheels coated with diamond dust.

The popular round brilliant cut has 57 or 58 flat faces called facets. In 1919, an engineer named Marcel Tolkowsky worked out angles that bounce the most light back to your eye. Light enters the top, bounces off the slanted bottom faces and comes back out split into colours.

Surat, the diamond city

Most of the world's diamonds pass through one Indian city. Surat in Gujarat cuts and polishes the large majority of them, often said to be around 90%, in thousands of workshops. A stone might be mined in Botswana or Russia, cut in Surat and sold in New York. The boom began in the 1960s and 70s, when Indian traders bought small, cheap rough stones that cutters elsewhere thought were not worth the work. Skilled hands made them sparkle, and the industry grew from there.

Diamonds made in a lab

Since the 1950s, scientists have been able to make diamonds by copying the heat and pressure of the deep Earth. Newer machines grow them layer by layer from carbon-rich gas. Lab-grown diamonds have the same atoms and the same hardness as mined ones. Factories use tiny ones on drill bits and saws.

And the strangest part? Scientists think the crushing depths inside Neptune and Uranus may squeeze carbon into crystals too, so diamonds could be falling inside those planets like hail.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Diamonds are unbreakable.

Truth: Diamond is very hard, which means it is hard to scratch. But it is also brittle. A sharp knock in the wrong direction can split or chip it, which is why cutters can cleave a rough diamond with one well-aimed tap.

Try this

Shine a torch through a glass of water, a clear marble and a piece of glass bangle in a dark room, and look at the patterns on the wall. See which one splits the light into the most colours. Diamonds are cut to bend and split light like this, but far more strongly.
