What kinds of soil does India have?

The black soil of the Deccan began as lava from giant volcanic eruptions about 66 million years ago. Today it grows much of India's cotton.

The short answer

India has a huge range of soils, because it has every kind of landscape: high mountains, wide river plains, old plateaus, deserts, rainforests and long coasts. Scientists at the Indian Council of Agricultural Research group them into about eight main types.

The most widespread is alluvial soil, laid down by rivers, which covers roughly two-fifths of the country. Then come black soil, red and yellow soil, laterite, desert soil, salty soil, marshy soil and mountain soil. Each has its own colour, texture and favourite crops, and together they explain a lot about what Indians grow and eat.

Surprising but true

Laterite, a red soil from Kerala, hardens like stone when it dries. It was first described and named there in 1807, from the Latin word for brick, because people cut it into building blocks.

Gifts of the rivers

Rivers like the Ganga, Yamuna and Brahmaputra carry fine mud down from the Himalaya. When they flood, they spread it across the plains. Over thousands of years this built up deep, soft alluvial soil, or alluvium, across Punjab, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, West Bengal and Assam, and in river deltas along the coast.

It is easy to plough and rich in nutrients, which is why the northern plains became India's great grain belt, growing wheat, rice and sugarcane. Farmers sometimes divide it into khadar, the newer soil near rivers that is refreshed by floods, and bhangar, older soil on higher ground.

Black soil from lava

About 66 million years ago, huge volcanic eruptions in western India poured out layer upon layer of lava, now called the Deccan Traps. Over millions of years, that dark rock weathered into black soil, also called regur.

Black soil covers much of Maharashtra and parts of Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh and Karnataka. It is full of clay, holds water for a long time and is excellent for cotton, which is why it is also called black cotton soil. In summer it cracks so deeply you could slide a ruler into the gaps.

Red, yellow and laterite

Across much of Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Odisha and Chhattisgarh, soils are red. The colour comes from iron in the old rocks underneath, which forms a rusty compound. Where the soil is damp, it can look yellow instead.

In places with heavy rain and heat, such as Kerala, Goa and parts of the Northeast, rain washes many minerals away and leaves soil rich in iron and aluminium. This is laterite. In 1807 a Scottish doctor, Francis Buchanan, saw people in Kerala cutting it into blocks that hardened in the air, and named it after the Latin word for brick. Laterite houses and walls are still common there.

Sand, salt and marsh

In the Thar Desert of Rajasthan, soils are sandy, dry and low in humus, the dark rotting plant matter that feeds crops. With irrigation, though, farmers grow millets, pulses and even wheat. Around the Rann of Kutch and parts of the coast, salty soils make farming hard. Over-watering fields and poor drainage can also make soil salty, as has happened in parts of Punjab and Haryana.

In wet places like Kuttanad in Kerala, where farmers grow rice below sea level, and in the Sundarbans, soils are marshy and heavy with plant remains. In the Himalaya, thin mountain and forest soils cling to the slopes.

Looking after soil

Soil forms very slowly. A single centimetre of topsoil can take hundreds of years to build up, yet a heavy storm can wash it away in a day. Healthy soil is full of life, from fungi to earthworms, which mix it and keep it loose.

Since 2015, India's Soil Health Card scheme has tested farm soils and told farmers which nutrients they lack. The idea is simple: feed the soil what it needs, and it will keep feeding us. Farmers in the American Midwest learned this the hard way in the 1930s, when poor farming and drought turned the Great Plains into the Dust Bowl.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Soil is just dead dirt.

Truth: Healthy soil is alive. A spoonful can contain more tiny living things than there are people on Earth, along with roots, fungi, insects and worms. They break down dead plants and keep the soil fertile.

Try this

Collect a handful of soil from two different places, such as a garden and a roadside, with an adult's permission. Put each in a clear jar, fill with water, shake well and leave overnight. The soil settles in layers, with heavy sand at the bottom, then silt, then fine clay on top. Compare the layers in the two jars.
