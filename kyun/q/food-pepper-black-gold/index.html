Why was black pepper called black gold?

When an Egyptian pharaoh named Ramesses II was mummified over 3,000 years ago, someone pushed peppercorns from India up his nose.

The short answer

Black pepper grows on a climbing vine native to the rainforests of the Western Ghats in south India. For thousands of years, it was one of the most wanted goods on Earth. Romans, Arabs, Egyptians, Chinese and Europeans paid huge sums for it.

Peppercorns were light, kept for years and could be shipped across oceans without rotting. In places far from India, they could be worth a lot, and they were sometimes used almost like money. That is why pepper earned the nickname "black gold". The hunt for it helped start long sea voyages, wars and empires, including the journey that brought the Portuguese to India.

Surprising but true

Peppercorns found in the mummy of Ramesses II, who died in 1213 BCE, show that Indian pepper was travelling thousands of kilometres more than 3,000 years ago.

Pepper up a pharaoh's nose

The oldest known pepper found far from India sits in the mummy of Ramesses II, an Egyptian pharaoh who died in 1213 BCE. Scientists examining his body found black peppercorns stuffed into his nostrils, probably as part of the preparation for burial.

Nobody grew pepper in Egypt. Those peppercorns must have travelled from south India, passed from trader to trader, over sea and land. The making of mummies used many precious goods, and pepper was one of them.

Rome's spice habit

By the time of the Roman Empire, ships were sailing straight across the Arabian Sea using the monsoon winds. They came to Muziris, a busy port on the Kerala coast, loaded with gold coins and went home with pepper. Hoards of Roman coins have been dug up in south India.

The Roman writer Pliny complained that India drained Rome of huge amounts of money every year for luxuries like pepper. A cookbook from Rome uses pepper in most of its recipes, even sweet ones. When the city of Rome was besieged in 408 CE, the attackers' demands reportedly included about 3,000 pounds of pepper.

What was the Silk Road?Silk from China reached Rome, and Buddhism reached China, along roads that almost nobody travelled from end to end.

Rent paid in peppercorns

In medieval Europe, pepper was so valued that people sometimes paid rent, taxes or dowries in peppercorns. In English law, a "peppercorn rent" once meant a real payment in pepper. Today it means a tiny, token rent, because pepper is cheap now.

Venice and Arab traders controlled much of the spice route to Europe and grew rich. Other countries wanted a cut. In 1498, the Portuguese explorer Vasco da Gama sailed around Africa and reached Calicut, now Kozhikode, in Kerala. Soon Portuguese, Dutch, French and British traders were fighting over the spice coast. The British East India Company began as a spice trader.

Why it tastes hot

Pepper's bite comes from a chemical called piperine. It tickles heat and pain sensors in your mouth, though more gently than the capsaicin in chillies. Chillies came to India only around the 1500s, from the Americas. Before that, pepper and long pepper were what made Indian food hot.

Pepper today

Black pepper is still the world's most traded spice. Today Vietnam grows the most, and Brazil, Indonesia and India are also big producers. India eats so much of its own pepper that it also imports some.

In Kerala and Karnataka, pepper vines still climb trees in coffee and cardamom gardens, often alongside cardamom, the other famous spice of the hills. Farmers pick the green berries by hand from ladders, then spread them out to dry on mats. In a few days they turn black and wrinkled, just as they have for thousands of years.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In the Middle Ages, people used pepper and spices mainly to hide the taste of rotten meat.

Truth: Historians say this is unlikely. Spices were so expensive that people who could afford them could also afford fresh meat. Pepper was prized for its flavour, as medicine and as a sign of wealth.

Try this

Look at a few black peppercorns with a magnifying glass and count the wrinkles. Then crush one between two spoons and smell it. Compare the smell with ready-ground pepper from a packet. Ground pepper loses its smell faster, which is why traders shipped whole peppercorns.
