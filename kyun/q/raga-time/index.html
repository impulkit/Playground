Why are some ragas sung only at certain times?

Bhairav at sunrise. Yaman at dusk. Malhar when the monsoon clouds roll in.

The short answer

In Hindustani music, the classical music of north India, many ragas have a time of day when they are traditionally performed. Raag Bhairav belongs to dawn, Raag Yaman to early evening and Raag Darbari to late night. Some ragas belong to a season instead. The Malhar family is linked with the monsoon.

A raga is more than a scale. It is a set of notes with rules about which to stress, which to skip and how to glide between them. Together these give it a mood. Musicians believe each mood suits a certain time, much as some songs just feel like mornings.

This is an artistic tradition passed down over centuries, not a law of science.

Surprising but true

Many ragas sung at sunrise and at sunset share the same flattened note, a soft Re.

Eight watches of the day

Traditional Indian timekeeping split the day and night into eight prahars, or watches, of about three hours each. Ragas came to be grouped by prahar. Morning ragas tend to sound calm and serious. Evening ragas often feel sweet and open, as lamps are lit. Midnight ragas can be deep and slow.

In the early 20th century, the musicologist Vishnu Narayan Bhatkhande tried to explain the pattern using the notes themselves. He noticed that many ragas sung around sunrise and sunset use a flattened version of the second note, Re. These twilight ragas are called sandhi prakash ragas, ragas of the light where night and day meet. Not every raga fits his scheme, and musicians still argue about the exceptions.

Morning and evening voices

Raag Bhairav, named after a fierce form of Shiva, is one of the best-known morning ragas. Its mood is grave and peaceful, a calm way to open the day. Raag Yaman, an evening raga, is one of the first that many students learn, because its notes are clear and its mood is gentle.

Late at night comes Darbari Kanada, slow and grand. Its name comes from darbar, a royal court, and tradition connects it with Tansen, the great singer at Emperor Akbar's court. The deep, swaying notes are meant for the quiet hours when the city has gone to sleep.

Ragas for rain

Seasonal ragas work a little differently. Raag Malhar and its relatives, such as Miyan ki Malhar, are sung during the rainy months, when the sky is heavy and the air smells of wet earth. Some musicians perform them at any hour during the rains. In spring, ragas such as Basant and Bahar take their turn.

Legends grew around these ragas. Tansen is said to have lit lamps by singing Raag Deepak, and to have brought rain with Malhar. These are stories, retold for centuries, not events anyone recorded. Real monsoon rain comes from winds carrying moisture in from the ocean.

Does anyone still follow the clock?

In concerts, many Hindustani musicians still choose ragas to suit the hour, and some refuse to sing a morning raga at night. Recordings, radio and streaming have loosened the rule, though. Great performers such as Ravi Shankar recorded ragas that listeners could play at any time, anywhere in the world.

Carnatic music, the classical music of south India, links some ragas with times of day but follows them much less strictly. Scientists have begun testing whether ragas really shift people's moods, with mixed results so far. For many listeners, the timing is simply part of the pleasure. The right raga at the right hour can make an ordinary evening feel like an occasion.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Singing Raag Deepak can light lamps, and singing Malhar can make it rain.

Truth: These are old legends, mostly told about the singer Tansen. They are loved stories, not recorded events. Rain comes from monsoon winds carrying moisture from the ocean.

Try this

With an adult's help, find a recording of Raag Bhairav and play a few minutes of it early one morning. That evening, play a recording of Raag Yaman. Write down three words that describe how each one made you feel. Then swap the times the next day and see if your words change.
