How did the telegraph send messages across oceans?

In 1858, Queen Victoria sent a 98-word message to the US president through a cable on the floor of the Atlantic. It took about 16 hours to get through.

The short answer

The electric telegraph sent messages along wires as bursts of electricity. Pressing a key at one end completed a circuit, and a pulse of electric current raced down the wire. At the other end, a machine clicked or made a mark. Short pulses were dots, longer ones were dashes, and patterns of dots and dashes stood for letters. This was Morse code.

To cross oceans, engineers laid insulated cables along the sea floor. The current travelled through a copper core, protected from seawater by layers of gutta-percha, tarred cloth and iron wire. Messages that once took weeks by ship could arrive in minutes.

Surprising but true

The first undersea cables were wrapped in gutta-percha, a rubbery sap from trees in Southeast Asia, because it kept seawater out better than anything else known at the time.

"What hath God wrought"

Samuel Morse was an American painter who became obsessed with sending messages by electricity. After years of experiments and struggling for money, he persuaded the US Congress to pay for a line between Washington, D.C. and Baltimore, about 60 km apart.

On 24 May 1844, Morse tapped out a message from the Capitol building in Washington: "What hath God wrought". His partner Alfred Vail received it in Baltimore and sent it straight back. Within a few years, telegraph wires on wooden poles stretched along railway lines across the United States and Europe.

How does Morse code work?SOS doesn't stand for anything. It was chosen because its dots and dashes are almost impossible to mistake.

A cable across the Atlantic

Laying a wire across the Atlantic Ocean was a huge gamble. The first attempts snapped and sank. In 1858 a cable finally linked Ireland to Newfoundland, now part of Canada. Crowds celebrated in New York with fireworks. Queen Victoria sent a 98-word message to President James Buchanan, and it took about 16 hours to get through.

Then the cable failed after about three weeks. An engineer had pushed too much voltage through it, trying to make the faint signals stronger.

In 1866, the giant steamship Great Eastern, the only vessel big enough to carry the whole cable, laid a stronger one that worked. Within a few decades, cables linked every inhabited continent. News, prices and orders could now cross the planet in a day instead of months.

Wires across India

The telegraph came to India early. In 1851, William O'Shaughnessy, a doctor and scientist working for the East India Company, ran an experimental line from Calcutta to Diamond Harbour. Within a few years, lines connected Calcutta, Bombay, Madras, Agra and Peshawar.

During the revolt of 1857, the British used the telegraph to send warnings and move troops quickly. Some rebels understood this and cut the wires. A cable to Britain followed in 1870, so messages between London and Bombay took hours rather than the weeks a letter needed.

For more than 150 years, Indians sent telegrams for births, weddings, exams and bad news. Each word cost money, so people wrote in a clipped style: "ARRIVING TUESDAY STOP". India's telegram service sent its last messages on 14 July 2013, after about 162 years.

Great-grandparent of the internet

The telegraph was the first technology to separate communication from transport. Before it, a message could only travel as fast as a horse, a train or a ship carrying it. After it, words moved at nearly the speed of electricity.

Today's undersea cables carry internet data as pulses of light through glass fibres, but many follow routes first mapped by telegraph crews. In that sense, every video call across the ocean travels a road the telegraph built.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Samuel Morse invented the telegraph all by himself.

Truth: Many people worked on electric telegraphs. In Britain, William Cooke and Charles Wheatstone had a working system in 1837. Morse built on the work of the scientist Joseph Henry, and his partner Alfred Vail helped develop the famous code.

Try this

Make a "telegraph" with a torch. Stand at opposite ends of a room with a friend. Use short flashes for dots and long flashes for dashes, and send the Morse code for SOS (three short, three long, three short). Then try your own name using a Morse code chart. Notice how slow it is.
