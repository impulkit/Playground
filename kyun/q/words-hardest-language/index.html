Which is the hardest language to learn?

The US government's language school estimates that an English speaker needs about 88 weeks of full-time classes to get good at Japanese, but only about 24 weeks for Spanish.

The short answer

There is no single hardest language. How hard a language is depends on where you start. For an English speaker, Dutch or Spanish is fairly easy, because they share many words and grammar ideas. Japanese, Arabic, Korean and Mandarin Chinese are much harder, because almost everything is different: the sounds, the grammar and the writing.

For a child growing up in Tamil Nadu, Malayalam might feel easy and English harder. For a child in Beijing, Cantonese might be easier than English. Every human baby can learn any language. Difficulty only appears when you learn later, and it comes from distance: how far the new language is from the ones you already know.

Surprising but true

Every healthy baby on Earth can learn any human language, and toddlers pick up Mandarin, Arabic or Tamil as easily as English if they grow up hearing it.

The government's list

The US Foreign Service Institute trains diplomats in more than 60 languages. Over many years, it has estimated how long English-speaking adults take to reach good working ability.

Spanish, French, Italian and Dutch are in the easiest group, about 24 to 30 weeks of full-time classes. German takes about 36 weeks. Hindi, Bengali, Russian and many others take about 44 weeks. The hardest group, about 88 weeks, includes Arabic, Mandarin, Cantonese, Korean and Japanese. That is almost two years of study, all day, every working day.

What makes a language hard

Writing can be a big hurdle. Mandarin uses characters, each standing for a word or part of a word. A reader needs to know a few thousand to read a newspaper. Japanese uses three scripts at once, including two sets of sound symbols and thousands of characters borrowed from Chinese.

Sounds matter too. Mandarin has tones: the syllable "ma" can mean mother, horse or to scold, depending on whether your voice rises, falls or dips. Some languages of southern Africa use click sounds. Arabic has sounds made deep in the throat that English speakers find tricky.

What happens in the brain of someone who speaks two languages?When a bilingual person hears a word, both their languages switch on at once. Their brain then has to quietly choose one and hold the other back.

Grammar puzzles

Some languages change words a lot. In Finnish and Hungarian, a noun can take more than a dozen different forms, depending on its job in a sentence. Russian nouns change endings in six ways. In Hindi, verbs change for whether the speaker is a man or a woman: main jaata hoon and main jaati hoon both mean "I go".

Some languages pack long ideas into a single word. In Turkish or in languages of the Arctic, one word can say what English needs a whole sentence for. And word order can flip: Japanese, Korean, Hindi and Tamil usually put the verb at the end.

Easy for some, hard for others

India shows how much starting point matters. Hindi, Marathi, Gujarati and Bengali share many words and grammar patterns, because they all grew from older Sanskrit-related languages. A Marathi speaker can often understand some Hindi without lessons.

Tamil, Telugu, Kannada and Malayalam belong to a different family, the Dravidian languages. They have their own scripts and grammar. A Hindi speaker learning Tamil faces a bigger jump. Yet millions of Indians grow up speaking three or four languages, switching between them in a single conversation.

The secret of children

Babies are born able to hear the sounds of all human languages. By around their first birthday, they start tuning in to the sounds they hear most. Young children learn grammar by listening and playing, without lessons. Adults are better at study and memory, but young children usually end up with the most natural accent.

So the hardest language might simply be the one you start latest. Scientists who study the bilingual brain say that learning any second language can make it easier to learn a third, and it may keep the brain sharper as you age. And if you find spoken languages hard, spare a thought for people who learn whistled languages, which turn words into tunes that carry across mountain valleys.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Some languages are so hard that only very clever people can learn them.

Truth: Hundreds of millions of ordinary children learn Mandarin, Arabic and Japanese as their first language. No language is too hard for a human brain. Some are just more different from the languages you already know.
