How can you tell if something online is true?

Professional fact-checkers can judge a strange website in about a minute, and the first thing they do is leave it.

The short answer

You cannot always tell if something is true just by looking at it. Fake websites can look professional, and edited photos and videos can seem real. The best method is to step away from the post and check what other trustworthy sources say.

Ask three questions. Who is behind this? What is the evidence? What do other reliable sources say? Professional fact-checkers do this in seconds by opening new tabs and searching. If no trusted news organisation, scientist or official body backs up a dramatic claim, be very careful before you believe or share it.

Surprising but true

A large study of Twitter found that false stories spread further and faster than true ones, largely because people shared them.

Why false stories spread

In 2018, scientists at MIT in the United States studied about 126,000 stories shared on Twitter over more than ten years. False stories reached more people, and faster, than true ones. Robots and fake accounts played a part, but most of the spreading was done by ordinary people.

The researchers thought false news often felt new and surprising, and stirred strong feelings like shock or disgust. That made people want to pass it on. Social media apps tend to show posts that get lots of reactions, so a shocking lie can travel far before anyone checks it.

Read sideways, not down

Researchers at Stanford University compared how students, history professors and professional fact-checkers judged websites. Many students and professors read a page carefully from top to bottom and were often fooled by a polished design or official-sounding name.

The fact-checkers did something different. They opened new tabs and searched for the organisation or author, to see what others said about them. This is called lateral reading, reading sideways. It is the same habit historians use when they compare several sources before deciding what happened.

How does news reach us?In 1850, Paul Julius Reuter used carrier pigeons to fly stock prices between two cities. His news agency, Reuters, still runs today.

Pictures and numbers can lie

A real photo can be used with a false caption. A picture of flooding from years ago, or from another country, may be shared as if it happened yesterday in your city. A reverse image search, where you upload a photo to a search engine, can often show where it first appeared.

New tools can now create fake images, voices and videos, sometimes called deepfakes. Watch for odd details like strange hands, blurry edges or lips that do not quite match the words. Numbers can also mislead, even when they are accurate, if they are chosen carefully. Learn how statistics can trick you.

Forwards and fact-checkers

In India, false rumours spread on WhatsApp have sometimes led to real harm. In 2018, WhatsApp limited Indian users to forwarding a message to only five chats at a time, and later marked messages that had been forwarded many times. The Indian government's Press Information Bureau runs a fact-check unit, and several independent fact-checking websites have grown up in both India and the United States.

Good journalists check facts before printing them, a habit going back to the early days of newspapers. They also correct mistakes in public. A source that admits its errors is usually more trustworthy than one that never does.

A simple pause

Before sharing anything that makes you gasp, pause. Look for the date, the original source and whether a trusted news outlet has reported it. If you are unsure, ask a parent or teacher. Not sharing is always an option, and a surprising number of false stories fade away when nobody passes them on.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The 1938 War of the Worlds radio play made millions of Americans panic, believing Martians had landed.

Truth: Historians have found that few people were fooled, and there was no nationwide panic. Newspapers exaggerated the story, partly to show that radio, their new rival, could not be trusted. The panic story itself became a famous piece of false news.

Try this

Play broken telephone with five or more people. Whisper a detailed sentence, like "Grandma's blue scooter broke down outside the bakery on Tuesday", from one person to the next. Compare the first and last versions. Then try again, but let each person check with the first speaker. Notice how much more accurate it stays.
