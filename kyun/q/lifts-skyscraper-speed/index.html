How do the fastest lifts in skyscrapers work?

A lift in Guangzhou, China, can shoot upwards at about 75 km/h, faster than a car on most city roads.

The short answer

The world's fastest passenger lift is in the CTF Finance Centre in Guangzhou, China. In 2019 Guinness World Records measured it at 21 metres per second, about 75 km/h. It carries people from the 1st floor to the 95th in about 42 seconds.

To go that fast, a lift needs a powerful motor at the top of the shaft, strong steel ropes and a heavy counterweight that balances the cabin. It also needs clever tricks for comfort: rail guides that stop it shaking, a cabin shaped to slip through the air in the shaft, and air pumps that protect your ears.

Surprising but true

The fastest lifts squeeze air into the cabin as they climb and let it out as they drop, so your ears don't pop.

A seesaw in a shaft

Most tall-building lifts work like a giant seesaw. The cabin hangs from ropes looped over a grooved wheel at the top of the building. On the other end of the ropes hangs a counterweight, a stack of steel or iron blocks about as heavy as the cabin when it is roughly half full.

Because the two sides balance, the motor doesn't lift the whole cabin's weight. It only lifts the difference. That is the same idea as a pulley at a well, and it saves a huge amount of electricity.

The man who made lifts safe

Before the 1850s, few people wanted to ride a lift. If the rope broke, you fell. Then Elisha Otis invented a safety brake. At a fair in New York in 1854, he stood on an open platform high above a crowd and had the rope cut. The platform dropped a few centimetres and stopped.

His trick was a spring that snapped teeth into the guide rails the instant the rope went slack. Modern lifts use a smarter version, but the idea is the same. Without it, skyscrapers would never have been built, because nobody would climb 50 floors of stairs.

Could we build a lift to space?Scientists have worked out how a lift to space could stay up. The problem is that no one can yet make a cable strong enough.

Why your ears don't pop

Go up 400 metres in 30 seconds and the air pressure around you drops quickly. Your eardrums bulge, and it can hurt. Super-fast lifts fix this by pumping air into the sealed cabin as it rises, slowing the pressure change. On the way down, they let air out gently.

They also come down more slowly, usually about half their top speed. Our ears cope worse with pressure rising fast than with pressure falling.

Smooth as a glass of water

At 75 km/h, a tiny bump in the rails would feel like a pothole. Fast lifts use rollers pressed against the rails, with sensors and small motors that push back against any wobble. Engineers sometimes test them by standing a coin on its edge on the floor. In a good lift, it stays standing for the whole ride.

The cabins are also shaped with rounded tops and bottoms, a bit like a bullet train, so air can slide past instead of whistling. In a narrow shaft, a fast cabin pushes air ahead of it like a piston in a pump, so the shape matters more than you might think.

The rope problem

Steel ropes are heavy. In a very tall shaft, the ropes can weigh more than the cabin, and beyond about 500 metres they start to struggle to hold themselves up. That is why the Burj Khalifa in Dubai makes people change lifts on the way to the top.

A Finnish company, KONE, has built a rope made of carbon fibre that is far lighter than steel. It could allow single lift rides of up to about 1 km. Push that idea to its absolute limit and you reach one of the wildest plans in engineering: a lift that climbs from the equator into space.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If a lift cable snaps, the cabin will drop to the bottom of the shaft.

Truth: Lifts hang from several steel ropes, each strong enough to hold the cabin alone. If the cabin ever falls too fast, automatic safety brakes clamp onto the rails and stop it.

Try this

Hold a bathroom scale in a building lift (ask an adult first) and stand on it. Watch the number as the lift starts going up, travels and stops. It jumps up when the lift speeds up and drops as it slows, because your body is being pushed and then left floating a little.
