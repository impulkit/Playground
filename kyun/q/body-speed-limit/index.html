How fast can a human run?

For a few strides in Berlin in 2009, Usain Bolt hit about 44 km/h, faster than a city bus is allowed to drive past a school.

The short answer

The fastest anyone has ever been timed running is about 44 km/h. Usain Bolt reached it in the middle of his 100 m world record, 9.58 seconds, at the 2009 World Championships in Berlin. That record still stood in 2026. Most fit adults top out somewhere between 20 and 30 km/h.

Could humans go faster? Probably a little. Lab studies suggest the real limit isn't how strong our legs are but how quickly our muscles can switch on and off. Some scientists think a perfect runner might one day reach 50 km/h or more, while others think we are already close to the ceiling.

Surprising but true

An ordinary pet cat can sprint at around 48 km/h, a little faster than the fastest human ever timed.

What happens in a sprint

A top sprinter isn't really running so much as bouncing. Each foot touches the track for less than a tenth of a second and smashes into it with a force of several times the runner's body weight. The faster you go, the less time your foot spends on the ground, so the trick is pushing very hard, very quickly.

Bolt was unusually tall for a sprinter at 1.95 m. He needed about 41 strides for 100 m, while most rivals needed 44 or 45. Longer legs usually make the start slower, which is why his late burst looked so dramatic.

Where the limit might be

In 2010, researchers at Southern Methodist University in Texas had sprinters run on a special treadmill that measured how hard they hit it. Even at top speed, the runners were nowhere near the most force their legs could produce. The real brake was time. Muscle fibres need a moment to contract, and at 44 km/h there simply isn't long enough. The researchers estimated that, in theory, our legs could carry us to around 60 km/h if muscles could fire faster.

Nobody expects that soon. Records now fall by hundredths of a second. Better shoes, springier tracks and training science help, which is the story of sprinting science.

Humans are built to go far

Over 100 m, a cheetah would humiliate any Olympic champion, at over 90 km/h. Over 30 km on a hot afternoon, the story flips. Cheetahs overheat within a minute or so of chasing. Humans can lose heat through sweat all over the body, and we breathe freely while running.

Some scientists think early humans used this to hunt. They would follow an antelope through midday heat until it collapsed from exhaustion. A few groups, such as hunters in the Kalahari Desert, have been recorded doing this in modern times.

The two-hour wall falls

For decades, running a marathon (42.195 km) in under two hours seemed impossible. Then, at the London Marathon in April 2026, Kenya's Sabastian Sawe won in 1 hour 59 minutes 30 seconds, the first time anyone broke two hours in a proper race. His average speed was just over 21 km/h. That is like running each 100 m in about 17 seconds, then doing it again more than 420 times without stopping.

Indian runners have their own heroes too. Milkha Singh, the "Flying Sikh", finished fourth in the 400 m at the 1960 Rome Olympics, missing a medal by a whisker and inspiring generations.

The fastest feet aren't ours

At 44 km/h, Bolt would lose a race to an ostrich, a racehorse, a greyhound and, embarrassingly, a cat that has spotted a pigeon. But none of those animals could run a marathon the next week. And the creature that outruns them all doesn't use legs. It dives through the air, and you can meet it among the fastest animals on the planet.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Humans are slow and weak runners compared with most animals.

Truth: Over a short sprint, yes. Over long distances in heat, humans are among the best runners on Earth, because we sweat to stay cool and can keep going for hours.

Try this

With a friend and a phone stopwatch, mark out 20 metres in a park or playground. Time each other running it, then work out your speed in metres per second by dividing 20 by your time. Multiply by 3.6 to get km/h, and compare it with Bolt's 44.
