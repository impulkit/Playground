Who was Rabindranath Tagore?

He wrote the national anthems of two countries, won Asia's first Nobel Prize, and only started painting seriously in his sixties.

The short answer

Rabindranath Tagore was a Bengali poet, writer, composer, painter and teacher. He was born in Calcutta, now Kolkata, in 1861, and died there in 1941. People often call him Gurudev, meaning "respected teacher".

In 1913 he became the first person from outside Europe to win the Nobel Prize in Literature, for a book of poems called Gitanjali, "Song Offerings". He wrote the words and music of India's national anthem, Jana Gana Mana, and also of Bangladesh's anthem, Amar Shonar Bangla. He wrote poems, novels, short stories, plays and essays, and he started a school that is still running.

Surprising but true

Tagore wrote more than 2,000 songs, and many people in Bengal still sing them every day. They have a name of their own, Rabindra Sangeet.

A house full of ideas

Tagore was the youngest of thirteen surviving children in a wealthy family at Jorasanko in north Calcutta. The house was always busy with music, plays, magazines and debates. His family took part in the Bengal Renaissance, a time of new thinking about religion, education and society. His father, Debendranath, was a religious reformer. One elder brother, Satyendranath, became the first Indian to join the Indian Civil Service.

He hated school. He found the classrooms dull and the teaching harsh, and he left several schools. He was taught mostly at home, and he wrote his first poems as a child. Later he studied briefly in England but returned without a degree.

How Gitanjali reached the world

Gitanjali was first published in Bengali in 1910. On a trip to England in 1912, Tagore translated some of the poems into English himself. He showed them to the painter William Rothenstein, who passed them to the Irish poet W. B. Yeats. Yeats was so moved that he wrote an introduction for the English edition.

Within a year, the book had won the Nobel Prize. One of its poems, which begins "Where the mind is without fear", is still recited in Indian schools. It describes a free country where knowledge is open to all.

The poet's school

In 1901 Tagore started a small school at Santiniketan, about 150 km north of Calcutta. Many lessons were held outdoors, under trees, with music, art and nature as part of learning. In 1921 it grew into Visva-Bharati University, meant to bring together ideas from India and the rest of the world.

Famous students include the filmmaker Satyajit Ray and Indira Gandhi. In 2023, Santiniketan was added to UNESCO's World Heritage list.

Songs, stories and paintings

Tagore's songs, called Rabindra Sangeet, number more than 2,000. Bengalis sing them at weddings, festivals and in quiet moments. His short story Kabuliwala, about a fruit-seller from Afghanistan and a little Calcutta girl who becomes his friend, has been filmed several times.

He began painting seriously when he was in his sixties. He started by turning crossings-out in his manuscripts into strange creatures and faces. He went on to make more than 2,000 paintings and drawings, and they were shown in Paris, London and New York.

A voice for freedom

Tagore supported India's freedom struggle, though he sometimes disagreed with Gandhi about how to win it. The two men argued in public and respected each other deeply.

The British government knighted him in 1915. In 1919, after soldiers fired on an unarmed crowd at Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar, he gave up the knighthood in protest. He wrote to the Viceroy that he wanted to stand beside his fellow Indians.

He travelled widely and met scientists and writers, including Albert Einstein in 1930. Their recorded conversation about truth and beauty is still read today. Every morning, millions of schoolchildren in India and Bangladesh begin the day singing his words as their anthems.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Jana Gana Mana was written to praise King George V.

Truth: It was first sung at a Congress meeting in Calcutta in 1911, during the king's visit to India, and some newspapers linked the two. Tagore later wrote that the "lord of destiny" in the song was not any king. Most historians accept his explanation.
