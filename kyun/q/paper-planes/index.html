What makes a paper plane fly far?

The world-record paper plane flew almost as far as five cricket pitches laid end to end.

The short answer

A paper plane flies far when its four forces work well together. Your throw gives it thrust. Its wings make lift by pushing air down as they move forward. Drag, the push of the air against it, slows it down. And weight pulls it towards the ground.

After it leaves your hand, a paper plane has no engine. From then on it is a glider, slowly trading height for distance. The best planes are balanced so they fly level, folded neatly so both sides match, and thrown at the right speed and angle. Good folding matters more than strong arms.

Surprising but true

A paper plane flies using the same four forces as a jumbo jet. Your arm is its engine, but only for the first second.

Darts and gliders

Most paper planes fall into two families. Darts are narrow and pointy. They slice through the air with little drag and go fast, so a strong throw sends them a long way, but they don't stay up long.

Gliders have wide wings. They make more lift and fall slowly, so they float gently and stay in the air for a long time. They need a soft, smooth launch. Thrown hard, a glider just loops or crashes.

Real aircraft show the same trade-off. Fighter jets have short, swept-back wings for speed. Gliders and long-distance planes have long, thin wings for lift.

Finding the balance

Every plane has a balance point, called the centre of gravity. If it is too far back, the plane tips its nose up, slows down and stalls. If it is too far forward, the plane dives. Most paper planes fly best with the balance point a little in front of the middle of the wings. A paper clip on the nose is the easiest fix.

The back edges of the wings act like the elevators on a real plane. Bend them up slightly and the nose lifts. Bend them down and it dives. Bend one up and one down, and the plane rolls into a turn.

Neat folding wins

A plane with one wing bigger or more bent than the other will veer off to the side. So the most important rule is symmetry: both halves should match as closely as possible. Sharp creases help too. Run a fingernail along each fold to make it crisp.

Paper matters a little. Ordinary printer paper works well. Thicker paper keeps its shape but is heavier. Very thin paper is light but floppy. Paper itself was invented in China around 2,000 years ago, and folding it into shapes became the art of origami in Japan. You can read how folding ideas are now used to pack giant telescopes into rockets in how origami is used in space.

Record breakers

For years, the distance record belonged to an American plane designed by John Collins. In 2012, it was thrown by a former American football player, Joe Ayoob, and flew 69.14 m. In 2022, a team from South Korea beat that record with a flight of more than 77 m. Later that year, an American, Dillon Ruble, reached about 88 m. Then in December 2025, a team of teenagers from China pushed the record to 98.43 m, almost as long as five cricket pitches.

Staying up longest is a different challenge. In 2010, the Japanese engineer Takuo Toda flew a paper plane for 29.2 seconds, launching it straight up so it could glide down in slow circles. His record lasted more than 15 years. In February 2026, a team of teenagers in China kept a paper plane in the air for 31.2 seconds.

Learning from paper

Before they built their aeroplane, the Wright brothers tested many small wing shapes in a homemade wind tunnel to see which made the most lift. Engineers today still make simple models before they build the real thing. A sheet of paper, a few folds and a careful throw are a quick way to try out ideas in flight.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The harder you throw a paper plane, the farther it goes.

Truth: Many paper planes loop or dive if thrown too hard. Gliders fly best from a smooth, gentle launch. Only planes built for distance, with stiff, narrow wings, are designed for a hard throw, angled upwards.

Try this

Fold one classic dart. Throw it five times and measure each flight in footsteps. Then add a paper clip to the nose and throw it five more times. Next, bend the back edges of the wings up very slightly. Write down which change helped, and remember to change only one thing at a time.
