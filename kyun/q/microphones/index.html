How does a microphone turn your voice into electricity?

The microphone in your phone is about the size of a grain of rice, and it works a lot like your own eardrum.

The short answer

When you talk, your voice pushes the air in quick little waves. A microphone has a very thin sheet inside, called a diaphragm, that wobbles back and forth when those waves hit it. The microphone then turns each wobble into a tiny change in electric current.

A big, fast wobble makes a big change in current, and a small one makes a small change. So the electrical signal has the same shape as the sound. That signal can be made louder, sent down a wire, stored on a phone or beamed across the world, and a speaker at the other end can turn it back into sound.

Surprising but true

A pair of ordinary wired headphones can work as a microphone. Plug them into a microphone socket and speak into them, and a computer will pick up your voice, faintly.

Your ear did it first

Microphones copy something your body already does. Your eardrum is a thin skin that shakes when sound waves arrive. Behind it, tiny bones and hair cells in your ear turn the shaking into nerve signals for your brain. A microphone's diaphragm plays the eardrum's part, and wires do the job of nerves.

The trick is in how the shaking becomes electricity. There are three common ways.

Coils, plates and chips

A dynamic microphone has a small coil of wire glued to the diaphragm, sitting next to a magnet. When the coil moves, the magnet makes a current flow in it. This is the same rule that makes power stations work. Dynamic microphones are tough, which is why singers on stage and reporters in the rain often use them.

A condenser microphone uses a diaphragm that acts as one of two metal plates very close together. As the gap changes, the electric charge between the plates changes too. These microphones need a little power, but they catch quiet sounds and fine detail, so recording studios love them.

A MEMS microphone is a condenser shrunk onto a silicon chip just a few millimetres across. Your phone, earbuds and smart speakers use these. Many phones have two to four of them, and they compare what each one hears to cancel out traffic and wind.

Where a microphone points

Not every microphone listens in every direction. Some pick up sound equally from all around, which suits a meeting table. Many stage microphones mostly hear what is in front of them and ignore sound from behind, so a singer's voice comes through but the band's speakers don't. Engineers call this pattern "cardioid", from the Greek for heart, because a map of where it listens looks heart-shaped.

Film crews use long, thin "shotgun" microphones on poles held just out of the camera's view. These hear a narrow beam of sound, so an actor's whisper can be caught from a few metres away. At football matches, operators point dish-shaped reflectors at the pitch to catch the thud of a kick.

From carbon to cricket stumps

The first useful microphones came with the telephone in the 1870s. Inventors such as Emile Berliner, Thomas Edison and David Edward Hughes found that loose grains of carbon changed how well they carried electricity when sound squeezed them. Carbon microphones were crackly, but they worked well enough to sit in telephones for about a hundred years.

Today microphones turn up in odd places. In international cricket, a microphone hidden in the stumps, called the stump mic, picks up the faint click of a ball brushing a bat. Hospitals use tiny microphones in digital stethoscopes. Voice assistants keep a microphone listening for one wake word before they start recording.

Why headphones can listen

A dynamic microphone and a small speaker are built almost the same way: a coil, a magnet and something that wobbles. Push electricity in, and the speaker moves air. Push air at it, and it makes electricity. That is why old wired headphones can work, weakly, as a microphone. It is also why some walkie-talkies use one part for both jobs.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The loud squeal you sometimes hear at school events means the microphone is broken.

Truth: That howl is called feedback. Sound from the speaker reaches the microphone, gets made louder, comes out of the speaker again and loops round faster and faster. Moving the microphone away from the speaker or turning it down usually stops it.

Try this

Stretch cling film tightly over a bowl and sprinkle a few grains of rice or salt on top. Hold a metal plate near it and tap the plate, or hum loudly close to the bowl. Watch the grains jump as the sound makes the film shake, just like the diaphragm inside a microphone.
