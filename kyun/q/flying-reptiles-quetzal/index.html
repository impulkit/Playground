How big was Quetzalcoatlus?

Quetzalcoatlus had wings about as wide as a small aeroplane's, and on the ground it stood about as tall as a giraffe.

The short answer

Quetzalcoatlus was one of the largest flying animals that ever lived. Its wings stretched about 10–11 metres from tip to tip, about as wide as a small aeroplane like a Cessna, or a little shorter than a school bus is long. When it folded its wings and walked on all fours, its head could be as high as a giraffe's, about 5 metres off the ground.

It was not a dinosaur but a pterosaur, a flying reptile. It lived about 68 to 66 million years ago in what is now Texas, and it died out with the dinosaurs.

Surprising but true

Despite its giant wings, Quetzalcoatlus probably spent much of its time walking, stalking small animals on the ground like an enormous stork.

Found in Texas

In 1971, a geology student named Douglas Lawson was exploring rocks in Big Bend National Park in Texas. He found part of a huge wing bone. It was clearly from a flying reptile, but it was far bigger than any known one. He named it in 1975 after Quetzalcoatl, the feathered serpent god of the Aztecs, who was often shown as a winged snake.

Only parts of the biggest Quetzalcoatlus have been found, mostly wing bones. Smaller relatives from the same rocks, including a second, smaller species named Quetzalcoatlus lawsoni and described in detail in 2021, gave scientists a better picture of its skull, neck and body. Fossils of similar giants called azhdarchids have also turned up in Romania, Jordan and elsewhere.

Light for its size

Flying at that size needs a very light body. Pterosaur bones were hollow, with walls sometimes only a few millimetres thick, and braced inside with thin struts, like the frame of a kite. This made them strong but very light.

Its weight is hard to work out without a full skeleton. Older studies guessed as little as 70 kg. Most researchers now think it was around 200–250 kg, about as heavy as three adult men. Its neck was over 2.5 metres long, and its head and beak alone may have been longer than a grown-up is tall.

Taking off

A heavy flying animal needs a strong launch. Birds usually jump with their legs. But pterosaurs walked on all four limbs, with their wings folded up. In 2008, scientist Michael Habib suggested they used their powerful arms to vault into the air, like a pole vaulter, then flapped hard to climb.

Once airborne, Quetzalcoatlus probably soared on rising warm air, much as vultures and eagles do today, saving energy. Its wings were made of skin stiffened with fibres, stretched from one enormously long finger back to the legs. Muscles and blood vessels inside the wing let it change the wing's shape in flight. Some scientists think it could have covered huge distances in a single flight, though nobody can be sure.

A giant stork, not a fishing bird

Old pictures showed Quetzalcoatlus skimming over the sea, scooping up fish. But its fossils come from inland areas with rivers and plains, far from the coast. Its neck was stiff, and its long, toothless beak was better for picking things up from the ground.

In 2008, palaeontologists Mark Witton and Darren Naish suggested it hunted on foot. It may have strode over the land snapping up small animals, perhaps even baby dinosaurs, rather as storks and ground hornbills do today. This idea is now widely accepted.

The end of the giants

Quetzalcoatlus lived in the last few million years before the asteroid strike 66 million years ago. That disaster wiped out all pterosaurs, along with the non-bird dinosaurs. After that, the skies belonged to birds and, much later, bats. No flying animal since has come close to the size of the giant azhdarchids.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Quetzalcoatlus was a flying dinosaur.

Truth: It was a pterosaur, a flying reptile that lived alongside the dinosaurs but belonged to a separate group. Its wing was a sheet of skin stretched from a hugely long fourth finger to its legs. The only flying dinosaurs were birds.
