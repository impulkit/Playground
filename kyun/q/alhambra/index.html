What is the Alhambra?

The Alhambra in Spain was the palace of the last Muslim kingdom in western Europe, and its tile patterns later inspired the Dutch artist M. C. Escher.

The short answer

The Alhambra is a hilltop palace and fortress in Granada, a city in southern Spain. Its name comes from the Arabic al-hamra, "the red one", after the reddish clay of its outer walls. From outside, it looks like a plain, tough castle.

Inside is a different world of courtyards, pools, fountains and rooms covered in carved plaster and coloured tiles. Most of it was built in the 1300s by the Nasrid sultans, the last Muslim rulers in Spain. Today it is one of the most visited monuments in Europe, and tickets often sell out weeks ahead.

Surprising but true

The walls of the Alhambra are covered in carved Arabic poems. Some of them describe the very room you are standing in.

Muslim Spain

For nearly 800 years, from 711, much of what is now Spain and Portugal was ruled by Muslim kings. They called the land al-Andalus. Its cities were known for their libraries, scholars, poets and gardens. Muslims, Christians and Jews often lived side by side there, though not always peacefully.

Over the centuries, Christian kingdoms in the north pushed south. By the 1200s, only the kingdom of Granada was left. The Nasrid family ruled it for more than 250 years, and they built the Alhambra as their palace, government centre and fortress all at once.

Water, light and patterns

The palaces were built for a hot, dry climate. Thick walls kept rooms cool. Courtyards opened to the sky. Water flowed everywhere, carried from the hills by channels, into pools and fountains. The sound of water was part of the design. Just up the hill, the sultans had a summer garden called the Generalife, where water jets arch over a long, narrow pool lined with flowers.

In the Court of the Lions, twelve stone lions hold up a fountain basin. Four channels run out from it, dividing the courtyard into quarters. This four-part garden plan, which came from Persia, spread across the Islamic world. The Mughals used a version of it centuries later, around the Taj Mahal.

Walls that talk

Look closely at almost any wall in the Alhambra and you'll find writing. Bands of Arabic calligraphy run across the plaster. One phrase, "There is no victor but God", appears thousands of times. There are also long poems, written for the rooms by court poets such as Ibn Zamrak.

Islamic art in religious and royal buildings often avoided pictures of people, so artists turned to geometry. The Alhambra's tiles fit together in patterns with no gaps, called tessellations. Ceilings are covered in muqarnas, thousands of tiny carved pieces that look like honeycomb or icicles.

The end of al-Andalus

In 1492, after a long siege, the last sultan of Granada, Muhammad XII, known in Spain as Boabdil, handed over the city to Queen Isabella and King Ferdinand. A Spanish legend says he looked back at the Alhambra and wept. The spot is still called "the Moor's last sigh".

The same year, the new rulers forced Jews to convert or leave Spain. Muslims were later forced to convert too. Isabella and Ferdinand also agreed to fund a sailor named Christopher Columbus, who set off across the Atlantic that August.

Forgotten and found again

Over the next centuries the Alhambra fell into neglect. In 1829, the American writer Washington Irving stayed in its empty rooms. His book Tales of the Alhambra (1832) made it famous in the US and Europe, and visitors began to arrive.

In 1922, and again in 1936, a young Dutch artist named M. C. Escher sketched the tiles. Their patterns pushed him to create his famous tilings of birds, fish and lizards. A palace built for sultans ended up changing modern art and inspiring maths teachers around the world.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Alhambra was built all at once by one ruler.

Truth: It grew over hundreds of years. There was a fort on the hill by the 800s, the main palaces went up in the 1300s, and Christian rulers added a large Renaissance palace in the 1500s.

Try this

Cut a square from card and snip a shape out of one side. Tape that piece onto the opposite side. Trace around your new shape again and again on paper, fitting each copy against the last. You'll end up with a tiling pattern that leaves no gaps, the same idea used in the Alhambra's tiles.
