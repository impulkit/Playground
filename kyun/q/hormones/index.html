What are hormones?

In January 1922, a very ill 14-year-old boy in Toronto got an injection of a newly discovered hormone. Within weeks, a boy who had been close to death was recovering.

The short answer

Hormones are chemical messengers. They are made in small organs called glands, released into the blood, and carried all around the body. Only cells with the right receptors respond to each hormone, a bit like a key that fits only certain locks.

Nerves send messages fast, in a fraction of a second. Hormones are slower but longer-lasting. They control things that unfold over minutes, hours or years: how fast you grow, when you feel sleepy, how much sugar is in your blood, and the changes of puberty.

Surprising but true

Much of your body's growth hormone is released while you are in deep sleep, especially in the first hours of the night.

The body's postal service

Think of nerves as phone calls and hormones as letters posted to many addresses at once. A single hormone released into the blood can reach every organ within about a minute.

The main glands include the pituitary, a gland the size of a pea at the base of the brain, which tells many other glands what to do. The thyroid in your neck sets how fast your body burns energy. The adrenal glands sit on top of your kidneys. The pancreas, tucked behind your stomach, controls blood sugar. Together, these glands are called the endocrine system.

The hormone that saved lives

Before 1922, children with type 1 diabetes usually lived only a short time after being diagnosed. Their pancreas had stopped making insulin, the hormone that lets cells take in sugar from the blood.

In 1921, at the University of Toronto in Canada, Frederick Banting and his student assistant Charles Best managed to extract insulin from animal pancreases. In January 1922, a 14-year-old boy named Leonard Thompson became the first person to receive an insulin injection for diabetes. After an improved version, he recovered dramatically. Banting and his boss John Macleod won the Nobel Prize in 1923. Today millions of people with diabetes live full lives with insulin.

Fright, sleep and growth

When you get a sudden scare, your adrenal glands release adrenaline. Within seconds your heart beats faster, you breathe quicker and your muscles get ready to act. This is often called the "fight or flight" response, and stress can switch it on too.

As evening falls and light fades, a tiny gland in the brain releases melatonin, which helps make you sleepy. Bright screens late at night can delay it. Then, during deep sleep, the pituitary releases growth hormone in bursts. That is one reason sleep matters so much for growing children.

Iodine and the thyroid

Your thyroid needs iodine, an element found in seafood and in soil, to make its hormones. In places where soil is low in iodine, including large areas along the Himalaya and in parts of the USA's Great Lakes region, many people once developed goitre, a swollen thyroid.

The USA began adding iodine to table salt in 1924. India started a national goitre control programme in 1962 and later moved towards iodising almost all edible salt. It is one of the simplest health measures ever invented, and it works quietly in kitchens across both countries.

Where the word came from

In 1902, two British scientists, William Bayliss and Ernest Starling, discovered that the gut releases a chemical into the blood that tells the pancreas to start working. In 1905, Starling named such chemicals hormones, from a Greek word meaning "to set in motion". Scientists now know of more than 50 different hormones in the human body, and they are still finding new ones.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Hormones only matter when you hit puberty.

Truth: Hormones work in your body from before birth to old age. They control growth, sleep, hunger, blood sugar, body temperature and how you react to a fright, every single day.

Try this

Check the salt packet in your kitchen. Look for the word "iodised" or "iodized" and the amount of iodine listed. Then look up on a map where the thyroid gland sits in the body and find the spot on your own neck, just below the voice box.
