What are sunspots?

Sunspots look like dark holes in the Sun, but if you could cut one out and hang it in the night sky, it would shine brighter than the full Moon.

The short answer

Sunspots are dark patches on the surface of the Sun. They are not holes. They look dark because they are cooler than the rest of the surface: about 3,500 °C, compared with about 5,500 °C around them. They are still extremely hot and bright, just less bright than their surroundings.

They form where the Sun's magnetic field gets tangled into strong knots. Those knots stop hot gas from rising up from inside the Sun, so the area cools a little.

Sunspots come and go. Some last a few days, others for weeks. Their numbers rise and fall in a cycle of about 11 years. Many are larger than the whole Earth.

Surprising but true

In May 2024, a storm from a giant sunspot group lit up auroras that were photographed from Ladakh in India, far south of where auroras are usually seen.

Spots on a perfect Sun

For a long time, many people in Europe believed the heavens were perfect and unchanging. Yet sunspots had been seen long before telescopes. Chinese astronomers recorded them more than 2,000 years ago, when haze or dust dimmed the Sun enough to glimpse large ones.

In the 1610s, several astronomers turned the new telescopes on the Sun. Galileo studied sunspots carefully and argued that they were on the Sun itself and moved because the Sun was rotating. A spotty, spinning Sun did not fit old ideas about perfect heavens, which added to the arguments around his work.

Never try what they did. Looking at the Sun through a telescope or binoculars without a proper solar filter can blind you instantly.

Who was Galileo?In January 1610, an Italian professor pointed a home-made telescope at Jupiter and saw four tiny dots that changed how people saw the universe.

The 11-year cycle

In 1843, a German amateur astronomer named Heinrich Schwabe, who had counted sunspots for 17 years, noticed that their numbers rose and fell over about a decade. Later astronomers measured the cycle at about 11 years.

At solar minimum, the Sun can go weeks without a single spot. At solar maximum, there may be dozens at once. The busy years bring more solar flares and huge clouds of particles that can trigger auroras and disturb satellites.

The current cycle, numbered 25, began in December 2019. In 2024, NASA and NOAA announced that the Sun had reached its maximum period. In May 2024, a giant sunspot group sent out storms that painted auroras across skies far from the poles. Astronomers at the Indian Astronomical Observatory in Hanle, Ladakh, photographed a red glow on the horizon, a very rare sight in India.

The quiet Sun and the cold winters

Between about 1645 and 1715, astronomers saw very few sunspots. This quiet stretch is called the Maunder Minimum, after the astronomers Annie and Walter Maunder, who studied the old records.

It overlapped with part of a chilly period known as the Little Ice Age, when rivers in Europe froze more often. Some scientists think the quiet Sun helped cool Earth. Others think volcanoes and natural changes in the oceans mattered more. Either way, the Little Ice Age was mild compared with the real ice ages, when giant ice sheets covered much of North America and northern Europe.

Watching the spots from India

The Kodaikanal Solar Observatory, high in the Palani Hills of Tamil Nadu, has been photographing the Sun since the early 1900s. Its long record of sunspots is one of the longest in the world and is still used to study how the Sun changes over many cycles.

Today, spacecraft watch the Sun all day. NASA's Solar Dynamics Observatory sends back images every few seconds, and India's Aditya-L1, launched in 2023, watches the Sun without ever losing sight of it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Sunspots are holes or burnt patches on the Sun.

Truth: Sunspots are areas of the surface that are a little cooler than their surroundings, so they glow less brightly. They still shine fiercely, and only look dark by comparison.

Try this

Never look at the Sun directly, even with sunglasses. Instead, look up the daily Sun images from NASA's Solar Dynamics Observatory online for a week. Draw where a sunspot is each day and watch it move across the Sun's face as the Sun turns.
