How are picture books made?

A picture book may have fewer words than this page, yet it can take an author and an artist two years to finish.

The short answer

A picture book is made by a small team. An author writes a short text, an illustrator plans and paints the pictures, an editor helps shape both, and a designer fits words and pictures onto each spread (two facing pages). Then the book is printed, folded, bound and shipped.

The hard part is the planning. Most picture books have just 32 pages, and the words are often under 1,000. So the makers think about every page turn like a film director thinks about every scene. What does the reader see first? What is hidden until the next page? That decision can make a child laugh out loud.

Surprising but true

Most picture books have exactly 32 pages, because that is how many pages fit neatly on the big sheets that printing presses fold and cut.

Words that leave room for pictures

A good picture book text sounds simple, but it leaves gaps on purpose. If the words say "Max was cross", the picture can show what he did about it. Authors cut descriptions that the art can show better, and keep the rhythm and the jokes for reading aloud.

Many picture books are written to be read by an adult to a child. That is why the sound matters so much. Dr Seuss wrote Green Eggs and Ham using only 50 different words after a bet with his publisher, and his books still bounce when you read them aloud.

How did the printing press change the world?Before the printing press, a single book could take a scribe months to copy by hand. Within fifty years of Gutenberg, Europe had printed millions.

From thumbnails to final art

Illustrators start with tiny sketches called thumbnails, sometimes no bigger than a stamp, for all 32 pages at once. This shows how the whole book flows. Then they make a dummy: a rough mock-up of the book with pencil drawings, so the editor can turn the pages and feel the pace.

Only after many changes does the final art begin. Some artists paint with watercolour or cut paper. Eric Carle made The Very Hungry Caterpillar (1969) from tissue paper he had painted himself. Others now draw on a tablet. Indian publishers such as Tulika in Chennai often use folk styles like Gond and Warli painting.

Into the printing press

Picture books are printed with four inks: cyan, magenta, yellow and black. Tiny dots of these four mix in your eye to make every other shade, which is why colour mixing matters to printers. Big sheets carrying many pages at once rush through the press. They are folded, trimmed and stitched or glued into a hard cover.

The first copies are checked carefully, because a skin colour or a sky that comes out slightly wrong can spoil a page. Board books for babies get extra thought. Their pages are thick card with rounded corners, and the inks must be safe, because babies chew them.

From the first draft to the bookshop, a picture book often takes one to two years. A popular one may then be reprinted for decades, read by parents who first heard it as children themselves.

Old pages, new pages

One of the earliest picture books for children was Orbis Pictus ("The World in Pictures"), made by the Czech teacher John Amos Comenius in 1658. It used woodcut pictures to teach Latin words. In 1902 Beatrix Potter's The Tale of Peter Rabbit showed how small, gentle paintings could carry a story.

Since 1938 the American Library Association has given the Caldecott Medal to the best American picture book each year. Maurice Sendak won it for Where the Wild Things Are. In India, Pratham Books puts thousands of free picture books online in many Indian languages, so a child in a village can read a story in her own mother tongue.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Picture books are easy to write because they are short.

Truth: Every word has to earn its place, and the pictures must tell part of the story the words leave out. Authors often rewrite a picture book dozens of times before it is printed.

Try this

Fold four sheets of paper in half and tuck them together to make a 16-page dummy book. Write a very short story, then decide what happens on each page before you draw anything. Notice where you want the reader to turn the page and gasp.
