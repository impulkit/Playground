What are your rights when you buy something?

A shop in India cannot charge you even one rupee above the price printed on a packet, and that rule protects a child buying a chocolate as much as an adult.

The short answer

Every time you buy something, even a pencil, you become a consumer. Laws in most countries give consumers certain rights. The product should be safe. The seller should tell the truth about what it is and what it costs. You should be able to choose between products, and if something goes wrong, you should be heard and get a fair fix.

In India, the Consumer Protection Act of 2019 sets out these rights. In the USA, they are spread across several federal and state laws. In both, shoppers can complain to special bodies without needing an expensive lawyer.

Surprising but true

World Consumer Rights Day falls on 15 March because on that date in 1962, US President John F. Kennedy gave a speech listing the rights every shopper should have.

A speech that started a day

On 15 March 1962, US President John F. Kennedy sent a message to Congress listing four rights for every consumer: the right to safety, the right to be informed, the right to choose and the right to be heard. "Consumers, by definition, include us all," he said.

The date is now marked around the world as World Consumer Rights Day. Over time, campaigners added more rights, such as the right to have a problem fixed and the right to learn about being a smart buyer.

The book that changed cars

In 1965, a young lawyer named Ralph Nader wrote Unsafe at Any Speed, arguing that car makers cared more about looks than safety. The book became a bestseller. Within a year, the US Congress passed a law setting federal safety standards for cars, and features like seat belts became required.

Nader's book showed that one determined consumer could take on huge companies. It also showed that consumer rights are partly about things you never notice, like a toy that doesn't break into sharp pieces. Since 1972, the US Consumer Product Safety Commission has been able to order recalls of dangerous goods, from cots to toys. In a recall, the maker must take the product back, repair it or refund the buyer.

Your rights in India

India's first consumer law came in 1986, and was signed on 24 December, now National Consumer Day. The 2019 law updated it for online shopping and misleading adverts, including those by celebrities.

Some rules you can check yourself. Every packet must show its MRP, the maximum price including taxes. Food packets carry a licence number from the food safety regulator, FSSAI, and a list of ingredients. Many electrical goods need a quality mark from the Bureau of Indian Standards. Reading food labels is one of the easiest ways to use your right to be informed.

If something is faulty, you can first call the National Consumer Helpline on 1915. If that doesn't work, you can file a case at a consumer commission. These exist at district, state and national level, and you can argue your own case.

Shopping in a click

Online shopping has made some old problems harder. You can't hold the product before you buy it, reviews can be fake, and countdown timers can rush you. Indian rules now require online sellers to show the country of origin, a clear return policy and a way to complain.

Many shoppers also get caught by "sale" prices. A tag that says 50% off a price nobody ever paid is not much of a deal, and knowing your percentages helps. A careful consumer reads the label, keeps the bill and asks questions before paying.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A sign saying "Goods once sold will not be taken back" means you can never return a faulty product.

Truth: In India, a shop cannot escape responsibility for faulty goods by putting up a sign. Consumer authorities have called such notices an unfair trade practice, and buyers can still complain about defective items.

Try this

Collect five packets from your kitchen. On each, find the MRP, the date it was made or packed, the best-before date and the maker's address or helpline. Notice which packet makes this information easiest to find, and which hides it in tiny print.
