Why do octopuses squirt ink?

A cloud of dark ink, a quick jet of water, and the octopus is gone.

The short answer

An octopus squirts ink to escape. When a hungry fish, seal or eel attacks, the octopus releases a cloud of dark ink and shoots away using a jet of water. The predator is left facing a murky cloud while the octopus changes colour and hides.

Ink can work in several ways. A big cloud acts like a smokescreen. A smaller, sticky blob can hang in the water in roughly the shape of an octopus, fooling the predator into attacking the wrong target. And some chemicals in the ink may irritate a predator's eyes or dull its sense of smell for a short while. Octopuses are clever escape artists in other ways too, as you can read in how smart octopuses are.

Surprising but true

Octopus ink gets its dark colour from melanin, the same pigment that colours human hair and skin.

What ink is made of

Octopus ink is mostly melanin, a dark pigment, mixed with mucus. Melanin is the same pigment that colours your hair, skin and eyes. The ink is made and stored in an ink sac, a small bag that sits near the octopus's gut. When needed, the octopus squeezes the ink into its funnel, where it mixes with the water being pushed out.

The amount of mucus changes what the ink does. Runny ink spreads out into a wide cloud. Ink with more mucus holds together in a blob. Melanin soaks up light very well, so even a thin cloud looks dark. Scientists call these blobs pseudomorphs, which means "false shapes".

Jet-powered getaway

The ink is only half of the escape. Octopuses, squid and cuttlefish belong to a group of animals called cephalopods, which means "head-foot". They can suck water into their bodies and blast it out through a tube called a siphon. This jet sends them shooting backwards. Jetting is tiring, though. An octopus has three hearts, and the main one pauses while it jets, so octopuses usually crawl and save the jet for emergencies.

At the same moment, many octopuses change colour. Special cells in their skin can turn them pale, dark or patterned in less than a second. Combined with the ink, this camouflage makes it very hard for a predator to follow what just happened.

Ink in the dark deep

Not every cephalopod uses dark ink. Many octopuses that live in the deep sea, such as the dumbo octopuses with ear-like fins, have no ink sac at all. In the darkness far below the surface, a black cloud would be useless.

Some deep-sea animals have a different trick. The vampire squid, which lives in dark, cold water, can release a cloud of sticky, glowing mucus instead. The blue sparks may startle or confuse a predator while the vampire squid slips away. Light made by living things is called bioluminescence. Among its distant relatives is the enormous giant squid, which can also release ink.

Ink for people

People have used cephalopod ink for a long time. Brown sepia ink, made from cuttlefish, was used for writing and drawing in Europe for centuries. "Sepia" is the Greek word for cuttlefish, and today it also names a reddish-brown colour in old photographs.

Squid and cuttlefish ink are also used in cooking, especially in Italy and Spain. They turn pasta and rice glossy black, with a salty taste of the sea, as in Italian black spaghetti (see noodles and pasta). In the wild, though, ink has one job: helping a soft, boneless animal survive long enough to hide again.

Myth or fact?

Myth: An octopus has eight tentacles.

Truth: An octopus has eight arms, with suckers all along them. Squid and cuttlefish have eight arms plus two longer tentacles, which have suckers mostly near the tips and shoot out to grab prey.
