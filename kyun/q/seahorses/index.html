Why do seahorse dads have the babies?

In seahorse families, the father gets pregnant and gives birth to hundreds of tiny babies.

The short answer

In seahorses, the female makes the eggs, but the male carries them. He has a pouch on his belly, a bit like a kangaroo's. During courtship, the female places her eggs into the male's pouch, and he fertilises them there. For the next two to four weeks, he is pregnant.

Inside the pouch, the father keeps the eggs safe, controls the saltiness of the water around them and helps supply oxygen. When the babies are ready, he pumps and twists until they shoot out of the pouch in little clouds. A single birth can release from a few babies to more than a thousand, depending on the kind of seahorse. They are on their own from the moment they are born.

Surprising but true

A seahorse has no stomach in the usual sense. Food passes through it so quickly that it must eat almost all day long.

Why the father?

Nobody knows for certain why male seahorses became the pregnant ones. One popular idea is speed. While the male carries one batch of babies, the female can start making the next batch of eggs. When he gives birth, she may be ready again almost straight away. Working as a team, the pair can produce more young in a season.

Another idea is that carrying the eggs lets the male be sure the babies are his own. Scientists are still testing these ideas, and the answer may involve both. Seahorses are not the only fish with caring dads. In their relatives, the seadragons, the male carries the eggs stuck to the underside of his tail. Among birds, emperor penguin fathers balance an egg on their feet through the Antarctic winter.

A very strange fish

Seahorses are fish, but they break most fish rules. They swim upright. Their heads point forward at an angle, like a horse's. They have no scales, but a coat of bony plates under their skin. Their curly tails can grip seagrass or coral, which very few other fish tails can do.

Their eyes can move separately, so one can watch for food while the other looks out for danger. They feed by sucking tiny shrimps into their snouts with a quick snap, like a vacuum cleaner. With a small back fin as their main engine, they are among the slowest fish of all. The dwarf seahorse may take an hour to travel a metre and a half.

Partners and dances

Many seahorses form pairs that stay together for at least a breeding season. In some kinds, the partners meet every morning for a short greeting dance. They change colour, circle each other and sometimes hold tails. Scientists think this daily dance keeps the pair in step, so they are ready to breed at the same time.

This partnership matters because seahorses live in small home areas and move slowly. Finding a new mate can be hard, so keeping the same one makes sense.

Seahorses in danger

Seahorses live in shallow coastal water around the world, in seagrass beds, coral reefs and the roots of mangroves. In India they are found in places such as the Gulf of Mannar and Palk Bay, the shallow waters between Tamil Nadu and Sri Lanka.

Millions of seahorses are taken from the sea each year, many caught by accident in nets meant for shrimp. Dried seahorses are also used in traditional medicines in parts of Asia, and some are sold as souvenirs or aquarium pets. Since 2004, international trade in all seahorses has been controlled under a global agreement called CITES. India gave seahorses its highest legal protection in 2001.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Seahorses are not real fish.

Truth: Seahorses are bony fish. They breathe with gills, have fins and a swim bladder, and are closely related to pipefish. They just look very different from most fish.

Try this

Test the seahorse's grip. Wrap your little finger around a pencil and try to hold it without using your other fingers. Then try with your whole hand curled. A seahorse's tail curls tightly around seagrass or coral to stop currents carrying it away, since it swims so slowly.
