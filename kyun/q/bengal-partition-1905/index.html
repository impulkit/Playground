What was the partition of Bengal in 1905?

In 1905 the British split Bengal in two. Six years later they joined it back together, and at the same moment moved India's capital from Calcutta to Delhi.

The short answer

In 1905 the British government split the large province of Bengal into two. The eastern part, with a Muslim majority, joined Assam to form a new province with its capital at Dacca (now Dhaka). The western part, with a Hindu majority, stayed with Calcutta, now Kolkata, as its capital, along with Bihar and Orissa.

The British said Bengal was simply too big to govern well. Many Indians believed the real aim was to weaken Bengal, the heart of the growing freedom movement, by splitting Hindus and Muslims. The partition sparked huge protests and the Swadeshi movement. In 1911 the British gave in and reunited Bengal.

Surprising but true

The song that is now Bangladesh's national anthem, "Amar Sonar Bangla", was written by Rabindranath Tagore during the protests against this partition.

A giant province

Bengal under British rule was enormous. It stretched over today's West Bengal, Bangladesh, Bihar, Jharkhand and Odisha, with about 7.8 crore people, more than most countries in Europe. Running it from Calcutta was hard, and the east, around Dacca, was often neglected.

Lord Curzon, the Viceroy, decided to divide it. Officials pointed to genuine problems with administration. But their private notes also show political aims. In 1904 a senior official, Herbert Risley, wrote that Bengal united was a power, and Bengal divided would pull in different ways.

Critics replied that Bengal could have been made smaller without splitting the Bengali-speaking people in two. One option was to separate off the regions where other languages, such as Hindi and Odia, were spoken. In the end, that is roughly what happened in 1911.

Protests and rakhis

The partition came into force on 16 October 1905. In Calcutta, many people fasted, closed shops and walked to the river to bathe. Rabindranath Tagore led processions where Hindus and Muslims tied rakhis on each other's wrists, to show that Bengal could not be split.

Crowds sang "Vande Mataram". Students joined in large numbers. People boycotted British goods and burned foreign cloth. The protest spread to other parts of India, led by figures such as Bal Gangadhar Tilak and Lala Lajpat Rai.

The protests also produced songs that are still sung today. Tagore wrote "Amar Sonar Bangla", meaning "My golden Bengal", in 1905. Painters, poets and playwrights joined in, and shops selling only Indian-made goods opened in Calcutta's markets.

Different views in the east

Not everyone opposed the partition. Many Muslim leaders in eastern Bengal welcomed it. The new province gave Dacca new offices, schools and jobs, and Muslims there, many of them poor farmers, had a larger voice.

In 1906 the All-India Muslim League was founded at a meeting in Dacca. Some historians see the partition and the arguments over it as an early step in the split between Hindu and Muslim politics that shaped the subcontinent later. Others warn against reading the future back into 1905.

Undone, then done again

In December 1911, King George V visited India for a ceremony called the Delhi Durbar. The Durbar was a vast gathering on open ground in north Delhi, with tens of thousands of people. Princes came in jewels, and the king and queen sat on thrones under a golden dome. There he announced that Bengal would be reunited. Bihar and Orissa became a separate province, and Assam went back to being separate too.

He also announced that the capital of British India would move from Calcutta to Delhi. That ended Calcutta's long role as capital. Many Muslims in the east felt let down by the reversal.

Bengal was divided again in 1947, during the Partition of India, when East Bengal became part of Pakistan. In 1971, after a war, it became the independent country of Bangladesh, whose anthem is a Tagore song written in 1905.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The 1905 partition created the border between India and Bangladesh.

Truth: The 1905 partition was undone in 1911. Bengal was divided again in 1947, along a different line, when East Bengal became part of Pakistan. It became Bangladesh in 1971.
