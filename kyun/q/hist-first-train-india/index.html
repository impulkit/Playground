What was India's first train journey like?

At 3:35 in the afternoon of 16 April 1853, a 21-gun salute boomed in Bombay, and India's first passenger train puffed off towards Thane.

The short answer

India's first passenger train ran on 16 April 1853, from Bori Bunder in Bombay to Thane, about 34 km away. It had 14 carriages and around 400 invited guests. Three steam engines pulled it, and the journey took about 1 hour and 15 minutes.

The day was declared a public holiday. Crowds lined the tracks and climbed onto rooftops to watch. A band played and guns fired a salute as the train left. For many onlookers, it was the first time they had seen any machine move by itself.

Surprising but true

The three engines that pulled the first train had names, Sahib, Sindh and Sultan.

A day like a festival

The train set off at 3:35 pm. Its guests included British officials, wealthy merchants and some of Bombay's leading citizens. Along the way, people gathered at every village to stare at the hissing, smoking engines.

Some onlookers had heard frightening rumours about the "fire carriage". Others simply came to see something new. The engines, built in England and shipped to Bombay, were named Sahib, Sindh and Sultan. The train stopped at Sion to take on water and reached Thane just before 5 pm. Fares for ordinary passengers were soon set in three classes. Ordinary travellers soon packed the cheapest carriages, and the line quickly proved that Indians were keen to ride trains.

How did the steam engine change the world?The first useful steam engines were built to pump water out of flooded coal mines. Within a century they were pulling trains across India.

Why Bombay to Thane?

The Great Indian Peninsula Railway company had bigger plans. It wanted to cross the steep Western Ghats and link Bombay to the cotton fields of the Deccan. Bombay to Thane was a short, flat first stretch to test the idea.

Building on from Thane was hard. The line had to climb the Bhor and Thal Ghats. Workers cut tunnels and built zigzag tracks called reversing stations. Thousands of labourers worked with hand tools, and many died of disease and accidents along the way.

When the Bhor Ghat section opened in 1863, trains could finally climb from the coast to the Deccan plateau, near Pune. Extra engines were added at the back to push heavy trains up the steep slope, a practice that continued on this route for well over a century. Rail lines soon linked Bombay with Calcutta, and in 1870 a traveller could cross India by train from coast to coast.

Not the first train of all

Trains had run in India before 1853, just not for passengers. In 1837, a small railway near Madras carried granite for building roads. In 1851, a steam engine called Thomason hauled earth and building material at Roorkee, where engineers were building the Ganga Canal.

So the Bombay to Thane train was India's first passenger train, not its first train. The first passenger train in eastern India ran from Howrah to Hooghly in August 1854.

A network for a nation

Lines spread fast across British India. They carried cotton to the ports, soldiers to the borders, and pilgrims to holy places. Indians began travelling far from home as never before, and the railway helped people from different regions meet, trade and organise, including during the freedom movement.

Today, Indian Railways runs about 69,000 km of route (2024), enough to wrap around the Earth more than one and a half times. It carries close to 2 crore passengers a day and employs over 12 lakh people, one of the biggest workforces of any organisation on Earth.

From steam to speed

The station where the first train began, Bori Bunder, was rebuilt as a huge Gothic building, now Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Terminus, a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Mumbai's local trains still run along the original route to Thane every few minutes. The old steam engines are long gone, and trains like the Vande Bharat can now reach 160 km/h on some lines, more than five times faster than Sahib, Sindh and Sultan.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The British built India's railways as a gift for the people of India.

Truth: The railways were built mainly to carry cotton and other goods to ports, and to move troops. Indians paid for much of it through taxes and guarantees to British investors. Over time, though, Indians made the trains their own.
