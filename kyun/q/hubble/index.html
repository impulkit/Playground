What has the Hubble telescope shown us?

When Hubble's first pictures came back in 1990, they were blurry. A tiny mistake in its mirror, thinner than a hair, had nearly ruined it.

The short answer

The Hubble Space Telescope has helped show us how big, old and busy the universe is. It has photographed galaxies so far away that their light left them more than 13 billion years ago. It helped measure the age of the universe at about 13.8 billion years. And it helped show that the universe is growing faster and faster.

Hubble was launched in 1990 by NASA, working with the European Space Agency. It is still working more than 35 years later, which makes it one of the most successful science instruments ever built.

Surprising but true

Hubble is only about 500 km above Earth, roughly the distance from Delhi to Lucknow, yet it photographs galaxies billions of light-years away.

A blurry start

Soon after launch, scientists realised something was badly wrong. Hubble's pictures were fuzzy. Its main mirror had been ground to slightly the wrong shape. The error was about 2 micrometres at the edges, around one-fiftieth the width of a human hair, but it was enough to spoil the focus.

The fix was a bit like giving Hubble glasses. In December 1993, astronauts on the Space Shuttle Endeavour caught the telescope, installed a new camera and a set of small corrective mirrors, and released it again. The first sharp pictures were a huge relief. Astronauts returned four more times, the last in 2009, to fix parts and add better instruments.

The deep field

In December 1995, astronomers pointed Hubble at a tiny, empty-looking patch of sky, about as big as a tennis ball seen from 100 m away. They collected light for ten days.

The result, the Hubble Deep Field, showed about 3,000 galaxies in that speck alone. Each contains billions of stars. Later deep fields found even more. Since the sky is full of patches like that, astronomers now estimate there are hundreds of billions of galaxies in the part of the universe we can see.

Big discoveries

Hubble helped show that almost every big galaxy has a giant black hole at its centre, millions or billions of times heavier than the Sun.

In 1998, two teams studying exploding stars in distant galaxies found that the universe's growth is speeding up. Hubble's measurements were part of the evidence. The leaders of those teams won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2011, and the mysterious push behind it is called dark energy.

In July 1994, Hubble watched pieces of a broken comet named Shoemaker-Levy 9 slam into Jupiter, leaving dark scars bigger than Earth in the planet's clouds.

How the pictures are made

Hubble doesn't take colour photos the way a phone does. Its cameras record light through one coloured filter at a time, as black-and-white images. Scientists then combine them, giving each a colour. Sometimes they include light our eyes can't see, such as ultraviolet, and show it in a visible colour so we can study it.

The colours are chosen carefully to show real differences, such as which gases are glowing. The famous "Pillars of Creation", towers of gas and dust where new stars are forming, were made this way in 1995.

Hubble and Webb

Hubble's orbit is slowly sinking as thin traces of air drag on it. NASA expects it to keep working into the 2030s. The James Webb Space Telescope, launched in 2021, has a bigger mirror and sees mainly infrared light. The two often look at the same objects and compare notes. Webb sees farther back in time. Hubble sees more of the visible and ultraviolet colours that Webb can't.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Hubble is far out in space, close to the stars and galaxies it photographs.

Truth: Hubble circles Earth only about 500 km up, a distance a car could drive in a day. It is so useful because it sits above the air, not because it is any nearer to the stars.

Try this

Put a coin at the bottom of a clear glass bowl of water and look at it from above. Ask someone to stir the water gently. The coin wobbles and blurs, the way moving air blurs starlight for telescopes on the ground.
