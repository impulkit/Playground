Could there be life on Jupiter's moons?

Under the cracked ice of Europa lies a dark, salty ocean that may hold more water than all of Earth's seas.

The short answer

Nobody knows yet. No life has ever been found on Jupiter's moons. But two of them, Europa and Ganymede, almost certainly have oceans of liquid water hidden beneath thick shells of ice. Liquid water is the first thing life as we know it needs.

Europa is the favourite. Its ocean may be 60 to 150 km deep and hold about twice as much water as all of Earth's oceans put together. It probably touches a rocky sea floor, where water and rock can react and make the kinds of chemicals that living things use for energy. Two spacecraft are now on their way to take a much closer look.

Surprising but true

In 2003, NASA deliberately crashed its Galileo spacecraft into Jupiter so that it could never hit Europa and leave Earth germs there.

Four dots beside a planet

In January 1610, Galileo turned his new telescope towards Jupiter. Next to it he saw three, then four, small points of light. Night after night they shifted from one side of the planet to the other. He realised they were moons circling Jupiter.

That was a shock. Many people at the time believed everything in the sky circled Earth. Here was proof that some things circled something else. Those four moons, Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto, are still called the Galilean moons. Jupiter is now known to have more than 90 moons, but most are only a few kilometres across.

Four very different worlds

Io is the most volcanic place in the solar system, with hundreds of active volcanoes. Europa is about the size of our Moon and covered in ice scored with long reddish-brown cracks. Ganymede is the largest moon in the solar system, wider than the planet Mercury, and the only moon known to make its own magnetic field. Callisto is the most heavily cratered and may also have a deep ocean.

What heats them is Jupiter itself. Its powerful gravity squeezes these moons as they orbit, a bit like a hand squeezing a rubber ball. The flexing makes heat. On Io that heat melts rock. On Europa it seems to be just enough to keep water liquid under the ice, the same way ice floats as a lid on a frozen pond.

Life in the dark

For a long time scientists thought all life depended on sunlight. Then, in 1977, explorers in a small submarine found animals crowded around hot springs on the floor of the Pacific Ocean, far below where any light reaches.

Those creatures live on energy from chemicals in the water. If vents like these exist on Europa's sea floor, its ocean could have food and warmth without a single ray of sunshine. That doesn't mean anything lives there. It means the idea is worth testing.

Spacecraft on the way

Europe's JUICE (Jupiter Icy Moons Explorer) launched in April 2023 and is due to reach Jupiter in 2031. It will study Europa, Callisto and especially Ganymede, which it will eventually orbit.

NASA's Europa Clipper launched in October 2024 and should arrive in 2030. It will fly past Europa about 50 times, measuring how thick the ice is and tasting any gases or dust thrown off the surface. It cannot dig into the ocean or find living things directly. What it can do is tell us whether Europa has everything life would need.

The Galileo spacecraft, which studied Jupiter from 1995 to 2003, helped prove Europa's ocean was there. When its fuel ran low, controllers steered it into Jupiter's clouds so that it could never crash on Europa and bring Earth microbes along. Whatever lives on Europa, if anything does, scientists want to find it untouched.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Life needs sunlight, so nothing could live in a dark ocean under ice.

Truth: On Earth, whole communities of tubeworms, crabs and microbes live around hot vents on the deep sea floor, where sunlight never reaches. They get their energy from chemicals, not light.

Try this

Ask an adult if you can put a small plastic tub of water in the freezer. Check it after an hour or two. You should find a lid of ice on top with liquid water still underneath, a tiny version of Europa.
