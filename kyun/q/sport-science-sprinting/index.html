How do sprinters run so fast?

At top speed, Usain Bolt's foot touched the ground for less than a tenth of a second per step, and pushed down with several times his own weight.

The short answer

Sprinters run fast because they push down on the ground very hard and very quickly. Each time a foot lands, it presses with a force of several times the runner's body weight, in less than a tenth of a second. That push launches them forward into the next stride.

Strong, springy muscles, mostly the "fast-twitch" kind, make this possible. So do long legs, a perfect start, a relaxed upper body and years of training. The very best put it all together in under 10 seconds.

Surprising but true

Fast sprinters don't move their legs much faster than slower runners. The real difference is how hard they hit the ground.

The fastest 9.58 seconds

On 16 August 2009, in Berlin, Usain Bolt of Jamaica ran 100 metres in 9.58 seconds. In 2026 it was still the world record. Between 60 and 80 metres, he was travelling at about 44 km/h, faster than a car in a city school zone.

Bolt is 1.95 metres tall, unusually tall for a sprinter. His long legs meant he took about 41 steps to finish, while most rivals needed 44 or 45. Tall runners usually struggle at the start, but Bolt's power let him catch up.

The women's world record is even older. Florence Griffith-Joyner of the USA ran 10.49 seconds in 1988.

Hit the ground hard

For years, coaches thought the fastest runners simply moved their legs faster. In 2000, scientists in the USA tested runners on special treadmills that measured their footsteps.

They found that fast and slow runners swing their legs forward at almost the same speed. The difference was in the push. Faster runners hit the ground harder, so each step carried them further. At top speed, an elite sprinter's foot is on the ground for under a tenth of a second, and pushes with around four to five times their body weight.

Fast-twitch muscle

Your muscles contain different kinds of fibres. Slow-twitch fibres are good for long, steady work, like marathon running. Fast-twitch fibres contract quickly and powerfully but tire fast.

Elite sprinters usually have a high share of fast-twitch fibres, partly from their genes and partly from training. Sprint training builds these muscles with explosive exercises, like jumps, short sprints and heavy lifts.

The start

A 100 m race can be won or lost at the gun. Sprinters crouch in starting blocks, ready to push off. When the gun fires, the sound reaches their ears and their brain sends signals to the muscles.

That takes time. The rules assume no human can react in less than 0.1 seconds, so anyone who leaves the blocks faster is called for a false start, and is usually out of the race. Good sprinters react in about 0.13 to 0.17 seconds, a gap that comes down to the science of reaction time.

Shoes, wind and track

Sprint spikes are light shoes with metal pins under the front of the foot. They grip the rubbery track so no push is wasted.

Wind matters too. A tailwind helps, so records only count if the wind behind the runners is no more than 2 metres per second. Modern tracks are springy, giving back some energy with each step.

India's sprinters

India's fastest sprinters are closing the gap. In May 2026, Gurindervir Singh ran 100 metres in 10.09 seconds in Ranchi, a new national record, beating a mark set by Animesh Kujur the day before.

Before them came legends like Milkha Singh, the "Flying Sikh", who finished fourth in the 400 m at the 1960 Olympics. And yet even Bolt would lose a race to a cheetah, which can reach around 100 km/h in short bursts and is now running again in India.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Sprinters are fastest right at the finish line.

Truth: Most top sprinters hit top speed around 60 to 80 metres into a 100 m race, then slow down slightly. The winner is often the one who slows down least.

Try this

Mark out 20 metres in a park or playground. Run it once taking short, quick steps and once taking long, powerful strides, while a friend times you and counts your steps. Which was faster, and how many steps did each take?
