What was the Emergency in India?

For 21 months from June 1975, India's elections were put off, newspapers were censored and many opposition leaders were jailed without trial.

The short answer

The Emergency was a period of 21 months, from 25 June 1975 to 21 March 1977, when the government of Prime Minister Indira Gandhi used special powers in the Constitution. President Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed declared a national emergency on her advice, citing a threat of "internal disturbance".

During this time, many fundamental rights were suspended. Newspapers had to show stories to censors before printing. More than 100,000 people, including opposition leaders and activists, were detained without trial, and elections due in 1976 were postponed. In 1977 the government called elections, lost heavily, and the Emergency ended.

Surprising but true

When elections were finally held in 1977, Prime Minister Indira Gandhi lost her own seat, and India got its first government not led by the Congress party.

How it started

In the early 1970s, India faced high prices, shortages and strikes. In Gujarat and Bihar, students led big protests. The veteran leader Jayaprakash Narayan, known as JP, called for "total revolution" against corruption and the government.

Then, on 12 June 1975, the Allahabad High Court found that Indira Gandhi had broken election rules in her 1971 campaign. It cancelled her election to Parliament. She appealed to the Supreme Court. Opposition parties demanded she resign, and JP called for a nationwide protest campaign, urging police and soldiers not to obey orders they believed were illegal. On the night of 25 June, the Emergency was declared.

What changed overnight

Before dawn, police arrested opposition leaders across the country, including JP, Morarji Desai, Atal Bihari Vajpayee and L.K. Advani. Many were held under a law called MISA, which allowed detention without trial.

Electricity to some newspaper offices in Delhi was cut that night, so they could not print. Censorship rules followed. Some editors protested in quiet ways. One newspaper printed a blank space where its editorial should have been. Foreign reporters who would not accept the rules were sent out of the country.

Courts and the Constitution

In 1976, the Supreme Court heard a case about whether detainees could challenge their arrest in court, a right known as habeas corpus. Four of the five judges ruled that they could not while the Emergency lasted. Justice H.R. Khanna wrote a lone disagreement, arguing that the right to life could not simply be switched off. He was later passed over for Chief Justice.

Parliament also passed the 42nd Amendment, one of the biggest changes ever made to the Constitution. It reduced the powers of courts, among other things, and added the words "socialist" and "secular" to the Preamble.

Hardships for ordinary people

The Emergency also brought campaigns that hurt many poor families. In some areas, officials, under pressure to meet targets, forced men to undergo sterilisation operations as part of a population control drive. Slums were demolished in Delhi, most notoriously near Turkman Gate. These drives were closely linked to the Prime Minister's son, Sanjay Gandhi.

Supporters of the Emergency pointed to other changes, such as fewer strikes and trains running more on time. The later Shah Commission, set up to investigate, found many cases of abuse of power.

The voters decide

In January 1977, Indira Gandhi announced elections and released many prisoners. Opposition parties joined together as the Janata Party. In March, voters threw out the government. Indira Gandhi and Sanjay Gandhi both lost their seats, and Morarji Desai became Prime Minister.

The new government passed the 44th Amendment in 1978. It replaced "internal disturbance" with "armed rebellion", and said the cabinet must advise the President in writing before an emergency could be declared. Indira Gandhi herself returned to power in the 1980 election. For many Indians, the lesson of the Emergency was that the vote could still change a government.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Emergency was a military takeover.

Truth: The army played no political role. The Emergency was declared by the President on the advice of the elected government, using powers in the Constitution. That is one reason the rules were later changed to make such powers harder to use.
