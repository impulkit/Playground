What happens to food when you cook it?

A hot pan is a chemistry lab. Heat breaks some molecules apart, glues others together and turns pale dough into golden, crackly toast.

The short answer

Cooking is heat doing chemistry. When food gets hot, its molecules jiggle faster and faster. Some fall apart, some link up with neighbours, and water inside the food moves around or escapes as steam.

Three big changes happen in most kitchens. Proteins in eggs, meat and paneer unfold and set firm. Starch in rice, wheat and potatoes soaks up water and turns soft. And sugars and proteins on hot surfaces react to make brown crusts packed with new flavours. Heat also kills most germs, which is one reason cooked food is safer.

Surprising but true

The brown crust on toast, roasted peanuts and grilled paneer comes from the same chemical reaction, first described by a French chemist in 1912.

Proteins that unfold

A protein is a long chain folded into a tight ball, a bit like a tangled rope. Heat shakes the rope loose. Once it unfolds, it grabs other loose chains and forms a mesh.

That is why a runny egg sets, why milk forms a skin, and why paneer firms up in a hot pan. It is also why overcooked chicken gets dry. Squeeze a protein net too hard with heat and it pushes out the water it was holding, like wringing a wet towel. The egg white sets first, at around 62 °C, while the yolk needs a little more heat.

Starch that swells

A grain of rice is packed with starch, stored in tiny hard granules. In hot water the granules soak up liquid and swell, sometimes to many times their size. Some burst and leak starch into the water, which is why pasta water turns cloudy and why kheer thickens as it simmers.

The same thing happens in roti dough on a tawa and in a potato in a pressure cooker. Soft, swollen starch is also much easier for the body to break down. Your stomach and gut get more energy from cooked starch than from raw grains.

The browning reaction

In 1912 a French chemist named Louis-Camille Maillard noticed that sugars and proteins react when heated together. The reaction now carries his name. It needs dry heat well above boiling point, roughly 140 °C, so it happens in roasting, frying and toasting, but hardly at all in boiling.

The Maillard reaction makes hundreds of new flavour molecules at once. It gives toast its smell, roasted coffee its depth, and the outside of a seekh kebab its crust. Plain sugar heated even hotter goes through a separate change called caramelisation, which turns it into amber caramel.

Why boiling has a speed limit

Water cannot get hotter than its boiling point while it stays liquid. At sea level that is 100 °C. High in the mountains the air pressure is lower, so water boils at a lower temperature. In Leh, about 3,500 m up, it boils below 90 °C, and dal takes far longer to cook.

A pressure cooker fixes this by trapping steam. The extra pressure pushes the boiling point up to about 120 °C, and food cooks much faster.

Did cooking shape us?

Some scientists think cooking changed human evolution. The biologist Richard Wrangham argues that cooked food gave early humans so much easy energy that their guts could shrink and their brains could grow. Other researchers say the evidence for early fire is too patchy to be sure. Either way, people have been cooking for hundreds of thousands of years, and the same chemistry still happens every time someone flips a dosa.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Cooking destroys all the goodness in vegetables.

Truth: Long boiling does wash out some vitamins, such as vitamin C. But cooking also makes other nutrients easier to absorb, like the orange colour in carrots and the red colour in tomatoes, so a mix of raw and cooked food works well.

Try this

Put a few grains of uncooked rice on a plate next to a spoon of cooked rice from a meal. Press each kind between two fingers and look at them with a magnifying glass. Write down how heat and water changed the hard grains.
