What were the Opium Wars?

Britain wanted Chinese tea, China wanted only silver, and a drug grown in India filled the gap. The result was two wars that China still remembers.

The short answer

In the early 1800s, people in Britain drank huge amounts of tea, and almost all of it came from China. China sold tea, silk and porcelain, but wanted little from Britain except silver. Britain did not like watching its silver drain away.

So British traders found something China's people would buy: opium, a drug made from poppies, which is very addictive. When China's government tried to stamp out the trade, Britain went to war, in 1839 and again in 1856. China lost both times, gave up Hong Kong, and was forced to open its ports.

Surprising but true

Much of the opium sold in China was grown in eastern India, around Patna and Ghazipur, and shipped from Calcutta under the East India Company.

The tea, silver and opium triangle

The opium came mostly from British-ruled India. The East India Company controlled poppy farming in Bengal and Bihar, and farmers often had little choice about growing it. The drug was processed in government factories at Patna and Ghazipur, then auctioned in Calcutta.

Private traders shipped it to the Chinese coast and smuggled it ashore. They were paid in silver, and that silver bought tea for London. By the 1830s, millions of people in China used opium, and the silver was now flowing out of China instead of in. In India, opium became one of the biggest sources of money for the colonial government.

Fast ships and big fortunes

To win the race to China, traders built sleek sailing ships called opium clippers, which could beat the monsoon winds that slowed ordinary ships. Huge fortunes were made. British firms such as Jardine Matheson grew rich, and so did merchants in Bombay, who shipped opium grown in central India alongside cotton. Some of the money built Bombay's early hospitals, schools and mansions.

The writer Amitav Ghosh set a trilogy of novels in this world, starting with Sea of Poppies (2008). It follows poppy farmers from Bihar, sailors and traders whose lives the drug pulled together.

Lin Zexu takes a stand

The Chinese emperor had banned opium, but smuggling went on. In 1839 he sent a tough official, Lin Zexu, to the southern port of Canton (now Guangzhou). Lin blocked foreign merchants inside their warehouses until they handed over their opium: more than 20,000 chests of it.

He destroyed it all at Humen. He even wrote a letter to Queen Victoria asking how Britain would feel if another country sold poison to its people. There is no sign she ever read it.

Gunboats and treaties

Britain sent warships. Its steam-powered gunboats and modern cannons easily beat China's older navy, and soldiers from India fought in the British forces. In 1842 China signed the Treaty of Nanjing. It handed Hong Kong Island to Britain, opened five ports to British trade, and paid a large sum of money.

The Second Opium War, from 1856 to 1860, was worse. British and French troops marched on Beijing and burned the Old Summer Palace, a vast park of halls and gardens built by emperors over 150 years. The new treaties made the opium trade legal.

A long memory

In China, these wars mark the start of what is often called the "century of humiliation", when foreign powers carved out pieces of the country. The anger they left behind helped fuel the revolutions that later remade modern China.

Hong Kong grew into a huge port city under British rule. In 1997 it was handed back to China. Meanwhile, the government factory at Ghazipur in Uttar Pradesh still processes opium today, but now it is used to make strong painkilling medicines.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Opium Wars were mainly about China refusing to modernise.

Truth: The fighting started because China tried to stop British merchants from smuggling a harmful drug into the country. Questions about trade and diplomacy were part of it, but opium was at the centre.
