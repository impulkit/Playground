Do families look the same everywhere?

Among the Khasi people of Meghalaya, children take their mother's family name, and the youngest daughter traditionally looks after the family home.

The short answer

Families come in many shapes. Some are a parent and a child. Some are two parents and their children. Some are large joint families with grandparents, uncles, aunts and cousins sharing one home. Some children are raised by grandparents, by one parent, by adoptive parents or by step-parents.

What families do is similar almost everywhere. They raise and protect children, share food and money, care for the sick and the old, and pass on language, stories, recipes and traditions. How they are arranged depends on history, culture, money, housing and personal choice.

Surprising but true

In countries like Sweden, around four in ten households are a single person living alone, while many Indian homes hold three generations under one roof.

Joint and nuclear

In a nuclear family, parents live with their children. In a joint family, common in India, several generations and the families of brothers often live together, sharing a kitchen and household costs. A grandmother might cook, a grandfather walk the children to school, and cousins grow up almost like brothers and sisters.

Indian households are getting smaller as people move to cities for work and live in apartments. Still, the average Indian household has between four and five people, compared with about two and a half in the United States. In Sweden and some other European countries, around four in ten households are just one person living alone.

Families traced through mothers

In most cultures, children take their father's family name. But some societies trace family through the mother, called matrilineal descent. The Khasi and Garo peoples of Meghalaya in northeast India are well-known examples. Among the Khasi, children belong to their mother's clan, and the youngest daughter traditionally inherits the family home and cares for her parents.

In Kerala, many Nair families once lived in large matrilineal households called tharavads. That system faded in the 20th century, after new laws changed how property was inherited.

Why do names have meanings?Almost every name was once an ordinary word. Aditya means sun, Smith meant a metal worker, and Johnson meant "son of John".

Grandparents back home

In the United States, families living with several generations under one roof are becoming more common again. In 2021, about 18% of Americans lived in such homes, up from about 7% in 1971. High housing costs, care for older relatives and immigrant traditions all play a part. Many Indian-American families, for example, share their homes with grandparents.

Fewer children

Around the world, families are having fewer children. In 1950, women had about five children on average. Today the worldwide average is about 2.3. In India, it has fallen to about two, roughly the level at which a population stops growing in the long run. Better health care, more girls in school and the move to cities are major reasons. This changes the shape of the world's population.

Family, chosen and given

Family words show how families work. Hindi has separate words for your father's brother (chacha) and your mother's brother (mama), and for their wives. English uses "uncle" and "aunt" for all of them. Languages with many relative words often come from cultures where the wider family plays a large part in daily life.

Some people also talk about "chosen family", close friends who feel like relatives. In many Indian neighbourhoods, children call older neighbours aunty and uncle, and family-like ties stretch beyond blood.

Families also differ in how they celebrate, from multi-day weddings to quiet dinners. Whatever shape a family takes, research suggests what matters most to children is feeling loved, safe and listened to. Your own genes come from your birth parents, but your family is the people who raise you.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The "normal" family has always been a mother, a father and two children living on their own.

Truth: Small nuclear families became common in many countries only in the last century or so. For most of history, and in much of the world today, families often included grandparents, aunts, uncles and cousins living close together or under one roof.
