What is a tidal bore?

Twice a day on some rivers, a wall of seawater roars upstream against the current, and surfers ride it for kilometres.

The short answer

A tidal bore is a wave that travels up a river, against the flow, when the tide comes in. Most river mouths just fill up gently as the sea rises. But in a few places, the incoming tide is so strong, and the river mouth so shallow and narrow, that the seawater piles up into a wall and charges upstream.

From the bank, it looks as if the river suddenly reverses. A rumble comes first, then a wave, from a small ripple to several metres high, rolls past. Behind it, the river is higher, choppy and flowing the wrong way.

Surprising but true

People in China have gathered to watch the Qiantang River's tidal bore, called the Silver Dragon, for about 2,000 years.

Why only some rivers?

A bore needs two things. First, a big tide. Tides are caused by the pull of the Moon and Sun, and in some places the difference between high and low tide is many metres.

Second, it needs a river mouth shaped like a funnel: wide and shallow at the sea, narrowing quickly inland. As the tide rushes in, the funnel squeezes it, and the water can't spread out fast enough. It rears up into a step-shaped wave. Bores are biggest around full moon and new moon, when the Sun and Moon pull together, giving the strongest "spring" tides. Only a small number of rivers in the world have strong bores.

The Silver Dragon

The most famous bore is on the Qiantang River, near Hangzhou in China. At its biggest, it can be several metres high and roar upstream at more than 20 km/h. People call it the Silver Dragon.

Chinese people have watched it for about 2,000 years. Poets wrote about it more than a thousand years ago, and crowds still gather each autumn around the 18th day of the eighth lunar month, when it is said to be strongest. It can be dangerous: waves sometimes splash over the walls and soak spectators, so viewing areas are kept well back.

How do surfers ride waves?At Nazaré in Portugal, a surfer rode a wave about 26 metres high, taller than an eight-storey building, which was still the Guinness world record in 2025.

Surfing up a river

Bores are a surfer's dream: a wave that keeps going for kilometres. The pororoca on the Amazon, in Brazil, can travel far inland. Its name comes from a local language word for "great roar". Surfers hold competitions on it.

In England, surfers ride the Severn bore, which can reach about 2 m. Some rides have lasted more than half an hour. In Alaska, the bore in Turnagain Arm, near Anchorage, also draws surfers wearing thick wetsuits against the cold. It's one of the few places where surfing means riding a river.

India's bores

India has bores too. On the Hooghly River near Kolkata, the bore is called the "baan". In the days of sailing ships, it could tip over small boats, and sailors timed their trips carefully. Today, the baan is smaller, partly because the river has changed.

The Gulf of Khambhat in Gujarat has some of the biggest tides in India, with a range of up to about 10 m. Bores travel up rivers that flow into it, such as the Narmada and Mahi.

Life in the rush

Bores churn up mud and stir food from the riverbed. Birds gather to feed on fish and shrimps tumbling in the foam. Fish that live in estuaries have adapted to the sudden change from fresh to salty water.

Across the Atlantic, the Bay of Fundy in Canada has the world's highest tides, up to about 16 m between high and low water. Small bores run up rivers there too. The rhythm of all these bores, like every tide, is set by the Moon, whose pull has shaped coasts for billions of years and may even have helped life on Earth begin.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A tidal bore is a kind of tsunami.

Truth: Tsunamis are caused by earthquakes or landslides and can strike any coast. Tidal bores are caused by the regular, predictable tides of the Moon and Sun, and only happen in certain rivers, so people can know the times in advance.

Try this

Pour water into a long baking tray so it's about 1 cm deep. Tilt the tray gently so water flows to one end, then quickly lift that end. Watch a small wave travel back up the tray against the flow. That's a mini bore.
