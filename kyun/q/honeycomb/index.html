Why are honeycomb cells hexagons?

Bees don't know geometry. But they build the best shape anyway.

The short answer

Only three regular shapes tile a floor with no gaps: triangles, squares and hexagons. Circles leave spaces between them, and so do pentagons.

Of those three, hexagons enclose the most space for the length of their walls. Wax is precious. Bees eat a lot of honey to make it, several times more honey than the wax they produce. So the shape that holds the most honey for the least wall saves the colony a great deal of work.

Scientists think bees start with round-ish cells that get pulled into hexagons as the warm wax settles. Others think the bees shape the corners themselves.

Surprising but true

Mathematicians suspected hexagons were best for about 2,000 years, but only proved it in 1999.

Wax from a bee's body

Worker bees, usually a couple of weeks old, have glands on the underside of the abdomen. These squeeze out tiny clear flakes of wax. The bees chew the flakes to soften them, then press them into walls about as thin as a sheet of paper.

Each cell is also tilted slightly upward, towards the back of the comb. That small slope helps stop runny honey from dripping out before the bees cap the cell with more wax. The same comb serves as nursery, larder and dance floor, where bees pass on directions to flowers in their waggle dance.

A 2,000-year-old guess

Around 1,700 years ago, the Greek mathematician Pappus of Alexandria wrote that bees had a kind of "geometrical forethought". He argued that hexagons hold more than squares or triangles of the same wall length. Writers before him had made the same guess.

Proving it was much harder. Mathematicians had to show that no shape at all, even one with curved or wiggly sides, could beat the hexagon. In 1999, the American mathematician Thomas Hales finally did it. His proof covered every possible way of dividing a flat surface into equal areas. The bees had been right all along.

Do bees build hexagons on purpose?

Here scientists disagree. In 2013, a team of engineers reported that bees first build round cells. Bees warm the wax with their bodies, and the soft walls flow and straighten where neighbouring cells meet, a bit like soap bubbles pushing against each other.

Other researchers watched bees closely and argued that the insects do much of the shaping themselves, measuring walls with their heads and legs. Both sides agree on one thing: the final shape is very close to a perfect hexagon.

Squares, triangles and hexagons compared

You can test the idea with a simple sum. Take a shape with an area of 1 square centimetre. As a square, it needs walls 4 centimetres long all the way round. As a triangle, it needs about 4.6 centimetres. As a hexagon, it needs only about 3.7 centimetres.

That sounds like a small difference. But a single comb can hold thousands of cells, and a strong colony may build many combs. Across the whole hive, choosing hexagons saves a lot of wax, and a lot of the honey that bees would otherwise have to eat to make it.

Hexagons far from the hive

In India, giant rock bees build single combs that can be more than a metre wide, hanging from cliffs, water tanks and even tall buildings. Up close, every cell is still a hexagon.

Engineers copy the shape too. The James Webb Space Telescope's main mirror is made of 18 gold-coated hexagons that fit together into one big mirror, as described in the Webb telescope. Hexagon floor tiles, football patterns and tessellations drawn by artists all borrow the same trick. A shape that wastes nothing turns out to be useful everywhere.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Bees collect wax from flowers, the way they collect nectar and pollen.

Truth: Bees make wax inside their own bodies. Young worker bees have special glands on the underside of the abdomen that release thin flakes of wax, which the bees chew and shape.

Try this

Put a little washing-up liquid in a shallow tray of water. With a straw, gently blow lots of small bubbles of about the same size so they crowd together. Look at the walls where three bubbles meet. Many of the bubbles squeeze into six-sided shapes, just like honeycomb cells.
