How do people get their names around the world?

In Iceland, most people's last name is just their father's or mother's first name with "son" or "dóttir" added, so the phone book is sorted by first names.

The short answer

Most people around the world get a personal name chosen by their family, and many also carry a family name, or surname, passed down from parents. But the rules for how names are built, and in what order they go, are very different from place to place.

In the USA and much of Europe, the personal name usually comes first and the family name last. In China, Japan, Korea and Hungary it's the other way round. Many Spanish speakers carry two surnames. Icelanders name children after a parent, and many people in Myanmar or Indonesia have no family name at all. In India, all of these patterns, and a few more, can be found within one country.

Surprising but true

In Ghana, many Akan children get a "day name" from the day of the week they were born. Kofi, as in Kofi Annan, means a boy born on a Friday.

Family names first

In China, the family name comes before the personal name. A tiny number of family names are shared by huge numbers of people. Wang and Li are each carried by around 100 million people. In South Korea, about one person in five is a Kim.

In Japan, Korea and Hungary too, the family name comes first. When people from these countries move abroad, some switch the order to fit in, and others keep it. Since 2020 the Japanese government has asked for Japanese names in English to be written family name first.

Sons, daughters and two surnames

In Iceland, a country of about 4 lakh people, a man named Jón might have a son, Ólafur Jónsson, and a daughter, Sigrid Jónsdóttir. This kind of name, based on a parent's first name, is called a patronymic when it comes from the father. Some Icelanders use their mother's name instead. Because surnames change every generation, the phone book is ordered by first name.

In Spain and much of Latin America, a child usually takes two surnames: the father's first surname followed by the mother's first surname. So a child of María García López and Juan Pérez Ruiz might be called Pérez García. The Spanish language carried this custom across the Americas.

Why do names have meanings?Almost every name was once an ordinary word. Aditya means sun, Smith meant a metal worker, and Johnson meant "son of John".

Names in India

India has many naming traditions side by side. In parts of north and west India, surnames often come from a family's old trade, village or community. Patel once meant a village headman, and Gandhi, in Gujarati, a seller of groceries and perfumes.

In Tamil Nadu, many people have no family surname. They write a parent's name as an initial before their own. In Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh, initials can stand for a home village and a father's name. The writer R.K. Narayan's initials stood for Rasipuram, a town, and Krishnaswami, his father's name. Since 1699, when Guru Gobind Singh, the tenth Sikh Guru, founded the Khalsa, many Sikh men have used the name Singh and many Sikh women Kaur. It was a way of setting aside caste names and treating everyone as equals.

Days, ceremonies and new names

Among the Akan people of Ghana, many children get a name for the day they were born. A boy born on Friday may be Kofi. A girl born on Saturday may be Ama. Many Hindu families hold a naming ceremony, the namkaran, within a few weeks of birth, and some choose a first letter based on the stars at the baby's birth.

Names also change during life. Some people take a new name at marriage, others when they join a religious order. Some simply pick a name they like better. In Iceland, a naming committee must approve any new first name that hasn't been used before. Naming customs can be a puzzle for the people who design forms and run each country's census. A box marked "surname" doesn't work for someone who has never had one.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Everyone in the world has a first name and a family surname.

Truth: Many people don't. Lots of people in Myanmar and Indonesia have no family name, many in Tamil Nadu use a parent's name as an initial instead, and Icelanders usually take a parent's first name. Forms with "first name" and "last name" boxes can puzzle people from all of these places.
