How do traffic lights know when to change?

Many traffic lights can feel your car waiting. A loop of wire buried under the road notices the metal sitting on top of it.

The short answer

Simple traffic lights run on a timer. A controller box beside the road switches them in a fixed pattern: green for a set time, then amber, then red, and round again.

Many lights are smarter. They use sensors to find out where cars are waiting. The most common sensor is a loop of wire buried in the road just before the stop line. Electricity flowing through it makes a magnetic field. When a car stops on top, its metal changes the field, and the controller knows someone is there. Other lights use cameras or radar mounted on poles. Pedestrians can push a button to ask for their turn.

The controller is a small computer. It follows a set of rules, an algorithm, to decide which road gets green next and for how long.

Surprising but true

The world's first traffic signal, outside the Houses of Parliament in London in 1868, was lit by gas. A few weeks later it exploded and injured the policeman working it.

A gas lamp in London

The first traffic signal was put up in December 1868 outside the Houses of Parliament in London. It was designed by a railway engineer, John Peake Knight, and copied railway signals. By day, a policeman raised arms on a pole. At night, he turned a lamp lit by gas to show red or green.

A few weeks later, a gas leak made the lamp explode, injuring the policeman. The idea was dropped for decades. Traffic lights returned in the USA once electric lamps were common. In 1914, the city of Cleveland, Ohio, put up electric red and green lights, and in 1920 a Detroit policeman named William Potts added a third colour.

Garrett Morgan's signal

In 1923, the African American inventor Garrett Morgan patented a new kind of signal. Its extra position stopped traffic in every direction for a moment before letting the other road go. He is said to have been moved by seeing a crash at a busy junction. The pause it gave drivers is similar to the amber light we use today.

Morgan was also known for inventing an early smoke hood, a kind of breathing mask, which he used himself to rescue workers trapped in a tunnel under Lake Erie in 1916.

Red on top

Red is almost always at the top of a vertical traffic light, or on the left or right in a horizontal one, depending on the country. This helps people who are colour-blind, who may find red and green hard to tell apart. They can read the signal by which lamp is lit.

Red probably means stop because railways used it for danger first, and red is the colour many cultures already linked with warning. In India, many signals add a countdown timer showing how many seconds are left. Drivers can switch off their engines at long reds, saving fuel and cutting fumes.

Lights that talk to each other

On a long main road, engineers can time a chain of lights so a driver going at the right speed meets green after green. This is called a green wave. Some cities, including Los Angeles, link thousands of signals to a control centre. Cameras watch the traffic, and computers adjust the timings minute by minute.

Ambulances and fire engines in some cities can turn lights green ahead of them. In the future, cars may talk directly to traffic lights by radio, and self-driving cars will need to read signals as reliably as any human driver.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pressing the crossing button again and again makes the light change faster.

Truth: One press is enough to tell the controller someone wants to cross. Extra presses usually do nothing. In some busy cities, many buttons are switched off at certain times of day, because the lights run on a fixed timer anyway.

Try this

Play traffic lights with three friends at a pretend crossroads. One person is the controller with a red, yellow and green card for each road. First, change the lights every 20 seconds, no matter what. Then try changing them only when a "car" is waiting. Which way keeps your cars moving better?
