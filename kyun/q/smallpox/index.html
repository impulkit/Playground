How did the world get rid of smallpox?

Smallpox killed hundreds of millions of people over the centuries. Then, in 1980, it became the first human disease ever wiped out.

The short answer

Smallpox was a disease caused by a virus. It spread from person to person, caused a high fever and covered the body in painful blisters. About three in ten people who caught the most common form died, and many survivors were left with scars or were blinded.

It was beaten by a vaccine and by a huge worldwide effort. Health workers in dozens of countries tracked down every case, vaccinated everyone around it, and kept going until there were no cases left. In 1980, the World Health Organization announced that smallpox was gone.

Surprising but true

The last person to catch smallpox naturally was Ali Maow Maalin, a hospital cook in Somalia, in 1977. He recovered and later worked to help end polio.

An ancient trick

Smallpox is very old. Scars on the mummy of the Egyptian pharaoh Ramesses V may have been caused by it. In India, many communities worshipped Shitala Mata, a goddess linked to smallpox, and prayed to her for protection.

People also noticed that anyone who survived smallpox never caught it again. So in parts of India, China, Africa and the Ottoman Empire, healers scratched material from a smallpox sore into the skin of healthy people. This was called variolation. It usually caused a milder illness, but not always.

In 1721, during an epidemic in Boston, an enslaved African man named Onesimus told the minister Cotton Mather how this was done in Africa. Mather pushed for it, and it saved many lives.

Jenner and the milkmaids

In England, people said that milkmaids who caught cowpox, a mild illness from cows, didn't get smallpox. In 1796, a doctor called Edward Jenner tested this. He put cowpox into a cut on the arm of an eight-year-old boy, James Phipps. Later he exposed James to smallpox, and the boy did not fall ill.

Jenner's method was much safer than variolation. He called it vaccination, from vacca, the Latin word for cow. Many people mocked him at first, and one famous cartoon showed vaccinated people sprouting little cows from their bodies. Within a few years it had spread across Europe, to America and to India, where the first vaccination was given in Bombay in 1802.

The final push

By the 1960s, smallpox had disappeared from Europe and North America but was still common in South Asia, Africa and South America. In 1967, the WHO began an intensified campaign.

Vaccinating everyone was impossible, so teams used a clever plan. Whenever a case was found, they vaccinated everyone around that person, building a ring of protected people the virus couldn't cross. A simple forked needle made vaccinating quick and cheap.

India's toughest year

In 1974, India faced a terrible smallpox outbreak, especially in Bihar. Thousands of health workers searched house to house, and rewards were offered for reporting cases. India's last case came in May 1975. Later that year, a little girl named Rahima Banu, on an island in what is now Bangladesh, became the last person to have the deadlier form of smallpox. She survived.

After the last case

The final natural case, of a milder form, was in Somalia in 1977. In 1978, a woman in Birmingham, England, died after catching the virus from a laboratory. Since then, no one has had smallpox.

Samples of the virus are still kept locked away in two laboratories, one in the USA and one in Russia. Scientists still argue about whether they should be destroyed. Meanwhile, the success inspired the fight against another disease, polio, and showed what vaccines can do when the whole world works together.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Edward Jenner invented the idea of protecting people against smallpox.

Truth: Long before Jenner, people in India, China, Africa and Turkey protected themselves by deliberately giving healthy people a mild dose from a smallpox sore. Jenner's safer cowpox method built on this older idea.
