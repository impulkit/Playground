How far did Ibn Battuta travel?

A young man left Morocco in 1325 to go on pilgrimage to Mecca. He came home 24 years later, having worked as a judge in Delhi and visited China.

The short answer

Ibn Battuta was a Moroccan scholar who travelled further than almost anyone before modern times. Over about 29 years, he covered roughly 1,20,000 km, by many estimates, three times around the Earth's equator.

He left Tangier in 1325 to make the hajj, the pilgrimage to Mecca. Then he kept going. He crossed North Africa, Arabia, Persia and Central Asia, worked in Delhi, lived in the Maldives, visited Sri Lanka and reached China. Later he travelled to Spain and across the Sahara to the empire of Mali. Back home, he told his story to a scholar, and the result was a book called the Rihla, which is still read today.

Surprising but true

Ibn Battuta worked for about seven years as a judge in Delhi for Sultan Muhammad bin Tughlaq, one of the most unpredictable rulers in Indian history.

A pilgrim who kept going

Ibn Battuta was 21 when he set out, travelling alone at first. He later wrote that leaving his parents was as painful for him as it was for them. He joined caravans for safety, and in Egypt a holy man told him he would visit faraway lands.

After reaching Mecca, he decided to see as much of the Muslim world as he could, and to never take the same road twice if he could help it. He trained as a judge in Islamic law, and that skill let him find work and respect wherever there were Muslim rulers.

Seven years in Delhi

In 1334, he arrived in Delhi, then ruled by Sultan Muhammad bin Tughlaq of the Delhi Sultanate. The sultan liked to reward foreign scholars, and appointed Ibn Battuta a judge with a large salary.

Ibn Battuta described the sultan as both generous and frighteningly cruel. He fell from favour once, and feared for his life. Then, in 1341, the sultan sent him to China as an ambassador, with gifts and a large group of attendants. He also wrote about Indian things that amazed him, such as coconuts, betel leaves and the speed of the royal postal runners.

Shipwreck and islands

The journey to China went wrong from the start. In Calicut, in Kerala, the ships were wrecked in a storm, and Ibn Battuta lost almost everything. Afraid to go back to Delhi, he sailed to the Maldives.

There he became a judge again and married. He found the islands beautiful, and noted that their money was cowrie shells. After less than a year, he left for Sri Lanka, where he climbed Adam's Peak, a mountain holy to Buddhists, Hindus, Muslims and Christians.

To China and home

Eventually he reached China by sea. He admired its silk, its porcelain and its paper money, and wrote that a traveller could journey safely there even with lots of goods. Historians are unsure how far inside China he really went.

Heading home, he passed through Damascus in 1348 during the Black Death, a plague that killed huge numbers of people. He reached Morocco in 1349 to find that his mother had died of it months earlier.

One last journey

Ibn Battuta still wasn't finished. He visited Muslim Spain, then crossed the Sahara with a camel caravan in 1352 to the empire of Mali, where he saw Timbuktu.

The Sultan of Morocco then asked him to share his adventures. A scholar named Ibn Juzayy wrote them down in 1355. Ibn Battuta died around 1368 or 1369. Today a large shopping mall in Dubai is named after him, with sections designed in the styles of the lands he visited.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Medieval Muslims rarely travelled beyond their homelands.

Truth: Pilgrims, scholars and traders travelled widely across Africa, Asia and Europe. Ibn Battuta found Muslim communities, mosques and fellow travellers almost everywhere he went.
