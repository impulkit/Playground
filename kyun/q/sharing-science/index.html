Why do humans share?

Toddlers just a year and a half old will stop playing to help a stranger pick up something he dropped, with no reward and no one telling them to.

The short answer

Humans share because, for most of our history, sharing kept us alive. Our ancestors lived in small groups of hunters and gatherers. A hunter might come home empty-handed for days, then catch something far too big to eat alone. Sharing the meat meant everyone ate on good days and bad. Groups that shared survived better than groups that didn't.

Over time, our brains and our cultures came to reward sharing. We feel good when we give, we notice who is generous, and we get angry when someone grabs more than their share. Some of this seems to be with us from a very young age. Parents, friends, religions and traditions build on it.

Surprising but true

Vampire bats share too. A bat that fed well at night will cough up some blood for a roost-mate that went hungry, and the favour is often returned later.

Little helpers

In a well-known experiment in Germany, a researcher "accidentally" dropped a clothes peg in front of toddlers about 18 months old, and reached for it as if he couldn't get it. Most toddlers crawled over and handed it back. They did it without praise or reward. When the researcher threw the peg down on purpose, they didn't bother. Young chimpanzees in the same study helped too, though less often, which suggests the urge to help is old in our family tree.

Children get more choosy as they grow. In a 2008 study in Switzerland, 3- and 4-year-olds mostly kept treats for themselves. By 7 or 8, many more preferred to split things evenly, even giving up a sweet to make it fair.

Insurance on legs

Among the Hadza people of Tanzania, who still hunt and gather, a big animal like an antelope is shared through the whole camp. The hunter doesn't get to keep most of it. Anthropologists see this as a kind of insurance. Today I share with you; next week, when I fail, you share with me.

It also builds reputation. People who share are trusted, chosen as partners and helped when they need it. People who never share find others less willing to help them. In many hunter-gatherer groups, boasting about a big catch is frowned upon, which keeps successful hunters humble.

The fairness alarm

Scientists test sharing with simple money games. In the ultimatum game, one person splits a sum of money with a stranger. If the stranger rejects the offer, neither gets anything. Pure logic says to accept any offer, since a little is better than nothing. Yet around the world, people often reject offers that feel insulting, giving up money to punish greed. Researchers in game theory have spent decades working out why.

Sharing is also tied to empathy, our ability to feel a bit of what someone else feels. Brain scans suggest that giving to others can switch on some of the same reward areas as getting a treat yourself.

Sharing in our cultures

Almost every culture has traditions built around sharing food. At the Golden Temple in Amritsar, the langar, a free community kitchen, serves meals to tens of thousands of people every day, whatever their religion, caste or wealth. Volunteers chop, cook and wash up side by side.

In the United States, families gather to share a big meal at Thanksgiving, and many towns run food banks. Across India, people share sweets at Diwali and Eid, offer prasad at temples and cook for neighbours at weddings.

Sharing beyond humans

Other animals share too, though usually less widely. Chimpanzees sometimes share meat with friends and allies. Vampire bats, which die if they go a couple of nights without blood, regurgitate meals for hungry roost-mates. Humans are unusual for sharing so much with people they barely know, which is one of the things that let us build towns, cities and nations.

Myth or fact?

Myth: People are naturally selfish, and children only share because adults force them to.

Truth: Experiments show very young children help and share without being asked or rewarded. They do become more generous and more focused on fairness as they grow, and family and culture shape that. Selfishness is part of human nature, but so is sharing.

Try this

Try a fair-share test with a younger brother, sister or cousin. Give them eight sweets or crayons and ask them to share with you and another person. Do they split them evenly, keep more, or give more away? Try it again with an odd number, like seven, and see what they do with the leftover one.
