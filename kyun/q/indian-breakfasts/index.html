What do people eat for breakfast across India?

In Indore, people eat poha sprinkled with sev and a side of hot jalebi, all before school or work.

The short answer

Breakfast changes almost every few hundred kilometres in India. In the south, people eat steamed idlis, crispy dosas and savoury upma with coconut chutney and sambar. In Maharashtra and Madhya Pradesh, poha, made from flattened rice, is a favourite. In Punjab, a stuffed paratha with butter and curd is a classic.

These breakfasts grew from what each region grows. Rice-growing areas tend to make rice-based breakfasts, wheat-growing areas make breads, and many dry regions once relied on millets. Almost everywhere, the morning starts with a cup of chai or filter coffee.

Surprising but true

Idli batter is left overnight so microbes can ferment it, and that's what makes it puffy and slightly sour.

The south: steamed, fermented and fried

Idli and dosa are made from a batter of rice and urad dal that is left overnight to ferment. Tiny yeasts and bacteria, the friendly microbes also found in curd, eat some of the starch and give off gas. That's why idlis are spongy and dosas slightly sour.

Are all germs bad?Right now, about as many bacteria live in and on you as there are cells in your body, and most of them are on your side.

In Kerala, breakfast might be puttu, steamed cylinders of rice flour and coconut, eaten with a black chickpea curry, or appam, lacy rice pancakes with soft centres. Tamil Nadu has ven pongal, rice and moong dal cooked with pepper, cumin and ghee. In Karnataka, many people eat akki rotti, a flatbread made from rice flour. In much of South India, a light snack meal like this is often called "tiffin".

The west: poha, thepla and misal

Poha is flattened rice, made by soaking and pressing rice grains. Rinsed and cooked with onions, mustard seeds, turmeric, peanuts and curry leaves, it takes about ten minutes to make. In Indore, it's served with sev, a crunchy gram flour topping, and often with jalebi.

In Maharashtra, misal pav pairs a spicy sprouted-bean curry with soft bread rolls. Gujaratis eat thepla, a thin flatbread with fenugreek leaves, which keeps for days and is a favourite for train journeys. On weekends, many families buy fafda, crunchy gram flour strips, from the local shop.

The north and east: breads and fried treats

In Punjab, a breakfast paratha might be stuffed with potato, cauliflower, radish or paneer, then cooked on a griddle with ghee and served with white butter and curd. In Delhi, chole bhature, spicy chickpeas with puffy fried bread, is a weekend treat.

Bengalis love luchi, small fried breads made from refined flour, with alur dom, a potato curry. In Bihar, sattu, flour made from roasted gram, is mixed with water as a filling drink on hot mornings. In Rajasthan, people may start the day with a spicy kachori stuffed with lentils or onion.

Breakfast in a bowl

In the USA, many people eat breakfast cereal with milk. That habit began in the late 1800s, when cereals like cornflakes were sold as healthy foods. Today packaged cereals and oats are also common in Indian cities, especially for busy families.

Many people in both countries eat a mix: cornflakes on school days, aloo paratha or dosa on Sunday. Pancakes in America and dosas in India are distant cousins, both thin batters cooked on a hot flat pan.

Morning drinks

Most of India starts the day with chai, black tea boiled with milk, sugar and often ginger or cardamom. In Tamil Nadu and Karnataka, filter coffee is more common, poured from a height between a steel tumbler and a bowl called a dabara to cool and froth it.

In Kashmir, some people drink noon chai, a pink, salty tea. In Ladakh, butter tea, made with salt and butter, gives warmth and energy in the thin, cold air.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Scientists have proved breakfast is the single most important meal for everyone.

Truth: The famous slogan was spread partly by cereal advertisements. For children, a good breakfast helps with energy and focus at school, but studies on adults give mixed results about whether it matters more than other meals.

Try this

With an adult, make a simple poha. Rinse flattened rice in a sieve, let it soften, and mix it with a little cooked onion, turmeric, salt and lemon juice. Compare it with a breakfast from another part of India that someone in your class eats.
