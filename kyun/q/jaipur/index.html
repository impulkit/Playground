Why is Jaipur called the Pink City?

In 1876, a maharaja painted his whole old city the colour of terracotta to welcome a royal guest. It has stayed pink ever since.

The short answer

Jaipur is the capital of Rajasthan. Its old walled city is painted a warm pinkish terracotta. In 1876, Maharaja Ram Singh II had the buildings painted to welcome the Prince of Wales from Britain, and pink was seen as a colour of hospitality. A writer later called Jaipur the Pink City, and the name stuck.

Shopfronts in the old city are still painted the same shade, so the bazaars look much as they did 150 years ago. Today over 30 lakh people live in Jaipur.

Surprising but true

Jaipur's giant stone sundial, the Samrat Yantra, can tell the local time to within about two seconds.

A city drawn on paper

Jaipur was one of the first planned cities in India. In 1727, Maharaja Sawai Jai Singh II decided to move his capital down from the hilltop fort of Amber. With the architect Vidyadhar Bhattacharya, he laid the city out in a grid of large blocks, with wide straight streets meeting at right angles.

The plan followed ideas from old Indian texts on building, called Vastu Shastra and Shilpa Shastra. Each block had its own purpose, from markets to palaces. Even now you can walk straight down the bazaars and see the symmetry of the arches and shopfronts on both sides.

The palace of winds

The Hawa Mahal, built in 1799, is Jaipur's most famous sight. Its pink sandstone face rises five storeys and is covered in small windows called jharokhas, often counted at 953. Breezes blowing through them kept the rooms cool, which is how it got its name: Palace of Winds.

Royal women followed purdah, a custom of staying out of public view. The windows let them watch festivals and processions in the street below without being seen. The building is mostly a screen, very thin compared with its height.

An observatory of giant instruments

Jai Singh II loved astronomy. In the 1720s and 1730s he built five observatories, called Jantar Mantar, in Jaipur, Delhi, Varanasi, Ujjain and Mathura. The one in Jaipur is the largest. Its instruments are made of stone and marble, big enough to walk through.

The Samrat Yantra is a giant sundial about 27 m tall. Its shadow moves across the marked scale at about 1 mm every second, so you can watch time passing. Astronomers used the instruments to track the Sun, Moon and stars, and to make calendars.

Forts on the hills

Jaipur is ringed by hills topped with forts. Amber Fort, the old capital, is built of pale yellow and pink sandstone and white marble. Its Sheesh Mahal, or mirror palace, has walls covered in tiny pieces of mirror that sparkle when a single candle is lit. Nahargarh and Jaigarh forts stand on the ridges above, and Jaigarh holds a huge cannon called Jaivana. A long wall with watchtowers runs along the hills between the forts, a little like a small Great Wall. From the top you can see why the kings chose this valley: hills on most sides made it easy to guard, and lakes stored the precious rain.

Colours, crafts and gems

Jaipur is famous for crafts. Nearby villages such as Sanganer and Bagru are known for block printing, where carved wooden blocks stamp patterns onto cloth. The city is also a centre for cutting gemstones and for blue pottery. Kite flying fills the sky on Makar Sankranti in January. In 2019, UNESCO named the walled city of Jaipur a World Heritage Site, two years after Ahmedabad became India's first World Heritage City.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Jaipur's Hawa Mahal is a huge palace with hundreds of rooms.

Truth: It is mostly a tall, thin screen wall at the edge of the City Palace, only one room deep in places. Its many small windows let royal women watch the street below without being seen.

Try this

Make a shadow clock. On a sunny morning, stand a pencil upright in a lump of clay on a sheet of paper in a sunny spot that won't be moved. Every hour, mark the end of the shadow and write the time. By afternoon you will have a simple sundial, like a tiny Samrat Yantra.
