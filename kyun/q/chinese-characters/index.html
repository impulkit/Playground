How does Chinese writing work?

Chinese has no alphabet. Instead, it has thousands of characters, and each one stands for a whole syllable with a meaning of its own.

The short answer

English uses 26 letters that stand for sounds. Chinese works differently. Each character stands for one syllable, and that syllable usually carries a meaning. The character 山 is pronounced shān and means mountain. 火 is huǒ, fire.

Some words are a single character. Many are two characters put together: 火山, "fire mountain", means volcano. So instead of spelling words out, a Chinese reader recognises characters, a bit like the way you recognise "&" or "₹" without sounding out letters.

A large dictionary holds about 50,000 characters, but most are rare. Knowing about 3,000 is enough to read most of a newspaper.

Surprising but true

The oldest known Chinese writing was found on animal bones and turtle shells that were being ground up and sold as medicine called "dragon bones" until scholars recognised the writing in 1899.

Pictures, and then much more

A few characters began as drawings. 日 (sun) was once a circle with a dot. 人 (person) is a figure walking. Some combine ideas: 木 is tree, 林 is woods, 森 is forest.

But most characters, perhaps four in five, are built from two parts. One part, called a radical, hints at the meaning. The other hints at the sound. For example, 妈 (mā, mother) combines 女 (woman) with 马 (mǎ, horse). The horse has nothing to do with mothers. It is there to tell you roughly how to say the word.

Once you know the common parts, unfamiliar characters become easier to guess. Characters about water often carry three little drops on the left side, as in 河 (river) and 海 (sea). Many dictionaries are arranged by radicals, the way an English dictionary is arranged by the first letter.

Written on bones

The oldest Chinese writing that experts are sure about comes from the Shang dynasty, around 1250 BCE. Kings asked questions about the harvest, the weather and wars, and priests carved them onto turtle shells and the shoulder bones of oxen. They then heated the bones until they cracked and read the answer in the cracks.

For centuries, farmers dug up these bones and sold them to medicine shops as "dragon bones". In 1899, a scholar noticed ancient writing on them. Thousands have since been found near Anyang, in northern China. Many of their characters are ancestors of ones still used today, which makes Chinese one of the oldest writing systems still in use.

One script, many languages

China has several spoken Chinese languages, such as Mandarin and Cantonese, which can sound as different as Hindi and Bengali. Yet speakers of both read the same characters, because the characters carry meaning, not exact sounds.

The same thing happens with numbers. A person who says "seven" and one who says saat both read "7". In the past, Chinese characters also spread to Japan, Korea and Vietnam. Japanese still uses thousands of them in its own writing system.

Simpler strokes and phones

Many characters take a lot of strokes to write. In the 1950s, the government of mainland China introduced simplified characters with fewer strokes, to help more people learn to read. Taiwan and Hong Kong still use the older, traditional forms. Mother is 媽 in traditional characters and 妈 in simplified ones.

In 1958, China adopted Pinyin, a way of writing Chinese sounds in Roman letters. That is why the capital is spelt Beijing today, not Peking. Pinyin is also how most people type Chinese. They type the sound, ma, and pick the right character from a list that pops up on the screen. The ancient art of brush writing, calligraphy, is still taught in Chinese schools.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Every Chinese character is a tiny picture of the thing it means.

Truth: A small number began as pictures, like 山 for mountain. But most characters combine a part that hints at the meaning with a part that hints at the sound.
