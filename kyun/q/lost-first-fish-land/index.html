How did fish first crawl onto land?

In 2004, scientists searching frozen Arctic cliffs found a 375-million-year-old fish with a neck, a wrist and lungs, a missing link between fish and us.

The short answer

About 375 million years ago, Earth had no animals with legs walking on land. Plants were spreading, and insects' relatives scuttled about, but backboned animals still lived in water.

Some fish in shallow, warm streams had thick, fleshy fins with bones inside, unlike the thin fins of a goldfish. These lobe-finned fish also had lungs as well as gills. Over millions of years, their fins became stronger, able to prop up the body. Their heads gained necks. Eventually their descendants walked out of the water. Every animal with four limbs today, from frogs to elephants to you, comes from them.

Surprising but true

The bones in your arm, from shoulder to fingers, follow the same pattern as the fin bones of a fish that lived 375 million years ago.

The fish that did push-ups

In the summer of 2004, three scientists, Neil Shubin, Ted Daeschler and Farish Jenkins, were digging on Ellesmere Island in Arctic Canada. It's a frozen, treeless place where polar bears roam. They found a flat skull poking out of the rock.

It belonged to a creature they named Tiktaalik, which means "large freshwater fish" in Inuktitut, the language of local Inuit people. It grew to perhaps 2.5 m long, with scales and fins like a fish. But it had a flat head like a crocodile and a neck that let it turn its head. Its front fins had bones like a shoulder, elbow and wrist, strong enough for push-ups in shallow water.

Why leave the water?

Scientists have several ideas, and they may all be partly right. Shallow streams could be warm and low in oxygen, so simple lungs that gulped air helped. Big fish hunted in deeper water, so the shallows were safer. And land offered food: plants and creeping bugs that nothing else was eating yet.

Early four-legged animals like Acanthostega, about 365 million years ago, still lived mostly in water. Acanthostega had eight fingers on each front limb, and Ichthyostega had seven toes on its back feet. Five fingers became the standard later.

Footprints that broke the timeline

In 2010, scientists described fossil footprints from a quarry at Zachełmie in Poland. They were made by an animal with four limbs, about 390 million years ago. That's older than Tiktaalik.

So Tiktaalik wasn't the very first. It was a late survivor of a group that had already started on the path. The footprints show that four-legged animals appeared earlier than the bones suggested, and some scientists think they may have walked on tidal flats by the sea as well as in streams. The hunt for older fossils goes on.

Walking fish today

Some fish still try out life on land. Mudskippers in Indian mangroves, like those of the Sundarbans, skip across the mud on their fins and breathe through their skin and mouth. Lungfish in Africa, South America and Australia can gulp air, and African lungfish survive dry seasons buried in mud.

The climbing perch of Indian ponds can wriggle across land from one pond to another. In some places it's said to climb trees, but that's mostly a story. These fish aren't our ancestors, but they show how the first steps could have worked.

Your body keeps the record too. Your arm bones follow the one-bone, two-bones, many-bones plan of Tiktaalik's fin. Some scientists even think your hiccups are a leftover reflex shared with tadpoles and air-breathing fish. It's one more clue in the long story of evolution that leads from Arctic streams to humans.

Myth or fact?

Myth: One day a fish decided to walk out of the sea and became a land animal.

Truth: It happened over millions of years, mostly in shallow streams and swamps, not the open sea. Many fish slowly evolved stronger fins, lungs and necks while still living in water. Walking came before leaving the water for good.

Try this

Hold out your arm and feel the bones. One bone from shoulder to elbow, two from elbow to wrist, then lots of little bones in the wrist and fingers. Now look at a picture of a fish fin skeleton like Tiktaalik's. You'll see the same one, two, many plan.
