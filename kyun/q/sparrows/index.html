Where have all the house sparrows gone?

The little brown bird that once nested in every Indian home has become scarce in many big cities. Nobody is completely sure why.

The short answer

House sparrows haven't vanished everywhere. In many villages and small towns in India, they are still common. But in big cities such as Delhi, Mumbai, Bengaluru, Chennai, Hyderabad and Kolkata, their numbers have dropped sharply over the last few decades. The same thing has happened in cities like London.

Scientists think several changes in modern cities are to blame, working together. New buildings leave sparrows few places to nest. Tidier gardens and fewer insects make it hard to feed chicks. Less grain is spilled in the open. Which cause matters most is still debated.

Surprising but true

House sparrows were brought to New York in the 1850s. Today they live across almost all of the USA.

A bird that lives with people

The house sparrow has lived alongside humans for thousands of years, probably ever since people began farming and storing grain. In India, sparrows nested in gaps in walls, behind photo frames, inside ceiling fan cups and under tiled roofs. Many grandparents remember sparrows hopping inside their homes, picking up spilled rice.

Sparrows are small, about 15 cm long. Males have a grey cap and a black bib. Females are plain brown and buff. They chirp constantly, which is one reason people noticed when the chirping stopped.

Fewer homes, less food

Older buildings were full of holes and ledges. Modern buildings, with smooth concrete, glass and sealed roofs, have almost none. Without a safe hole to nest in, sparrows can't breed.

Food has changed too. Adult sparrows eat seeds and grain, but their chicks need insects. Cities now have more concrete and neatly trimmed foreign plants, and fewer patches of wild weeds and native shrubs where insects live. Grain is now sold in sealed packets rather than in open sacks at the corner shop. Cats, crows and air pollution may also play a part.

Why do cities keep growing?In 1837, Chicago had about 4,000 people. Just over 50 years later, it had more than a million.

What the counts say

In 2020, a large report called the State of India's Birds used millions of sightings sent in by birdwatchers. It found that house sparrow numbers across India as a whole had been roughly stable over the long term. The steep drops were in the biggest cities.

So the real story is less "the sparrow is disappearing" and more "the sparrow is leaving big cities". As cities grow, the way they are built makes a difference to which birds can live in them.

A lesson from history

In 1958, China's government launched a campaign against pests, and sparrows were on the list because they ate grain. Millions of people banged pots and pans to keep sparrows flying until they dropped from exhaustion. Sparrow numbers crashed.

But sparrows also eat insects. Without them, locusts and other crop pests increased. The campaign is often linked to the damage to harvests in the terrible famine that followed. Sparrows were soon taken off the list.

Sparrows in America

House sparrows are not native to the Americas. In 1851 and 1852, people released them in Brooklyn, New York, hoping they would eat caterpillars damaging trees. They spread across almost all of North America within about 50 years. Today many Americans see them as pests, because they compete with native birds such as bluebirds for nest holes.

Bringing them back

Since 2010, people around the world have marked World Sparrow Day on 20 March. The idea started in India with a conservationist named Mohammed Dilawar. In 2012, Delhi made the house sparrow its state bird. Bihar has it as its state bird too.

You can help. Wooden nest boxes, native plants that attract insects such as butterflies and their caterpillars, and a shallow dish of water all make a city friendlier. Families across India have put up boxes, and sparrows often move in within a season.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Mobile phone towers killed off the sparrows.

Truth: There is no strong scientific evidence for this. Sparrows also declined in places and times without phone towers. Loss of nesting holes and fewer insects for chicks are more likely causes.

Try this

Do a 15-minute bird count. Sit by a window, balcony or garden at the same time each day for a week and tally every sparrow you see or hear. Every February, thousands of people do the same thing in the Great Backyard Bird Count, which runs in India and the USA, and share their numbers with scientists.
