Why do cities keep growing?

In 1837, Chicago had about 4,000 people. Just over 50 years later, it had more than a million.

The short answer

Cities keep growing because they pull people in. They offer more jobs, better schools and colleges, bigger hospitals and more ways to earn a living than most villages. When a factory, port or office opens, workers arrive. Then shops, schools and restaurants open to serve them, which brings more workers still.

Cities also grow from within, as children are born there, and outwards, as they spread and swallow nearby villages. Around 2007, more than half the world's people were living in towns and cities for the first time. The UN expects about two in three people to be city dwellers by 2050.

Surprising but true

Around 2007, for the first time in human history, more people on Earth lived in towns and cities than in the countryside.

Why people come

Most people move to cities for work. A factory job, a construction site, a shop or an office can pay more than farming a small plot, especially in years when the rains fail. Cities also offer things that are hard to find in a village: colleges, specialist doctors, cinemas and a wide choice of friends.

Being close together helps businesses too. A company in Bengaluru can find skilled engineers nearby, along with suppliers, banks and customers. Economists call this the benefit of clustering. It is a big reason why cities that are already large keep getting larger.

A very old story

The first cities appeared more than 5,000 years ago in Mesopotamia, Egypt and the Indus Valley, after farmers began growing more food than they needed. Extra food meant some people could become potters, priests, traders or rulers instead.

Cities grew fastest during the Industrial Revolution of the 1800s, when factories needed huge numbers of workers. Chicago is a famous example. It had about 4,000 people in 1837. Railways, meat-packing plants and grain markets drew workers from across America and Europe, and by 1890 the city had more than a million people.

Growing up as well as out

When land runs short, cities build upwards. Tall buildings let thousands of people live and work on one small plot. The first skyscrapers rose in Chicago and New York in the late 1800s, after steel frames and safe elevators were invented.

India's cities

About 31% of Indians lived in towns and cities at the 2011 census, and the share has grown since. Delhi and Mumbai are among the largest urban areas anywhere, each with more than 2 crore people. Mumbai began as seven separate islands, joined over two centuries by land reclaimed from the sea.

Many people arrive in Indian cities faster than homes, water pipes and buses can be built. So some live in crowded informal settlements, such as Dharavi in Mumbai, which is also home to thousands of small businesses making leather goods, pottery and recycled plastic.

Growing pains

In 1950, New York and Tokyo were the only urban areas in the world with more than 1 crore people. Today there are dozens of these "megacities", and many are in Asia, Africa and Latin America, including Kolkata, Chennai, Bengaluru, Lagos and São Paulo.

Fast growth brings traffic jams, air pollution, flooding when drains cannot cope, and high rents. Cities around the world are trying to solve these with metro railways, better planning and more parks. Still, cities use land efficiently. Packing people together leaves more countryside free for farms and forests. Whether a city feels crowded or comfortable depends a lot on how well it is planned, and the world's population means the choices made now will shape life for billions.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Cities grow only because people leave villages.

Truth: Migration from villages is important, but a large share of city growth comes from children born in cities and from villages near the edge being reclassified as towns as the city spreads.
