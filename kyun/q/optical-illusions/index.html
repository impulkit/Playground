Why do optical illusions fool us?

Your eyes don't send your brain a photograph. They send clues, and your brain guesses the rest. Illusions catch it guessing wrong.

The short answer

Seeing feels effortless, but it isn't simple. Your eyes catch patterns of light, and your brain has to turn them into a world of objects, distances and colours. It does this very fast by using shortcuts, rules of thumb learned over millions of years and over your own lifetime.

Most of the time, those shortcuts work beautifully. An optical illusion is a picture designed so that the shortcuts give the wrong answer. Two lines look different lengths when they're the same. A still drawing seems to move. Two squares look different colours when they're printed with exactly the same ink.

Illusions aren't signs that your eyes are broken. They show how hard your brain is working behind the scenes.

Surprising but true

The Moon looks much bigger when it's low over the horizon, but if you photograph it, it's the same size as when it's high in the sky.

The brain fills in the gaps

Your eyes send your brain a lot of information, but not everything. Each eye even has a blind spot where the nerve leaves the back of the eye, and there are no light-sensing cells. You never notice it because your brain fills in the gap with whatever seems likely.

The brain also uses past experience. It knows that things further away look smaller, that light usually comes from above, and that shadows make things darker. It applies these rules automatically, even when you know a picture is a trick. That's why an illusion keeps working after someone explains it to you.

Lines that lie

In 1889, a German scientist named Franz Müller-Lyer published a drawing of two equal lines. One had arrowheads pointing out at its ends. The other had arrowheads pointing in. Almost everyone sees the second line as longer.

One popular explanation is that the brain reads the arrowheads as the corners of a building or a room, near or far, and adjusts the size it thinks the line must be. Researchers in the 1960s found the illusion seemed weaker for some people who grew up in places with fewer straight-edged buildings. That idea is still argued about, but it suggests illusions may depend partly on where we live.

Colours that aren't there

In 1995, Edward Adelson, a scientist at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, made a picture of a chessboard with a green cylinder casting a shadow on it. One square in the light looks dark grey. Another square in the shadow looks light grey. They are exactly the same shade. Your brain "corrects" for the shadow, the way it does in real life.

In February 2015, a photo of a striped dress spread across the internet. Some people saw it as blue and black. Others were sure it was white and gold. The real dress was blue and black. Scientists think people's brains made different guesses about whether the dress was in bluish shade or yellowish light, and corrected the colours differently.

The giant Moon

For thousands of years, people have noticed that the rising full Moon looks huge over the horizon and smaller when high in the sky. Photos show it's the same size. It is even a tiny bit further away when it's rising.

Nobody is completely sure why the illusion happens. One idea is that the brain sees the sky as a flattened dome and thinks the horizon is further away, so it enlarges the Moon there. Another is that trees and buildings next to the Moon make it seem big by comparison. Artists use similar tricks of size and distance in perspective drawing, and magicians use the brain's guesses in their tricks.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Moon is closer or bigger when it's near the horizon.

Truth: The Moon's distance barely changes during a night, and it's actually a little further from you when it's rising. It only looks bigger because of how your brain judges size near the ground.
