What makes Kashmir special?

In the Kashmir valley, people shop for vegetables from floating gardens, rowed to market in narrow boats before sunrise.

The short answer

Kashmir is a high valley in the Himalaya, ringed by snowy mountains, with lakes, rivers, orchards and gardens on its floor. People have called it beautiful for centuries. It is famous for Dal Lake and its houseboats, for saffron and apples, for soft shawls, and for a grand feast called the wazwan.

The name is also used for a much larger region, and this is where things get complicated. Since 1947, the wider Kashmir region has been divided between India and Pakistan, with a part controlled by China. Each country claims areas held by the others, and the dispute has led to wars and long periods of tension. This article is mostly about the valley on the Indian side, around the city of Srinagar.

Surprising but true

The fields around Pampore, near Srinagar, turn purple each autumn with saffron crocuses, and it takes the stigmas of about 150 flowers to make one gram of saffron.

Lakes, boats and floating gardens

Dal Lake lies at the edge of Srinagar, with mountains reflected in its water. Hundreds of carved wooden houseboats are moored along its shores. Visitors are rowed around in shikaras, narrow boats with painted roofs and cushioned seats.

Parts of the lake hold floating gardens, made from mats of reeds and weeds piled with soil. Farmers grow tomatoes, lotus stems and greens on them. Early in the morning, a floating vegetable market gathers on the water, with sellers and buyers bargaining from boat to boat.

Gardens of the emperors

The Mughal emperors loved Kashmir. They built terraced gardens on the slopes above Dal Lake, with water running down the middle in stone channels and fountains. Emperor Jahangir laid out Shalimar Bagh in 1619. Nishat Bagh, nearby, has twelve terraces climbing the hill.

These gardens followed a Persian idea of paradise as a walled garden full of flowing water. Huge chinar trees, a kind of plane tree, shade them. In autumn their leaves turn fiery red and orange, and the chinar leaf has become a symbol of Kashmir. Some chinars in the valley are said to be several hundred years old, planted in Mughal times.

Why is saffron so expensive?Each saffron thread is a tiny part of a purple flower, picked by hand, and you need about 1.5 lakh flowers for one kilogram.

Purple fields and soft wool

Each autumn, fields around the town of Pampore bloom with purple saffron crocuses. Each flower has three red threads, the stigmas, picked by hand. It takes around 150 flowers to make one gram of saffron, which is why it costs so much.

Kashmir is also known for pashmina, a very fine wool combed from the soft undercoat of mountain goats that live high on the cold plateau of Ladakh and Tibet. Kashmiri weavers spin and weave it into shawls so light that, people say, a fine one can be pulled through a finger ring. Craftspeople also make papier-mâché boxes, carpets and walnut-wood carvings.

Winter in the valley

The coldest stretch of winter is called Chillai Kalan, about 40 days from late December to the end of January. Snow can cut off roads, and parts of Dal Lake sometimes freeze. People wear the pheran, a long woollen cloak, and keep warm with a kangri, a small pot of hot coals held underneath it.

Food helps too. Families dry vegetables in summer to eat in winter, and drink kahwa, a green tea with saffron, cardamom and almonds. On special days, a wazwan feast may include dozens of dishes, many slow-cooked with lamb. When spring finally returns, one of Asia's largest tulip gardens opens on the hills above the lake, and people come out to see the valley turn green again.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Kashmir is cold and snowy all year round.

Truth: The valley has four clear seasons. Winters are freezing and snowy, but summers are warm and green, with temperatures often above 30 °C in Srinagar. Spring brings tulips and almond blossom, and autumn turns the chinar trees red.
