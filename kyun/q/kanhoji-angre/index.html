Who was Kanhoji Angre?

In 1712, a Maratha admiral's ships captured the British governor of Bombay's own armed yacht, and the British had to pay 30,000 rupees to get it back.

The short answer

Kanhoji Angre was the chief of the Maratha navy in the early 1700s. For about 30 years he controlled the Konkan coast, the stretch of sea between Mumbai and Goa. British, Portuguese and Dutch fleets all tried to beat him. None of them managed it while he was alive.

His title was Sarkhel, which means admiral of the fleet. He worked from sea forts such as Vijaydurg and Kolaba, and he made foreign ships buy a pass, called a dastak, to sail his waters. Ships that refused could be chased down and seized. That is why his enemies called him a pirate, and why many Indians today call him a hero.

Surprising but true

The British called Kanhoji Angre a pirate, yet today the Indian Navy's Western Naval Command headquarters in Mumbai is named INS Angre after him.

A navy built on forts

Kanhoji grew up in a coastal world shaped by Shivaji, who had built forts on rocky islands to guard the sea. Kanhoji took that idea and ran with it. In 1698 he set up his first base at Vijaydurg, a fort with triple walls on a headland in today's Sindhudurg district.

His ships were small and fast. Many were gurabs, wide sailing ships with cannons, and gallivats, long rowed boats that could dart into shallow creeks. Big European ships could not follow them there. A British ship might be stuck at sea with no wind while Angre's oarsmen rowed right up to it.

Kolaba, his favourite base, sits on a rock just off the beach at Alibag. At low tide you can walk out to it across wet sand. At high tide it becomes an island again, with the sea doing the job of a moat. Inside the walls there is even a freshwater well, which meant defenders never ran dry during a siege. Forts like this gave his ships safe harbours every few hours' sail along the coast.

The governor's yacht

On 4 November 1712, Angre's fleet captured an armed yacht called the Algerine. It belonged to William Aislabie, the British governor of Bombay. Angre held it for three months. The East India Company paid 30,000 rupees in February 1713 to get the yacht and its passengers back.

Picture the scene in Bombay: the most powerful Englishman in western India, and he could not keep his own boat safe in his own harbour. After that, the Company built more warships and spent years trying to break Angre's hold on the coast.

Two empires against one admiral

In November 1721, the British and Portuguese joined forces. They brought around 6,000 soldiers and four large warships to attack Kolaba, Angre's fort off Alibag. The attack failed. In 1724 the Dutch attacked Vijaydurg with their own fleet and were beaten back too.

Angre was not against all Europeans, though. He hired Dutch and other European sailors when it suited him, and one Dutchman held the rank of commodore in his fleet. He cared about skill more than where a sailor came from.

What he left behind

Kanhoji Angre died at Alibag on 4 July 1729. His sons carried on, but they quarrelled, and in 1756 a British and Maratha force from Pune captured Vijaydurg. The great Maratha navy never fully recovered.

He is remembered all along the coast. A statue stands in Mumbai's naval dockyard, and in 1951 the navy named INS Angre after him. The old Kennery lighthouse on Khanderi island now carries his name, sweeping its beam across waters his ships once ruled. Even a sunken reef far out in the Arabian Sea, Angria Bank, is named after him.

Seven hundred years before Angre, Tamil kings had sent their own war fleets across the ocean. The story of the Chola navy shows just how old India's sea power really is.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Kanhoji Angre was simply a pirate who robbed ships for himself.

Truth: He was an admiral appointed by the Maratha state. He made foreign ships buy Maratha passes to sail his waters and seized those that refused. The British and Portuguese called him a pirate because he blocked their trade.
