Why are some cities sinking?

Parts of Mexico City are sinking more than 2 cm a month, so fast that a satellite built by NASA and ISRO can watch it happen from space.

The short answer

Some cities are sinking because the ground beneath them is squashing down. This is called subsidence. Many big cities are built on soft layers of clay, silt and sand, laid down by old lakes, rivers and deltas. These layers are soaked with water, which helps hold them up.

When people pump that water out through wells to drink, farm and run factories, the layers squeeze together like a sponge being wrung out. The ground on top drops, slowly but steadily. Heavy buildings and roads add to the problem. Near the sea, the sinking makes floods much worse.

Surprising but true

A monument in Mexico City, the Angel of Independence, has needed 14 extra steps added at its base since 1910, because the ground around it keeps dropping.

A city on an old lake

Mexico City is one of the fastest-sinking cities on Earth. It was built on the bed of a lake that the Aztecs once lived beside. About 2 crore people now live there, and much of their water is pumped from under the city.

Between October 2025 and January 2026, a new radar satellite called NISAR, built jointly by NASA and ISRO, measured parts of the city sinking more than 2 cm a month. That adds up to over 24 cm a year, close to the length of a school ruler. In the 1990s and 2000s, some areas were dropping even faster, around 35 cm a year. The Angel of Independence, a tall golden statue built in 1910, has needed 14 extra steps added to its base so people can still climb up to it.

Jakarta's escape plan

Jakarta, the capital of Indonesia, sits on soft coastal land. Millions of people rely on private wells, and parts of the north of the city have sunk several metres over the past few decades. Sea walls now guard neighbourhoods that are lower than the water beside them.

Indonesia's government decided in 2019 to build a new capital, Nusantara, on the island of Borneo. Jakarta's sinking and crowding were among the reasons. Engineers are also building bigger sea walls and asking people to switch to piped water.

What is groundwater?Beneath your feet, rainwater is soaking slowly through the rock. India pumps up more of it than any other country, about a quarter of all the groundwater used on Earth.

The city that stopped sinking

There is good news. In the 1900s, parts of Tokyo sank by around 4 metres as factories pumped out groundwater. From the 1960s onwards, Japan passed laws limiting the pumping and brought in water from rivers and dams instead. The sinking slowed to almost nothing.

That shows the problem can be fixed. Once the soft layers are squashed, though, they rarely spring back. The land that was lost stays lost. So the sooner a city acts, the better. Venice shows the same story. In the 1900s, wells pumping water for nearby industry made the ground sink faster. Italy closed most of them in the 1970s, but the city still floods more often as the sea rises.

Weight, ice and rising seas

Buildings matter a little too. In 2023, scientists worked out that New York City's buildings weigh about 762 billion kg, as much as about 14 crore elephants. That weight helps press the city down by a millimetre or two a year in some places.

In India, Joshimath in Uttarakhand made news in 2023 when cracks spread through homes and roads as the hillside town sank. Experts blamed loose ground, water seeping underneath and heavy building. Studies have also found sinking in parts of Delhi, Kolkata and other cities where wells pull up lots of groundwater.

Sinking land and rising seas together are a double trouble for coastal cities, which is why places like Venice now raise giant barriers against high tides. The Netherlands has lived below sea level for centuries, and its tricks with dikes and pumps are the story of Dutch water engineering. Underground, a different kind of collapse can happen in seconds instead of years. When the roof of a hidden cave gives way, the result is a sinkhole big enough to swallow a car.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Cities are sinking mainly because of heavy skyscrapers.

Truth: Buildings do add weight, but in most fast-sinking cities the main cause is pumping water out of the ground. Stop the pumping, and the sinking often slows a lot.

Try this

Soak a kitchen sponge in water and put it on a plate. Rest a small book on top and mark its height. Now gently press the water out from the sides and watch the book sink. That is roughly what happens when cities pump out groundwater.
