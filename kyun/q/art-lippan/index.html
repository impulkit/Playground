What is Lippan mirror art?

In the round mud houses of Kutch, women once pressed tiny mirrors into clay walls so a single oil lamp could make a whole room sparkle.

The short answer

Lippan is a mud-and-mirror relief art from Kutch, in Gujarat. Artists roll clay into thin coils and press them onto a wall to make raised patterns: circles, diamonds, peacocks, camels and trees. Then they push small pieces of mirror into the gaps, so the whole design shines when light hits it.

The name comes from lipan, which means plastering. It began as decoration on the inside walls of village homes, especially the round huts called bhungas. Today you are just as likely to see it on a painted wooden board hanging in a flat in Mumbai, Bengaluru or New Jersey.

Surprising but true

Traditional Lippan was made from clay mixed with camel or donkey dung, which helps the mud bind together and crack less as it dries.

Mud, dung and mirrors

The traditional recipe sounds strange. Women mixed local clay with camel or donkey dung, kneaded it smooth and rolled it into coils by hand. The fibres in the dung help hold the mud together, so it cracks less as it dries in the hot Kutch sun. Camels were everywhere in the desert, so their dung was easy to find.

Once dry, the mud was painted white with a clay wash. The mirrors, called abhla, were cut into tiny circles, triangles and diamonds. Some were barely bigger than a fingernail. A big wall might hold hundreds of them, each pressed in by hand while the mud was still soft.

Light in a round room

A bhunga is a round house with thick mud walls and a cone-shaped thatched roof. It stays cool in summer and warm in winter, but the windows are small, so the inside can be dim. The mirrors bounce the light from the doorway or from an oil lamp around the room. At night, one flame makes the walls glitter like stars.

Bhungas also proved surprisingly tough in a disaster. When a huge earthquake struck Kutch in 2001, many round mud houses survived while square concrete buildings nearby collapsed. Their circular shape spreads the shaking evenly, a lesson engineers still study when they look at earthquakes.

Who makes it

Different communities in Kutch have their own styles. Rabari women, from a pastoral community that has herded camels, sheep and goats for centuries, are known for patterns that echo their embroidery. Among the Mutwa people of the Banni grasslands, men also made Lippan. Meghwal artisans have their own designs too.

The patterns are mostly geometric, built from repeated shapes that wrap around a centre. That gives Lippan a lot in common with rangoli and with the mirror-work embroidery Kutch is famous for. A Rabari woman's skirt and her wall could carry the same design.

From village walls to city flats

In the last 20 years, Lippan has moved out of the village. Artists now use craft clay or putty instead of dung, and stick it onto wooden boards instead of walls. Mirrors come pre-cut. Kits are sold online, and city children make Lippan in school art classes.

Some traditional artists worry the shortcuts lose the skill of real Lippan, where every coil is rolled by hand. Others are glad the craft is earning artisans a living and reaching new homes. Kutch's tourists help, especially during the winter festival in the white salt desert of the Rann of Kutch.

Why mirrors?

Mirror-work has deep roots in Kutch and Rajasthan, in clothing as well as walls. Many people believe the flashes of light turn away the evil eye. Others simply love sparkle in a desert landscape of browns and whites. Either way, Kutch has turned shiny glass into one of India's most recognisable crafts, and Lippan panels now hang in airports and hotel lobbies.

And the region has plenty more where that came from: block printing, tie-dye and leatherwork, all part of the story of Kutch's crafts.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Lippan art is an old craft that only exists in villages now.

Truth: It is very much alive. Artists from Kutch make Lippan panels for homes, offices and hotels across India and abroad, and many young people learn it in workshops and online.

Try this

Roll a small snake of clay or play dough and press it onto a piece of cardboard in a simple shape, like a circle or a diamond. Push in sequins, bindis or bits of shiny foil where mirrors would go. Put it under a lamp and move your head to watch it glint.
