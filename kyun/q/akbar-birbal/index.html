Who were Akbar and Birbal in the stories?

The emperor was real, the clever minister was real, and yet most of their famous adventures probably never happened.

The short answer

Akbar was one of the greatest Mughal emperors. He ruled much of north and central India from 1556 to 1605. Birbal was one of his courtiers, a Hindu poet and musician who became a close friend and adviser.

In the stories, Akbar asks tricky questions or sets strange tests, and Birbal always finds a smart way out. Jealous courtiers try to trap him and fail. The king gets puzzled, then laughs. These tales are still told across India and Pakistan in homes, schoolbooks, comics and cartoons.

Surprising but true

The real Birbal was a poet and musician as well as a courtier, and he died far from home on a military campaign in the mountains of the north-west.

The real men behind the tales

Akbar became emperor at just thirteen. He grew curious about many faiths and invited scholars of different religions to debate at his court. He also built a red sandstone city called Fatehpur Sikri, near Agra.

Birbal was born in a Brahmin family, and his birth name is often given as Mahesh Das. He wrote poems in Braj Bhasha, a form of Hindi, and Akbar gave him the titles Birbal and Raja. In 1586, Birbal was sent with an army into the mountains of the north-west, near the Swat valley. He was killed there in an ambush. Court records say Akbar grieved for him deeply.

Later tradition counts Birbal among Akbar's "nine jewels", or navaratnas, along with the singer Tansen, the finance minister Todar Mal and the historian Abul Fazl. These were all real people at the court. Historians doubt there was ever an official club of exactly nine, but the idea shows how famous the court's talent became.

Famous tales

In one story, Akbar says a poor man may win a reward if he stands all night in a freezing lake. The man survives, saying he kept his eyes on a lamp burning far away in the palace. A courtier claims the lamp warmed him, so the reward is refused.

The next day, Birbal doesn't come to court. Akbar finds him sitting by a small fire, with a pot of khichdi hanging high above it. "It will cook soon," says Birbal. Akbar laughs and calls it foolish, until he sees the point. If a faraway lamp cannot cook rice, it cannot warm a man either. The poor man gets his reward.

Why the stories grew

After Birbal died, people kept telling stories about him. New ones were added, and old jokes from elsewhere were given his name. Some of the same plots turn up in tales of Tenali Raman in the south, and in stories of Mulla Nasruddin from Turkey to Central Asia. A good trick travels.

The tales usually follow a pattern. A powerful person is about to do something unfair or silly. A clever person shows them the mistake without saying it directly. That lets listeners enjoy a small, safe kind of rebellion: brains beating rank.

From the court to the comic book

In the twentieth century the stories moved into print. Amar Chitra Katha published Birbal comics that many Indian children read before they could spell "Mughal". Television shows and cartoons followed.

Today a house at Fatehpur Sikri is still called Birbal's Palace. Historians aren't sure he ever lived in it, which suits him. Like the stories, the name sticks because people want it to be true. The real history of the Mughals is full of wars and politics. The Birbal stories keep a lighter memory of the same court, where a joke could sometimes win an argument.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Birbal is a made-up character, like a fairy-tale hero.

Truth: Birbal was a real man at Akbar's court, and the emperor gave him the title of Raja. What is made up is most of the stories. They were added over centuries by storytellers who loved the idea of a clever adviser.
