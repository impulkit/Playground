What chores do kids do around the world?

In Japan, schoolchildren scrub their own classrooms and corridors every day, and some toddlers go shopping alone on television.

The short answer

Kids almost everywhere help at home, but the jobs change a lot from place to place. In India, children often help set out water, fold clothes, shell peas or prepare the house for a festival. In the United States, many kids load dishwashers, take out rubbish bins and feed pets, sometimes for pocket money.

In Japan, children clean their own schools every day. In farming villages across Africa, Asia and Latin America, children may help care for younger siblings, fetch water or look after animals.

What counts as a normal chore depends on the family, the place and the age of the child. But the urge to help seems to start very young.

Surprising but true

In experiments, toddlers just 18 months old will stop playing to help an adult who drops something, without being asked or rewarded.

Japan's cleaning time

In many Japanese schools there is a daily time called o-soji, meaning cleaning. Pupils grab brooms, cloths and buckets. They sweep classrooms, mop corridors, wipe windows and clean the stairs. In some schools they clean the toilets too. Teachers often work alongside them.

The idea is that if you clean a place yourself, you are less likely to make a mess of it. Many Japanese schools also have pupils serve lunch to their classmates, wearing white caps and aprons.

Japanese children are also known for running errands young. A television show called "Hajimete no Otsukai", meaning "My First Errand", has shown toddlers going to shops alone since 1991. A camera crew follows them secretly. In 2022 it reached viewers worldwide on Netflix as "Old Enough!" You can see more everyday life in Japan's culture.

Helping without being asked

In 2006, two scientists in Germany, Felix Warneken and Michael Tomasello, ran a famous experiment. An adult "accidentally" dropped a pen or struggled to open a cupboard while carrying books. Toddlers just 18 months old would stop playing, toddle over and help. No one asked them, and no one rewarded them.

Psychologist Barbara Rogoff has studied children in Maya communities in Guatemala and Mexico. There, she found, children are often expected to join in family work from an early age, learning by watching and pitching in. Many of them help without being told, because they see themselves as part of the team.

Some researchers think that paying children for every small job can change how they feel about helping. Others think pocket money teaches useful lessons about earning and saving. Families decide differently, and there is no single right answer, as you can read in the pocket money debate.

Chores for festivals

Festivals bring extra jobs everywhere. Before Diwali, many Indian families clean and paint their homes, and children help draw rangoli patterns at the door. Before Lunar New Year in China, families sweep away the old year's dust. In the US, kids may help rake autumn leaves, or bake and decorate for Thanksgiving or Christmas.

These chores feel different from ordinary ones. They are tied to celebration, and many people remember them fondly for life.

When work isn't a chore

Small jobs at home are part of family life. But some children around the world do heavy, dangerous work for long hours instead of going to school. That is child labour, and it is very different. UNICEF and the International Labour Organization estimated that about 138 million children were in child labour worldwide in 2024, down from 160 million in 2020.

Chores are safe, short and suited to a child's age. They leave time for school, rest and play, which every child has a right to. School matters a great deal, and you can see how it works for children elsewhere in schools around the world.

And the strangest part? The washing that took grandmothers all day can now take a machine an hour, and the clever spinning inside it is explained in how washing machines work.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Toddlers are too young to help with real chores.

Truth: Experiments show that children as young as 18 months will try to help an adult pick up a dropped object or open a cupboard, without being asked. Many families around the world build on this urge early.

Try this

Make a chore map of your home. List every job that keeps it running for one day, from filling water bottles to folding clothes. Then mark who usually does each one. You may be surprised how many jobs one person is doing.
