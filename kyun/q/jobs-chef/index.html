What is it like to be a chef?

A busy restaurant kitchen at dinner time runs like an army, with a chain of command that a French chef worked out more than a hundred years ago.

The short answer

Being a chef is hot, fast, tiring and, for people who love it, deeply satisfying. A chef cooks, but also plans menus, orders ingredients, keeps costs down, trains other cooks and makes sure every plate leaving the kitchen is right.

The hours are long. Restaurants are busiest when everyone else is relaxing: evenings, weekends and festivals. A cook may be on their feet for ten hours or more in a kitchen that can feel as hot as a summer afternoon in Nagpur. But many chefs say there is nothing like the rush of a full dining room and a kitchen working well together.

Surprising but true

The word "chef" is French for "chief". Strictly, it means the person in charge of a kitchen, not everyone who cooks in it.

An army in aprons

In the late 1800s, a French chef named Auguste Escoffier organised big hotel kitchens like an army. His system, called the brigade, gave each cook one job. At the top is the head chef. Under them is the sous-chef, the second in command. Then come the section cooks, each running one station: sauces, fish, vegetables, grill or pastry.

Many kitchens around the world still use a version of this system. When an order comes in, the person running the pass, the counter where finished food is collected, calls it out. Each station shouts back to show it has heard. It sounds noisy, but it stops mistakes. The head chef tastes sauces, checks how each plate looks and sends back anything that isn't right.

A day in the kitchen

The cooking you see is only the end of a long day. Chefs arrive hours before service to check deliveries of vegetables, meat and spices. Then comes the preparation: chopping onions by the kilo, making stocks and sauces, marinating meat, rolling dough.

French cooks call this mise en place, "everything in its place". By the time the first customer orders, each station has its ingredients ready in small containers. During service, there is no time to stop and chop. After the last order, the kitchen must be scrubbed clean before anyone goes home.

How do ratios help in cooking?Many cooks remember recipes as ratios, not lists. Two cups of water for one of rice, or one part urad dal to about three or four parts rice for idli batter.

How people become chefs

There are many paths. In India, many chefs study at Institutes of Hotel Management after Class 12, getting in through a national entrance exam. In the USA, some go to cooking schools such as the Culinary Institute of America in New York State. Many great cooks never go to college at all. They start as kitchen helpers and learn by watching and doing.

India also has old family traditions of cooking for others, such as the halwais who make sweets and the cooks who prepare grand feasts for weddings. Their skills are often passed from parent to child. A wedding cook in Lucknow or Hyderabad may cook biryani for a thousand guests without ever writing down a recipe.

Cooking for thousands

Not every chef works in a restaurant. Some cook in hospitals, schools, airlines, ships or army camps. The community kitchen at the Golden Temple in Amritsar serves free meals to tens of thousands of people every day, cooked mostly by volunteers in giant pots. Planning food on that scale needs the same skills as running any big kitchen, from getting ratios right to keeping everyone safe.

The hard parts

Kitchens are full of hazards: hot oil, sharp knives, slippery floors and heavy pots. Good chefs take kitchen safety seriously and teach it to new cooks from day one. The work is also tiring, and starting pay can be low. Even so, many chefs love the job because cooking is something you can share straight away. You make something with your hands, and a few minutes later you can watch a stranger enjoy it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Chefs spend all day inventing fancy new dishes.

Truth: Most of a chef's day goes on preparation, ordering ingredients, checking costs, training staff and cleaning. Creating new dishes is a small, special part of the job.

Try this

Before you help make a simple snack, such as a sandwich or a bowl of chaat, set out every ingredient and tool first, in the order you will use them. Chefs call this mise en place, which means "everything in its place". Then make it and notice whether it felt calmer and faster than usual.
