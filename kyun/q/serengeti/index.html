What happens in the Serengeti?

Every year, well over a million wildebeest walk a giant loop across Tanzania and Kenya, following the rain and the fresh grass it brings.

The short answer

The Serengeti is a huge stretch of grassland in northern Tanzania, in East Africa. Its name comes from a Maasai word meaning "endless plains". On a clear day, flat yellow grass seems to stretch all the way to the sky.

It is famous for the Great Migration. Wildebeest are a kind of antelope, though with their beards and big heads they look more like small cattle. Every year, around 1.3 to 1.5 million wildebeest, along with hundreds of thousands of zebras and gazelles, travel in a giant loop through the Serengeti and into Kenya's Maasai Mara. They follow the rains to find fresh grass. Lions, cheetahs, hyenas and crocodiles wait along the way.

Surprising but true

A newborn wildebeest calf can usually stand within minutes of birth and run with the herd within a day.

A year-long loop

The journey has no start or finish, but January is a good place to begin. The herds gather on the short-grass plains of the southern Serengeti, where rain brings rich new grass. From late January to March, hundreds of thousands of calves are born in a few weeks. So many arrive at once that hunters can't catch more than a small share.

As the plains dry out around May, the herds head west and north. Lines of animals stretch for kilometres. By July they reach the Mara River on the border with Kenya, where they spend the dry months. When the short rains return in November, they turn south again.

The river crossing

The most dramatic moment comes at the Mara River. Thousands of wildebeest gather on the bank, nervous and noisy. Then one jumps, and the rest pour after it in a rush of dust and splashing.

Huge Nile crocodiles wait in the brown water. Some animals are caught, and others are swept away by the current or trampled in the crush. Most make it across. Some herds cross the river several times in a season, heading back and forth as rain falls on one side or the other.

Grass, grazers and hunters

Zebras and wildebeest don't really compete. Zebras often go first and eat the tall, tough tops of the grass. Wildebeest then eat the shorter, leafier parts underneath, and gazelles nibble the fresh shoots that grow back. Each animal makes the grass better for the next.

The grassland is dotted with kopjes, piles of rounded granite boulders sticking out of the plains. Lions rest on them in the heat and use them as lookouts. The whole grassland depends on a balance between rain, grass, grazers and hunters, and a long drought can tip it fast.

Saved by a vaccine

In the 1950s and early 1960s, the wildebeest population was only about a quarter of a million. A cattle disease called rinderpest was killing many calves. Then vets began vaccinating cattle around the park, which stopped the disease spreading. Within about twenty years, wildebeest numbers had grown to well over a million. It's one of the clearest examples of vaccines protecting wild animals as well as farm ones.

In 1959, a German father and son, Bernhard and Michael Grzimek, made a film called Serengeti Shall Not Die, which won an Oscar. Michael was killed during the filming when a vulture struck his small plane. The film made the park famous around the world.

Today, people still argue about how to share the land around the park between wildlife, farmers and Maasai herders, whose cattle have grazed these plains for centuries. Roads, fences and farms on the edges could block the herds' path. The wildebeest ignore the border between Tanzania and Kenya. So the Serengeti and the Maasai Mara, the heart of many a safari in Kenya, have to be protected together, as one great loop of grass.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Hyenas are mostly scavengers that steal food from lions.

Truth: Studies in the Serengeti found that spotted hyenas kill most of their own food, often hunting in packs at night. Lions also steal from hyenas, and sometimes the other way round.
