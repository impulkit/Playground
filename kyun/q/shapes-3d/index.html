What are 3D shapes?

Count the corners, edges and faces of a dice, a pyramid or a cardboard box, and the same small sum always comes out as 2.

The short answer

A 3D shape is a solid shape that takes up space. It has three dimensions: length, width and height. A drawing of a square is flat, or 2D. A dice, a brick or a cricket ball is 3D, because you can hold it and turn it around.

Some 3D shapes have flat sides and straight edges, like cubes, pyramids and prisms. Mathematicians call these polyhedra, from Greek words meaning "many seats". Others are curved, like spheres, cylinders and cones. Almost everything you can pick up is a 3D shape, or a mixture of several.

Surprising but true

There are exactly five solid shapes whose faces are all the same regular shape, and they have been known for more than 2,000 years. Players of board games still roll them as dice.

Faces, edges and corners

A polyhedron has three kinds of parts. Faces are the flat surfaces. Edges are the lines where two faces meet. Vertices, or corners, are the points where edges meet.

A cube has 6 square faces, 12 edges and 8 corners. A square-based pyramid, like the pyramids of Egypt, has 5 faces, 8 edges and 5 corners. A triangular prism, shaped like a tent, has 5 faces, 9 edges and 6 corners.

Curved shapes need different words. A cylinder has two flat circular faces and one curved surface. A cone has one flat circle and a point at the top, called the apex. A sphere has no flat faces, edges or corners at all.

A rule that always works

In the 1750s, the Swiss mathematician Leonhard Euler noticed something. For a cube, take the corners and subtract the edges, then add the faces: 8 − 12 + 6 = 2. For a square pyramid: 5 − 8 + 5 = 2. For a prism: 6 − 9 + 5 = 2.

It works for every solid without holes through it, however lumpy or strange. A shape with a tunnel through it, like a picture frame or a bead, gives a different answer. Mathematicians use this to tell shapes apart in a field called topology.

Why are bubbles round?Blow a bubble through a square wand and it still comes out round. The bubble's skin is quietly solving a geometry problem.

The five perfect solids

Some solids are especially neat. Every face is the same regular shape, and the same number of faces meet at every corner. There are only five: the tetrahedron, with 4 triangles; the cube, with 6 squares; the octahedron, with 8 triangles; the dodecahedron, with 12 pentagons; and the icosahedron, with 20 triangles.

The Greek philosopher Plato linked them to fire, earth, air, water and the heavens, so they are called Platonic solids. Euclid proved that there can be only five. Today they are the dice of board games: four-sided, six-sided, eight-sided, twelve-sided and twenty-sided.

Shapes in the world

Nature is full of 3D shapes. Salt crystals grow as tiny cubes. Soap bubbles form spheres, because a sphere holds the most air inside the smallest skin. Bees build honeycomb from six-sided prisms that fit together without gaps.

People build them too. The Great Stupa at Sanchi is a hemisphere, half a sphere. Water tanks are often cylinders, ice-cream cones are cones, and a Rubik's Cube is a cube made of 26 smaller visible cubes. The classic black-and-white football, first used at the 1970 World Cup, is made of 12 pentagons and 20 hexagons stitched together, which puffs up into something close to a sphere.

Flattening a solid

If you unfold a cardboard box carefully, you get a flat shape called a net. Every polyhedron has at least one net. A cube has 11 different ones, and some look nothing like a cross. Packaging designers think about nets all the time, trying to waste as little card as possible.

Going the other way, from flat to solid, is how origami artists and engineers fold flat sheets into boxes, domes and even solar panels for satellites.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A circle and a sphere are the same shape.

Truth: A circle is flat, like a drawing on paper, with only length and width. A sphere is solid and round in every direction, like a ball or a marble. Every slice through a sphere makes a circle.

Try this

Make 3D shapes from toothpicks or ear buds joined with small balls of clay or soaked chickpeas. Build a cube, a pyramid with a square base and a triangular pyramid. For each, count the corners, edges and faces and test Euler's rule. Then try to build a shape where the rule fails.
