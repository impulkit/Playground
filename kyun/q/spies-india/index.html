Who were India's famous spies?

In the 1860s, an Indian schoolteacher walked into forbidden Tibet dressed as a pilgrim, secretly counting every step on a string of 100 prayer beads.

The short answer

India has a long history of spies, going back more than 2,000 years. The Arthashastra, a book about statecraft linked to Chanakya, describes networks of secret agents watching the kingdom and its enemies. Later rulers, from the Mughals to Tipu Sultan, kept their own news-gatherers and messengers.

Some of the most famous Indian spies lived in the last 200 years. A group of Himalayan explorers mapped Tibet in disguise. Noor Inayat Khan, an Indian-origin woman, worked as a secret radio operator in Paris during the Second World War. Teenagers joined spy units in the struggle for independence. Most of the stories of modern spies are still secret.

Surprising but true

The Arthashastra, a book on running a kingdom over 2,000 years old, advises kings to send spies disguised as monks, merchants and students.

A spy manual from ancient India

The Arthashastra gives kings very detailed advice. It describes spies disguised as wandering monks, traders, students, farmers and even fortune-tellers. Some were to stay in one place for years, and others were to travel and report back.

The book also talks about using signals and hidden messages so that spies couldn't be caught with letters. Scholars argue over exactly when it was written and by whom. Many think it was put together over several centuries, with its earliest parts more than 2,000 years old.

The Pundits who mapped Tibet

In the 1800s, the British wanted maps of Tibet, but foreigners were not allowed in. So the Survey of India trained Himalayan men to go instead, disguised as traders or pilgrims. They were called the Pundits.

The most famous was Nain Singh Rawat, a schoolteacher from Kumaon. He trained himself to take steps of the same length, uphill or down. He counted them on a string of 100 beads, not the normal 108, and hid notes in a prayer wheel. In 1866 he reached Lhasa and worked out its height above sea level by checking the temperature at which water boiled. His careful counts helped put Tibet on British maps.

A princess with a radio

Noor Inayat Khan was born in Moscow in 1914 to an Indian father, a Sufi musician, and was a descendant of Tipu Sultan. She grew up in Paris and London, and wrote children's stories. During the Second World War she joined a secret British organisation that sent agents into German-held France.

In 1943 she became the first woman radio operator sent into occupied France. With other agents arrested, she kept sending messages to London alone for months, moving her radio from flat to flat. She was eventually betrayed and captured, and died in a prison camp in 1944. Britain awarded her its George Cross for bravery, and in 2020 she became the first woman of Indian origin honoured with a blue plaque in London.

A teenage spy in Burma

During the freedom struggle, Subhas Chandra Bose's Indian National Army fought the British in Southeast Asia. Saraswathi Rajamani, a girl from a Tamil family in Burma, joined its intelligence wing at about 16. She cut her hair short and dressed as a boy named Mani to collect information from British camps. In one famous episode, she went back in disguise to help free a captured fellow agent.

Spies today

India's Intelligence Bureau traces its roots back to 1887, which makes it one of the oldest intelligence services in the world. In 1968, India set up the Research and Analysis Wing (RAW) to gather information from abroad. Its founding chief, R. N. Kao, was famously private and rarely photographed.

Modern spies use satellites, computers and code-breaking as well as people. And the strangest part? One of the biggest spy victories of the Second World War was won by puzzle-solvers who cracked the Enigma code, and no one was allowed to talk about it for 30 years.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Spies are mostly people who fight and chase like in films.

Truth: Most real spying is quiet, patient work, such as watching, listening, remembering, counting and passing on information without being noticed. Being forgettable is a spy's best skill.

Try this

Measure your normal step by walking 10 steps and measuring the distance with a tape, then dividing by 10. Now walk across your school ground or a park with an adult and count your steps. Work out how far you walked, the way the Pundits mapped Tibet.
