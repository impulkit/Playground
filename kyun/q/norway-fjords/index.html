What are fjords?

Norway's longest fjord reaches over 200 km inland, and in places it is more than a kilometre deep.

The short answer

A fjord (say "fee-ORD") is a long, narrow arm of the sea that winds far inland between steep mountain walls. Fjords were carved by glaciers during the ice ages. As the ice melted and the sea level rose, seawater flowed into the deep valleys the ice had left behind.

Norway's west coast is the most famous fjord coast on Earth. The word "fjord" itself is Norwegian. Its fjords can be more than a kilometre deep, with waterfalls pouring off cliffs hundreds of metres high and tiny farms clinging to ledges above the water. Fjords also exist in Greenland, Alaska, Canada, Chile and New Zealand, all places that were covered by thick ice.

Surprising but true

Many fjords are deepest far inland and shallow at the mouth, because the glaciers that carved them dug hardest where the ice was thickest.

How ice digs a fjord

During the last ice age, a sheet of ice up to about 3 kilometres thick covered Scandinavia. The ice flowed slowly towards the sea, squeezing into existing river valleys. As it moved, it dragged rocks frozen into its base, which scraped and ground the valley floor like giant sandpaper.

River valleys are usually V-shaped. Glaciers turned them into deep U-shapes with flat floors and steep sides. The ice dug deepest where it was thickest, far inland, so many fjords are deeper inside than at their mouths. Near the sea there is often a shallow underwater ridge called a sill.

When the ice melted about 10,000 years ago, the sea rushed in. The land, freed from the weight of the ice, is still rising slowly, by a few millimetres a year in some places.

The king of fjords

The Sognefjord is Norway's longest fjord, stretching about 205 kilometres inland from the coast north of Bergen. That is longer than the drive from Mumbai to Nashik. Its deepest point is about 1,300 metres below the surface.

One of its narrow side branches, the Nærøyfjord, is only about 250 metres wide in places. Together with the Geirangerfjord, famous for the Seven Sisters waterfalls, it became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2005. Because there are so many fjords and islands, Norway's coastline, if you followed every bend, would be about 100,000 kilometres long, enough to go around the Earth two and a half times.

Living on a fjord

For centuries, the easiest way to travel in western Norway was by boat. Steep mountains blocked roads, but fjords made water highways. The Vikings built their longships in sheltered fjords and sailed out from them to trade and raid across Europe.

Today, car ferries still cross many fjords. Some of them, on routes such as the Sognefjord, run on batteries. Engineers have also drilled long road tunnels through the mountains. The Lærdal Tunnel, opened in 2000, is about 24.5 kilometres long, the longest road tunnel in the world. Drivers pass through three large caves lit in blue and yellow, designed to keep them alert.

Warm water in the far north

Norway reaches far north of the Arctic Circle, yet its fjords and harbours mostly stay free of ice in winter. The reason is the North Atlantic Current, a branch of the Gulf Stream, which carries warm water from the tropics up the coast. These ocean currents make Norway's coast much milder than places at the same latitude in Canada or Siberia.

In winter in the far north, the sun doesn't rise for weeks, but the dark skies are often lit by the green curtains of the northern lights. In summer the opposite happens, and the midnight sun shines all night.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Fjords were carved by rivers.

Truth: Rivers usually made the first valleys, but glaciers turned them into fjords. Huge tongues of ice ground the valleys much deeper and wider, into a U shape, and the sea flooded in when the ice melted.

Try this

Fill a long baking tray with damp sand and shape a steep V-shaped river valley through it. Press an ice cube or a rounded spoon along the valley again and again, digging deeper. Notice how the V turns into a U. Then gently pour in water at one end to "flood" your fjord.
