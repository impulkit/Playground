How do we know what ancient worlds looked like?

Rocks are diaries. You just need to know how to read them.

The short answer

Each layer of rock holds clues about what a place was like when that layer formed. Wavy ripple marks mean water once flowed there. A layer of coal means a swampy forest. Sea shells mean an ocean.

Geologists read these clues the way detectives read a crime scene. Put enough of them together, from rocks, fossils, ancient mud and even old ice, and a lost world appears: its coastline, its weather, even what grew there.

Surprising but true

Shaligram stones, kept in many Hindu homes, are often fossil ammonites (ancient sea animals) from high in the Himalayas.

Reading the layers

Rock that forms from mud and sand is laid down in layers, one on top of another, like a stack of newspapers piling up day by day. In 1669, a Danish scientist named Nicolas Steno wrote down the simple rule behind this. Unless something has turned them over, the bottom layers are the oldest and the top ones the youngest.

Each layer also records how it was made. Sand with sloping lines inside it often comes from desert dunes. Cracked mud means a puddle dried in the sun. Rounded pebbles mean a fast river. The walls of the Grand Canyon show layer after layer of these changing worlds, from shallow seas to sandy deserts.

Shells on mountains

Some of the best clues are fossils. Ammonites were sea animals with coiled shells, related to today's octopus and squid. So when ammonites turn up high in the Himalayas, at places like Langza village in Spiti, they prove those rocks were once the floor of an ocean. That ocean was squeezed and lifted up when India crashed into Asia.

Coral works the same way. Corals grow only in warm, clear, shallow seas. A band of fossil coral in a cold country means that land was once tropical sea.

Leaves as thermometers

Plants are good at recording weather. In warm, wet places, many trees have leaves with smooth edges. In cooler places, more trees have jagged, toothed edges. The American scientist Jack Wolfe used this pattern to work out the temperatures of ancient forests from fossil leaves alone.

Antarctica has coal and fossil leaves of a plant called Glossopteris. So does India. That is one of the clues that the two were once joined, and that India drifted across the planet. The coal itself is squashed ancient plants, as you can read in how coal and oil were made.

Stone trees of Tiruvakkarai

Near Puducherry, in Tamil Nadu, lie fallen tree trunks that have turned entirely to stone. Over millions of years, minerals in groundwater replaced the wood, cell by cell, keeping the shape of the rings and bark. The Geological Survey of India protects them at the National Fossil Wood Park in Tiruvakkarai. They show that a great forest once stood where farms are today.

Ice and mud that remember

Some diaries are written in ice. In Antarctica, snow has piled up for hundreds of thousands of years, squashing into layers of ice. Scientists drill out long cylinders called ice cores. Tiny bubbles inside hold real samples of ancient air. The longest record studied so far goes back about 800,000 years and shows ice ages coming and going. In 2025, a European team drilled ice more than 1.2 million years old, which scientists are now studying.

Lake mud keeps a record too. Pollen grains settle to the bottom each year, showing which plants grew nearby. Mud from old lakebeds in the Sahara is full of pollen from grasses and trees. Ancient rock paintings there show giraffes, cattle and swimmers, in a place that is now sand.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Sahara has always been a desert.

Truth: Between roughly 11,000 and 5,000 years ago, much of the Sahara was green, with grassland, lakes, hippos and crocodiles. Rock paintings and lake mud from that time show it. Shifts in Earth's orbit slowly weakened the rains, and the desert returned.

Try this

Be a leaf-shape detective. Collect 20 fallen leaves from different kinds of trees near your home or school. Sort them into two piles, smooth edges and jagged or toothed edges, and count each. Scientists use the share of smooth-edged leaves in fossil collections to estimate how warm an ancient forest was.
