How did the Giant's Causeway form?

On the coast of Northern Ireland, about 40,000 stone columns fit together like a honeycomb, and most of them have six sides.

The short answer

The Giant's Causeway formed about 50 to 60 million years ago, when lava flooded the area that is now Northern Ireland. A thick sheet of this hot liquid rock settled in a low basin and cooled slowly. As it cooled, it shrank and cracked into tall columns, most of them with six sides.

Over millions of years, the sea wore away the land, uncovering the columns. Today about 40,000 of them stand along the shore like stepping stones. Some are taller than a two-storey house. The rock is basalt, the same dark volcanic stone that makes up the Deccan plateau in India.

Surprising but true

India has its own giant's causeway. The St. Mary's Islands near Udupi in Karnataka are made of six-sided rock columns about 88 million years old.

A land on fire

Around 60 million years ago, Europe and North America were slowly pulling apart as the Atlantic Ocean opened. Huge cracks split the Earth's crust. Lava welled up through them, not in tall cone volcanoes, but in floods that spread across the land.

Layer after layer piled up, some of them tens of metres thick. Between eruptions, soil formed and plants grew, and then fresh lava would pour over them. You can still see these red soil layers in the cliffs along the Causeway Coast.

Why six sides?

When lava cools, it shrinks. In a thick, still pool, it cools from the top and bottom inwards. As it shrinks, it cracks. Cracks tend to meet at angles of about 120 degrees, because that releases the stress most evenly. And shapes with 120-degree corners are hexagons.

The same thing happens in drying mud and in cornflour paste. The cracks grow slowly down through the cooling lava, building long columns. The slower the cooling, the bigger and more regular the columns. Bees build hexagons for a different reason, but the shape works because it fits together with no gaps, like a tessellation.

Finn McCool and the Scottish giant

Irish legend tells a different story. The giant Finn McCool, or Fionn mac Cumhaill, wanted to fight a Scottish giant called Benandonner. So he built a stone causeway across the sea to Scotland.

In one version, Finn saw how huge Benandonner was and ran home. His clever wife dressed him as a baby. When Benandonner arrived and saw the "baby", he thought, "If the baby is that big, how big is the father?" He fled back to Scotland, tearing up the causeway behind him. Only the two ends were left.

The legend has a geological twist. Across the sea, on the Scottish island of Staffa, Fingal's Cave is made of the same kind of basalt columns from the same lava flows.

The stones today

In 1986, UNESCO named the Giant's Causeway a World Heritage Site. Hundreds of thousands of people visit each year. Some columns have nicknames: the Wishing Chair, the Giant's Boot and the Organ, a row of tall columns that look like pipes.

Visitors used to take pieces home as souvenirs, so now the site is protected by the National Trust. You can still climb on the lower columns, but people are asked to leave the stones where they lie.

India's own causeway

Off the coast near Udupi in Karnataka lie the St. Mary's Islands. They are made of six-sided basalt columns too, but these are about 88 million years old. They formed when Madagascar and India split apart, and lava welled up along the crack.

The Geological Survey of India has made the islands a National Geological Monument. About 20 million years later, India drifted north over a hot spot in the Earth. It poured out the lava that built the Deccan plateau, an outpouring so vast it may have helped finish off the dinosaurs.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Giant's Causeway was built by giants or carved by people.

Truth: The columns are natural. They formed when a thick layer of lava cooled slowly and cracked, around 50 to 60 million years ago. The giant Finn McCool is a much-loved legend.

Try this

Mix cornflour and water into a thick paste about 2 cm deep in a dish. Leave it in a warm, dry place for a few days. As it dries, it cracks into columns with five, six or seven sides, much like the Giant's Causeway.
