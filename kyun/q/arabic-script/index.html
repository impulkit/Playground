Why is Arabic written right to left?

Arabic words run from right to left across the page, but numbers in the same sentence are read from left to right.

The short answer

Arabic is written right to left because it inherited that direction from older scripts of the Middle East. The Phoenician alphabet, the Aramaic script and the Hebrew script were all written right to left. Arabic grew out of a form of Aramaic used by the Nabataeans, the people who built the rock city of Petra in Jordan, and kept the same direction.

Why those first scripts ran right to left is less certain. One popular idea is that early writers carved stone with a chisel in the left hand and a hammer in the right, which made working leftwards natural. Others point to writing on clay or with ink. Nobody has proved any of these, and most experts say we simply don't know.

Surprising but true

The digits we call "Arabic numerals", 0 to 9, were first developed in India. Europeans learned them from Arab mathematicians, so they gave them the wrong name.

A family of directions

Scripts have not always picked a side. The Phoenicians, who lived on the coast of today's Lebanon about 3,000 years ago, wrote right to left. The early Greeks borrowed their letters and at first wrote right to left too. Sometimes they went back and forth, turning at the end of each line like an ox ploughing a field. Later the Greeks settled on left to right, and Latin and English followed them.

Arabic, Hebrew and Persian kept the older direction. Chinese and Japanese were traditionally written top to bottom. Indian scripts like Devanagari and Tamil run left to right.

Letters that hold hands

The Arabic alphabet has 28 letters. Most join to the letters next to them, so a word looks like one flowing line. Because of this, a letter can change shape depending on whether it comes at the start, middle or end of a word, or stands alone.

Arabic is mostly an abjad, a kind of writing that shows consonants but not every vowel. Short vowels can be added as small marks above or below the letters, as in the Quran and in children's schoolbooks. Everyday writing usually leaves them out, and readers fill them in from context, the way "txt msgs" can still be read.

One script, many languages

The Arabic script spread with Islam and trade, from the 600s onwards, across the Middle East, North Africa and Central and South Asia. Many languages adopted it and added letters for sounds Arabic does not have. Persian, Pashto, Kashmiri and Sindhi all use forms of it.

In India and Pakistan, Urdu is written in a flowing style called Nastaliq, which slopes downward from right to left. Arabic itself became one of the official languages of the United Nations in 1973. Turkish was written in Arabic script until 1928, when Turkey switched to Latin letters. After the Latin alphabet, the Arabic script is used by more of the world's countries than any other.

Art made from words

Because Islamic tradition avoided pictures of people in religious places, calligraphy became one of its greatest arts. Verses were carved in stone, painted on tiles and woven into carpets. Styles range from the square, angular Kufic to the rounded Naskh used in most printed books.

You can see Arabic calligraphy across India too. The Taj Mahal's great gateway and walls are framed with verses from the Quran, inlaid in black stone. The lettering was designed to look the same size from the ground all the way up, so the letters near the top are larger.

Numbers that travelled backwards

The digits 0 to 9 began in India, where mathematicians were using a place-value system with zero by about 1,400 years ago. Arab and Persian scholars learned them, and al-Khwarizmi of Baghdad wrote a famous book about them around 825 CE. Europeans then learned the digits from Arabic books and called them Arabic numerals.

Many Arab countries today use a different set of digit shapes, sometimes called Eastern Arabic numerals. But in both systems, numbers are still written with the biggest place first, on the left.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The numbers 0 to 9 were invented by Arabs.

Truth: The digits and the idea of a zero placeholder were developed in India. Arab and Persian mathematicians such as al-Khwarizmi wrote about them, and Europeans learned them from Arabic books, so they called them Arabic numerals.

Try this

Write your name from right to left on a sheet of paper, keeping each letter the right way round. Notice which hand gets smudged with ink or pencil. Then try it with your other hand and see which direction feels easier.
