What are Phad paintings?

In Rajasthan, a painting can be a temple you roll up and carry, unrolled at night so a singer can perform a whole epic in front of it.

The short answer

A phad is a long painting on cloth from Rajasthan that tells the story of a folk hero. The most famous are about Pabuji, a warrior who is said to have brought camels to Rajasthan, and Devnarayan, a hero honoured by many herding families. Some phads stretch several metres, and the biggest Devnarayan scrolls are around 9 m, longer than three autorickshaws parked nose to tail.

A phad isn't made just to be looked at. At night, in a village, a pair of singer-priests called a bhopa and bhopi unroll it between two poles. They sing, dance and act out the hero's story, pointing at each scene with a lamp. A performance can last all night, and sometimes many nights.

Surprising but true

When an old phad wears out, it is not thrown away. It is often "cooled" by being gently sunk in a holy lake, such as at Pushkar.

A comic book from the past

A phad is crowded with people, horses, camels, palaces and battles. Scenes flow into each other without borders, a bit like a giant comic with no panels. The hero is painted biggest, usually near the middle. Less important people get smaller and smaller.

Faces are almost always shown from the side, with big almond-shaped eyes. The colours are bright and flat: yellow, orange, red, green, brown and blue, with bold black outlines. There is little shading, so the figures look like they are cut from coloured paper.

Paint from stones and plants

Traditionally, phad painters made their own colours from minerals and plants. Yellow from a stone, red from earth or other minerals, green from copper-based colours, and black from lamp soot. Colours were ground into powder and mixed with plant gum and water.

The cloth is hand-woven cotton, soaked in rice or wheat starch and polished smooth with a stone, so the paint doesn't soak in. Each pigment is applied in a fixed order, one colour across the whole cloth before moving to the next. The bold black outlines usually go on at the very end.

The family that paints

For generations, phads were painted mainly by the Joshi family of Bhilwara and Shahpura. The bhopas, who were mostly from herding communities, ordered them and paid with money or grain. The painter started work on an auspicious day and often finished by painting the hero's eyes. That moment brought the phad to life.

In the 20th century, Shree Lal Joshi became famous for phad painting and trained many students, including people outside his family. He received the Padma Shri, one of India's highest civilian honours, in 2006. Members of the family now teach workshops, and women paint phads too.

A temple on a roll

For the people who watch, a phad is a moving temple. The gods painted on it are believed to be present when it is unrolled. Families invite the bhopas when someone is ill, when animals are sick, or to give thanks.

The bhopa plays the ravanhattha, a bowed string instrument made with a coconut shell, said to be one of the oldest bowed instruments in India. The songs are long, in a local language, with spoken parts and jokes.

When a phad becomes too worn to use, it is retired with care. It may be "cooled" by being placed in water, often at the holy lake of Pushkar.

New stories on old cloth

Today phad painters also paint the Ramayana, the life of the Buddha, stories of Gandhi, and even messages about clean water or girls' education. Tourists buy small phads as souvenirs.

Fewer bhopas perform now, because television and phones fill village evenings. But some still unroll their scrolls at fairs. And Pabuji's tale has a memorable link to animals: in the story he brings the first camels to Rajasthan, a gift for his niece's wedding.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Phad paintings are just decorations meant to hang on walls.

Truth: Traditionally, a phad is a portable shrine. It is unrolled for night-long performances in villages, where a bhopa and bhopi sing and dance the story. Smaller phad-style paintings for walls and gifts came much later.

Try this

Make a mini phad. Take a long strip of paper, like a few A4 sheets taped side by side. Draw a story you know, scene by scene, from left to right, making your hero bigger than everyone else. Then roll it up, unroll it bit by bit and tell the story to your family.
