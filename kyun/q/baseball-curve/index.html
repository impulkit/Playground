How does a baseball curve in the air?

Spin makes the air push the ball sideways, the same trick spin bowlers use.

The short answer

When a ball spins, it drags a thin layer of air around with it. On one side, the spinning surface moves with the oncoming air; on the other side, it moves against it. That makes the air flow unevenly, and the ball gets pushed sideways. This is called the Magnus effect.

A curveball spins with topspin, so the push is mostly downward. It dives as it reaches the batter. Pitchers can tilt the spin to make the ball break down and sideways at the same time. The spin itself is about 2,500 turns a minute, so the ball rotates around 15 to 20 times on its short trip to the plate.

Surprising but true

Some people once thought the curveball was an optical illusion. Experiments with wind tunnels and cameras proved the ball really does curve.

Pitcher vs batter

The pitcher stands 18.4 m from home plate. A fast pitch reaches the batter in under half a second, so batters must decide whether to swing almost before the ball leaves the hand.

Here is the speed maths. At 150 km/h, a ball travels about 42 metres every second. The pitcher lets go a little in front of the mound, so the ball covers about 17 metres, which takes roughly 0.4 seconds. A batter needs about a quarter of a second just to swing. That leaves only a split second to judge the pitch.

A curve that dips late is very hard to hit. Curveballs are slower, often around 120 to 130 km/h, and during their flight they can drop many centimetres more than a ball with no spin.

Spin, air and gravity

Every pitch falls because of gravity. Spin changes how much. Backspin on a fastball makes the air push upward, so it falls less. Topspin on a curveball pushes downward, so it falls more.

The air matters too. In thin mountain air, such as at the baseball stadium in Denver, Colorado, about 1.6 km above sea level, curveballs break less, because there is less air to push them. The same physics that lifts an aeroplane wing is at work on a ball the size of an orange.

Is it real?

For years, some people argued that curveballs only looked as if they curved. In 1959, the American physicist Lyman Briggs tested spinning baseballs in a wind tunnel and measured the sideways push. Later, high-speed cameras and radar tracked thousands of pitches. The ball really does curve, and its path can be predicted from its speed and spin.

Today every pitch in a top-level game is tracked by cameras and radar. Teams know the spin rate of every pitch a pitcher throws.

The ball with no spin

The strangest pitch of all is the knuckleball. The pitcher pushes it out with the fingertips so that it barely spins at all. With almost no spin, small changes in the air around the seams make it flutter and wobble unpredictably. Even the catcher often does not know where it will go.

Cricket's version

Cricket spin bowlers use the same physics. A ball spinning sideways can drift in the air before it even bounces.

There is one big difference. In cricket, the ball usually bounces before reaching the batter, and the spin grips the pitch to change direction again. In baseball, the ball must reach the batter on the full, so all the movement happens in the air.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A good fastball really rises as it reaches the batter.

Truth: No pitch thrown overarm can rise against gravity at these speeds. A fastball has strong backspin, so it drops less than the batter expects. To the batter, that looks like it jumped upward.

Try this

Throw a light plastic ball or a beach ball to a friend, first with no spin, then flicking your fingers down the front of the ball to give it topspin, then down the side for sidespin. Watch how each throw bends. Light balls curve much more, so the effect is easy to see.
