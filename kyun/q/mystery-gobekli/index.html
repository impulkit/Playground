Who built Gobekli Tepe?

About 11,500 years ago, people who had no farms, no metal and no writing carved giant stone pillars covered in foxes, snakes and vultures.

The short answer

Gobekli Tepe was built by hunter-gatherers, people who lived by hunting wild animals and gathering wild plants. They lived in what is now south-eastern Türkiye about 11,500 years ago, and they had no metal, no wheel and no writing.

Even so, they dug round enclosures and set up huge T-shaped stone pillars, some about 5.5 m tall and weighing several tonnes. They carved them with foxes, boars, snakes, scorpions and vultures. Gobekli Tepe is older than Stonehenge by about 6,000 years. Nobody knows its original name. Göbekli Tepe is Turkish for "Hill of the Navel" or "Potbelly Hill".

Surprising but true

Gobekli Tepe is about 6,000 years older than Stonehenge and about 7,000 years older than the Great Pyramid of Giza.

A hill that hid a secret

In the 1960s, researchers surveying the area noticed broken pieces of stone on the hill and thought it was a medieval graveyard. Farmers ploughed around big stones for years.

In 1994, the German archaeologist Klaus Schmidt looked again and realised the stones were much older. From 1995, his team began to dig. They found circle after circle of pillars, buried under soil. Radar scans show there are more enclosures still underground. Only a small part has been dug.

Pillars that are people

Look closely at the biggest pillars and you see arms carved down their sides, with long fingers meeting over a belt. Some have a fox-skin loincloth. The T-shape is a head and body. These are figures, perhaps gods, ancestors or spirits, standing in a ring.

The animals are mostly dangerous ones: snakes, scorpions, wild boars, big cats and vultures. One famous pillar, called the Vulture Stone, shows a vulture with a round object above its wing. Some researchers say it records a comet or the sky. Most archaeologists are not convinced.

Temple or village

Klaus Schmidt thought Gobekli Tepe was a temple, a place people came to for feasts and ceremonies but didn't live in. He called it "the first temple". He believed hunters gathered from far away to build it, and feasted on gazelle meat and something like beer as they worked.

Since Schmidt died in 2014, other archaeologists have kept digging. They found water channels, rainwater cisterns, houses and huge numbers of grinding stones. Now many think people did live there, at least part of the time. It may have been a village with special buildings, a bit like a town with a big temple at its heart.

Either way, the work needed hundreds of people to plan, quarry, carve and move stones. Something brought them together.

Did a temple lead to farms

Here is the big idea. Wild wheat grew on hills nearby, and genetic studies show that one of the earliest kinds of farmed wheat came from this region. Some archaeologists think the need to feed crowds of builders pushed people to plant and look after wild grains. If so, building Gobekli Tepe may have helped kick-start farming.

Other experts think farming and big buildings grew up together, each pushing the other. Either way, Gobekli Tepe turned an old belief upside down. People used to think farming came first, then villages, then temples. Here, the giant stones came very early.

Around 8000 BCE, the site was given up, and its enclosures filled with soil. Nearby, a similar site called Karahan Tepe is now being dug. Its carvings include human heads and snakes, and it may be just as old. On the Gobekli pillars, vultures appear again and again. These birds still soar over the region, and they were once common in India too, where vultures have played their own part in burials and beliefs.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Gobekli Tepe is so old and so big that it must have been built by a lost advanced civilisation.

Truth: There is no evidence of any lost civilisation. Tools, food waste and bones found at the site show it was built by local hunter-gatherers using stone tools, rope and a lot of people working together.

Try this

Make a mini pillar from a bar of soap or a block of clay. Carve an animal on it using only a blunt pencil or a lolly stick, never a knife. Notice how long a small picture takes, then think about carving a 5-metre stone with a sharp rock.
