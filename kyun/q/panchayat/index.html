What is a panchayat?

India has more elected village leaders than the population of many countries, and almost half of them are women.

The short answer

A panchayat is an elected council that runs local affairs in rural India. The word comes from panch, meaning five, because village disputes were once settled by a group of five respected elders.

Today panchayats are part of India's democracy, written into the Constitution since 1992. Villagers elect them every five years. They look after things close to home: village roads, drains, water supply, street lights, local schools and some welfare schemes. Their head is usually called the sarpanch or pradhan.

Surprising but true

By law, at least one in three seats in every panchayat must go to women, and many states have raised that to one in two.

Three steps from village to district

India's panchayat system has three tiers. The gram panchayat looks after a village or a group of small villages. Above it, the panchayat samiti covers a block, which is a cluster of villages. At the top, the zila parishad covers a whole district.

There are roughly 2.5 lakh gram panchayats. Together, the three tiers have more than 30 lakh elected members, which makes this one of the largest systems of local democracy anywhere.

The gram sabha

Every gram panchayat answers to a gram sabha, which is a meeting of all the adult voters in the village. The gram sabha can question the panchayat about how money was spent and help choose who should benefit from government schemes. In many villages, though, attendance is low and meetings can be dominated by a few powerful families. Making the gram sabha work well is still a challenge.

From an old idea to the Constitution

Mahatma Gandhi believed India's freedom should start in its villages. He spoke of gram swaraj, or village self-rule, where each village would manage most of its own needs.

After independence, the Constitution only asked states to encourage panchayats, so their strength varied a lot. On 2 October 1959, Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru launched a panchayati raj system in Nagaur district, Rajasthan. Still, many states held panchayat elections rarely or not at all.

That changed with the 73rd Amendment, which came into force on 24 April 1993. It made elections every five years compulsory, set up state election commissions to run them, and reserved seats for women and for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes. India now marks 24 April as National Panchayati Raj Day.

Women in charge

Reserving seats for women brought lakhs of them into local government, many for the first time. Studies have found that women leaders often push for things like drinking water and village roads.

There are problems too. In some places, a husband or relative quietly runs the panchayat while the elected woman is leader only in name. This practice is widely criticised, and several states have run training and awareness campaigns to stop it.

Why local matters

A minister in Delhi cannot know which handpump in a village has broken. A panchayat member who lives down the road probably does. Panchayats also get money to spend. Funds come from the central and state governments, and some panchayats collect small local taxes, such as on houses or markets. With it they might build a new drain, repair a school roof or pay workers under rural job schemes. Villagers can ask to see the accounts at the gram sabha. That is the main idea behind panchayats: decisions are often better when they are made close to the people they affect. Cities have their own version, called municipalities. Myth or fact?

Myth: Village councils in India are only for older men.

Truth: The Constitution reserves at least a third of panchayat seats, and a third of the head posts, for women. Seats are also reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, so the council reflects the whole village.

Try this

Hold a "gram sabha" at home about one shared decision, like a weekend plan. Elect a sarpanch to run the meeting, give everyone a turn to speak, and then vote. Afterwards, talk about whether the sarpanch listened to everyone equally.
