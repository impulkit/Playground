What is a paradox?

In a class of just 23 children, it's more likely than not that two share a birthday. Most people refuse to believe it until they check.

The short answer

A paradox is an argument that starts from ideas that seem obviously true and arrives at something that seems obviously false or impossible. It feels like a trick, and sometimes it is. Other times, the strange answer turns out to be correct and it's our instinct that was wrong.

Paradoxes come in a few kinds. Some are puzzles about words and logic. Some are about infinity. Some are about chance, where the true answer just feels wrong. Mathematicians love them because each one exposes a weak spot in how we think.

Surprising but true

When a magazine columnist explained the Monty Hall puzzle correctly in 1990, thousands of readers wrote in to say she was wrong, including people with maths doctorates.

Can Achilles catch a tortoise?

Around 450 BCE, the Greek thinker Zeno of Elea told a story. Achilles, the fastest runner, races a tortoise that has a head start. By the time Achilles reaches where the tortoise was, it has crept a little further. By the time he reaches that point, it has moved again. This goes on forever, so how can he ever pass it?

Of course runners do overtake. The answer came much later, with the maths of limits. An endless list of ever-smaller steps can add up to a finite distance. Half a metre, plus a quarter, plus an eighth, and so on, never goes past one metre.

Infinity causes other headaches. The German mathematician David Hilbert described a hotel with endless rooms, every one of them full. A new guest arrives. No problem: everyone moves from room n to room n + 1, and room 1 is free. Endless sets simply don't behave like ordinary ones.

Sentences that bite their own tail

Consider the sentence "This sentence is false." If it's true, then it's false. If it's false, then it's true. This is the liar paradox, and philosophers have argued about it since ancient Greek times.

In 1901, the British thinker Bertrand Russell found a version inside maths itself. A simple form goes like this. A village barber shaves everyone who does not shave themselves. Who shaves the barber? Either answer contradicts the rule. Russell's discovery forced mathematicians to rebuild the rules of sets more carefully.

Why do optical illusions fool us?Your eyes don't send your brain a photograph. They send clues, and your brain guesses the rest. Illusions catch it guessing wrong.

When the right answer feels wrong

Some paradoxes have perfectly clear answers. They just clash with our gut feeling. Take birthdays. With 23 people in a room, the chance that at least two share a birthday is just over 50%. With 70 people, it's above 99.9%.

It seems too high because we think about our own birthday. But the question is about any pair, and 23 people make 253 different pairs. That's a lot of chances for a match.

The Monty Hall puzzle is another. When the columnist Marilyn vos Savant explained in 1990 that switching doors wins two times in three, she was flooded with letters calling her wrong. Computer simulations and simple experiments with cups proved her right.

Puzzles about time and change

Paradoxes also turn up outside maths. In the old Ship of Theseus puzzle, a ship's planks are replaced one by one until none of the original wood is left. Is it the same ship? Science fiction loves the grandfather paradox: what happens if a time traveller stops their own grandparents from meeting?

Physics has real puzzles too. In Einstein's theory, a twin who flies fast through space comes back younger than the twin who stayed at home. It sounds like a paradox, but experiments with very precise clocks on fast planes and satellites show that time really does stretch. Sometimes the universe is simply stranger than common sense.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A paradox means maths or logic is broken.

Truth: Most paradoxes point to a hidden mistake, a sloppy definition or our poor sense of chance and infinity. Solving them has often led to new maths, such as limits and set theory.

Try this

Collect the birthdays (just day and month) of 23 or more people, such as your class, your cousins or a sports team. Write them in a list and look for a match. Try it with a few different groups. You'll find a shared birthday surprisingly often.
