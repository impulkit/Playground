How can computers copy voices?

Some AI programs can copy a person's voice from a recording just a few seconds long, then make it say anything at all.

The short answer

Every voice has a sound of its own, shaped by the size of your throat, your mouth and nose, your accent and how you speak. A voice-cloning program listens to recordings of a person and learns those patterns. Then it can make new speech in that voice, saying words the person never said.

Modern tools use AI that has already learned from thousands of hours of many people talking. So for a new voice, it may need only a short clip, sometimes just a few seconds, to copy the basic sound. Longer recordings make a better copy.

The same technology can give a voice back to someone who has lost theirs, or trick a grandparent into sending money to a scammer.

Surprising but true

In 1939, visitors to the New York World's Fair heard a machine called the Voder "talk", played by a trained operator using keys and a foot pedal like an organ.

From organ keys to AI

People have tried to make machines talk for centuries. In 1939, at the New York World's Fair, Bell Labs showed off the Voder. A trained operator pressed keys and a foot pedal to produce buzzes and hisses shaped into words. It sounded strange and ghostly, but people could understand it.

Later computer voices pieced together tiny recorded sounds, which is why the voices of early sat-navs and talking toys sounded jumpy. In the 2010s, neural networks started producing much smoother speech. Today the voice of a phone assistant can sound almost like a real person reading aloud.

How the copy is made

A cloning system first turns sound into a kind of picture of the voice, showing which pitches are loud and quiet over time. It studies how your voice rises and falls, how you say "s" and "r", and the little breaths between words.

The AI stores this as a set of numbers, a sort of voice fingerprint. Give it new text, and it builds speech one tiny slice at a time, matching that fingerprint. Some tools can even copy a voice in real time, changing your words into someone else's voice as you speak.

Your voice starts as a buzz from the vocal folds in your throat. The AI never sees your throat. It only copies the sound that comes out.

How does your voice work?Every word you speak starts as two small folds of tissue in your throat, slapping together hundreds of times a second.

Giving voices back

For people with illnesses that take away their speech, such as motor neurone disease, cloning can be a gift. Before their voice fades, they record themselves reading for a while, a process called voice banking. Later, a computer can speak for them in something close to their own voice, instead of a stranger's.

Film studios use voice cloning to dub films into other languages in an actor's own voice. Audiobook makers use it too. Some uses are controversial, such as recreating the voices of performers who have died, and many actors and singers now ask for contracts that control how their voices can be used.

The scam call problem

The darker side is fraud. Scammers take a short clip of someone from a video posted online, clone the voice, then phone a relative pretending there is an emergency: an accident, an arrest, a lost wallet abroad. They beg for money to be sent immediately. In 2023, the US Federal Trade Commission warned families about exactly this trick.

Fake voices have also been used to imitate politicians and company bosses. In 2024, US regulators ruled that robocalls using AI-generated voices were illegal without consent.

This is a close cousin of deepfake video, where faces are swapped as well as voices.

Staying safe

The best defence is simple. If a call asks for money or secrets in a hurry, stop and check. Hang up and call the person back on a number you already know, or ask a parent to. Many families agree on a code word that a real relative would know and a scammer would not, a little like a spoken password.

Be careful about posting long videos of yourself talking publicly, especially with your full name. And if something feels wrong, tell a trusted adult. Good online safety habits work against fake voices as well as fake messages.

Meanwhile, researchers are building detectors that look for tiny clues a cloned voice leaves behind, patterns too faint for human ears. Similar AI is also used to make music, and some programs can now compose a whole song and sing it too.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You can always tell a fake voice because it sounds robotic.

Truth: Old computer voices sounded flat and robotic, but modern clones can copy breathing, accents and emotion. Over a phone line, many people cannot tell the difference, which is why checking in other ways matters.

Try this

Record yourself saying the same sentence three ways, happy, bored and scared, using a phone. Play them back to a family member and see if they can guess which is which. Your real voice carries feelings in tiny changes of speed and pitch, and that is part of what AI tries to copy.
