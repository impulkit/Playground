How can scientists tell if a painting is fake?

A forger can copy a painting stroke for stroke. What they can't easily copy is time, and science can measure it.

The short answer

A good forger can copy every brushstroke of a famous painting. What they can't easily copy is time. Paint, canvas and wood change over the centuries in ways that scientists can measure.

Scientists who study and care for paintings are called conservators. They use X-rays, microscopes and chemistry to look at the hidden layers of a picture. If a painting claims to be 300 years old but contains a paint invented 80 years ago, the game is up.

These same tools also show how famous artists worked: the sketches under the paint, the changes of mind, and sometimes a whole different picture hidden underneath.

Surprising but true

Under some famous paintings, X-rays and infrared scans have found entirely different paintings that the artist painted over.

Seeing through paint

X-rays pass easily through canvas and most paints, but not through heavy pigments such as lead white. So an X-ray photo of a painting shows where the artist laid on thick, lead-rich paint, much as a hospital X-ray shows bones.

Infrared cameras see through the top layers to the charcoal sketch beneath. Under ultraviolet light, old varnish glows a milky green, while recent touch-ups look like dark patches.

Scans of Pablo Picasso's The Blue Room, made from 2008 onwards, revealed a portrait of a bearded man in a bow tie hidden underneath. Picasso was young and poor when he painted it in 1901, and canvas cost money. So he simply painted over an earlier picture.

Paint that's too new

Every pigment has a birthday. Prussian blue was first made in Berlin in the early 1700s. Titanium white went on sale as an artists' paint around the 1920s. Bright modern blues and greens called phthalocyanines arrived in the 1930s.

So scientists take a speck of paint, smaller than a grain of salt, and test what it is made of. A painting signed 1650 with titanium white in it cannot be genuine. This simple rule has caught many forgers, because they buy their paints in modern shops.

Old Indian paintings have a timeline too. The painters of Ajanta used earth colours and lapis lazuli, so a modern factory-made blue in a "Mughal" miniature is a warning sign.

Clocks inside atoms

Canvas is woven from flax or cotton, and panels are cut from trees. Both were once living plants. Living things take in a type of carbon, carbon-14, that slowly breaks down after they die. Measuring how much is left gives an age, using the same half-life trick that dates ancient bones.

There's a twist. Nuclear bomb tests in the 1950s and 1960s added extra carbon-14 to the air. Plants that grew after that carry a tell-tale "bomb peak". In 2019, scientists in Switzerland found it in threads from a painting that claimed to be from the 1800s, proving the canvas was woven a century later. Wooden panels can also be dated by counting their tree rings.

Looking after old paintings

Most of the time, art scientists aren't hunting fakes at all. They are working out how to protect paintings from light, damp and dust, and how to clean them without harm. Under a microscope, tiny cracks called craquelure show how paint has dried and shrunk over many years.

In India, labs at museums and at the Archaeological Survey of India use similar tools to care for wall paintings, palm-leaf manuscripts and miniatures. For the human side of the hunt, with its forgers, fooled experts and courtroom drama, read about how experts spot fake paintings.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Carbon dating tells you exactly when a painting was painted.

Truth: Carbon dating measures when the plants in the canvas or wood stopped growing, not when the paint went on. A forger can paint on an old canvas, so scientists combine carbon dating with tests of the paint itself.

Try this

Make a hidden picture. Draw a simple shape on white paper with a white wax crayon or a candle stub, pressing hard. Then brush watery paint over the whole sheet. The wax pushes the paint away and your secret drawing appears, a bit like the hidden layers that scientists reveal in old paintings.
