How does Morse code work?

SOS doesn't stand for anything. It was chosen because its dots and dashes are almost impossible to mistake.

The short answer

Morse code turns letters and numbers into patterns of short and long signals, called dots and dashes. A dot is a quick signal. A dash lasts three times as long. The letter A is dot-dash. B is dash-dot-dot-dot. SOS is dot-dot-dot, dash-dash-dash, dot-dot-dot.

The signals can be anything that can be switched on and off: electric pulses along a wire, beeps on a radio, flashes of light or taps on a pipe. Gaps matter too. A short gap separates the parts of a letter, a longer gap separates letters and a longer one still separates words.

With just two signals, Morse code can spell any message, a bit like the 0s and 1s of binary code that computers use today.

A painter who changed communication

Samuel Morse was an American painter. In 1825, while he was away painting a portrait, his wife became ill and died. The letter telling him took days to arrive, and he got home too late. Some historians think this helped spark his interest in faster ways to send news.

In the 1830s, Morse worked on an electric telegraph that sent pulses of electricity along a wire. With his partner Alfred Vail, he developed a code to go with it. On 24 May 1844, Morse sent a message on a new line from Washington to Baltimore. It was a line from the Bible: "What hath God wrought".

Within a few decades, telegraph wires crossed continents, and cables under the oceans linked them.

Short codes for common letters

Morse and Vail made a smart choice. Letters used often got short codes, and rare letters got long ones. E, the most common letter in English, is a single dot. T is a single dash. Q is dash-dash-dot-dash.

It is said that Vail worked out which letters were most common by counting the metal letters in a printer's type box. The more pieces of type a printer kept for a letter, the more often it was used.

This idea of giving common things short codes is still used in computers today, to squeeze files into less space.

The signal for help

Ships at sea used radio Morse code from the early 1900s. They needed a distress call that anyone would recognise. In 1906, an international meeting in Berlin chose SOS, and it came into use in 1908.

On the night of 15 April 1912, the radio operators on the sinking Titanic sent both the older call, CQD, and the newer SOS. Ships that heard them raced to help, and one, the Carpathia, rescued the survivors from the lifeboats.

Morse code in India

India's first experimental telegraph line opened in 1851 between Calcutta, now Kolkata, and Diamond Harbour, about 50 km away. Within a few years, lines linked Calcutta, Agra, Bombay, Madras and Peshawar. Telegrams carried news of births, deaths, exam results and jobs for generations.

As mobile phones and email took over, fewer people sent telegrams. India's state telegraph service sent its last messages on 14 July 2013, after 162 years. People queued at telegraph offices to send one final telegram as a keepsake.

Still in use

Morse code is no longer used by big ships, which switched to satellite systems in 1999. But amateur radio operators around the world still send and receive it for fun.

Pilots also hear it. Radio beacons near airports send their short identity codes in Morse, so pilots can check they are tuned to the right one. And because it needs only a switch, Morse has helped some people with disabilities communicate by blinking or tapping.

Myth or fact?

Myth: SOS stands for "Save Our Souls" or "Save Our Ship".

Truth: SOS doesn't stand for anything. It was chosen because the pattern of three dots, three dashes and three dots is simple, quick and hard to confuse with anything else. The phrases came later as ways to remember it.

Try this

With a friend, send messages using a torch or by tapping on a table. Use a short flash or tap for a dot and a long one for a dash, with a pause between letters. Start with SOS (three short, three long, three short), then try spelling your names.
