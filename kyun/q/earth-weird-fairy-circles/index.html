What makes fairy circles in deserts?

Across the Namib Desert, millions of bare circles dot the grass like polka dots on a giant tablecloth, and scientists have argued for decades about who or what makes them.

The short answer

Fairy circles are round patches of bare ground, usually 2 to 12 metres across, each ringed by taller grass. They are found by the million along the edge of the Namib Desert in southern Africa, and in the Pilbara region of Western Australia. Seen from a plane, they look like a giant spotty blanket.

No one is completely sure what makes them. One idea is that sand termites living underground eat the roots of grass, killing it in a circle and saving the water in the soil for themselves. The other is that the grass makes the pattern itself: in very dry soil, plants compete for water, and bare circles act like water stores for the grass around them. Scientists are still collecting evidence for both.

Surprising but true

Fairy circles can last for decades. One study estimated that an average circle lives for about 40 years before grass fills it in.

Polka dots from the air

In Namibia, fairy circles cover a strip of land about 2,000 km long, between the desert and the grasslands. They are spaced surprisingly evenly, each about the same distance from its neighbours, like the cells of a honeycomb. That regular spacing is one of the best clues about how they form.

The circles appear, grow and slowly fill in again. One study estimated that a typical circle lasts around 40 years, and big ones may last much longer. Local stories say the circles are footprints of gods, or places where a dragon breathed fire.

The termite team

In 2013, the scientist Norbert Juergens reported that a sand termite, Psammotermes allocerus, was found in nearly every fairy circle he studied. These termites live in the sand and eat grass roots.

His idea is that the termites create the bare patch. With no grass, rain soaks into the sand and isn't sucked up by plants. The circle becomes a hidden underground water store. Termites survive there through long droughts. He compared them to ecosystem engineers, like beavers that build dams.

The grass team

Other scientists, including Stephan Getzin, think the grass does it alone. In a dry land, each tuft of grass sends roots out to grab water. Where plants are thin, they keep winning water from nearby, leaving gaps. This kind of pattern was first worked out mathematically by Alan Turing, the codebreaker, who in 1952 showed how simple rules could make spots and stripes in nature.

In 2016, Getzin's team described fairy circles in Australia, near the town of Newman. There, they found no termite species that matched the Namibian pattern. To them, that showed the grass could do it on its own. Termite researchers replied that Australian termites might still be involved.

Maybe both

Some researchers think termites and water competition work together. Termites may start some circles, and water competition may keep them evenly spaced. Computer models that combine both ideas produce patterns that look a lot like the real thing.

In 2023, a big survey using satellite images and computer analysis reported fairy-circle-like patterns in hundreds of places across 15 countries. Other researchers questioned whether all of them were the real thing. Fieldwork is hard. The circles are in remote, hot places, and experiments take years because the circles change so slowly. Scientists dig trenches, measure soil water after rain and count termites, one square metre at a time.

Patterns all over the planet

Fairy circles are one of many self-made patterns in nature: stripes on zebras, ripples on sand dunes and the hexagons on a giraffe. Dry places can also grow bands of plants called tiger bush in parts of Africa and Australia. Somewhere between rain, roots and termites lies the answer. And in a desert in the USA, scientists solved a different mystery only after setting up cameras: rocks that seemed to move by themselves, called the sailing stones.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Fairy circles are made by aliens or by something mysterious nobody can study.

Truth: Fairy circles are measured, mapped and dug up by scientists every year. The disagreement is between two natural explanations, termites and plants competing for water, not between science and magic.

Try this

Make your own self-organising pattern. Sprinkle a thin, even layer of salt or sugar on a dark tray, then tap the side of the tray gently many times. Watch whether gaps and clumps appear. Patterns can form without anyone planning them, which is the idea behind one fairy circle theory.
