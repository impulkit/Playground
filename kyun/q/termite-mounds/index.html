How do termites build air-conditioned mounds?

Insects smaller than a grain of rice build towers taller than a person, and the towers breathe.

The short answer

Termite mounds work less like an air conditioner and more like a pair of lungs. A big termite colony, with up to millions of insects, uses up a lot of oxygen and makes a lot of carbon dioxide. The termites live mostly in a nest at the bottom, and the tall tower above it helps fresh air get in and stale air get out.

The trick depends on the Sun. During the day, the thin outer walls of the mound warm up faster than the thick middle, so air moves one way. At night, the outside cools first, and the air moves the other way. This daily swing keeps air flowing through the mound, a bit like breathing.

The walls are full of tiny holes, so gases can seep through without letting rain, predators or dry wind in.

Surprising but true

Some termites are farmers. They grow a fungus inside their nests, and in India that fungus sprouts from the mounds as edible mushrooms after the monsoon.

Built by blind workers

Most termite workers are blind and smaller than a grain of rice. They build with soil, mixed with spit and droppings, carrying it grain by grain. No termite has a plan of the whole mound. Each one follows simple rules, like dropping soil where others have dropped soil, and the shape grows from millions of tiny actions.

The results can be huge. In parts of Africa, mounds tower over 5 m, taller than a double-decker bus. If termites were as tall as people, their mounds would be taller than the world's highest skyscrapers.

Breathing mounds in India

For a long time, people believed termite mounds kept the nest at a perfectly steady temperature, like a building with air conditioning. Careful measurements showed it is more complicated. Some nests stay fairly steady, while others warm and cool with the seasons.

In 2015, scientists studied mounds of an Indian termite near Bengaluru. They placed sensors inside and found that the mounds have ridges, called flutes, which heat and cool faster than the centre. In the day, warm air rises through the flutes and sinks down the middle. At night, the flow turns around. The mounds were using the daily rhythm of heat and cold to breathe.

Fungus farmers

Many mound-building termites are farmers. Workers collect dead leaves, grass and wood, chew it and pack it into spongy combs in special chambers. A fungus grows on the combs, breaking down tough plant matter that the termites couldn't digest alone. The termites eat the fungus and the softened food.

After the first monsoon rains, this fungus sometimes pushes up through the mound as tall, pale mushrooms. In parts of India, from Kerala and Odisha to the northeast, people gather them as a seasonal treat.

Buildings that copy termites

In Harare, the capital of Zimbabwe, the architect Mick Pearce designed an office and shopping building called the Eastgate Centre, opened in 1996. Inspired by termite mounds, it uses thick walls, chimneys and night-time breezes to stay cool without ordinary air conditioning. It uses much less energy than similar buildings.

Scientists now know the real mounds work a little differently from the original idea, but the lesson still stands. Nature had worked out clever cooling long before the fridge.

Old mounds and old stories

In northeast Brazil, there are about 200 million termite mounds spread over an area about the size of Great Britain. Some are around 4,000 years old, built by termites digging out tunnels and piling up the soil.

In India, termite mounds appear in tradition too. The poet Valmiki, who is said to have composed the Ramayana, is often linked to the Sanskrit word valmika, meaning a termite mound. A popular story says he meditated so long that a mound grew over him. In parts of South India, people offer prayers at termite mounds, which are believed to be homes of serpent spirits, and snakes do often shelter in old mounds.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Termites are a kind of white ant.

Truth: Termites are often called white ants, but they aren't ants at all. Their closest relatives are cockroaches. Termites and ants just happen to have evolved similar busy colonies.

Try this

On a warm afternoon, stand in a closed room and hold one hand near the floor and the other as high as you can reach. If you have a thermometer, check both spots. The air up high is usually a little warmer, because warm air rises. Termite mounds use rising and sinking air like this to swap stale air for fresh.
