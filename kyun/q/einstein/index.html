What did Albert Einstein discover?

A young clerk in a Swiss patent office wrote four papers in one year that changed how we understand light, time and energy.

The short answer

Albert Einstein showed that space and time are not fixed. They can stretch and shrink depending on how fast you move and how strong gravity is. This is his theory of relativity. He also showed that mass and energy are two forms of the same thing, written as E = mc², and that light travels in tiny packets of energy.

These ideas sound strange, but you use them every day. Phone navigation, solar panels and camera sensors all depend on physics Einstein helped discover.

Surprising but true

The clocks on GPS satellites would drift by about 38 millionths of a second a day if engineers did not correct them using Einstein's ideas.

The patent clerk

Einstein was born in Ulm, Germany, in 1879. He did well in maths and science but disliked strict schools that made pupils memorise. After university in Zurich he could not find a teaching job, so in 1902 he became a clerk at the patent office in Bern, Switzerland, checking other people's inventions.

In his spare time he thought about physics. In 1905, at 26, he published four papers. One explained the photoelectric effect. Another gave strong evidence that atoms really exist by explaining how tiny grains jiggle in water. The third set out special relativity. The fourth, only three pages long, introduced E = mc².

Time that stretches

Special relativity begins with one rule: light always travels at the same speed, whoever measures it. Einstein worked out that if this is true, then moving clocks must tick more slowly. The faster you go, the bigger the effect.

In 1915 he went further with general relativity. He said gravity is not a pull but a bending of space and time around heavy objects. Clocks run slightly faster high above the Earth, where gravity is weaker. That is why GPS satellites, which are both fast and high, must correct their clocks every day.

Does time pass differently in space?Astronauts who spend a year on the space station come home a tiny bit younger than if they had stayed on Earth.

The eclipse that made him famous

General relativity predicted that the Sun's gravity would bend starlight passing near it. In 1919, British teams led by Arthur Eddington photographed stars during a total solar eclipse, from Brazil and an island off West Africa. The stars appeared slightly shifted, as Einstein had said. Newspapers around the world made him a celebrity overnight.

His theory also predicted black holes and ripples in space called gravitational waves. Scientists first detected those waves in 2015, a century later. It added to rather than replaced the work of Isaac Newton, whose laws still work well for everyday speeds.

A letter from Dhaka

In 1924, a young physicist in Dhaka, Satyendra Nath Bose, sent Einstein a paper on how particles of light can be counted. Einstein was so impressed that he translated it into German himself and got it published. Their combined ideas are called Bose–Einstein statistics, and one whole family of particles is called bosons, after Bose.

Einstein also met the poet Rabindranath Tagore near Berlin in 1930. Their conversation about whether truth exists without humans was printed and is still read today.

Leaving Germany

Einstein was Jewish, and when the Nazis took power in 1933 he left Germany for the United States. He worked at Princeton until his death in 1955. In 1939 he signed a letter warning President Roosevelt that Germany might build an atomic bomb. He did not work on the American bomb himself, and later said he regretted signing the letter. He spent his last years speaking out for peace.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Einstein failed maths at school.

Truth: He was excellent at maths and had taught himself calculus by about the age of 15. He did fail the general section of a university entrance exam when he was 16, two years younger than most applicants, which may be where the story began.

Try this

Stretch an old bedsheet tight with three or four people holding the edges. Put a heavy ball, like a melon or a cricket ball, in the middle. Now roll a marble across the sheet. It curves around the dip. Einstein said gravity works a bit like this, with heavy objects bending space itself.
