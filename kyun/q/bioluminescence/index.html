Why do some sea creatures glow?

On some nights, waves on Indian beaches break in electric blue, lit by billions of living things smaller than a grain of sand.

The short answer

Sea creatures glow by making light inside their bodies, a trick called bioluminescence. A chemical called luciferin reacts with oxygen, helped along by an enzyme, and the energy comes out as light instead of heat. It is the same basic idea that lights up fireflies on a summer evening.

In the dark ocean, light is a powerful tool. Animals glow to find food, to hide, to scare off attackers and to find mates. A 2017 study by scientists in California found that about three out of every four animals they filmed in the deep sea could make light. Glowing is not rare down there. It is normal.

Surprising but true

Some tiny sea creatures flash light when they are attacked, like a burglar alarm, to attract bigger animals that might eat their attacker.

A glowing toolbox

Different animals use light in different ways. The anglerfish dangles a glowing lure in front of its mouth, and small fish swim towards it. Some squid and fish have lights along their bellies that match the faint light coming from above. Seen from below, they blend in, as if they had no shadow at all. This trick is called counter-illumination.

Light can also be a weapon. The vampire squid can squirt out a cloud of glowing mucus to confuse a predator, then slip away into the dark. Other animals flash brightly when grabbed, a "burglar alarm" that may attract a larger hunter to eat the attacker.

Blue waves on the beach

You don't need a submarine to see bioluminescence. On some nights, tiny single-celled plankton called dinoflagellates fill coastal waters. Each one flashes when the water around it is disturbed. When a wave breaks or a person wades in, millions flash together and the water glows blue. Footprints in wet sand can sparkle, and a hand swept through the water leaves a trail like a comet. The glow fades within seconds, then flares again with the next splash.

In India, glowing waves have been seen on beaches in Goa, Karnataka, Kerala, Mumbai, Chennai and Lakshadweep. In Puerto Rico, Mosquito Bay on the island of Vieques is famous as one of the brightest glowing bays in the world. Its narrow mouth keeps the plankton trapped inside, and nearby mangroves feed them.

Why scientists care

Glowing sea animals have changed medicine. In the 1960s, the Japanese scientist Osamu Shimomura studied a glowing jellyfish from the coast of Washington state in the United States. He found a protein in it that glows green under blue light.

Other scientists later learned to attach this green protein to cells and genes, so they could see exactly where those parts were working inside living things. It became one of the most widely used tools in biology, helping researchers watch nerve cells grow and cancer cells spread. In 2008, Shimomura and two American scientists, Martin Chalfie and Roger Tsien, shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this work.

Lighting up the deep

Bioluminescence also helps explorers. The American scientist Edith Widder designed a camera that films using red light, which most deep-sea animals cannot see, so they behave normally. She also built an electronic lure that copies the glowing alarm of a jellyfish. In 2012, it helped a team film a living giant squid in its home waters off Japan for the first time.

Even on land, a few living things glow, including some mushrooms and fungi on rotting wood. But the ocean is where living light really rules. Far below the reach of the Sun, nearly all the light there is comes from animals.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Glowing waves are a sign that the sea is clean and healthy.

Truth: Glowing waves are often caused by huge blooms of plankton. Big blooms can be fed by nutrients washing in from farms and sewage, and can use up oxygen that fish need. They are beautiful, but not always good news.

Try this

In a dark room, shine a torch through a clear glass of water, then add a few drops of milk and shine it again. Notice how the light spreads and glows in the cloudy water. Divers see something similar when glowing plankton light up the water around them.
