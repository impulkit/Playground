Who was B.R. Ambedkar?

As a boy he wasn't allowed to drink from the school tap. As a man he led the team that wrote India's Constitution.

The short answer

Bhimrao Ramji Ambedkar, often called Babasaheb, was a lawyer, economist, writer and political leader. He is best known for leading the committee that drafted India's Constitution, the rulebook the whole country runs on.

He was born into a community that the caste system treated as "untouchable". People from his community were kept out of temples, wells and many schools. Ambedkar spent his life fighting this unfairness. He wanted an India where every person had the same rights, whatever family they were born into.

Surprising but true

Ambedkar earned doctorates from both Columbia University in New York and the London School of Economics, at a time when very few Indians studied abroad at all.

A thirsty schoolboy

Ambedkar was born in 1891 in Mhow, an army town in central India, where his father served in the British Indian Army. At school he had to sit apart from other children, sometimes on a sack he brought from home. He later wrote that he could only drink water if the school's peon poured it for him. When the peon was away, he went thirsty.

He studied anyway, and kept studying. He became one of the first in his community to finish college, at Elphinstone College in Bombay.

Columbia and London

With a scholarship from the ruler of Baroda, Ambedkar went to Columbia University in New York in 1913. Away from the caste rules of home, he could live, eat and study alongside anyone. He then studied at the London School of Economics and trained as a barrister.

By the 1920s he had doctorates from both universities. He came home to practise law, teach and start newspapers that spoke for oppressed communities. One of them, Mooknayak, means "leader of the voiceless". He also wrote on money and banking, and his ideas fed into discussions that led to the Reserve Bank of India.

His message to his followers was short: educate, agitate, organise.

Water, temples and votes

In 1927 he led the Mahad Satyagraha, when thousands of people drank from a public tank they had been barred from. Later campaigns demanded entry to temples.

Ambedkar and Gandhi both opposed untouchability, but they disagreed sharply on how to end it. In 1932 Ambedkar wanted separate electorates, so oppressed communities could choose their own representatives. Gandhi went on a fast against the idea. The two signed the Poona Pact, which reserved seats for these communities instead. Historians still discuss that moment, and both men's views have supporters.

Writing the rulebook

In 1947, Ambedkar became free India's first Law Minister and chairman of the Constitution's Drafting Committee. Over nearly three years, the Constituent Assembly debated every article.

The finished Constitution banned untouchability. It promised equality before the law and gave every adult the vote. It was adopted on 26 November 1949, now marked as Constitution Day, and came into force on 26 January 1950. Ambedkar warned that rights on paper were only a start, and that real equality would need people to live by them.

A new path

Late in life Ambedkar turned to the teachings of the Buddha, which he saw as a path of equality and reason. On 14 October 1956, in Nagpur, he became a Buddhist along with several hundred thousand followers. The place is now called Deekshabhoomi.

He died less than two months later, on 6 December 1956. Every year on that date, huge crowds gather at Chaityabhoomi in Mumbai, where he was cremated. India awarded him its highest honour, the Bharat Ratna, in 1990. His birthday, 14 April, is celebrated across India, and his statues, often holding a copy of the Constitution, stand in towns and villages everywhere.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ambedkar wrote the whole Constitution by himself.

Truth: The Constitution was debated and passed by a Constituent Assembly of about 300 members. Ambedkar chaired the Drafting Committee and guided it through, which is why he is called its chief architect.
