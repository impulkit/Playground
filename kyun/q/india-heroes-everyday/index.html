Who are India's everyday heroes?

An orange seller who built a school. A labourer who cut a road through a hill with a hammer. A boy who planted a forest on a sandbank.

The short answer

India's everyday heroes are ordinary people who saw a problem near them and spent years fixing it, often alone, with no money and no fame. They aren't film stars or cricketers. They are farmers, labourers, street sellers and village women.

Their stories travel by word of mouth, then newspapers, and sometimes end with a Padma Shri, one of India's highest honours. Since about 2014, the government has made a point of giving Padma awards to such unsung people. Here are a few of the best known.

Surprising but true

Harekala Hajabba sold oranges on the streets of Mangaluru for a living, never went to school himself, and used his savings to build a school for his village.

The forest man of Majuli

In 1979, a teenager named Jadav Payeng saw snakes dying on a hot, bare sandbank of the Brahmaputra river in Assam, near Majuli. Nothing grew there to shade them.

He started planting bamboo, then other trees, one by one, carrying water every day. Decades later, the Molai forest covers hundreds of hectares, by many reports larger than New York's Central Park. Rhinos, deer, tigers and herds of wild elephants now visit it. His story became a documentary, Forest Man, and he received the Padma Shri in 2015. People still call him the Forest Man of India.

The man who moved a mountain

In Gehlaur village in Bihar, a rocky hill stood between Dashrath Manjhi's home and the nearest town. To reach doctors, villagers had to walk the long way around. After his wife was badly hurt crossing it, he took a hammer and chisel to the hill.

He worked for about 22 years, from around 1960 to 1982. In the end he cut a path about 110 metres long through solid rock, wide enough for carts. A walk of dozens of kilometres shrank to just a few. People now call him the Mountain Man, and a Hindi film was made about his life in 2015.

The mother of trees

Saalumarada Thimmakka and her husband, a poor couple from Karnataka with no children, started planting banyan saplings along a road near their village. They carried water in pots for kilometres and protected each sapling from goats with thorny fences.

Over the years they raised 385 banyan trees along about 4 km of road. "Saalumarada" means "row of trees" in Kannada. She received the Padma Shri in 2019 and died in November 2025, aged over 110 by her family's count.

An orange seller's school

Harekala Hajabba sold oranges in Mangaluru. One day a foreign tourist asked him the price in English, and he couldn't answer. He realised the children of his village, Newpadpu, had no school to learn in.

So he saved from his small earnings, year after year, and started a school in a mosque. Slowly it grew into a proper government school building. In 2020 he was named for the Padma Shri. Photos of him receiving it, barefoot and in a simple white dhoti, were shared across the country.

Heroes in plain sight

Not every hero gets an award. Mumbai's dabbawalas carry tens of thousands of lunches daily with hardly a mistake. Village postmen trek through hills to deliver pensions. Fishermen in Kerala rowed their boats into the floods of 2018 and rescued thousands of people.

Then there are inventors like Arunachalam Muruganantham from Coimbatore, who built a cheap machine for making sanitary pads so women in villages could afford them. His work inspired the 2018 film Pad Man.

What they share

None of these people set out to be famous. They saw something wrong and began, usually with whatever tools they had. Then they kept going, through years when people called them foolish.

Scientists who study kindness say helping others makes the helper happier too. That may explain how these heroes kept going for decades. And perhaps the most surprising thing about Jadav Payeng's forest is not its size but that it began with a boy, a sandbank and a handful of bamboo.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You need money, power or fame to change the world.

Truth: Many of India's most admired heroes started with almost nothing. They had a problem in front of them, a simple tool, and the patience to keep going for years.

Try this

Think of someone in your street, school or family who quietly helps others, like a bus driver, a cook or a neighbour. Write them a short thank-you note saying exactly what they do and why it matters. Everyday heroes rarely get told.
