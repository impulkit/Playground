How did Japan change so fast in the Meiji era?

In 1853 Japan had samurai with swords and no railways. Fifty years later it had factories, a modern navy, and had beaten Russia in a war.

The short answer

For more than 200 years, Japan was ruled by military leaders called shoguns, and the emperor had little real power. Foreign trade was tightly controlled, and society was split into fixed classes, with samurai warriors near the top.

Then, in 1853, American warships arrived and forced Japan to open its ports. Young leaders decided Japan must change fast or be pushed around like China had been. In 1868 they took power in the name of the teenage Emperor Meiji. Over the next four decades they rebuilt the country, borrowing ideas from the West and shaping them to suit Japan.

Surprising but true

In 1871 about half of Japan's top government leaders left the country for nearly two years to tour the United States and Europe and study how they worked.

The black ships

In July 1853 four American ships steamed into the bay near Edo, today's Tokyo. Two of them belched black smoke from their coal engines, and the Japanese called them the "black ships". Commodore Matthew Perry demanded that Japan trade with the United States.

Japan's leaders knew what had happened next door. In the Opium Wars, Britain had beaten China and seized Hong Kong. Japan signed treaties with the Americans and Europeans, but many people felt shamed, and the old shogun government began to crumble.

A new emperor and a new capital

In 1868 rebels from the south overthrew the shogun and "restored" the emperor. The court moved from Kyoto to Edo, which was renamed Tokyo, meaning "eastern capital". The emperor's reign was called Meiji, meaning "enlightened rule".

The new leaders moved quickly. They abolished the old feudal domains and set up a central government. They also did something hard to picture: they ended the samurai as a class. Samurai lost their payments from the state, and in 1876 most people were banned from carrying swords in public. Some samurai rebelled, and the biggest rising, in 1877, was crushed by the new army of ordinary conscripts.

Trains, schools and silk

Japan sent students abroad and invited foreign experts in. In 1872 the first railway opened between Tokyo and Yokohama, a ride of under an hour that had taken most of a day on foot. In the same year, the government ordered schools for all children, girls as well as boys.

Factories came next. The Tomioka Silk Mill, opened in 1872, used French machines to reel silk thread. Silk became one of Japan's biggest exports and paid for much of the modernising. The mill is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Everyday life turned upside down

Change reached the dinner table and the wardrobe too. Buddhist custom had discouraged eating meat for centuries, but in 1872 the palace let it be known that the emperor ate beef. Officials started wearing Western suits, and the emperor was photographed in a military uniform rather than court robes.

Even time changed. At the end of 1872 Japan dropped its old moon-based calendar for the one used in Europe. The switch was so sudden that the third day of the twelfth month simply became 1 January 1873, and nearly a month vanished from the year.

A constitution and a parliament

In 1889 Japan adopted a written constitution with an elected parliament, though the emperor kept wide powers and only a small share of men could vote.

Power and its shadow

By 1900 Japan had a modern army and navy. It defeated China in 1895 and Russia in 1905. It was the first time in modern history that an Asian country beat a European great power in a major war, and the news thrilled people across Asia. Jawaharlal Nehru, then a schoolboy in India, later wrote of the excitement he felt.

But Japan also built an empire of its own. It took Taiwan in 1895 and made Korea a colony in 1910, ruling both harshly. When Emperor Meiji died in 1912, Japan was rich and strong, and set on a path that would lead to World War Two.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Japan was completely cut off from the world until 1853.

Truth: Japan limited foreign contact for over 200 years, but never shut it off entirely. Dutch and Chinese traders kept doing business at Nagasaki, and Japanese scholars studied European books on medicine and science.
