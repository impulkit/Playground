How much power is a gigawatt?

When leaders promise "500 gigawatts" of clean power, it sounds huge. But how huge? One gigawatt could light about 10 crore LED bulbs at the same moment.

The short answer

A watt is a unit of power. It measures how fast energy is being used or made. A small LED bulb uses about 10 watts. A gigawatt is a billion watts, which in Indian numbers is 100 crore watts.

So one gigawatt is enough to light about 10 crore LED bulbs at the same time, or run the fans, lights and TVs of several lakh homes on a hot evening. It is the unit countries use to talk about very big power plants and national targets.

Surprising but true

A power plant rated at 1 gigawatt does not always make 1 gigawatt. A solar plant in India produces, on average over a year, only about a fifth of its rated power, because the Sun sets and clouds pass.

How it works

Power units grow in steps of a thousand, a bit like lakhs and crores grow in steps of a hundred.

  • - 1,000 watts make 1 kilowatt (kW). An air conditioner uses about 1.5 kW.
  • - 1,000 kilowatts make 1 megawatt (MW). That could run a small town's streetlights.
  • - 1,000 megawatts make 1 gigawatt (GW). That is the size of a very big power station.

In maths, a billion is 10 to the power 9, written 10⁹. Using powers of ten makes these giant numbers easier to handle.

Now the tricky part. Power is different from energy. Think of water flowing from a tap. Power is how fast the water comes out. Energy is how much water fills your bucket. If a 1 kW heater runs for one hour, it uses 1 kilowatt-hour of energy. That is one "unit" on your electricity bill.

A power plant's "capacity" is the most power it can make at one moment, like a tap fully open. Over a year, real plants make less, because of night, clouds, calm days or repairs.

Inside most power plants, something spins a generator: steam from burning coal, falling water, or wind pushing giant blades. Solar panels are different, with no moving parts at all. Either way, the plant's gigawatt rating tells you its biggest possible output, not what it makes every hour.

Where it came from

The watt is named after James Watt, a Scottish engineer who greatly improved the steam engine in the 1760s and 1770s. To sell his engines, Watt compared them to horses, and invented the "horsepower". One horsepower is about 746 watts.

In 1882, scientists in Britain agreed to name the unit of electrical power after Watt. In 1960, the watt became part of the international system of measuring units, used by almost every country.

As power stations grew from small city plants to giant dams and nuclear stations, engineers began talking in megawatts and then gigawatts. Today, countries set their climate and energy goals in gigawatts.

India's story

By mid-2026, India had more than 550 GW of power capacity in total. About 300 GW came from non-fossil sources: solar, wind, hydropower and nuclear. That is more than half, a goal India reached in 2025, five years early.

India also has some of the biggest power projects in the world. The Bhadla solar park in Rajasthan's desert can make more than 2 GW. A renewable energy park at Khavda, in Gujarat's Rann of Kutch, is planned to reach 30 GW, bigger than the city of Paris in area. The Tehri dam in Uttarakhand makes about 1 GW from falling water, and each of the two big reactors at Kudankulam in Tamil Nadu is about 1 GW.

On the hottest days, the whole country's demand can climb above 270 GW at once, as millions of fans, coolers and air conditioners switch on.

The numbers that matter

  • - 100 crore watts: one gigawatt. About 10 crore LED bulbs of 10 watts each could glow at once.
  • - More than 550 GW: India's total power capacity in mid-2026. About 300 GW of it is clean, non-fossil power.
  • - 500 GW more: the extra clean capacity India aims to add over the next 10 to 11 years, announced in November 2026.
  • - About 20%: how much of its full capacity an Indian solar farm actually produces, on average, across a year. A coal plant can manage far more.
  • - About 271 GW: India's record demand at a single moment, reached on 21 May 2026 during a heatwave. More homes, cars and factories using electricity will push it higher.

What people argue about

People agree India needs much more electricity. They argue about how to get it.

Supporters of fast solar and wind growth say these are now among the cheapest ways to make power, cut pollution and reduce the money India spends on importing fuel. Others point out that solar and wind are not steady. They say India must also build batteries, energy storage, stronger power lines and steady sources like hydropower and nuclear power, which all cost a lot.

There is also debate over land. Giant solar parks need huge areas, sometimes in deserts used by herders or near wildlife. Some people prefer panels on rooftops, canals and farms, closer to where power is used.

Myths and mix-ups

"A 1 GW plant makes 1 GW all day, every day." No. That is its top speed. Real output depends on sunlight, wind, water and repairs.

"Watts and units on the bill are the same." Watts measure speed. Units, or kilowatt-hours, measure the amount used over time.

"More gigawatts always means fewer power cuts." Only if the power can reach homes when it is needed. That needs good wires, storage and planning, not just big plants.

What it means for you

Every switch you flick at home adds a few watts to India's demand. When millions of families switch on air conditioners in summer, those watts add up to gigawatts, and power companies must be ready.

Big clean power targets can mean cleaner air in cities and steadier electricity prices over time, because sunshine and wind cost nothing once panels and turbines are built. Most panels are photovoltaic, turning light straight into electricity.

Try reading your family's electricity bill. Find the units used last month. Then remember: behind each unit is a power plant somewhere, measured in megawatts and gigawatts, and that is where your electricity comes from.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A gigawatt is an amount of electricity, like a big tank of power.

Truth: A gigawatt is a speed, not an amount. It tells you how fast electricity is being made or used. The amount is measured in watt-hours, like the "units" on your electricity bill.

Try this

Look at the labels on three things at home, like a fan, a phone charger and a fridge, and note their watts. Add them up. Then work out how many homes like yours, with those three things on, would add up to 1 gigawatt. Hint, 1 GW is 100 crore watts.
