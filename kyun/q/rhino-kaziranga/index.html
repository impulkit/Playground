How did Kaziranga save the one-horned rhino?

A hundred years ago there may have been only a few dozen rhinos in Kaziranga. Now about two out of every three on Earth live there.

The short answer

At the start of the 1900s, the greater one-horned rhino was in deep trouble. Hunters shot rhinos for sport and for their horns, and farms were swallowing the grasslands where they lived. In Kaziranga, on the banks of the Brahmaputra in Assam, perhaps only a few dozen were left.

In 1905, the British government of the time set Kaziranga aside as a protected forest. Over the next century it became a sanctuary, then a national park in 1974. Forest guards fought off poachers, and the rhinos had space and safety to breed.

The count in 2022 found about 2,600 rhinos in Kaziranga. That is around two-thirds of all the one-horned rhinos on Earth.

Surprising but true

A rhino's horn is made of keratin, the same stuff as your fingernails and hair.

How it began

There is a famous story about how Kaziranga was saved. In 1904, Mary Curzon, the wife of Lord Curzon, the British Viceroy of India, visited the area hoping to see a rhino. She saw only their footprints, and was so worried that she persuaded her husband to protect the land.

Nobody is sure how much of the story is true, but the dates fit. Kaziranga was declared a proposed reserve forest in 1905. It became a reserve forest in 1908 and a game sanctuary in 1916. In 1950 the word "game" was dropped, because the animals were there to be protected, not shot.

Armour, mud and a single horn

A one-horned rhino can weigh over 2 tonnes, about as much as two small cars. Its thick skin hangs in deep folds that make it look as if it is wearing armour plates.

Rhinos spend hot afternoons wallowing in mud and pools, which keeps them cool and protects their skin from insects. They graze on the tall elephant grass that covers much of the park, and they leave large shared dung piles that other rhinos sniff to learn who has passed by.

Strangely, rhinos don't fight with their horns. They slash with sharp lower teeth instead. The horn is mostly for show.

A park shaped by floods

Every monsoon, the Brahmaputra spills over its banks and floods most of Kaziranga. The floods are good for the grasslands, washing in fresh soil and clearing old growth. But they are hard on the animals.

When the water rises, rhinos, deer and elephants head south across a busy highway to the Karbi Anglong hills. Speed limits and wildlife crossings help them get there. Inside the park, forest staff have built raised mounds of earth where animals can stand above the water.

The fight against poaching

Rhino horn is worth a great deal of money on illegal markets, mainly because some people believe it is a medicine. It isn't. Poachers have killed hundreds of Kaziranga's rhinos over the years.

Kaziranga answered with hundreds of armed forest guards and anti-poaching camps spread across the park. The approach has saved many rhinos, though some human rights groups have criticised how tough it can be on suspected poachers. In September 2021, Assam publicly burned about 2,500 rhino horns from its stores, to show that horns have no value except on a living rhino.

More than rhinos

Kaziranga is also home to tigers, Asian elephants, wild water buffalo and swamp deer. It became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1985, one of India's best-known national parks.

Rhinos from Kaziranga have also been moved to restart populations elsewhere, such as Manas National Park, which had lost all its rhinos. Together with Nepal's parks, they have brought the one-horned rhino from the edge of extinction to about 4,000 animals. It is still listed as vulnerable, but it is one of Asia's great wildlife comebacks.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Powdered rhino horn can cure illnesses.

Truth: Rhino horn is keratin, like fingernails. There is no scientific evidence that it treats any illness, yet the belief has driven poachers to kill thousands of rhinos.

Try this

Look closely at your fingernail and a strand of your hair, with a magnifying glass if you have one. Both are made of keratin, the same tough material as a rhino's horn. Press your nail gently and notice how hard yet slightly bendy it is.
