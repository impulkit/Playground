Who are the Native American nations?

The United States officially recognises 575 Native nations, each with its own government. Some have their own police, courts, flags and even passports.

The short answer

Native American nations are the peoples who lived in North America long before Europeans arrived, and their descendants today. They are not one group. There are hundreds of nations, such as the Navajo, Cherokee, Lakota, Ojibwe and Hopi, each with its own language, history and way of life.

The United States recognises 575 of them as having their own governments, which can make many of their own laws. Many Native people live on reservations, but most live in towns and cities across the country. Together, about 9.7 million people identified as American Indian or Alaska Native in the 2020 US census, some of them alongside another heritage.

Surprising but true

Native Americans are called "Indians" because Columbus believed he had sailed to the Indies, the lands around India.

Many peoples, many homes

Before 1492, North America was home to a huge variety of cultures. In the Southwest, the ancestors of the Pueblo peoples built many-storey towns of stone and adobe. On the Pacific coast, nations carved tall cedar totem poles and caught salmon. In the Northeast, the Haudenosaunee lived in long bark houses and formed a league of nations that made decisions together.

Near today's St. Louis, a city called Cahokia grew around 1100 CE, with an earthen mound about as tall as a ten-storey building at its centre. At its peak it may have been home to more people than London at the time.

Farmers across the Americas also bred crops the whole world now eats. Maize, beans, squash, potatoes, tomatoes and chillies all began as Native American crops. Without them, Indian cooking would look very different.

The names

The word "Indian" came from Columbus, who thought he had reached the Indies in 1492. The name stuck. Today people use "Native American" or "American Indian", and many prefer the name of their own nation, such as Diné for the Navajo. In Canada, the term "First Nations" is common.

Losing land

As European settlers spread west, Native nations lost most of their land through wars, broken treaties and forced moves. Diseases from Europe, such as smallpox, had already killed huge numbers of people.

In 1830 the US Congress passed the Indian Removal Act. Thousands of Cherokee, Muscogee, Choctaw and others were forced from the Southeast to land west of the Mississippi. The Cherokee call their 1838 march the Trail of Tears. Several thousand people died of cold, hunger and disease on the way. Later, many Native children were sent to boarding schools that tried to wipe out their languages and cultures.

Keeping cultures alive

Despite all this, Native nations survived. In the Second World War, Navajo Code Talkers used their language to send military messages that enemy forces never cracked. In 1924 all Native people born in the United States became citizens, although some states still blocked them from voting for decades.

Today nations run their own schools, hospitals and businesses. Many are working hard to teach their languages to children, because many have only a few elderly speakers left. The bison, once almost wiped out, are being brought back to Native lands by groups such as the InterTribal Buffalo Council.

An Indian connection

Because of Columbus's mistake, Americans and Indians share a word that causes endless confusion. India has its own Indigenous peoples too, the Adivasi communities, who numbered more than 10 crore in the 2011 census. Like Native Americans, many have fought to protect their forests, languages and land rights.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Native Americans all lived in tepees and hunted bison.

Truth: Tepees and bison hunting belonged to some Plains nations. Others lived in wooden longhouses, adobe towns, cedar plank houses or earth lodges, and many were farmers who grew maize, beans and squash.
