Who were the Ottomans?

A small Turkish chiefdom in north-west Anatolia grew into an empire that lasted more than 600 years and ruled from Hungary to Yemen.

The short answer

The Ottomans were a dynasty of Turkish rulers who built one of the largest and longest-lasting empires in history. It began around 1299 with a chief called Osman, whose small territory lay in north-western Anatolia, today's Turkey. The name "Ottoman" comes from his name.

In 1453 the Ottomans captured Constantinople and made it their capital, Istanbul. At their height in the 1500s and 1600s, they ruled south-eastern Europe, Turkey, most of the Middle East including Mecca and Medina, and North Africa. The empire slowly weakened, sided with Germany in the First World War, and ended in 1922. The Republic of Turkey took its place.

Surprising but true

Tulips, now a symbol of the Netherlands, reached Western Europe in the 1500s from the gardens of the Ottoman Empire.

Taking the great city

For a thousand years, Constantinople, the capital of the Byzantine Empire, had resisted attackers. In 1453 the 21-year-old Sultan Mehmed II besieged it with a large army and some of the biggest cannons ever made, built by a Hungarian engineer named Orban.

The Byzantines had stretched a chain across their harbour, the Golden Horn. Mehmed had dozens of ships dragged over a hill on greased logs and launched them behind the chain. After almost two months, on 29 May 1453, his troops broke through the walls. Mehmed rebuilt the damaged city, invited people of many faiths to settle there and turned Hagia Sophia into a mosque.

Suleiman the Lawgiver

The empire reached its peak under Suleiman I, who ruled from 1520 to 1566. Europeans called him "the Magnificent". In Turkey he is known as Kanuni, "the Lawgiver", because he organised the empire's laws.

His armies took Belgrade and much of Hungary, and in 1529 besieged Vienna, though they failed to take it. His navy controlled much of the Mediterranean. The architect Mimar Sinan, who served Suleiman and later sultans, designed more than 300 buildings, including the Süleymaniye Mosque, whose domes still crown one of Istanbul's hills.

Many peoples, one empire

The Ottoman Empire was a patchwork of peoples and religions. Muslims, Christians and Jews lived side by side. Under what is often called the millet system, religious communities ran many of their own affairs, such as marriage and schools, under their own leaders.

When Spain expelled its Jews in 1492, many found refuge in Ottoman cities. Ottoman cities were also places of trade and new habits. Coffee houses spread in Istanbul in the 1500s, and coffee drinking later travelled to Europe. The empire also had links with India. In 1538 an Ottoman fleet sailed to help the Sultan of Gujarat fight the Portuguese at Diu.

Decline and a terrible war

From the late 1600s the empire began to lose ground. After a second failed siege of Vienna in 1683, European powers pushed it back. In the 1800s, nations such as Greece, Serbia and Bulgaria won independence, and Europeans began calling the empire "the sick man of Europe". Sultans tried reforms, building railways, schools and a new army.

The Ottomans joined the First World War on Germany's side. During the war, the Ottoman government deported Armenians from their homes, and huge numbers died in massacres and forced marches. Estimates range from about 600,000 to 1.5 million people. Most historians, and many countries, call this the Armenian genocide. The government of Turkey disputes that word, though it accepts that many Armenians died.

The end of the empire

After defeat in 1918, the empire was carved up by the winners. In India, many Muslims, supported by Gandhi, joined the Khilafat movement, which campaigned to protect the Ottoman sultan's role as caliph, a religious leader for many Sunni Muslims.

In Turkey, an army officer named Mustafa Kemal, later called Atatürk, led a war of independence. The sultanate was abolished in 1922, the Republic of Turkey was declared in 1923, and the caliphate was abolished in 1924. Topkapı Palace, where sultans lived for centuries, is now a museum overlooking the Bosporus.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Ottoman Empire was the same thing as modern Turkey.

Truth: Turkey grew out of the Ottoman heartland, but the empire was far bigger and more mixed. It included Greeks, Arabs, Armenians, Kurds, Jews, Serbs, Bulgarians and many others, speaking dozens of languages.
