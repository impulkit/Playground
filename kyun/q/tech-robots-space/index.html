Which robots work in space?

A Mars rover built to last 90 days kept driving for about 14 years, and covered more than a marathon on another planet.

The short answer

Robots work all over space. Robotic arms catch spacecraft and move equipment on the International Space Station. Wheeled rovers explore Mars and the Moon. Small flying robots float around inside the station, helping astronauts. And probes like the Voyagers, which are robots too, have travelled beyond the planets.

Robots go where it is too far, too dangerous or too expensive to send people. They do not need air, food or a ride home. Some keep working for years after their planned missions end.

Surprising but true

A little helicopter called Ingenuity was planned to make 5 test flights on Mars. It made 72 before a broken blade grounded it in 2024.

Arms that build in orbit

One of the hardest workers in space is Canadarm2, a robotic arm about 17 metres long, longer than a school bus. Built by Canada, it has been on the space station since 2001. It helped assemble the station, catches cargo spacecraft and moves astronauts around on spacewalks.

It can "walk" across the outside of the station, end over end like an inchworm, by plugging into special sockets. A smaller two-armed robot, Dextre, rides on it to do fiddly jobs like changing batteries, so astronauts don't have to risk a spacewalk.

Rovers that outlived their plans

NASA's twin rovers Spirit and Opportunity landed on Mars in 2004. Each was meant to work for about 90 Martian days. Spirit lasted six years. Opportunity kept going for more than 14 years and drove about 45 km, more than a marathon. A huge dust storm in 2018 finally covered its solar panels.

The newer rovers, Curiosity and Perseverance, are as big as a small car and powered by nuclear batteries. Perseverance collects rock samples in tubes for a future mission to bring home. Because radio signals take minutes to cross the gap, the rovers steer themselves around rocks using cameras and software.

A helicopter on Mars

Perseverance carried a stowaway: Ingenuity, a helicopter weighing about 1.8 kg, lighter than a big bottle of water. Mars's air is about 100 times thinner than Earth's, so its blades had to spin very fast, around 2,500 times a minute. In April 2021, it made the first powered flight on another planet.

It was meant to make 5 test flights. It made 72, scouting routes for the rover. In January 2024, a damaged blade ended its flying days. It stayed switched on afterwards, sending back data from the spot where it last landed.

India's robots in space

In August 2023, India's Chandrayaan-3 landed near the Moon's south pole. Its rover, Pragyan, rolled out down a ramp and travelled about 100 metres, studying the soil with lasers and X-rays. It confirmed sulphur in the Moon's surface near the pole. It even printed the Indian national emblem and ISRO's logo into the lunar dust with its wheels.

For the Gaganyaan human spaceflight programme, ISRO has built Vyommitra, a humanoid robot with a head, arms and a face. She is planned to fly on uncrewed test missions to check conditions before astronauts go.

Floating helpers

Inside the space station, three cube-shaped robots called Astrobee have floated around since 2019. Named Honey, Queen and Bumble, they move using small fans, take pictures and test new tools. Earlier, a humanoid called Robonaut 2 worked on the station from 2011 to 2018. Japan sent a tiny talking robot, Kirobo, in 2013 to keep an astronaut company.

Robots have also gone farther than anything else humans have built. The Voyager probes, launched in 1977, are still sending data from beyond the planets. The story of everything the rovers have found, from ancient riverbeds to strange rocks, is told in Mars rovers. And India's next step is putting people, not just robots, into orbit through Gaganyaan.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Robots in space are controlled live by people with joysticks all the time.

Truth: Some robots near Earth can be guided closely, but far-off rovers must think for themselves. Radio delays mean they drive using their own software, following plans sent from Earth.

Try this

Play "Mars rover" with a friend. One person is blindfolded and is the rover. The other gives one command at a time, like "two steps forward", but the rover must count to ten before moving. Try to guide the rover around a chair and see how hard the delay makes it.
