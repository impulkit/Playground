Why was the Eiffel Tower built?

The Eiffel Tower was meant to stand for only 20 years. It survived partly because it made a very good radio aerial.

The short answer

The Eiffel Tower was built as the grand entrance to the 1889 World's Fair in Paris, called the Exposition Universelle. That year marked 100 years since the French Revolution, and France wanted to show the world what modern French engineering could do.

The tower was built from wrought iron by the company of the engineer Gustave Eiffel, and at 300 metres it was by far the tallest structure in the world. It was only supposed to stand for 20 years. It was saved because scientists found it useful for experiments, especially for sending radio signals.

Surprising but true

The Eiffel Tower grows taller in summer. Heat makes its iron expand, and the top can rise by up to about 15 cm on a hot day.

A competition for a giant

In 1886 France held a competition for a centrepiece for the fair. More than a hundred designs were sent in. The winner came from Eiffel's company. Two of his engineers, Maurice Koechlin and Émile Nouguier, had first sketched the idea of a giant iron tower. The architect Stephen Sauvestre made it more elegant, adding the big arches at the base.

Gustave Eiffel was already famous for iron bridges and for designing the iron skeleton inside the Statue of Liberty in New York.

Built like a giant kit

The tower was made from more than 18,000 iron pieces. Each was designed on paper first, down to the millimetre, then made in Eiffel's factory outside Paris. On site, workers fitted them together like a giant construction set, fixing them with about 2.5 million rivets, hammered in while red-hot.

Building took just over two years, from January 1887 to March 1889. About 300 workers did the job. The open iron lattice, full of triangles, makes the tower strong while letting wind blow straight through. That's partly why it is so light for its size.

Artists who hated it

Not everyone was impressed. In 1887, while it was being built, a group of famous artists and writers published a letter of protest. They called it useless and monstrous and said it would ruin the beauty of Paris.

When it opened, though, crowds loved it. Nearly two million people visited during the fair. People rode lifts up the curved legs and looked out over the whole city, something few had ever done.

Saved by radio

Eiffel's permit lasted only 20 years. After that, the city could take the tower down. Eiffel worked hard to prove it was useful. He let scientists use it for experiments in weather, air resistance and, most importantly, wireless telegraphy, sending messages by radio waves.

The tower was a perfect aerial, taller than anything else around. In the early 1900s the French army used it to send and receive radio messages. It was too valuable to scrap. Today it still carries antennas for radio and television, which is why it has grown to about 330 metres.

Fighting rust

Iron's great enemy is rust, which forms when iron reacts with oxygen and water. To protect it, the tower is repainted by hand roughly every seven years, with around 60 tonnes of paint.

It has even been painted different colours over the years, from reddish-brown to yellow. From 1968 it was a bronze-brown, a little darker at the bottom and lighter at the top so the tower looked evenly coloured against the sky. The latest repainting, begun in 2019, brought back a yellow-brown that Gustave Eiffel himself chose in 1907.

The Iron Lady today

The Eiffel Tower was the tallest structure in the world until 1930, when the Chrysler Building in New York overtook it. Today it is one of the most visited paid monuments on Earth, with millions of visitors a year.

Many cities have built copies of it, and its shape is so famous it can stand for Paris on a single postcard. Not bad for a building that was supposed to be temporary.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The people of Paris loved the Eiffel Tower from the start.

Truth: Many hated it. In 1887 a group of well-known artists and writers published a letter calling it useless and monstrous. Over time, most Parisians grew fond of it, and it became the symbol of the city.
