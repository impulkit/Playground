How do valleys form?

You can often tell what carved a valley just from its shape. Rivers cut a V, and glaciers scoop out a U.

The short answer

A valley is a long, low stretch of land between hills or mountains. Most valleys are carved by something moving downhill, usually a river or a glacier. A few form when the ground itself cracks and sinks.

Rivers cut down into the ground using the sand and pebbles they carry, like a slow saw. Rain and frost loosen the sides, which slide in, so a river valley ends up shaped like a V. Glaciers are wider and heavier. They grind away both the floor and the walls, leaving a U shape. Where the Earth's crust stretches apart, a block of land can drop down between two cracks and make a rift valley.

Surprising but true

The Kashmir Valley was once the bed of a huge lake. Old lake mud still lies under its fields, and a local legend tells of a lake called Satisar that was drained long ago.

V-shaped: the river's work

High in the Himalaya, rivers fall steeply and run fast. Rushing water rolls boulders along the bottom and cuts deep, narrow valleys called gorges. The Kali Gandaki in Nepal and the Siang (as the Brahmaputra is called in Arunachal Pradesh) flow through some of the deepest gorges on Earth.

As a river reaches flatter ground, it slows down. It starts to swing from side to side, wearing away its banks and spreading out mud. The valley becomes wider and gentler. Over a long time, a V can open into a broad plain. In dry places, where little rain wears the sides back, the walls stay steep. That is how the Colorado River cut the Grand Canyon in Arizona, about 1.8 km deep in places (more than a mile).

U-shaped: the glacier's work

Glaciers are rivers of ice that creep downhill, often less than a metre a day. Rocks frozen into the ice scrape the ground like sandpaper on a giant scale. Glaciers fill a valley from side to side, so they carve its walls as well as its floor.

During the last ice age, glaciers filled many mountain valleys that are now ice-free. Yosemite Valley in California is a famous example, with granite cliffs nearly a kilometre high and a flat floor. Smaller side glaciers carved shallower valleys that now end high on the main valley's wall. Streams pour out of these "hanging valleys" as waterfalls. Yosemite Falls drops about 740 metres.

What is Yosemite?Yosemite's granite cliff El Capitan rises about 900 metres straight up from the valley floor, almost as tall as three Eiffel Towers stacked together.

Rift valleys: when the ground cracks

Some valleys aren't carved at all. Deep forces can pull the Earth's crust apart, and a long block of land sinks between two faults, or cracks. This is a rift valley.

The Great Rift Valley in East Africa runs for thousands of kilometres. In India, the Narmada flows west through a rift valley between the Vindhya and Satpura hills. This is one reason it flows into the Arabian Sea, while most big rivers of peninsular India flow east to the Bay of Bengal.

A sinking block can even drop below sea level. Death Valley in California formed as the land between two mountain ranges sank along faults. Its lowest point, Badwater Basin, is 86 metres below sea level, the lowest spot in North America. Hardly any rain falls there, so instead of a river, its floor is covered with glittering salt.

Valleys and people

Valleys have water, flat land and shelter from the wind, so people have farmed and built towns in them for thousands of years. The Kashmir Valley sits between high ranges at about 1,600 metres. Geologists have found thick layers of old lake mud there, called karewas, which match the old story of a vast lake that once filled the valley. Saffron grows on some of these karewas today.

Uttarakhand's Valley of Flowers, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, is a glacier valley that turns pink, purple and yellow with wild flowers in the monsoon. And in California, the Santa Clara Valley was once full of fruit orchards. Today it is better known as Silicon Valley.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A river just finds a valley that was already there and flows through it.

Truth: In most river valleys, the river made the valley. Over thousands to millions of years, water and the stones it carries cut down into the rock, and the sides slowly slump and wear back.

Try this

Make a valley in a tray of damp sand. Pour water slowly down a slope from a cup and watch a V-shaped channel form. Then smooth it out, push an ice cube (or a bar of soap) down the same slope several times, and compare the shape it leaves.
