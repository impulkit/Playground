How much can a human lift?

In 2026, Mexico's Raúl Flores deadlifted 511 kg, about as heavy as a concert grand piano, lifting it from the floor to his hips.

The short answer

The heaviest weight ever deadlifted, pulled from the floor to standing, is 511 kg. Mexico's Raúl Flores lifted it in 2026, beating the 510 kg that Iceland's Hafþór Björnsson pulled in 2025. That is about as heavy as a concert grand piano, or seven grown-up men. In the Olympics, where lifters push weights over their heads, the best men lift more than 260 kg in the clean and jerk.

Most adults can lift much less, usually somewhere around their own body weight from the floor with good training. Strength depends on muscle size, practice, technique and body shape. Scientists also think the brain sets a safety limit, keeping some strength in reserve so muscles don't tear tendons or snap bones.

Surprising but true

Your muscles are probably capable of pulling harder than you can make them. The nervous system seems to hold some strength in reserve to protect your tendons and bones.

How muscles pull

Muscles are made of long cells called fibres. Inside them, tiny protein threads slide over each other and shorten the muscle. A muscle can only pull. To bend your arm, the biceps pulls. To straighten it, a different muscle, the triceps, pulls the other way.

Bigger muscles have more fibres working side by side, so they can pull harder. Training makes fibres thicker. It also teaches the nervous system to switch on more fibres at once. That is why beginners get stronger fast, long before their muscles look bigger.

The body as a set of levers

Your bones work as levers, and your joints are the pivot points. Most muscles attach close to a joint, which means they must pull very hard to move a weight held far away. This set-up is good for speed, and less good for strength.

Body shape matters too. Lifters with short arms find the bench press easier, because the bar travels a short distance. Lifters with long arms often find the deadlift easier. Champions often have body shapes that suit their sport. Strongmen are often very tall and heavy, while Olympic lifters in the lighter classes are short and compact, with arms and legs that move the bar a shorter distance.

Small but mighty

In July 2021, Mirabai Chanu of Manipur won India a silver medal in the women's 49 kg class at the Tokyo 2020 Olympics. She lifted a total of 202 kg in two lifts, about four times her body weight.

Smaller lifters often lift more for their size. Strength depends on the cross-section of a muscle, which grows more slowly than body weight as animals get bigger. That is why ants and beetles are champions compared with their size. One kind of dung beetle can pull more than a thousand times its own weight. A human carrying a thousand times their weight would be holding up a fully loaded freight train.

The brain's safety brake

Scientists have measured muscles under electric stimulation and found they can pull harder than people can manage by choice. It seems the brain usually holds some strength back.

There are stories of people lifting a car to save someone. Most turn out to be lifting one corner or side, which is much easier than lifting a whole car. Adrenaline, a hormone released in fear or excitement, probably does help a little. How much extra strength people can really find in an emergency is hard to test safely.

Getting stronger safely

Children and teenagers can safely build strength with body-weight exercises like push-ups, squats and climbing, and with light weights under an adult's supervision. Bones and muscles keep growing into the twenties. Strength training also makes bones denser, which helps for life. Astronauts lose bone strength in space for the opposite reason: with no weight to push against, their bones get weaker. For raw power for their size, though, no human comes close to the strongest animals, from dung beetles to rhinoceros beetles.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In an emergency, ordinary people can suddenly lift a car off someone because of adrenaline.

Truth: There are a few news stories of people lifting part of a car, often just one side or corner, which needs far less force. Adrenaline can help a little, but no study has shown people lifting whole cars. These stories are hard to check.

Try this

Test the power of levers. Place a ruler over a pencil like a see-saw and put a heavy book on one end. Press down on the other end with one finger. Move the pencil closer to the book and try again. Notice how much easier it gets. Your arms and legs work as levers too.
