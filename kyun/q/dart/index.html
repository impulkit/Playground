Can humans push an asteroid away?

In 2022, NASA crashed a spacecraft into an asteroid. On purpose.

The short answer

Yes. In 2022, NASA proved it by crashing a spacecraft called DART into a small asteroid named Dimorphos. The crash changed Dimorphos's orbit. Its trip around its bigger partner got about 32 minutes shorter.

The trick is time. A tiny push given years before a possible collision adds up. By the time the asteroid reaches Earth's path, it can be thousands of kilometres off course, and miss us completely.

Surprising but true

DART weighed about as much as a small car, and it moved an asteroid the size of a stadium.

A test with no danger

DART stands for Double Asteroid Redirection Test. Its target was a pair of asteroids. The bigger one, Didymos, is about 780 m wide. Dimorphos, about 160 m wide, circles it like a small moon. That is roughly the length of one and a half football fields.

DART's final pictures suggest Dimorphos is a loose pile of boulders and rubble, held together by its own weak gravity, rather than one solid rock.

Neither asteroid was ever a threat to Earth, and the test could not change that. NASA chose a pair on purpose. A tiny change in an orbit around the Sun would be very hard to measure. A change in how long a little moon takes to circle its partner can be timed from Earth with telescopes.

The crash

DART launched in November 2021 from California and travelled for ten months. It weighed about 570 kg at the end, about as much as a small car. Its camera did all the steering in the final hours, locking on to a target it had never seen.

On 26 September 2022, it slammed into Dimorphos at about 22,000 km/h. The last pictures showed a field of boulders rushing closer. Then the signal stopped. A small Italian satellite called LICIACube, released a couple of weeks earlier, flew past to photograph the blast.

Better than expected

Telescopes around the world, and the Hubble and Webb space telescopes, watched the cloud of dust and rock that sprayed out. Hubble saw it stretch into a tail thousands of kilometres long, like a comet's.

That flying rubble mattered. As it blasted off, it pushed Dimorphos back like the exhaust of a rocket, so the asteroid got several times more push than the spacecraft alone could give. NASA had hoped to change the orbit by at least 73 seconds. The real change was about 32 minutes.

Dimorphos's speed changed by only a few millimetres per second, about the pace of a crawling snail. Over years, that is plenty.

Why scientists care

About 66 million years ago, an asteroid around 10 km wide hit what is now Mexico. It helped wipe out the dinosaurs and most other life on Earth. Rocks that big are rare, and none known is heading for us. Smaller ones, the size of Dimorphos, could still badly damage a city. In 2013, a rock only about 20 m wide exploded high above the city of Chelyabinsk in Russia. Its shock wave shattered windows across the city and injured more than a thousand people, mostly from flying glass.

Astronomers search the sky every night for asteroids and track their orbits years ahead. If they ever find one on a collision course, DART shows that a well-aimed push could work.

Going back to look

Nobody has yet seen the crater DART left. The European Space Agency launched a spacecraft called Hera in 2024 to visit the pair, measure Dimorphos's mass and study the damage close up. Its findings will help turn one successful experiment into a plan the whole world could use.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The best way to stop a dangerous asteroid is to blow it to pieces, like in the movies.

Truth: Smashing an asteroid apart could leave many smaller pieces still heading our way. Scientists' first choice is a gentle push, made years in advance, so the whole rock simply misses Earth.
