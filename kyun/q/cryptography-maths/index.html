How does maths keep messages secret?

Every time someone pays with UPI, a puzzle about prime numbers stands guard. Nobody knows a fast way to solve it.

The short answer

Cryptography is the science of scrambling a message so only the right person can read it. The scrambling method is called a cipher, and the secret setting that opens it is called the key.

For most of history, ciphers were clever word games. Today they are built from maths, especially prime numbers. Your phone uses this maths every time you send a message, log in to a website or make a payment, so that anyone listening in sees only nonsense.

Surprising but true

Multiplying two huge prime numbers takes a computer a split second, but working backwards to find them from the answer could take ordinary computers longer than the age of the universe.

Caesar's shift and how it broke

The Roman writer Suetonius tells us that Julius Caesar wrote secret letters by shifting each letter three places along the alphabet. A became D, B became E, and so on. It fooled people who weren't expecting it.

But shift ciphers, and even ciphers that swap every letter for a random symbol, have a weakness. In any language, some letters appear far more than others. Around the 800s CE, the Arab scholar al-Kindi described how to count the symbols in a secret message and match the most common ones to the most common letters. This method, called frequency analysis, cracked simple ciphers for centuries.

Machines and a war

In the Second World War, German forces used the Enigma machine. It looked like a typewriter, with spinning wheels inside that changed the cipher with every key pressed. The number of possible settings was so vast that guessing was hopeless.

At Bletchley Park in England, thousands of people worked to break it. Among them was the mathematician Alan Turing, who helped design machines called bombes to test settings quickly. They built on earlier work by Polish mathematicians. Historians believe the decoded messages helped the Allies win sooner.

Who was Alan Turing?Before a single modern computer existed, a 24-year-old in Cambridge described on paper a machine that could do any calculation at all.

The padlock trick

Old ciphers had one big problem. Both people needed the same secret key, so they had to meet or send it somehow without anyone stealing it.

In 1976 and 1977, researchers in the USA published a way around this, called public-key cryptography. Think of a padlock. You hand out open padlocks to anyone who asks. They snap your padlock shut on a box, but only you have the key to open it. Anyone can lock the box, but only one person can open it.

The best-known version, RSA, is named after Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir and Leonard Adleman. Its padlock is a giant number made by multiplying two secret primes. Multiplying is easy. Splitting the giant number back into its two primes is extremely hard if the primes are hundreds of digits long.

Guarding your phone

That one-way trick protects online shopping, email and payments, including India's UPI system. The small padlock beside a website's address in your browser means your connection is being scrambled. Strong passwords add another lock on top.

Mathematicians keep checking whether anyone has found a shortcut. One worry is the quantum computer, which in theory could factor huge numbers fast. Cryptographers are already designing new ciphers based on different maths problems, so they are ready before such machines get powerful enough.

Hide the key, not the method

In 1883, a Dutch-born teacher named Auguste Kerckhoffs wrote down a rule that cryptographers still follow. A good cipher should stay safe even if the enemy knows exactly how it works. Only the key needs to be secret. That is why the maths behind RSA is printed in textbooks, and anyone can check it for weaknesses.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A secret code is safe as long as nobody knows how it works.

Truth: Modern cryptography assumes the enemy knows the method. Only the key is kept secret. Codes whose safety depends on hiding the method tend to get broken.

Try this

Write the alphabet in a row, then write it again underneath, shifted three places so A sits over D. Use it to turn a short message into code and give it to a friend with the hint "shift 3". Then try cracking a friend's longer message by counting which letter appears most.
