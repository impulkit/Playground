What is an equation?

The equals sign was invented in 1557 by a Welsh doctor who was tired of writing "is equal to" over and over again.

The short answer

An equation is a statement that two things are equal. It has an equals sign in the middle, with something on the left and something on the right. 4 + 3 = 7 is an equation. So is x + 3 = 7, where x stands for a number we need to find.

You can think of an equation as a balance scale that is perfectly level. Whatever is on the left weighs exactly the same as what is on the right. To solve it, you change both sides in the same way, so the scale stays level, until the unknown is alone on one side. Take 3 from both sides of x + 3 = 7 and you get x = 4.

Surprising but true

Robert Recorde chose two parallel lines for the equals sign because, he wrote, no two things could be more equal.

The birth of the equals sign

For most of history, people wrote "is equal to" in words, in whatever language they used. Then, in 1557, a Welsh doctor and mathematician named Robert Recorde wrote a maths textbook in English called The Whetstone of Witte.

In it, he introduced a sign made of two short parallel lines. He explained that he chose them because no two things could be more equal than a pair of parallel lines. His lines were much longer than ours, but the idea caught on. The plus and minus signs had first appeared in print only about 70 years earlier, so maths was slowly learning to write in symbols.

Older than the sign

Equations themselves are far older than the equals sign. About 3,800 years ago, scribes in Babylon, in Mesopotamia, pressed maths problems into clay tablets. Some asked questions like: a field's length and width together make a certain number, and its area is another number. What are the sides? Today we would call that a quadratic equation, one with a squared unknown.

In India, mathematicians such as Brahmagupta, in the 7th century, wrote general rules for solving equations, including quadratic ones. Later, Sridhara gave a method that works much like the formula students learn in school today.

Doing the same to both sides

The golden rule of equations is fairness. Whatever you do to one side, you must do to the other. Add, subtract, multiply or divide, as long as you do it to both sides, and the equation stays true.

Suppose 3 notebooks and a ₹20 pen cost ₹110. Write it as 3n + 20 = 110. Take 20 from both sides: 3n = 90. Divide both sides by 3: n = 30. Each notebook costs ₹30. In dollars or rupees, the steps are the same.

You can always check your answer by putting it back in. Three notebooks at ₹30 make ₹90, and the pen adds ₹20, giving ₹110. The two sides match, so the answer is right. Mathematicians call this substituting, and it catches a lot of careless mistakes.

How do computers forecast the weather?In 1922, a scientist dreamed of a giant hall where 64,000 people with pencils would calculate tomorrow's weather. Today one supercomputer does far more, in minutes.

Equations that describe the world

Scientists use equations as short descriptions of how the world works. Isaac Newton wrote that force equals mass times acceleration: push harder and an object speeds up faster. Albert Einstein's E = mc², from 1905, says that a tiny amount of mass holds an enormous amount of energy.

Some equations are too hard to solve with pencil and paper. To forecast tomorrow's rain, computers solve huge numbers of equations about air pressure, temperature and wind, for millions of points across the Earth. That is why weather forecasts need some of the fastest computers in the world. And every one of those calculations still follows Recorde's two little lines.

Myth or fact?

Myth: An equation always has exactly one answer.

Truth: Some equations have two answers, some have many, and some have none. x × x = 9 is true for both 3 and −3. x + 1 = x has no answer at all, because adding 1 always changes a number.
