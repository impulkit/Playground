Why is a sandwich called a sandwich?

The sandwich is named after an English nobleman who, the story goes, asked for meat between bread so he wouldn't have to stop playing cards, and Hawaii was once named after him too.

The short answer

The sandwich is named after John Montagu, the 4th Earl of Sandwich, an English nobleman of the 1700s. The popular story is that he loved playing cards so much that he refused to stop for meals. Instead, he asked for meat tucked between two slices of bread, so he could eat with one hand and keep playing with the other. Others started asking for "the same as Sandwich", and the name stuck.

The Earl didn't invent eating things in bread. People had been doing that for thousands of years. But he made the idea fashionable among the rich in London, and the word spread from there around the world.

Surprising but true

Sandwich is a real town in Kent, England, and its name means something like "sandy harbour" in Old English.

The earl and the card table

The best-known account comes from a French writer named Pierre-Jean Grosley, who visited London in the 1760s. He described a government minister who spent 24 hours at a gambling table, eating only beef between slices of toast. He didn't name him, but readers took it to be the Earl of Sandwich.

The Earl was a busy politician. He was First Lord of the Admiralty, in charge of Britain's navy, for many years. His biographer, the historian N. A. M. Rodger, thinks the Earl was more likely eating at his desk while working late. Since the main source is one visitor's travel book, we will probably never know for sure.

A town, a title and some islands

Before it was a snack, Sandwich was a small port town in Kent, in southeast England. The name comes from Old English and means something like a sandy harbour or market town. The Montagu family took their title from it in 1660.

The 4th Earl's name also travelled across the Pacific. As head of the navy, he supported Captain James Cook's voyages. In 1778, Cook reached a group of islands and named them the Sandwich Islands in his honour. Today, those islands are Hawaii, which has its own name, from its own people.

What is Hawaiian culture like?The first Hawaiians crossed thousands of kilometres of open Pacific in canoes, steering by stars, waves and birds, and found some of the most remote islands on Earth.

Bread meals before the earl

Long before the Earl, people wrapped food in bread. In ancient times, the Jewish teacher Hillel is said to have eaten lamb and bitter herbs between pieces of matzah, a flatbread, at Passover. Across the Middle East, people stuffed pita. In India, a roti rolled around sabzi or a kathi roll does the same job.

In medieval Europe, thick slices of stale bread, called trenchers, served as plates. Diners ate the food on top, and sometimes the soggy bread too, or gave it to the poor.

Sandwiches around the world

Once the word spread, every country made the sandwich its own. In the USA, it became the peanut butter and jelly sandwich, the club sandwich and the burger. Is a hot dog a sandwich? People still argue about that. Vietnam has the banh mi, Mexico the torta, Italy the panino.

In Mumbai, the "Bombay sandwich" is layered with green chutney, potato, cucumber, tomato and beetroot, often toasted on the street. The vada pav, a spicy potato fritter in a soft bun, is often said to have been first sold in 1966 by Ashok Vaidya near Dadar station. It became the fuel of Mumbai's mill workers, who needed cheap, fast food on the go. Today it's one of the city's best-loved street foods.

A word that became a verb

"Sandwich" has become more than food. We say a car was sandwiched between two buses, or that an ice cream sandwich holds ice cream between biscuits. Many other everyday words came from people's names, such as cardigan, from another British earl. Language is full of these accidental tributes, and the story of how English picks up new words is told in borrowed words.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Earl of Sandwich invented the idea of eating food between bread.

Truth: People ate food in or on bread long before him, from Middle Eastern flatbreads to Indian rotis rolled around sabzi. The Earl only gave his name to the English version, which made it fashionable.
