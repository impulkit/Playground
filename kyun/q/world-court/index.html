How does the world's top court decide if a country committed genocide?

The world's top court has no army, no police and no jail. Yet countries send their best lawyers to The Hague to argue in front of it.

The short answer

The International Court of Justice, often called the world court, is the United Nations' top court. It settles legal arguments between countries, not between people.

To decide if a country committed genocide, judges ask two questions. Did that country's forces or officials do certain acts against a group? And did they do them with the intent, or plan, to destroy that group, in whole or in part? The second question is the hardest. The judges weigh the evidence, often for years, and the majority decides.

Surprising but true

In 2019 The Gambia, a small country in West Africa, took Myanmar in South-East Asia to the world court over the Rohingya. It was the first time a country with no direct link to the people harmed brought a genocide case there.

How a genocide case works

Think of two neighbours in a long quarrel. They agree to let a respected judge decide, and they promise to accept the answer. The world court works the same way, because it can only hear a case when countries have agreed to let it.

For genocide, that agreement sits inside a treaty, the Genocide Convention of 1948. Its Article IX says that arguments between member countries about the treaty can be taken to the court. A case then moves in steps:

  • 1. The application. One country files a written case against another, saying which treaty gives the court the power to decide.
  • 2. Emergency orders. A case can take years, so the first country may ask for "provisional measures": urgent orders to protect people and preserve evidence while the case goes on.
  • 3. Objections. The accused country often argues the court has no right to hear the case at all. The judges decide this first.
  • 4. The merits. Both sides send thick written arguments, then argue in public hearings. They bring witnesses, maps, photos and United Nations reports.
  • 5. The judgment. Each judge votes, and the majority decides. Judges who disagree can write a dissenting opinion explaining why. The President reads the verdict aloud in the Peace Palace.

The Convention lists five kinds of acts, such as killing members of a group or stopping births within it. Proving the acts happened is not enough. The court must be sure that the only reasonable explanation for them is a plan to destroy the group.

Where it came from

The world court began with the United Nations in 1945, at the end of the Second World War. Its first judges were elected in 1946.

The word genocide is younger than you might think. In 1944 a Polish-Jewish lawyer, Raphael Lemkin, joined the Greek word genos (race or tribe) with the Latin -cide (killing). He had escaped the Nazis, who were trying to wipe out Europe's Jews in the Holocaust, and he wanted a name for the crime of destroying a whole people. He then spent years persuading diplomats to make it illegal.

On 9 December 1948, the UN adopted the Genocide Convention. Member countries promised to prevent genocide and to punish it.

What was the Holocaust?Between 1941 and 1945, Nazi Germany and its helpers set out to murder every Jewish person they could reach. They killed about six million.

The court's first full judgment in a genocide case came in 2007. That year, in a case brought by Bosnia and Herzegovina, the court found that the 1995 killings at Srebrenica were genocide. It ruled that Serbia had not committed that genocide itself, but had failed to prevent it and failed to punish those responsible. In 2015, in a case between Croatia and Serbia, the court rejected both sides' genocide claims, because neither proved the intent to destroy.

India's story

India has had a seat on the bench more than once. B. N. Rau served from 1952 to 1953. Nagendra Singh joined in 1973 and was the court's President from 1985 to 1988. R. S. Pathak served from 1989 to 1991. Dalveer Bhandari, once a judge of India's Supreme Court, joined in 2012 and was elected again in 2017, after the United Kingdom withdrew its own candidate.

India has also argued its own case there. In 2017 a Pakistani military court sentenced Kulbhushan Jadhav, an Indian citizen, to death for spying. India said he was a retired Navy officer and denied the charge. India went to the world court, saying Pakistan had not let Indian officials meet him. In July 2019 the judges voted 15 to 1 that Pakistan had broken a treaty on such visits. They ordered Pakistan to review his case properly, but they turned down India's request to set him free.

Genocide law touches India's neighbourhood too. In 2017 about seven lakh Rohingya, a mostly Muslim minority from Myanmar, fled into Bangladesh. Many still live as refugees in crowded camps near the town of Cox's Bazar, which together make up one of the largest refugee settlements in the world.

The numbers that matter

  • - 15 judges, 9-year terms. Every three years, the UN General Assembly and the Security Council elect five of them. A candidate must win a majority in both.
  • - No two from one country. No two judges can come from the same country. If a country in a case has no judge of its own on the bench, it can choose a "judge ad hoc", an extra judge for that case only.
  • - 5 kinds of acts. These are the acts listed in the Convention's definition of genocide. Every one must come with the intent to destroy a group.
  • - 14 years. That is how long the Bosnia case took, from 1993 to 2007. Genocide cases are slow because the evidence is huge.
  • - 0 appeals. A judgment is final. No higher court exists to overturn it.

What people argue about

Is the bar too high? The court says genocide must be proved by "fully conclusive" evidence. Some lawyers and survivors' groups say this makes genocide almost impossible to prove, since leaders rarely write their plans down. Others reply that genocide is the gravest charge a country can face, so the proof must be very strong, or the word loses its meaning.

Can rulings be enforced? The court has no police. If a country ignores a judgment, the other side can turn to the UN Security Council. But any of its five permanent members can block action with a veto. In one famous case in 1986, Nicaragua against the United States, the court ruled against the USA. Later that year the USA vetoed a Council resolution calling on it to obey. Critics say this shows the court's weakness. Supporters say most countries do follow most rulings, because ignoring them costs trust and friends.

Do court cases change behaviour? Some people argue that a ruling years later cannot undo harm already done. Others point out that the court's emergency orders, its record of facts and its official findings shape how the world sees a crisis, and can help victims seek repair.

Myths and mix-ups

"The world court puts leaders in jail." It does not. It judges countries. The separate International Criminal Court, a much newer court, puts individual people on trial.

"Genocide just means a very large number of deaths." In law, numbers alone do not decide it. The test is the intent to destroy a group as such. Many terrible wars involve crimes that are not legally genocide, and those are judged under other laws.

"Any country can be dragged to court for anything." The court can only hear a case when countries have agreed to it, often by signing a treaty like the Genocide Convention.

What it means for you

Most families will never set foot in the Peace Palace. But the idea behind it is the same one your school uses when it settles a fight with rules and a fair hearing instead of fists. The court is the world's attempt to make even powerful governments answer to rules they signed.

It also connects to the human rights you hold just by being born. When a court orders a country to protect a group, you can ask what the judges ask. What happened, who did it, and what did they intend?

Myth or fact?

Myth: The world court can arrest and jail the leaders of a country that commits genocide.

Truth: The International Court of Justice judges countries, not people, and it has no police of its own. Putting individual leaders on trial is the job of criminal courts, such as the separate International Criminal Court, which is also in The Hague.
