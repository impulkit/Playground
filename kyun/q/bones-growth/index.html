How do bones grow and heal?

You were born with around 300 bones. An adult has 206. Nothing went missing, because many of them grew together.

The short answer

Bones grow mostly at their ends. Near each end of a long bone, like the thigh bone, there's a disc of softer tissue called a growth plate. It's made of cartilage, the bendy stuff in your ears and the tip of your nose. Cells in the growth plate keep multiplying, and the new cartilage slowly turns to hard bone, pushing the ends further apart. That makes you taller.

When a bone breaks, the body rebuilds it in stages: first a clot, then a soft bridge of new tissue, then hard bone. In children, a simple broken arm often heals in three to six weeks, much faster than in adults.

Surprising but true

Your skeleton is constantly being taken apart and rebuilt. Over roughly ten years, most of an adult's bone tissue is replaced.

More bones at birth

A newborn has around 300 bones and bone-starts, many still partly made of cartilage. As a child grows, some fuse together. The skull, for example, starts as separate plates with soft spots between them, which lets a baby's head squeeze through at birth. The plates join up over the first years of life. The bones at the bottom of the spine and in the hips also fuse.

By adulthood, the count settles at 206. More than half of them are in the hands and feet.

The growth plates

Growth plates are busiest during babyhood and again during the growth spurt of puberty, when some teenagers shoot up several centimetres in a year. Hormones from the pituitary gland and, later, puberty hormones drive this growth.

Towards the end of the teens, the growth plates turn completely into bone and close. After that, the bones can't get longer. Doctors can look at an X-ray of a child's hand and estimate their "bone age" from how open the growth plates are.

How a break heals

Within hours of a fracture, blood gathers around the break and forms a clot. Over the next days, cells move in and build a soft bridge of cartilage and tough fibres, called a callus. Over several weeks, bone-making cells turn that callus into hard bone.

For months afterwards, the body reshapes the repair, removing extra bone until it looks almost like new. A doctor's job is to line up the broken ends and hold them still, often with a plaster cast, so they heal straight. X-rays show whether it's healing properly.

Children's bones are more flexible than adults', so they sometimes break in a special way called a greenstick fracture. The bone bends and cracks on one side only, like a fresh green twig that won't snap cleanly.

Building strong bones

About 99% of the body's calcium is stored in the bones and teeth. Calcium comes from foods such as milk, curd, paneer, ragi, green leafy vegetables and sesame seeds. Vitamin D, which your skin makes in sunlight, helps the gut absorb that calcium.

Bones also respond to use. Running, jumping, skipping and dancing put healthy stress on bones, and the bones answer by getting denser. People build up most of their bone strength during childhood and the teenage years, reaching a peak around their late twenties.

Bones in space

In the weightlessness of orbit, bones don't have to hold up the body. Astronauts on long missions can lose around 1% of bone mass in parts of the skeleton each month, according to NASA. They exercise for about two hours a day on special machines to slow this down, as explained in space and the body. Back on Earth, their bones slowly rebuild.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Bones are dead, dry things inside your body, like the skeletons in museums.

Truth: Living bones are full of blood vessels, nerves and busy cells. They grow, repair themselves and even store minerals the rest of your body needs.

Try this

Soak a clean, dry chicken bone (ask an adult for one left from a meal) in a jar of vinegar for about five days. The acid slowly removes the hard minerals, leaving the soft, rubbery framework underneath. You'll be able to bend it, which shows how much the minerals give bone its strength.
