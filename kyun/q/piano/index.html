How does a piano make sound?

Press a key and a felt hammer flies up, strikes steel strings and falls back, all in a fraction of a second.

The short answer

A piano is a box of strings that are hit by hammers. When you press a key, it works like a seesaw. The far end of the key rises and flicks a small felt-covered hammer up towards the strings. The hammer strikes, then drops back at once so the strings can ring.

The vibrating strings are thin, so on their own they would be quiet. They pass their vibrations to a large, thin wooden sheet called the soundboard, which shakes a great deal of air. That is what you hear. Let go of the key, and a felt pad called a damper falls onto the strings to stop the sound.

Surprising but true

The strings inside a concert grand piano pull on its iron frame with a combined force of around 20 tonnes.

A piano is full of levers

Each key is a lever, and behind it sits a clever set of more levers called the action. A grand piano's action has dozens of small wooden and felt parts for each key.

The trickiest part is called the escapement. As the hammer nears the string, it is set free and flies the last bit on its own. If it stayed pushed against the string, it would stop the note dead. Thanks to the escapement, a pianist can repeat a note very fast and play from a whisper to a crash.

Loud and soft

Before the piano, the most common keyboard was the harpsichord. Its keys plucked the strings with small hooks, so every note came out at about the same loudness however hard you pressed.

Around 1700, in Florence, an instrument maker named Bartolomeo Cristofori built a keyboard that hit the strings with hammers instead. Press gently and you got a soft note. Press hard and you got a loud one. He called it, in Italian, a "harpsichord with soft and loud". People shortened it to pianoforte, then piano. It was born in the same Italian cities that had led the Renaissance.

Strings under huge tension

A concert grand piano has about 230 strings. The low notes use one or two thick strings wrapped in copper wire. Most middle and high notes use three thinner steel strings tuned to exactly the same note, which makes them louder.

Together, the strings pull on the frame with a force of around 20 tonnes, close to the weight of a loaded lorry. Early wooden frames couldn't take this, so modern pianos have a heavy cast-iron frame. That is one reason a concert grand weighs about half a tonne, as much as seven or eight grown-ups. Shorter, thinner strings vibrate faster and make higher notes, as explained in sound travel.

Pianos get bigger

When Mozart was writing music in the 1780s, his piano had a range of about five octaves, about 61 notes. Composers kept wanting more notes and more power. Beethoven wrote music that seemed to push his pianos to breaking point, and makers responded with stronger frames and extra keys.

By the late 1800s, the 88-key piano had become standard. Companies like Steinway, founded in New York in 1853, built pianos for concert halls and for millions of homes. For a time, owning a piano in the parlour was a sign of a comfortable American family.

Pianos in India and beyond

Pianos arrived in India with Europeans and were found in colonial homes, schools and churches. Many older Hindi films have scenes of a hero singing at a piano.

Today, the piano is one of the most popular instruments for children to learn, from Mumbai to Minneapolis. Many homes now have digital pianos, which use recorded sounds of real piano notes instead of strings. They are lighter and cheaper, and they never need tuning, but pianists can still feel the difference.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The piano is a string instrument, so it has nothing in common with drums.

Truth: The piano's sound comes from strings, but they are struck by hammers, as a drum is struck. That is why music experts often describe the piano as both a string and a percussion instrument.

Try this

Stretch a few rubber bands around an open box. Pluck one with your finger, then tap it with the rubber end of a pencil. Plucking is how a harpsichord makes sound, and striking is how a piano does it. Listen for which sound is softer and which is sharper.
