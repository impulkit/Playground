What did ancient people scribble on walls?

In 78 BC, a Roman named Gaius Pumidius Dipilus scratched "was here" on a wall in Pompeii, and you can still read it.

The short answer

Ancient people wrote on walls a lot, and much of it sounds very familiar. They scratched their names and the date. They left jokes, love notes, insults and drawings of gladiators. They advertised shows and backed candidates in elections. Travellers carved "I was here" on famous monuments, just as some tourists still do.

These scribbles are a gift to historians. Official carvings tell us what kings wanted remembered. Graffiti tells us what ordinary people were thinking on an ordinary Tuesday.

Surprising but true

Viking visitors carved their names into the stone of Hagia Sophia in Istanbul over 1,000 years ago, and one of the scribbles still reads "Halfdan".

Pompeii, the wall of the people

When Mount Vesuvius buried Pompeii in AD 79, it also preserved its walls. More than 10,000 inscriptions have been found there. Most are scratched into plaster with something sharp, or painted.

One reads, in Latin, "Gaius Pumidius Dipilus was here", with a date that works out to 3 October 78 BC. Painted notices backed candidates for town jobs, a bit like posters before an Indian election. Someone wrote a price list. Someone else practised the alphabet, letter by letter. Others drew gladiators, boats and animals.

Some messages are too rude to print. Others are sweet: lovers wrote each other's names, and one person simply greeted a friend by name. Near the snack bars where Romans bought hot food, customers complained about the wine.

Soldiers who signed a pharaoh's leg

At Abu Simbel in southern Egypt, giant statues of Ramesses II sit in front of a temple. In 591 BC, Greek soldiers serving an Egyptian king marched past on a campaign. Some of them carved their names and a short note into the leg of one statue.

The statues were already about 700 years old by then. To those soldiers, Ramesses was ancient history, the way the age of Ashoka feels ancient to us.

Vikings in a great church

Hagia Sophia in Istanbul was one of the grandest buildings in the medieval world. Up on a marble balcony, someone carved letters in runes, the alphabet of the Vikings. Experts think it says "Halfdan carved these runes", and they date it to around the 9th century. Many Vikings worked as guards for the emperor in what was then Constantinople. Perhaps Halfdan got bored during a long ceremony.

Vikings left scribbles in other places too. In the 1100s, a group of them broke into Maeshowe, a 5,000-year-old stone tomb in the Orkney Islands of Scotland. They carved around 30 messages in runes on its walls. Some boast about treasure, some show off who was best at carving runes, and one is a drawing of a dragon-like beast.

Poems on a mirror

In Sri Lanka, a palace once stood on top of Sigiriya, a rock nearly 200 m high. Along the path up runs a wall that was once polished so smooth that people could see their reflection in it.

Visitors loved it so much that, roughly between the 8th and 10th centuries, they wrote around 1,800 short poems and comments on its surface. Many describe the beautiful women painted on the rock above. Some are in an early form of the Sinhala language, so the wall helps scholars study how it changed. Today visitors are banned from writing on the Mirror Wall. Guards watch the path, and anyone caught scratching a name can be fined or even jailed.

Then and now

Graffiti was often illegal in the past too, and many people disliked it. Today, carving into ancient monuments is banned almost everywhere, and it can damage things that have lasted for thousands of years. Some modern street art, painted with an owner's permission, is now protected and even sold for huge sums. Whether the secret paintings of Banksy count as art or vandalism is still argued about, which is exactly what Romans grumbled about too.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Graffiti is a modern habit that began with spray paint.

Truth: People have scratched names, jokes, drawings and complaints into walls for thousands of years. Spray cans arrived only in the 20th century.

Try this

Make a "graffiti wall" on a big sheet of paper stuck to a door at home or school (with permission). Invite everyone to add a short message, drawing or joke over a week. At the end, look at it like an archaeologist and work out what it tells a future reader about your family or class.
