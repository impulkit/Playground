What was Pangaea?

The first dinosaurs could have walked from what is now India to what is now New York without ever getting their feet wet.

The short answer

Pangaea was a supercontinent: a single giant landmass that held almost all of Earth's land. It came together roughly 335 to 300 million years ago and stayed in one piece for over a hundred million years. A single vast ocean, called Panthalassa, surrounded it.

Then, roughly 200 to 175 million years ago, Pangaea began to crack apart. The pieces slowly moved away from each other and became the continents on today's map. They are still moving now, a few centimetres a year.

The name comes from Greek words meaning "all land". It was used by Alfred Wegener, the scientist best known for arguing that the continents drift.

Surprising but true

India was once wedged between Africa, Madagascar, Antarctica and Australia, thousands of kilometres south of where it is now.

A world with one coastline

Pangaea was shaped a bit like a giant letter C, wrapped around a sea called Tethys. Most of North America, Europe and Asia sat in the north. South America, Africa, Antarctica, Australia and India were packed together in the south.

With so much land in one lump, the middle was far from the ocean. Moist sea air rarely reached it, so huge areas were hot, dry desert, much larger than any of the deserts we have today. Near the coasts, the climate was wetter, with forests of ferns and early conifers.

Life on the supercontinent

Pangaea was home to some of the biggest moments in the history of life. About 252 million years ago, giant volcanic eruptions in what is now Siberia set off the Great Dying, which wiped out most species in the sea and many on land.

Life recovered, and about 230 million years ago the first dinosaurs appeared. With one connected landmass, animals could spread almost everywhere. That is why fossils of closely related early dinosaurs and reptiles turn up on continents that are now oceans apart.

One of the best examples is Lystrosaurus, a stocky, pig-sized plant-eater with two short tusks. It survived the Great Dying and became one of the most common land animals for a while afterwards. Its bones have been dug up in South Africa, in India's Damodar valley, in China and even in Antarctica. An animal that size could never have swum between those places. It simply walked across one continent, long before the oceans opened between them.

How it broke up

Pangaea did not split all at once. First, a long crack opened between North America and Africa. Sea water flooded in, and the Atlantic Ocean was born. Pangaea split into a northern half, Laurasia, and a southern half, Gondwana.

Gondwana then broke into pieces too. India pulled away from Africa, then from Madagascar, and raced north at speeds of up to about 15 cm a year, which is fast for a continent. Around 50 million years ago it crashed into Asia and pushed up the Himalayas. Some of India's dinosaurs, like those found in Gujarat and Madhya Pradesh, lived on this island continent during its long voyage.

The next Pangaea

Supercontinents seem to come and go in a long cycle. Before Pangaea there was Rodinia, about a billion years ago, and perhaps others even earlier.

Many geologists think the continents will join again in about 200 to 300 million years. They disagree about how. Some models close the Atlantic, some close the Pacific, and some push everything together around the North Pole. These future maps have names like Pangaea Proxima and Amasia, and nobody will be around to check which one is right.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pangaea was the first and only supercontinent.

Truth: Earth's continents have joined and split several times. An older supercontinent called Rodinia came together about a billion years ago, long before Pangaea.
