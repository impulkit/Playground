Who are the Sherpas?

In May 2026, a Sherpa named Kami Rita stood on top of Mount Everest for the 32nd time, more than anyone in history.

The short answer

The Sherpas are a people of the high Himalaya. Most live in the Solukhumbu district of Nepal, in the valleys around Mount Everest. Others live in Sikkim and Darjeeling in India, and in Tibet. They have their own language, related to Tibetan, and most follow Tibetan Buddhism.

The name means "people from the east". Their ancestors crossed from eastern Tibet into Nepal about 500 years ago, over high mountain passes, and settled on land few others wanted.

For the past century, the world has known Sherpas mainly as the climbers who make Everest expeditions possible. They fix ropes, carry loads, guide climbers and often save lives.

Surprising but true

The word Sherpa means "people from the east". Their ancestors moved from eastern Tibet to the Everest region of Nepal about 500 years ago.

Life in the high valleys

Sherpa villages, such as Namche Bazaar and Khumjung, sit between about 3,000 and 4,000 metres above the sea. For centuries, families grew potatoes and barley, kept yaks, and traded salt and wool across the passes with Tibet.

Buddhism is part of daily life. Prayer flags flap on bridges and passes. Stones carved with prayers line the paths, and people walk past them on the left. Tengboche Monastery, with Everest rising behind it, is one of the most important in the region.

Bodies built for thin air

High up, each breath carries less oxygen. At Everest's summit, about 8,849 metres, the air holds roughly a third of the oxygen at sea level. Most visitors get headaches and breathlessness at Base Camp. Some fall seriously ill.

Sherpas cope better. Scientists studying them have found that their bodies seem to use oxygen more efficiently, getting more energy from less. Blood flows well through their smallest vessels. Over thousands of years of high-mountain living, their ancestors developed genes that help with altitude.

They still feel the cold and the thin air, and many still use bottled oxygen near the top. But it gives them an edge.

Tenzing Norgay and the first ascent

On 29 May 1953, Tenzing Norgay, a Sherpa who lived in Darjeeling, and Edmund Hillary of New Zealand became the first people known to stand on top of Everest. Tenzing had already been on several Everest attempts, including one in 1952 when he climbed higher than anyone before.

Asked later who reached the top first, the two men gave the same answer for years: they got there together, as a team. Tenzing went on to train young climbers at the Himalayan Mountaineering Institute in Darjeeling, founded in 1954.

Record breakers

Today, Sherpas hold many of Everest's biggest records. Kami Rita Sherpa reached the summit for the 32nd time in May 2026, more than any other person. Lhakpa Sherpa, a woman who grew up in the Makalu region, reached the top for the 11th time on the same day in May 2026, the most for any woman.

Sherpa climbers have also set records for speed and for climbing without bottled oxygen. In 2021, a team of ten Nepali climbers, most of them Sherpas, became the first to climb K2, the world's second-highest mountain, in winter.

The risks they carry

Mountain work pays far better than most jobs in the region, so many young Sherpa men take it on. But it is dangerous. Climbing guides cross the Khumbu Icefall many more times than their clients, carrying gear through a maze of moving ice. In April 2014, an avalanche there killed 16 Nepali mountain workers, most of them Sherpas.

After that disaster, many Sherpas demanded better pay, insurance and respect. Sherpa-owned companies now run many Everest expeditions themselves. Some families also send their children abroad to study, and the next generation may choose very different jobs.

High above their heads, a bird named the bar-headed goose flies over the Himalaya each year on migration, crossing passes at heights where most humans would struggle to stand.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The word Sherpa is just another name for a mountain porter or guide.

Truth: The Sherpas are an ethnic group with their own language, culture and history. Many work in mountaineering, but many others are farmers, teachers, doctors and business owners. Some porters and guides on Everest belong to other groups entirely.
