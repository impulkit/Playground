How do decimals work?

Neeraj Chopra won India's first Olympic gold in athletics with a throw of 87.58 metres, and every digit after that dot mattered.

The short answer

Decimals are a way of writing parts of a whole using our ordinary base-ten digits. The decimal point separates the whole number from the parts. The first digit after the point counts tenths, the next hundredths, the next thousandths, and so on.

So 3.75 means 3 wholes, 7 tenths and 5 hundredths, which is the same as three and three quarters. Decimals are easier to add, subtract and compare than fractions, which is why we use them for money, sports times and measurements. Your height, your weight and the price of a samosa can all be written as decimals.

Surprising but true

In much of Europe, people write a comma instead of a dot, so three and a half is written 3,5.

Places that shrink by ten

In a whole number like 333, each 3 is worth ten times the one to its right: 300, 30 and 3. Decimals simply keep the pattern going past the ones place. The next 3 to the right is worth three tenths, then three hundredths, then three thousandths.

That is why 0.1 is the same as 1/10, 0.01 is 1/100 and 0.5 is 5/10, or one half. Some fractions do not fit neatly. One third becomes 0.3333..., with threes going on forever. Mathematicians write that with a little line or dot over the 3.

Who put the dot in?

Chinese mathematicians were using decimal fractions over 1,500 years ago, and Arab mathematicians such as al-Kashi in Samarkand used them with great skill in the 1400s. The idea took longer to reach ordinary people.

In 1585, a Flemish engineer named Simon Stevin published a short booklet called The Tenth. He argued that everyone, from merchants to soldiers, should use decimal fractions because they were so much easier. He even suggested decimal money and measures. His way of writing decimals was awkward, with little circled numbers, and the dot came into use later. Different countries chose differently. India, the USA and Britain use a dot, while much of Europe and South America uses a comma.

Decimal money

The United States was one of the first countries to make its money fully decimal. From 1792, a dollar was divided into 100 cents, rather than awkward fractions. That made sums much simpler for shopkeepers and banks.

India's rupee used to be split into 16 annas, each worth 4 paise. On 1 April 1957, India switched to decimal coins, with 100 naye paise, or "new paise", to the rupee. Britain followed in 1971, giving up a system where a pound had 20 shillings and each shilling 12 pennies. Today almost every currency in the world is decimal.

Measuring to the hundredth

The metric system is built on decimals. A metre has 100 centimetres and 1,000 millimetres, so 1.5 metres is simply 150 centimetres. A kilogram has 1,000 grams, so 0.25 kilograms is 250 grams.

In sport, decimals can decide everything. At the Tokyo Olympics, held in 2021, Neeraj Chopra won the javelin with a throw of 87.58 metres, India's first Olympic gold in athletics. Usain Bolt's 100-metre world record, set in 2009, is 9.58 seconds. Races are timed to hundredths of a second, and sometimes the winner is decided by just 0.01 seconds.

A tiny mistake that grew

Rounding decimals can cause trouble when it happens again and again. In 1982, a stock exchange in Vancouver, Canada, started a new index, a single number showing how share prices were doing. Each time it was updated, the computer chopped off the last decimal places instead of rounding properly. After about two years of thousands of updates a day, the index had lost roughly half its true value. When it was fixed, it jumped up overnight. Small digits, it turned out, can add up.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A decimal with more digits is always bigger, so 0.25 is more than 0.5.

Truth: Each place after the point is worth ten times less than the one before. 0.5 means 5 tenths, while 0.25 means 2 tenths and 5 hundredths. So 0.5 is bigger. Adding a zero to the end, as in 0.50, does not change the value at all.
