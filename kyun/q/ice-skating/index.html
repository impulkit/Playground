Why is ice slippery?

People have skated on ice for thousands of years, and scientists still argue about exactly why it's so slippery.

The short answer

Most solids aren't slippery. Wood, stone and metal grip your shoes. Ice is different, because its surface doesn't behave like the rest of it.

The water molecules deep inside a block of ice are locked in a tidy pattern. The ones on the very top have no neighbours above them, so they're held less tightly. They wobble and roll about in a thin, loose layer only a few molecules thick. On top of that, when a skate or a shoe slides over ice, friction creates a little heat, which can melt a film of water.

Together, these make a slick surface that a skate blade glides over. Exactly how much each one contributes is still being studied.

Surprising but true

Ice isn't most slippery right at its melting point. Experiments suggest it is slipperiest at around minus 7 °C.

The pressure idea

For more than a century, many textbooks said something different. They explained that a skater's weight, pressing down through a thin blade, melts the ice under it. The water then acts like oil.

It's true that pressure lowers the melting point of ice. But the effect is tiny. Even a heavy skater on a narrow blade lowers it by only a fraction of a degree. People skate happily on ice at minus 10 °C or colder, and you can slip on ice wearing wide, flat shoes. Pressure melting can't explain either. Friction heating helps explain why a moving skate glides better than one standing still, but it doesn't explain why ice feels slippery the moment you step on it.

Faraday's surface layer

In the 1850s, the English scientist Michael Faraday noticed that two ice cubes pressed together stick, even well below freezing. He suggested that ice has a thin, watery layer on its surface. For many years, other scientists doubted it.

Modern instruments that can study surfaces molecule by molecule have shown he was largely right. In 2018, scientists in Germany and the Netherlands reported that the top molecules of ice are loosely bound and can roll across the surface, almost like tiny ball bearings. They also found ice was slipperiest at about minus 7 °C. Colder than that, the surface grows stickier. In Antarctica, where ice can be colder than minus 40 °C, explorers found that sledges dragged across the snow almost as if it were sand.

Skates through history

People have been skating for a very long time. Archaeologists have found skates made from animal leg bones in northern Europe, some probably several thousand years old. People tied them to their feet and pushed themselves along with sticks. Metal blades, which bite into the ice and let skaters steer, spread much later, especially in the Netherlands, where frozen canals made winter roads.

Today figure skating, speed skating and ice hockey are all part of the Winter Olympics. The best speed skaters reach over 50 km/h, faster than many city cars.

Skating on the roof of India

In winter, ponds and lakes in Ladakh freeze solid, and ice hockey has become one of the region's favourite sports. Children play on natural ice with hand-me-down skates, and teams from Ladakh have produced many of the players in India's national ice hockey squads, including the women's team.

Ice that's too smooth and wet, though, can be a hazard. Roads and pavements in cold places become dangerous in winter, which is why people spread grit and salt on icy roads. The same slippery surface that delights a skater can send a walker flying.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ice skates glide because the pressure of the blade melts the ice.

Truth: The pressure only lowers the melting point by a tiny amount. Ice stays slippery far below freezing, where pressure melting can't happen. A loose surface layer and friction heating are better explanations, though scientists still argue about the details.

Try this

Take two ice cubes straight from the freezer and two that have sat out for five minutes. Push each across a plate with one finger and compare how easily they slide. Then dry one cube with a paper towel and try again. Notice how the wet, slightly melted surface changes the slide.
