What is the difference between snow, sleet and hail?

Snow and hail are both frozen water, but hail is born inside thunderstorms, often on hot days.

The short answer

All three are frozen water falling from clouds, but they form in different ways. Snow is made of ice crystals that grow inside cold clouds and fall without melting. Hail is lumps of ice that grow inside thunderstorms, carried up and down by strong winds until they are too heavy to stay up.

Sleet means different things in different countries. In the USA, it means small ice pellets: snowflakes that melted on the way down and froze again before landing. In Britain and India, it usually means a slushy mix of rain and snow falling together.

Surprising but true

The heaviest hailstone on record, about 1 kg, fell in Gopalganj, Bangladesh, in 1986, about as heavy as a full one-litre water bottle.

How snow forms

High in a cloud, where it is well below freezing, water vapour turns straight into ice on tiny specks of dust. A crystal forms. More vapour freezes onto it, and it grows into a snowflake. Because of the way water molecules fit together, ice crystals have six sides, so almost every snowflake has six arms.

If the air all the way to the ground stays cold, the flakes arrive as snow. Different clouds and temperatures make different kinds of flakes. In India, that happens every winter in the high Himalaya, in places like Gulmarg in Kashmir and Auli in Uttarakhand. In the USA, cities like Buffalo, near the Great Lakes, can get well over two metres of snow in a winter.

The snowflake man

Wilson Bentley was a farmer in Jericho, Vermont. In 1885, at the age of 19, he became the first person known to photograph a single snowflake, using a camera attached to a microscope. Over his life, he photographed more than 5,000 of them. He found that each one had its own pattern, and his pictures helped make the idea that "no two snowflakes are alike" famous.

Sleet and freezing rain

Sometimes snow falls through a layer of warmer air partway down, and melts into rain. If there is a cold layer close to the ground, the drops freeze again into small, hard pellets of ice. Americans call these ice pellets sleet. They bounce when they hit the ground.

If the cold layer near the ground is very thin, the raindrops don't have time to freeze in the air. They freeze the moment they touch something cold, like a road or a branch. That is freezing rain, and it can coat everything in a glassy layer of ice.

How hail grows

Hail needs a thunderstorm, the kind of storm that also brings lightning, with powerful winds blowing upwards inside it, called updrafts. A small ice pellet is carried high into the freezing top of the cloud, where supercooled droplets freeze onto it. It falls, gets swept up again, and adds another layer. The stronger the updraft, the bigger the hailstone can grow before it falls.

That's why hail is most common in warm seasons, when thunderstorms are strongest. In India, hailstorms often strike in March, April and May, damaging crops of wheat, mangoes and vegetables. In the USA, the central plains, from Texas up to South Dakota, get some of the most frequent hail in the world.

Record hailstones

The biggest hailstone measured in the United States fell in Vivian, South Dakota, in 2010. It was about 20 cm across, about the size of a volleyball, and weighed close to 0.9 kg. The heaviest hailstone on record anywhere fell in Gopalganj, Bangladesh, in 1986, and weighed about 1 kg.

Big hail can dent cars and break windows, so if hail starts falling, the best place is indoors, away from windows.

Snow is not white

A snowflake is made of clear ice. A pile of snow looks white because light bounces around between all the tiny crystals and comes back out mixed together, all colours at once. Deep snow and glacier ice can look blue, because they soak up a little red light. Hail can look white or clear, depending on how much air was trapped as each layer froze.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Hail only falls in cold weather.

Truth: Hail comes from thunderstorms, which are most common in warm months. In India, hailstorms are most frequent in the spring, before the monsoon, and in the USA in late spring and summer.

Try this

Make a "hailstone" in layers. Freeze a small bead or button in a spoonful of water in an ice tray. Once frozen, add another spoonful of water on top and freeze again. Repeat a few times. Each freeze adds a layer, a slow version of how a hailstone grows as it goes up and down in a storm.
