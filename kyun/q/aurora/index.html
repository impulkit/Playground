What makes the sky glow green at the poles?

Curtains of green and purple light, dancing in the sky.

The short answer

Auroras happen when particles from the Sun hit our air and make it glow.

Earth's magnetic field guides them towards the poles, so auroras are usually seen near the Arctic and Antarctica.

During a huge solar storm in May 2024, an aurora was photographed from Hanle in Ladakh!

The glowing gases are the same ones you breathe. Oxygen makes most of the green and red, and nitrogen adds blue and purple. Together they form rippling curtains, arcs and rays that can stretch across the whole sky.

Surprising but true

In May 2024, a reddish aurora glowed over Ladakh, something seen from India very rarely.

A wind from the Sun

The Sun constantly blows out a thin stream of electrically charged particles, mostly protons and electrons. This is called the solar wind. It rushes through space at around 400 km every second, fast enough to cross from Kanyakumari to Kashmir in about 10 seconds.

Sometimes the Sun hurls out a much bigger cloud of charged gas, called a coronal mass ejection. If one is aimed at Earth, it arrives about one to three days later. India's Aditya-L1 spacecraft watches the Sun from 1.5 million km away and helps scientists see such storms coming.

Earth's invisible shield

Deep inside Earth, molten iron swirling in the outer core creates a magnetic field that stretches far out into space. It acts like a shield, steering most of the solar wind around our planet.

But the field lines curve down into Earth near the North and South Poles. Some particles get trapped and are funnelled down along these lines. About 100 to 300 km above the ground, they crash into atoms of oxygen and nitrogen and give them extra energy. The atoms quickly get rid of it as flashes of light. Billions of these flashes together make an aurora.

The result is usually an oval ring of light around each magnetic pole. Places under the ring, like northern Norway, Iceland, Alaska and northern Canada, see auroras on many clear, dark nights.

Colours and names

The most common colour is a pale green, made by oxygen around 100 to 250 km up. Above that, where the air is extremely thin, oxygen glows a deep red. Nitrogen, lower down, adds blue and purple fringes. Aurora photos often look brighter than the real thing, because cameras collect light longer than our eyes do.

The northern lights are called the aurora borealis, after Aurora, the Roman goddess of the dawn, and Boreas, the Greek name for the north wind. The southern lights are the aurora australis. Auroras in the north and south often happen at the same time, almost mirror images of each other.

When auroras reach India

When a really powerful solar storm hits, the oval of light spreads far from the poles. On 10 and 11 May 2024, during the strongest geomagnetic storm in more than 20 years, auroras were seen as far south as Mexico and Florida.

In India, cameras at the Indian Astronomical Observatory at Hanle, in Ladakh, recorded a reddish glow on the northern horizon. Ladakh is high, dry and dark, so faint light is easier to catch there. Seeing an aurora from India is very rare.

Storms like these can also disturb radio signals, upset GPS and damage satellites, so space weather forecasters keep a close eye on the Sun.

Auroras on other worlds

Earth isn't the only planet with auroras. The Hubble Space Telescope has photographed glowing rings around the poles of Jupiter and Saturn. Jupiter's auroras are hundreds of times more powerful than Earth's, fed partly by material from its volcanic moon Io.

Like lightning, an aurora is electricity lighting up the air, but on a scale large enough to be seen from space. Astronauts on the space station sometimes look down on green curtains of light flowing beneath them.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Auroras only happen in winter.

Truth: Auroras happen all year round. Near the poles, summer nights are so short and bright that the glow is hard to see, so people mostly notice them in the long, dark winter.

Try this

Put a bar magnet under a sheet of paper and gently move a small compass around it, drawing an arrow wherever the needle points. Join up the arrows. The lines crowd together at the two ends of the magnet, just as Earth's magnetic field lines crowd together near the poles, where auroras appear.
