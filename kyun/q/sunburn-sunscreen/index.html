How does sunscreen protect your skin?

A sunburn isn't a heat burn at all. It is your skin reacting to invisible rays that have damaged the DNA inside its cells.

The short answer

Sunlight carries invisible rays called ultraviolet, or UV. Too much UV damages the DNA inside skin cells. Your body reacts by sending extra blood to the area, which makes it red, hot and sore. Damaged cells die, and a few days later the skin peels. That is a sunburn.

Sunscreen puts a thin layer between your skin and those rays. Some sunscreens contain minerals, such as zinc oxide, that block UV at the surface. Others contain chemicals that soak up UV and turn its energy into a tiny amount of harmless heat. Either way, less UV gets through to the living cells underneath.

Surprising but true

Fresh snow can bounce back up to 80 per cent of the sun's ultraviolet rays, which is why skiers and mountaineers can burn under their chins.

Two kinds of invisible rays

The UV that reaches the ground comes in two main kinds. UVB rays are shorter and do most of the burning. UVA rays are longer, reach deeper into the skin and add to wrinkles and long-term damage. UVA can even pass through window glass.

Most UV is stopped high in the sky by the ozone layer. Enough gets through to matter, especially near the equator, high in the mountains and around midday. Much of India gets very strong UV for many months of the year.

What is the ozone layer?If you squeezed all the ozone in the sky down to ground level, it would make a layer only about 3 mm thick. That thin shield protects all life on land.

What the numbers on the bottle mean

SPF stands for "sun protection factor". It measures protection against UVB. An SPF 15 sunscreen blocks about 93 per cent of UVB, SPF 30 about 97 per cent and SPF 50 about 98 per cent. No sunscreen blocks all of it.

The words "broad spectrum" mean it also guards against UVA. Those tests assume a thick layer. Most people use far less than the testers do, and sunscreen rubs off with sweat, towels and water. That is why doctors say to apply it generously and again every two hours, and after swimming.

A common guide for an adult's whole body is about a shot glass full, roughly 30 millilitres, or about a teaspoon for the face and neck. Children need less, but the idea is the same. A thin smear does far less than the label promises.

How your skin fights back

Skin has its own defence: a dark pigment called melanin. It sits inside skin cells like a little umbrella over the DNA. People with darker skin colour have more melanin, which is why they burn less easily.

A tan is skin making extra melanin after UV has already done some damage. It is a sign of injury, not of health. A tan gives only a little extra protection, about the same as a very weak sunscreen. Summer sun also brings out freckles, which are small patches of extra melanin.

Shade, hats and the UV index

Sunscreen works best as one tool among several. Shade, a wide hat, sunglasses and loose clothes with long sleeves all block UV well, and they don't wash off in the pool. Many weather apps now show the UV index, a scale created with help from the World Health Organization. At 3 or above, protection is a good idea. In Indian summers it often climbs past 10.

Sand and water reflect some UV back up at you, and fresh snow reflects most of it. The sun isn't all bad, though. Your skin uses a little UVB to make vitamin D, which keeps bones strong. A short time outdoors is enough for most children, without getting burned.

The modern SPF scale was popularised in the 1970s by an Austrian chemist, Franz Greiter. According to his company's story, he had been badly burned while climbing a mountain in the Alps called Piz Buin, and he named his sunscreen after it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: People with dark skin can't get sunburned and don't need sun protection.

Truth: More melanin gives darker skin extra protection, so it burns less easily, but it can still burn in strong sun. Doctors advise everyone to protect their skin and eyes when the UV index is high.

Try this

Ask an adult for a piece of dark coloured paper. Draw a shape on one half with sunscreen, put a coin on the other half, and leave the paper in strong sunlight for a few hours. When you bring it in, the paper under the sunscreen and under the coin should have faded less than the rest.
