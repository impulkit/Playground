How does archery work?

An Olympic archer hits a gold circle the size of a CD from 70 metres away, as far as six school buses parked end to end.

The short answer

Archery is the sport of shooting arrows with a bow at a target. A bow is a spring. When the archer pulls back the string, the bent limbs of the bow store energy. When the string is released, that energy flings the arrow forwards at around 200 km/h.

In Olympic archery, archers shoot at a round target 70 metres away. The target has ten rings. The tiny gold centre is worth 10 points and is about 12 centimetres across, roughly the size of a CD. Hitting it again and again takes strength, perfect form and a very calm mind.

Surprising but true

Sheetal Devi, an Indian archer born without arms, shoots with her feet and won a Paralympic bronze medal in Paris in 2024.

A very old invention

People have used bows for tens of thousands of years. Stone points found in Sibudu Cave in South Africa, about 64,000 years old, may be the oldest arrowheads known. Bows let Stone Age hunters catch animals from a safe distance.

Later, bows were used in war. English longbowmen were famous at the Battle of Agincourt in 1415. Mongol riders shot arrows from galloping horses. In Japan, a style called kyudo turned archery into a calm, almost spiritual practice.

Arjuna and Ekalavya

In India, archery is woven into old stories. In the Mahabharata, the teacher Drona tests his students by asking them to shoot a wooden bird in a tree. Only Arjuna says he can see nothing but the bird's eye. It is still a famous lesson in focus.

Another story is about Ekalavya, a tribal boy refused as a student. He taught himself by practising in front of a clay statue of Drona and became a brilliant archer. The story raises questions about fairness that people still discuss.

Bows of different kinds

Olympic archers use the recurve bow, whose tips curve away from the archer. This shape stores more energy. The bow has a sight to help aim and stabiliser rods to keep it steady.

The compound bow, invented in the USA in the 1960s, uses wheels called cams and cables. It makes the bow easier to hold at full draw. Compound archery is not yet in the Olympics, but has its own world championships, and Indian compound archers such as Jyothi Surekha Vennam and Aditi Swami have won world titles.

The science of a good shot

Every shot follows the same steps: stance, nock the arrow, draw, anchor the string at the chin, aim, release and follow through. Tiny differences matter. Over 70 metres, a wobble of a fraction of a millimetre at the bow can mean missing the gold.

Arrows begin to fall as soon as they leave the bow, so gravity must be allowed for. Wind pushes arrows sideways too. Archers train their breathing and try to keep their heart rate calm.

India's archers

India has many fine archers. Deepika Kumari, the daughter of an autorickshaw driver in Jharkhand, became world number one in 2012. But India has not yet won an Olympic archery medal. In Paris in 2024, Dhiraj Bommadevara and Ankita Bhakat came fourth in the mixed team event.

In archery, the Paralympics brought a breakthrough. In 2024 Harvinder Singh won India's first Paralympic archery gold. Sheetal Devi, born without arms, holds the bow with her foot and pulls the string back using her shoulder and a tab gripped in her mouth. She won bronze with Rakesh Kumar in the mixed team event, and photos of her shooting were shared around the world. In Bhutan, archery is the national sport, and village contests come with songs, dances and cheerful teasing.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Archers aim straight at the bullseye.

Truth: Arrows start dropping as soon as they leave the bow, so archers set their sights to aim slightly above the target. The further the target, the more they adjust for gravity.

Try this

Make a mini target by drawing rings on paper and taping it to a wall. Stand two metres away and throw a soft ball or a rolled sock at it ten times. Record each score from 1 to 10. Move back a metre and try again. Notice how small changes in your arm make big misses at longer distances.
