How do we map the ocean floor?

We have sharper maps of the surface of Mars than of most of the floor of our own oceans.

The short answer

Most of the ocean floor is too deep and dark to photograph, so we map it with sound. A ship sends pulses of sound down into the water and listens for the echoes. Sound travels through seawater at about 1,500 m a second. By timing how long each echo takes to return, a computer works out the depth.

Modern ships use sonar that sends out a fan of sound beams at once, sweeping a strip of sea floor several kilometres wide. But ships move slowly and the ocean is vast. By 2024, only about a quarter of the sea floor had been mapped this way. The rest we know only roughly.

Surprising but true

Satellites can map underwater mountains without seeing them. A big seamount's gravity pulls extra water towards it, making a gentle bump on the sea surface above.

From ropes to pings

For most of history, sailors measured depth with a weighted rope called a lead line. They lowered it until it hit bottom, then counted the marks on the rope. In the deep ocean, a single measurement could take hours.

During the 1870s, the British ship HMS Challenger sailed around the world doing exactly that. Near Guam in the Pacific, its crew measured a depth of more than 8 km, hinting at the trench now called the Challenger Deep, the deepest point in the ocean.

In the 1900s, echo sounders replaced ropes. Ships could now take a depth reading every few seconds while sailing along. Navies used the same idea to hunt for submarines, and the maps they gathered began to reveal the shape of the sea floor.

How did we discover that continents move?A weather scientist said the continents were drifting. Experts laughed at him for decades, until maps of the sea floor proved him right.

The woman who found a crack in the world

In the 1950s, an American scientist named Marie Tharp worked at Columbia University in New York. Women were not then allowed on the research ships, so her colleague Bruce Heezen collected depth readings at sea, and Tharp turned thousands of numbers into drawings.

As she plotted the middle of the Atlantic Ocean, she noticed a deep valley running down the centre of a long underwater mountain range. She suggested the sea floor was splitting apart there. At first Heezen dismissed it as "girl talk". The evidence won, and her maps helped convince scientists that continents drift. The Library of Congress now keeps her maps.

Mountains bigger than the Himalaya

The mountain range Tharp mapped is part of the mid-ocean ridge, which winds around the globe like the seam on a cricket ball. It runs for about 65,000 km, making it the longest mountain range on Earth. Most of it lies under 2 km or more of water.

The sea floor also has deep trenches, vast flat plains of mud and tens of thousands of underwater volcanoes called seamounts. Satellites help find the biggest ones. A large seamount's gravity pulls a little extra water towards it, making a gentle bump on the sea surface that satellites can measure. The bump may be only a couple of metres high, spread over many kilometres, far too gentle for a sailor to notice. Satellites measure it by bouncing radar off the water.

Filling in the gaps

In 2017, a project called Seabed 2030 set out to map the whole ocean floor in detail by the year 2030. It collects data from research ships, fishing boats, navies and even robot boats that sail for weeks with no crew.

Better maps matter. They help ships sail safely, show where tsunamis might travel, guide where undersea internet cables are laid and reveal homes for deep-sea animals. Every new survey still turns up mountains and canyons nobody has ever seen.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The whole ocean floor has already been mapped in detail, like streets on an online map.

Truth: By 2024, only about a quarter of the sea floor had been mapped to modern standards. The rest is known only roughly, from satellites that can miss anything smaller than a few kilometres across.

Try this

Stand about 50 big steps from a tall wall and clap sharply, listening for the echo. Move closer and clap again. The echo returns sooner as you get closer, which is exactly how a ship's sonar measures the depth of the sea.
