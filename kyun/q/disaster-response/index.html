What happens after a disaster?

In 1999, a super cyclone in Odisha killed about 10,000 people. When Cyclone Phailin hit the same coast in 2013, early warnings and mass evacuations kept the death toll to a few dozen.

The short answer

What happens after a disaster follows a rough order. First comes rescue, getting people out of danger and treating the injured. Then relief: food, clean water, shelter and medicine for survivors. After that comes recovery, which means rebuilding homes, roads, schools and livelihoods. That part can take years.

Before any of this, there is preparation. Good warnings, practice drills and strong buildings save more lives than any rescue. In India, disaster work is led by the National Disaster Management Authority and carried out by state teams and the National Disaster Response Force. In the US, the federal agency FEMA supports states and towns.

Surprising but true

Most people pulled alive from collapsed buildings after an earthquake are rescued by neighbours and passers-by in the first hours, before professional teams arrive.

The first hours

The first 72 hours matter most. Trapped people need air, water and medical help quickly. The very first rescuers are usually neighbours, shopkeepers, teachers and passers-by, who dig through rubble with their hands or wade into floodwater with ropes.

Then trained teams arrive. In India, the NDRF has battalions placed around the country so they can reach a disaster quickly. They bring cutting tools, boats, sniffer dogs, and cameras on poles that can look into gaps in the rubble. Fire services, police and the armed forces join in, using helicopters to reach cut-off villages.

Food, water and a roof

Once people are safe, they need the basics. Dirty water can spread diseases like cholera, so relief teams bring purifying tablets, tankers and pumps. Camps are set up in schools, stadiums or tents. Families who have lost everything also need simple things, like cooking pots, sanitary pads and a phone charger.

Charities and groups like the Red Cross hand out food, blankets and hygiene kits. Doctors check for injuries and illness. Volunteers help families find relatives who were separated, sometimes using lists on noticeboards and, today, phone apps.

Warnings that worked

In October 1999, a super cyclone struck Odisha. Warnings did not reach many villages in time, and about 10,000 people died. Odisha then built hundreds of cyclone shelters, set up volunteer teams in villages and practised evacuations.

When Cyclone Phailin approached in October 2013, the India Meteorological Department tracked it days ahead using weather satellites. About 10 lakh people were moved to safety in Odisha and Andhra Pradesh. A few dozen people died. The UN praised the effort, and Odisha's approach has been copied in other countries.

Rebuilding

Recovery is the slowest stage. Houses must be rebuilt, often to stronger designs. Farmers need seeds and tools. Children need schools to reopen, because routine helps them cope. Governments, charities and insurance companies share the costs.

The aim is often to "build back better". After the 2001 earthquake in Gujarat, many rebuilt homes in Kutch followed rules to make them safer in future earthquakes. After the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami, India joined other countries in setting up a tsunami warning centre, which opened in Hyderabad in 2007.

What kids can do

Children can help a great deal, mostly by being prepared. Learn your address and a parent's phone number by heart. Agree on where to meet if your family gets separated. Know the emergency number: 112 in India and 911 in the US. These are real safety skills, and many adults don't have them.

Many schools hold earthquake and fire drills. In an earthquake, the advice is to drop to the ground, take cover under a sturdy table and hold on until the shaking stops. Learning basic first aid is another way to be useful. After a disaster, relief groups usually say money helps more than old clothes, because it lets them buy exactly what is needed close to the disaster zone. A school fundraiser can reach a family who has lost almost everything.

Myth or fact?

Myth: After a disaster, people usually panic and turn on each other.

Truth: Studies of disasters show the opposite is far more common. People tend to stay calm, help strangers, share food and organise themselves long before official help arrives.

Try this

Make a family emergency card. Write down two phone numbers to call, a meeting place near home and one outside your area, and where the torch, water and first-aid kit are kept. Stick it on the fridge and check it with your family once a year.
