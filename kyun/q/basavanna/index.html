Who was Basavanna?

Over 850 years ago, a minister in a Karnataka kingdom wrote short poems in everyday Kannada saying that a person's work and heart matter more than their caste.

The short answer

Basavanna, also called Basaveshwara, was a poet, thinker and royal minister who lived in the 12th century in what is now northern Karnataka. He is remembered for teaching that all people are equal, whatever family or caste they are born into, and that honest work is a kind of worship.

He spread these ideas through vachanas, short poems in plain Kannada, the language people spoke at home. Thousands of vachanas survive, written by Basavanna and by the many men and women who joined him.

Today he is honoured as the guiding figure of the Lingayat tradition. People of many backgrounds in Karnataka and beyond quote his lines.

Surprising but true

Some of the best-known poets in Basavanna's circle were women and working people, including Akka Mahadevi, whose verses are still sung in Karnataka.

A minister who asked questions

Basavanna was probably born in the early 12th century, in a town now called Basavana Bagewadi. Stories say he grew uneasy with rituals he felt had lost their meaning, and left home as a young man to study at Kudalasangama, where two rivers meet.

He later went to Kalyana, today's Basavakalyan, capital of King Bijjala II. There he rose to become chief minister, in charge of the treasury. A man close to power, he used that position to argue that the kingdom's ordinary people mattered.

Poems anyone could hear

A vachana is a short piece of free verse, with no fixed rhyme or metre, meant to be said or sung. Many of Basavanna's end with the name he used for God, Kudala Sangama Deva, "lord of the meeting rivers". Because they were short and sharp, vachanas were easy to memorise, and many people in Karnataka still know a few by heart.

In one famous vachana he says that the rich build temples for Shiva, but he is poor. So his body will be the temple: his legs the pillars, his body the shrine, his head a dome of gold. The idea was that devotion comes from inside, not from wealth.

This belongs to the wider bhakti movement, in which poets across India spoke to God in their own languages. Later poets in the north, like Kabir and Mirabai, did something similar.

Who were Mirabai and Kabir?A weaver from Varanasi and a princess from Rajasthan wrote songs that Indians still sing, five hundred years later.

The hall of experience

Basavanna and his companions met in a gathering known as the Anubhava Mantapa, the "hall of experience". There, poets, thinkers and workers discussed religion and how to live. Women spoke as well as men.

Some modern writers call it an early parliament. Historians are more careful, because most of what we know comes from texts written centuries later. What seems clear is that people from many backgrounds sat and talked together, which was unusual for the time.

His followers rejected caste divisions and many temple rituals. Each wore a small personal linga, a symbol of Shiva, on a cord, so worship could happen anywhere.

A troubled ending

The story told in later accounts is that Basavanna's circle approved a marriage between a family thought of as high caste and one thought of as low caste. This caused fury at court, and violence followed in Kalyana. Basavanna left and is said to have died at Kudalasangama. The exact details and dates are still debated.

Remembered today

Basava Jayanti, his birth anniversary, is celebrated across Karnataka each spring. A statue of him stands on the bank of the Thames in London, unveiled in 2015. In 2024, the Karnataka government declared him the state's "cultural leader".

His followers, who call themselves Lingayats or Veerashaivas, are one of Karnataka's largest communities. They still debate whether those two names describe the same tradition, and what Basavanna would have thought of later customs. That arguing would probably not have surprised a man who taught people to think for themselves.

Myth or fact?

Myth: All the vachanas were written by Basavanna.

Truth: Hundreds of people in his circle wrote vachanas, known as vachanakaras. They included women like Akka Mahadevi and working people such as weavers, ferrymen and cobblers. Basavanna is the most famous of them, but he was one voice among many.

Try this

Write your own vachana-style poem of four to six short lines in your home language. Pick one everyday thing, like a broom or a school bag, and use it to say something you believe about being fair or honest. Read it aloud to someone at home and see if they can repeat a line from memory.
