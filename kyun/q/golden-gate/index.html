How was the Golden Gate Bridge built?

The Golden Gate Bridge hangs from two cables, each made of more than 27,000 thin steel wires bundled together, and it opened in 1937.

The short answer

The Golden Gate Bridge is a suspension bridge. Two tall towers stand in the water, and two thick cables loop over them from shore to shore. Hundreds of thinner ropes hang down from those cables and hold up the road.

Workers built it between 1933 and 1937 across the Golden Gate, the windy strait where San Francisco Bay meets the Pacific Ocean. They had to fight fierce currents, thick fog and strong winds. When it opened, its main span of 1,280 metres was the longest of any suspension bridge on Earth.

Surprising but true

A safety net under the bridge caught 19 workers who fell during construction. They called themselves the Halfway to Hell Club.

A bridge nobody thought possible

For years, people said the Golden Gate could not be bridged. The water is more than 100 metres deep in places, tides rush through it, and fog rolls in almost every summer afternoon. Ferries carried people across instead.

The engineer Joseph Strauss pushed for a bridge for over a decade. Much of the hard maths was done by another engineer, Charles Ellis, whose part was forgotten for years. Work began in January 1933, during the Great Depression, and the jobs mattered to thousands of families.

Towers in the sea

The south tower stands in open water. Divers had to work in the strong current to help build a huge concrete wall around the spot, a bit like a giant bucket. Water was pumped out so crews could pour the foundation on solid rock.

Then the steel towers rose, piece by piece, until they reached 227 metres above the water. That is roughly as tall as a 65-storey building. The steel came by train from Pennsylvania, on the other side of the country.

How do bridges hold up so much weight?Every bridge is a tug-of-war between two forces, a push and a pull. Good engineers decide which parts get which.

Spinning the cables

The main cables were not lifted into place in one piece. Machines carried single wires back and forth over the towers, again and again, like a spider spinning a web. Each cable holds 27,572 wires, packed into a bundle almost a metre thick.

Altogether there is enough wire in the two cables to circle the Earth about three times. Once the cables were done, workers hung the road deck from them, section by section, working out from the towers so the weight stayed balanced.

A suspension bridge is meant to bend a little. In strong winds the road can swing several metres sideways, and on hot days the steel stretches so the middle of the deck sags lower. The engineers planned for all of this. A bridge that could not move would be more likely to crack.

Keeping workers alive

Bridge building in the 1930s was dangerous. Strauss insisted that workers wear hard hats, which was unusual then, and he paid for a huge net to be strung under the bridge. It caught 19 men who fell. They joked that they had joined the Halfway to Hell Club.

Even so, 11 workers died, ten of them in a single accident in February 1937 when a platform tore through the net. Their names are remembered at the bridge. The safety rules Strauss pushed for, from hard hats to nets, helped change how big building sites were run across the country.

The bridge today

The bridge opened to walkers on 27 May 1937, and about 200,000 people crossed on foot that day. Cars came the next day. It held the world record for the longest suspension span until 1964, when the Verrazzano-Narrows Bridge in New York beat it.

It is now one of the most photographed places in San Francisco, often with its towers poking out of the fog. Kolkata's Howrah Bridge, finished a few years later in 1943, is a different design that needs no cables at all. It holds its road up with steel arms that reach out from each bank.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Painters start at one end of the Golden Gate Bridge and, when they reach the other end, start again.

Truth: The bridge isn't repainted end to end on a loop. A team of painters works all year, touching up spots where salty fog has damaged the paint and steel.

Try this

Make a mini suspension bridge. Stand two stacks of books a ruler-length apart and drape a long piece of string over them, taping the ends to the table. Hang a strip of card from the string with paper clips and see how many coins it holds before it sags.
