How does a parachute slow you down?

A skydiver falling belly-first tops out at about 200 km/h. Open a parachute, and seconds later they drift down slower than a person jogging.

The short answer

When you fall, gravity pulls you down and you speed up. But as you go faster, you crash into more and more air molecules every second. The air pushes back. This push is called air resistance, or drag.

A falling skydiver with arms and legs spread soon reaches a speed where the drag pushing up equals the pull of gravity. They stop speeding up. This top speed, about 200 km/h belly-first, is called terminal velocity.

A parachute is a big canopy of light, strong cloth. When it opens, it catches a huge amount of air, so the drag becomes far stronger. The skydiver slows sharply until a new, much gentler top speed is reached, slow enough to land safely on their feet.

Surprising but true

In 2000, a British skydiver tested a parachute built from Leonardo da Vinci's 500-year-old sketch, made of wood and canvas, and it worked.

Why size matters

Drag depends on how big you are from the front and how fast you're moving. A pebble and a sheet of paper weigh about the same, but the paper drifts slowly down because it's wide and flat. Crumple the paper into a ball, and it drops like the pebble.

A parachute might be as wide as a classroom. Spread out, it pushes against so much air that a person under it falls at only around 15 to 20 km/h. Many parachutes have a small hole at the top. This lets some air escape steadily, so the canopy doesn't swing wildly from side to side. Nature uses the same idea: dandelion seeds float on tiny parachutes of fluffy threads.

Leonardo's pyramid

Around 1485, the Italian artist and inventor Leonardo da Vinci sketched a parachute in his notebook. It was a pyramid of linen stretched over a wooden frame. He wrote that a person could jump from any height with it and not be hurt.

For more than 500 years, nobody knew if it would work. In 2000, a British skydiver, Adrian Nicholas, built one from wood, canvas and rope and jumped with it from a hot-air balloon over South Africa. It worked, giving a smooth ride. Because it weighed so much, he cut it loose partway down and used a modern parachute to land.

One of the first parachute jumps from a balloon came in 1797, when André-Jacques Garnerin dropped above Paris under a silk canopy.

How do seeds fly?Plants can't walk, so many of them build their seeds parachutes, propellers and gliders, and let the wind do the rest.

Parachutes on other worlds

Parachutes can work wherever there's air. In 2021, NASA's Perseverance rover landed on Mars with the help of a parachute about 21.5 metres wide. Mars has very thin air, so the parachute alone couldn't slow it enough, and rockets did the last part. Engineers hid a secret message in the parachute's pattern of red and white panels, written in a computer code.

India's Gaganyaan programme will use parachutes too. When the crew capsule comes back from space, a set of parachutes will open one after another to slow it down for a splashdown in the sea. ISRO has tested them by dropping a dummy capsule from a helicopter.

Steering in the sky

Old round parachutes simply drifted wherever the wind blew. Most modern sport parachutes are rectangles that work like a wing. Skydivers pull on handles connected to the back edges to turn left or right, and pull both together to slow down just before landing.

Every skydiver carries a spare parachute, and many carry a small device that opens it automatically if they fall too low too fast. With good training, skydiving is a carefully managed sport.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Heavy things always fall faster than light things.

Truth: Without air, a hammer and a feather fall at the same speed, as an astronaut showed on the Moon in 1971. On Earth, air resistance slows light, spread-out things much more than heavy, compact ones. A parachute uses this to slow a person down.

Try this

Make a mini parachute from a square of plastic bag, four pieces of string and a small toy or a cotton reel. Drop it from the same height as the toy on its own. Then try a bigger square, and one with a small hole in the middle. Which falls slowest, and which falls straightest?
