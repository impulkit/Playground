How do earplugs and ear defenders work?

A good foam earplug can make a roaring motorbike sound about as quiet as people chatting, just by squashing into your ear and slowly springing back.

The short answer

Earplugs and ear defenders protect your hearing by blocking sound before it reaches your eardrums. Foam earplugs are squashed thin, pushed into the ear canal, and then slowly expand to seal it tight. Ear defenders, also called earmuffs, cover the whole ear with a hard cup lined with foam and pressed to your head by a band.

Sound is a wave of moving air. To stop it, you need a seal with no gaps and a material that soaks up or reflects the wave's energy. A good earplug can cut the noise reaching your ear by about 30 decibels. That's like turning a jackhammer next to you into a noisy road.

Surprising but true

Wax earplugs have been sold since 1907, when a German chemist started making them in Berlin. The brand, Ohropax, still exists.

Squeeze, insert, wait

Foam earplugs are made from a special slow-recovery foam. Roll one tightly between your fingers into a thin worm, then slide it into your ear. Over about 30 seconds, it springs back and fills the canal, pressing gently on every side.

That tight fit is the trick. Sound can't easily sneak around the plug, and the foam's tiny air bubbles turn some of the sound energy into a little heat. Foam earplugs appeared in the US in the early 1970s and are now used by factory workers, musicians, swimmers and anyone sharing a room with a snorer.

Silicone and wax earplugs work differently. You mould them over the entrance of the ear, like a soft cap. Wax earplugs were already being sold in Berlin in 1907.

Cups that trap sound

Ear defenders use hard plastic cups that reflect sound away. Inside, soft foam absorbs what gets through. A cushion around the edge seals each cup against your head. Even a small gap, from hair or the arms of a pair of glasses, can let in a lot of noise.

Airport ground crew, drummers and people mowing big lawns wear them. Many children who find crowds or fireworks overwhelming use them too.

How do noise-cancelling headphones work?Noise-cancelling headphones fight sound with more sound. They play a mirror-image copy of the noise around you, and the two wipe each other out.

How loud is too loud?

Loudness is measured in decibels. The scale is tricky. Every rise of about 3 decibels means roughly twice as much sound energy. Every 10 decibels sounds about twice as loud to your ears.

Normal talking is about 60 decibels. Heavy city traffic can reach 85. American safety experts at NIOSH say 85 decibels is safe for about 8 hours a day, but at 100 decibels, the safe time drops to about 15 minutes. Firecrackers can top 120 decibels close up. Loud sounds can damage tiny hair cells deep in your ears, and those cells don't grow back.

Diwali, concerts and headphones

In India, the Central Pollution Control Board sets limits on how loud firecrackers can be. Doctors in many cities report more ear problems around Diwali, from crackers burst too close. Wearing earplugs, or simply standing further away, makes a big difference. Doubling your distance from a sound makes it noticeably quieter.

The WHO estimates that over a billion young people could be at risk of hearing loss from listening to loud music. A common rule of thumb is to keep headphones below about 60% of full volume and take regular breaks.

The oldest earplugs in stories

In Greek myth, the hero Odysseus sailed past the Sirens, creatures whose song lured sailors to their deaths. He had his crew plug their ears with beeswax, while he tied himself to the mast to listen. It's one of the oldest earplug stories ever told.

Blocking sound is one way to get quiet. Another way is to fight sound with sound. Noise-cancelling headphones listen to the noise around you and play its exact opposite, so the two waves wipe each other out.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Stuffing cotton wool in your ears protects them from loud noise.

Truth: Plain cotton wool blocks only a little sound, because air and sound pass through its loose fibres. Proper foam or silicone earplugs seal the ear canal and block much more.

Try this

Test materials for blocking sound. Put a phone playing music in a shoebox and listen from a set distance. Then line the box with different materials, such as newspaper, cotton wool, foil or a towel, and note which makes the music quietest.
