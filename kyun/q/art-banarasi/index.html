What makes Banarasi silk special?

The gold in an old Banarasi sari is real metal, drawn into threads finer than a hair and wound around silk.

The short answer

Banarasi silk is famous for its brocade: patterns of shining metallic thread woven right into the silk, so they look embroidered but are part of the cloth itself. Flowers, vines, birds and paisleys seem to float on the fabric. The best pieces use zari, fine silver wire coated in gold.

These saris come from Varanasi, also called Banaras, and the districts around it. Weaving them takes great skill and a lot of time. That's why a Banarasi sari is often bought for a wedding and then passed from mother to daughter for generations.

Surprising but true

A single richly woven Banarasi sari can take one weaver, working with a helper, anywhere from two weeks to six months to finish.

A thread of metal

To make traditional zari, craftspeople pulled silver through smaller and smaller holes until it became a wire thinner than a human hair. They flattened it, coated it with gold and wound it around a silk core. Today most zari is made with copper, or even plastic film coated to look like metal, which costs far less.

Old pieces made with real zari were so valuable that families sometimes burned worn-out saris to recover the silver and gold. Museums like the Victoria and Albert in London keep Banarasi brocades in their collections.

Weaving a picture, one thread at a time

A loom holds thousands of long threads called the warp. The weaver passes other threads, called the weft, over and under them. To make a pattern, certain warp threads have to be lifted at the right moment.

On older looms, a helper sat on top of the loom pulling cords to lift the right threads. In the early 20th century, Varanasi weavers adopted the Jacquard loom, which uses chains of punched cards. Each hole lets one thread rise. A complicated sari can need thousands of cards. This system of holes and no holes later inspired the punch cards of early computers, a story told in binary code.

The kadhua secret

The most prized technique is called kadhua. Each motif, such as a small flower called a buta, is woven separately with its own little bobbin of thread, by hand. On the back of the cloth, there are no loose threads hanging across. It is slow, careful work.

In cheaper phekwa weaving, the extra thread runs right across the back of the fabric and the loose parts are cut off later. Turn a sari over, and an expert can tell you how it was made in seconds.

From Mughal courts to weddings

Silk weaving in Varanasi goes back many centuries, but the gold-heavy style people picture today grew in the time of the Mughals. Persian designs like the jaal, a net of vines, and the kalga, a paisley shape, mixed with Indian flowers and temple patterns.

Most weavers work in family workshops in neighbourhoods of Varanasi, many from Muslim Ansari families, while many traders and buyers are Hindu. Banarasi silk links both communities, and the saris are worn at weddings and festivals of every faith.

A craft under pressure

Weaving by hand is hard to make a living from. A power loom can turn out a sari in hours, and cheap copies flood the markets. Many young people from weaving families have left for other jobs.

In 2009, the government gave "Banaras Brocades and Sarees" a Geographical Indication tag. That means only cloth from the Varanasi area, made in the traditional way, can legally use the name. Some weavers now sell directly online, and designers have brought Banarasi brocade into jackets, lehengas and even sneakers.

Silk isn't the only Indian fibre with a story. Far to the north, in the cold Himalaya, goats grow a winter coat so fine it becomes pashmina.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Every Banarasi sari is handwoven with real gold thread.

Truth: Many saris sold as Banarasi today are made on power looms with plastic-based metallic thread. A genuine handwoven sari from the GI area can carry a Banaras Brocades GI tag.

Try this

Make a paper weave. Cut slits in a sheet of coloured paper, leaving a border, then weave strips of another colour over and under. Try changing the pattern, such as over two and under one, and see what new designs appear.
