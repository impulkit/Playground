Who was A.P.J. Abdul Kalam?

A boy who sold newspapers in a small temple town grew up to launch India's first home-built satellite rocket and then became President.

The short answer

Avul Pakir Jainulabdeen Abdul Kalam was an Indian engineer who helped build the country's rockets and missiles, and then served as India's 11th President from 2002 to 2007. He led the team behind India's first home-built satellite launcher, the SLV-3, which reached orbit in 1980.

He is remembered as much for his warmth as for his engineering. He loved talking to schoolchildren, answered thousands of letters, and was often called "the People's President". Many Indians still quote his advice to dream big and work hard.

Surprising but true

Kalam's missile team used light rocket materials to make leg supports for children with polio that weighed about a tenth of the old metal ones.

Newspapers in Rameswaram

Kalam was born in 1931 in Rameswaram, a temple town on an island off the coast of Tamil Nadu. His father owned boats and was the imam of a local mosque. The family was not rich, and young Abdul helped by delivering newspapers in the early morning before school.

He was good at maths and curious about flight. He watched seabirds wheel over the shore and wondered how they stayed up. He studied physics at St Joseph's College in Tiruchirappalli, then aerospace engineering at the Madras Institute of Technology. His dream was to be a fighter pilot, but he narrowly missed selection for the Indian Air Force.

Building rockets

In 1969 Kalam joined the new Indian Space Research Organisation, which had grown out of Vikram Sarabhai's plans. He became project director of the SLV-3, a four-stage rocket using solid fuel.

The first full launch, in 1979, failed and the rocket fell into the Bay of Bengal. Kalam later wrote that his boss, Satish Dhawan, faced the press and took the blame himself. A year later, in July 1980, SLV-3 lifted off from Sriharikota and put the Rohini satellite into orbit. Dhawan let Kalam announce the success. Learning how rockets work the hard way, through failure, became part of ISRO's culture.

Missiles, calipers and controversy

From the early 1980s Kalam led India's guided missile programme, which built the Prithvi and Agni missiles. This earned him the nickname "Missile Man of India". He was also a key figure in the 1998 nuclear tests at Pokhran. Many Indians saw this as a proud moment for the country's defence. Others, including people who oppose nuclear weapons, were uneasy that a gentle, much-loved teacher was also a top weapons scientist.

His team also found peaceful uses for their materials. Working with doctors in Hyderabad, they made leg supports, called calipers, for children affected by polio. The new ones weighed about a tenth of the heavy metal versions, making it far easier for the children to walk. Kalam wrote that this gave him more joy than many of his bigger projects.

The People's President

In 2002, Kalam was chosen as President of India. He was the first scientist to hold the job and one of very few Presidents with no background in politics. He opened the gates of Rashtrapati Bhavan to visitors and spent much of his time meeting students across the country. He lived simply, played the veena and loved Tamil poetry, especially the ancient book of verses called the Thirukkural.

After his term, he returned to teaching. On 27 July 2015, while giving a lecture to students at IIM Shillong, he collapsed and died. He was 83. His autobiography, Wings of Fire, has sold millions of copies and been translated into many Indian and foreign languages.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Kalam was a nuclear scientist who designed India's atomic bombs.

Truth: Kalam was an aerospace engineer who worked on rockets and missiles. The nuclear devices tested in 1998 were designed by scientists at the Bhabha Atomic Research Centre and other teams. Kalam, then the government's chief scientific adviser, helped coordinate the tests.
