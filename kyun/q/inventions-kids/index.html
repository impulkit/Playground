Which famous things were invented by kids?

The writing system used by millions of blind people around the world was worked out by a 15-year-old boy in Paris.

The short answer

Plenty of everyday things began in the head of a child. The ice lolly, earmuffs, the trampoline, an early snowmobile and the braille alphabet were all dreamed up by kids aged about 11 to 16.

Kids notice problems that grown-ups have stopped seeing. Cold ears, slow reading, a boring winter. They also aren't afraid to try silly-looking ideas. Some of those ideas went on to change millions of lives, and a few became famous brands.

Surprising but true

Frank Epperson made the first ice lolly at about age 11, but he waited until he was in his late twenties to patent it.

Six dots and a 15-year-old

Louis Braille was born in France in 1809. At age 3 he hurt his eye with a sharp tool in his father's workshop, and an infection left him blind in both eyes. At school in Paris, blind students read books with huge raised letters. They were heavy and painfully slow.

A French army captain had invented "night writing", a code of raised dots so soldiers could read in the dark. It used 12 dots and was clumsy. Young Louis cut it down to just six dots in a small grid, enough for every letter, number and punctuation mark. He was about 15. Today braille is read by millions of blind people in dozens of languages, including Hindi and Tamil.

An ice lolly by accident

In 1905, in San Francisco, an 11-year-old named Frank Epperson mixed a fruity soda drink with a stirring stick and left the cup outside one cold night. By morning, it had frozen solid around the stick. He pulled it out and licked it.

He called it the "Epsicle". Years later, in 1923, he applied for a patent, and his own children called it "Pop's 'sicle". That's how the Popsicle got its name. The full tale of that cold night sits with other accidental inventions.

Did a boy invent the ice lolly?In 1905, an 11-year-old in California left a cup of fizzy drink outside with a stick in it. By morning, he had invented the ice lolly, or so the story goes.

Cold ears and bouncing beds

In 1873, Chester Greenwood, 15, from Maine in the USA, kept getting freezing ears while ice skating. He bent some wire into loops and asked his grandmother to sew fur onto them. Earmuffs. He patented an improved version in 1877, and his hometown still holds a Chester Greenwood Day parade.

George Nissen, 16, of Iowa, watched trapeze artists drop into safety nets and bounce. In 1930 he started building a frame with stretched canvas so he could keep bouncing. He later made his name with the word "trampoline", from the Spanish word for diving board. Today the trampoline is even an Olympic sport.

In Canada, Joseph-Armand Bombardier was 15 in 1922 when he built a snow machine with a car engine and a propeller. His later inventions led to the snowmobile and the giant Bombardier company, which went on to build trains and planes.

Kid inventors today

Children still invent. In 2020, Gitanjali Rao, a 15-year-old from Colorado, was named TIME magazine's first Kid of the Year. She had designed a device to test for lead in drinking water, using a sensor and a phone app. In India, school science fairs and programmes such as the Dr. A.P.J. Abdul Kalam IGNITE awards have honoured children's ideas for farm tools, safer roads and helping elderly people.

Some kid patents are less serious. In 2002, a five-year-old in the United States was granted a patent for a new way of swinging on a swing, sideways. His father, a patent lawyer, filed it to teach him how patents work. It joins a long list of weird inventions that got official approval.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Only grown-ups are allowed to get a patent.

Truth: In the United States and many other countries there is no minimum age to be named as an inventor on a patent. Children have been granted patents, sometimes with an adult helping with the paperwork.
