What were the great kingdoms of ancient Africa?

Sudan has more pyramids than Egypt. A West African king once gave away so much gold in Cairo that its price there dropped for years.

The short answer

Africa is the second-largest continent, and over thousands of years it was home to many powerful kingdoms. Egypt is the most famous, but it was far from alone.

To Egypt's south lay Kush, whose kings once ruled Egypt itself. In the highlands of Ethiopia stood Aksum, a great trading power. In West Africa, the empires of Ghana, Mali and Songhai grew rich on gold. In the south, people built the stone city of Great Zimbabwe. Each had its own rulers, beliefs, art and trade links reaching across oceans and deserts.

Surprising but true

The country of Zimbabwe is named after Great Zimbabwe, a stone city built without any mortar, whose walls still stand after 600 years.

Kush, land of pyramids

Kush grew up along the Nile in what is now Sudan. For centuries Egypt ruled it. Then, in the 700s BCE, Kushite kings marched north and ruled Egypt as pharaohs for about a century.

Later, the Kushites built their capital at Meroë. They buried their kings and queens under small, steep pyramids. Sudan has more than 200 of them, more than Egypt. Meroë was also a centre of ironworking, with heaps of slag, the waste left from smelting iron, still visible today.

Aksum, a trading giant

Aksum, in northern Ethiopia, was one of the great powers of the ancient world from about the first century CE. Its port on the Red Sea traded ivory, gold and incense with Rome, Arabia and India. Aksum issued its own coins in gold, silver and bronze.

Its kings raised giant carved stone pillars called stelae. The tallest still standing is about 24 metres high. In the 300s CE King Ezana made Christianity the kingdom's religion, and Ethiopia has one of the world's oldest Christian traditions.

Kingdoms of gold and salt

In West Africa, the Ghana Empire (not the same place as modern Ghana) controlled trade routes across the Sahara. Camel caravans carried salt south and gold north. Arab writers called Ghana "the land of gold".

The Mali Empire followed. In 1324 its ruler, Mansa Musa, made the pilgrimage to Mecca with a huge caravan. He handed out so much gold in Cairo that, according to the writer al-Umari, its value there stayed low for years afterwards. Mali's city of Timbuktu became famous for its scholars and libraries, and families there still keep thousands of old manuscripts.

The traveller Ibn Battuta visited Mali in the 1350s. He wrote that its roads were safe and its people strict about justice.

Great Zimbabwe

In southern Africa, between about 1100 and 1450, the ancestors of the Shona people built Great Zimbabwe. Its biggest structure, the Great Enclosure, has a curving granite wall about 250 metres long and up to 11 metres high, made of stones fitted together without mortar. The city was a hub of trade, and archaeologists have found glass beads from India and pottery from China there.

Benin and its bronzes

In the forests of what is now southern Nigeria, the Kingdom of Benin made thousands of brass and bronze plaques and heads. Artists worked in a guild that served the king. Their plaques once covered the wooden pillars of the royal palace, showing kings, warriors, ceremonies and even Portuguese traders who arrived by sea in the late 1400s. Benin City itself was ringed by long earthen walls and ditches, dug by hand over centuries. In 1897 British soldiers looted the royal city and carried many to Europe. Today museums are debating how many should go home, and some, including pieces from Germany, have already been returned to Nigeria.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Africa had no great cities or kingdoms before Europeans arrived.

Truth: Africa had powerful kingdoms, rich trading cities, universities, libraries and stone monuments for thousands of years. Some colonial writers claimed outsiders must have built places like Great Zimbabwe, but archaeologists proved that Africans built them.

Try this

Make a mortar-free wall. Using small building blocks, books or flat stones, build a short wall where each block sits across the gap between two blocks below. Then try a wall with blocks stacked straight up. Push both gently and see which stays standing, as at Great Zimbabwe.
