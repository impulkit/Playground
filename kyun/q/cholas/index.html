How did the Cholas build a sea empire?

A thousand years ago, a king from Tamil Nadu sent his navy across the Bay of Bengal to attack kingdoms in Malaysia and Sumatra.

The short answer

The Cholas were a Tamil dynasty who ruled from the fertile Kaveri delta in Tamil Nadu. They were most powerful from about 850 to 1279 CE.

Rich from rice farming and trade, the Cholas built a strong army and a navy. Under Rajaraja I and his son Rajendra I, they conquered much of south India and northern Sri Lanka, and took over the Maldives. In 1025 they sent a fleet across the Bay of Bengal to attack the Srivijaya kingdom in Malaysia and Sumatra. Their aim was largely to control the busy sea trade between India, Southeast Asia and China. At home they built huge granite temples and cast some of the finest bronze sculptures ever made.

Surprising but true

Rice, rivers and riches

The Kaveri river spreads into many channels as it nears the sea, watering a wide green delta. Early Chola kings learned to manage that water.

The Kallanai, a stone dam across the Kaveri, is traditionally linked to Karikala, a Chola king who lived around 2,000 years ago. Rebuilt and strengthened over time, it still guides water into canals today, making it one of the oldest water works in the world still in use. Reliable harvests fed large populations, and taxes on rice paid for armies and temples.

A king and his temple

Rajaraja I came to the throne in 985. He was a careful ruler who surveyed land for taxes and kept records on temple walls.

In 1010 he completed the Brihadeeswarar temple in Thanjavur, also called the Big Temple. Its tower rises about 66 metres, as tall as a 20-storey building. The whole thing is built of granite, though there is little hard stone nearby, so huge blocks must have been brought from far away. Inscriptions list the temple's dancers, musicians, cooks and lamp-lighters by name. It is still a working temple, and part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Ships to Southeast Asia

Rajendra I, who ruled from 1014 to 1044, pushed further. His army marched north to the Ganga, and he built a new capital called Gangaikonda Cholapuram, "city of the one who took the Ganga".

Then, in 1025, he sent a fleet against Srivijaya, a rich sea kingdom that controlled the Strait of Malacca, the narrow passage between Malaysia and Sumatra. Chola inscriptions list the ports that were captured, including Kadaram, today's Kedah in Malaysia. Sailors timed voyages with the monsoon winds. The Cholas did not rule Southeast Asia for long, but the raid made sure Tamil merchants could trade freely.

Village councils chosen by lot

The Cholas left thousands of inscriptions in Tamil. The most famous for ordinary life are at Uttaramerur, near Kanchipuram, from around 920 CE.

They describe how the village chose its council. Names of qualified men were written on palm-leaf tickets and placed in a pot. A young boy then drew them out. The rules even barred people who had been dishonest, or whose relatives had been, from standing. This system of choosing by lot is different from modern voting, but it shows a village running its own affairs a thousand years ago.

Gods in bronze

Chola artists made bronze images of gods using the lost-wax method. They modelled a figure in beeswax, covered it in clay, melted the wax out and poured metal into the hollow.

The most famous image is Shiva as Nataraja, the lord of dance, dancing inside a ring of flames. Chola bronzes are carried in temple processions to this day, and museums from Chennai to New York treasure them. A statue of Nataraja stands outside CERN, the physics laboratory in Switzerland, a gift from India in 2004.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ancient Indian kings never went to sea, and only traded by land.

Truth: South Indian kingdoms, especially the Cholas, had strong links across the ocean. Chola ships carried armies to Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia, and Chola envoys travelled to China.
