How does a lightning rod protect a building?

The Empire State Building is struck by lightning about 20 times a year, and the people inside barely notice.

The short answer

A lightning rod is a pointed metal rod fixed to the highest part of a building. A thick strip or cable of copper or aluminium runs from it down the outside of the building and into the ground, where it connects to metal rods buried in the soil.

If lightning strikes, it hits the rod first because it's the highest point. The huge electric current then rushes down the metal cable into the earth, where it spreads out harmlessly. Without the rod, the current might go through the roof, walls or wiring, starting fires and smashing brick and stone.

The rod doesn't attract lightning from far away, and it doesn't stop strikes. It just makes sure that when they happen, the electricity takes a safe route.

Surprising but true

A lightning rod doesn't stop lightning from striking. It gives the strike an easy path into the ground, so the bolt doesn't tear through the building.

Why lightning needs a path

During a thunderstorm, ice and water inside clouds crash together. This builds up enormous electric charges, like the static electricity that makes your hair stand up after you pull off a woolly jumper, but billions of times stronger. When the charge gets big enough, it jumps to the ground as a giant spark.

A bolt can heat the air to about 27,000 °C, hotter than the surface of the Sun. Electricity always looks for the easiest path. Air is a poor conductor, and so are wood, brick and stone. Metal is excellent. A lightning rod offers a metal highway from the sky to the ground, and the current takes it.

Franklin's kite

In the 1740s, the American printer and scientist Benjamin Franklin began experimenting with electricity in Philadelphia. He suspected lightning was a giant electric spark. Around 1750, he suggested that a pointed iron rod on a rooftop, connected to the ground, could protect buildings.

In 1752, French scientists followed his plan and drew sparks from a storm cloud with a tall metal rod. Around the same time, Franklin is said to have flown a kite in a storm and collected electric charge from a key on the string. That experiment was very dangerous. In 1753, a scientist in Russia, Georg Richmann, was killed while trying a similar test. Lightning rods soon spread across Europe and the American colonies.

Struck again and again

There's a popular saying that lightning never strikes the same place twice. It isn't true. Tall, pointed things are hit far more often than flat ground, and they get hit again and again.

The Empire State Building in New York is struck about 20 to 25 times a year, according to the US National Weather Service. Its rod and cables carry each strike safely away. Many temples, church spires, minarets and mobile phone towers have lightning protection for the same reason.

Staying safe in India

India has some of the world's most lightning-filled skies, especially in the weeks before and during the monsoon. Lightning is one of the most common causes of death from natural events in India, and many victims are farmers working in open fields.

To help, the Ministry of Earth Sciences launched an app called Damini. It warns people when lightning is likely nearby. Experts advise staying indoors during storms, keeping away from tall trees and water, and not lying flat in the open, but crouching low if caught outside. A building with a lightning rod, or a closed car with a metal roof, is one of the safest places to be.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Lightning never strikes the same place twice.

Truth: Lightning often hits the same place again, especially tall, pointed objects like towers, trees and hilltops. Some skyscrapers are struck many times every year.

Try this

Work out how far away a storm is from indoors. When you see a flash, count the seconds until you hear the thunder. Divide by three to get the distance in kilometres. If the gap keeps shrinking, the storm is getting closer. If you can hear thunder at all, stay inside.
