Who was Euclid?

A Greek teacher in Egypt wrote a geometry book about 2,300 years ago, and students were still learning from versions of it in the 20th century.

The short answer

Euclid was a Greek mathematician who lived around 300 BCE in Alexandria, a great city in Egypt. He wrote the Elements, a book that collected the geometry and number knowledge of his time and arranged it into a clear chain of proofs.

The Elements starts with a few simple definitions and assumptions, then proves hundreds of results step by step, each built on the ones before. It was copied, translated and taught for more than 2,000 years. The geometry you learn about triangles, circles and parallel lines at school is still called Euclidean geometry.

Surprising but true

Abraham Lincoln studied Euclid's Elements as a young lawyer, to train himself to understand what it really means to prove something.

A mystery man

We know almost nothing about Euclid as a person. He probably taught in Alexandria, which the rulers of Egypt had turned into a centre of learning with a famous library. Scholars came from all over the Greek world to study at the great library there.

A story written about 700 years later says that King Ptolemy asked Euclid for a shortcut to learning geometry. Euclid is said to have replied that there is no royal road to geometry. It is a good story, but nobody knows if it really happened.

Building from the ground up

The Elements is divided into 13 parts, called books. Euclid began with definitions, such as "a point is that which has no part". Then he stated five postulates, things he asked readers to accept without proof. One says you can always draw a straight line between two points. Another says you can draw a circle with any centre and any size.

From these, he proved everything else, one step at a time. The very first result shows how to draw a triangle with three equal sides using just two circles. Later books cover shapes and angles, areas, ratios, solid shapes and the properties of numbers.

Primes and an old recipe

In Book IX, Euclid proved that prime numbers never run out. His argument goes like this. Suppose you had a complete list of primes. Multiply them all together and add 1. The new number cannot be divided exactly by any prime on your list, so either it is prime itself, or it has a prime factor missing from the list. Either way, the list was not complete.

He also described a step-by-step method for finding the highest common factor of two numbers by repeatedly subtracting or dividing. It is one of the oldest algorithms still in use. Computers use it today, including in some of the maths that protects online data.

The troublesome fifth rule

Euclid's fifth postulate, about parallel lines, was longer and less obvious than the others. For centuries, mathematicians tried to prove it from the first four and failed. In the 1800s, several mathematicians, including Nikolai Lobachevsky in Russia and János Bolyai in Hungary, showed that you could build a consistent geometry where it is not true. These non-Euclidean geometries later helped Einstein describe curved space.

Euclid around the world

The Elements was translated into Arabic, then into Latin, and was one of the first maths books ever printed, in Venice in 1482. In the United States, Abraham Lincoln studied the first six books as a young lawyer in the 1850s, to sharpen his reasoning.

In India, the ruler of Jaipur, Sawai Jai Singh II, loved astronomy and built huge stone observatories. In the 1700s his court astronomer, Jagannatha Samrat, translated the Elements from Arabic into Sanskrit under the name Rekhaganita. In 2023 the European Space Agency launched a space telescope named Euclid, to map billions of galaxies and study the shape of the universe.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Euclid discovered all the geometry in the Elements himself.

Truth: Euclid gathered and organised the work of earlier Greek mathematicians, such as Eudoxus and Theaetetus, and added proofs of his own. His great achievement was arranging it all in a clear, logical order, starting from a few simple assumptions.

Try this

Draw Euclid's very first construction. Tie a pencil to a piece of string. Ask a friend to hold the other end on a point on a large sheet of paper and draw a circle. Draw a second circle of the same size, centred on any point on the first circle. Join both centres to one of the points where the circles cross. You have made a triangle with three equal sides.
