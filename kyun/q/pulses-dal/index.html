Why is dal so good for you?

A humble bowl of dal is packed with protein, and the plants it comes from feed the soil as they grow.

The short answer

Dal is made from pulses, the dried seeds of legume plants such as lentils, chickpeas, pigeon peas and beans. These seeds are rich in protein, which your body uses to build muscles, skin and almost everything else.

Dal is also full of fibre, which keeps your gut working well, and has iron, folate and other nutrients. Eaten with rice or roti, it makes a meal with a good balance of protein. And pulses are kind to farms too, because their roots make the soil richer.

Surprising but true

Pulse plants carry bacteria in little lumps on their roots that pull nitrogen out of the air and turn it into natural fertiliser.

A seed made for growing

A pulse seed is a packed lunch for a baby plant. It holds the energy and building material the seedling will need before it can make its own food from sunlight. That is why dry lentils are about one fifth to one quarter protein, far more than rice or wheat.

When you eat dal, your stomach and gut break that protein into small pieces called amino acids. Your body then rebuilds them into its own proteins.

Dal and rice: a good team

Proteins are made from about 20 different amino acids, and the body cannot make some of them. Pulses are rich in one called lysine but low in another called methionine. Rice and wheat are the other way round.

So when you eat dal with rice, or rajma with chawal, or chana with roti, the two foods fill each other's gaps. People across the world found the same answer without knowing any chemistry: beans and corn tortillas in Mexico, and peanut butter on bread in American lunchboxes.

Plants that feed the soil

Pulses have a secret partner. Their roots grow small round lumps called nodules, and inside live bacteria called rhizobia. These bacteria take nitrogen gas from the air, which plants cannot use, and turn it into a form they can.

Nitrogen is one of the main things plants need, and the main thing in many fertilisers. After a crop of pulses, some of that nitrogen stays in the soil for the next crop. Farmers in India have rotated pulses with cereals for centuries.

India's dal bowl

India grows more pulses than any other country, and eats even more, so it also buys pulses from countries such as Canada, Australia and Myanmar. In the United States, the states of Montana and North Dakota grow large amounts of lentils and dry peas, much of it for export.

Every Indian region has favourites. Toor dal goes into sambar in the south, Gujarat makes it gently sweet, and Bengal has masoor dal with fried onions. Urad dal is ground into idli batter, and chana becomes besan for pakoras.

A seed with a long history

People have eaten pulses since the very beginning of farming. Lentils and chickpeas were among the first crops grown in the Fertile Crescent of West Asia, more than 10,000 years ago. Archaeologists have found charred lentils and other pulses at Harappan sites in India too.

Pulses suited early farmers well. Dried seeds are light, keep for a long time without spoiling and can be carried on long journeys. Ancient Indian texts mention mudga, which is moong, and masha, which is urad. The names have changed a little over time, but the bowl of dal has not.

The tummy problem

Pulses have a few downsides. Some of their sugars are not digested in the stomach, so bacteria in the large intestine break them down instead and make gas. Soaking, sprouting and cooking well help, and Indian cooks add a pinch of hing, which many people believe eases it.

Raw kidney beans contain a substance that can make people sick, which is why rajma is always soaked and boiled thoroughly. Properly cooked, it is perfectly safe and very good food.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You need to eat meat to get enough protein.

Truth: Pulses, milk, curd, nuts and grains together can give people all the protein they need. Hundreds of millions of vegetarians in India get their protein mostly this way.

Try this

Soak a handful of whole moong overnight. Drain it, wrap it in a damp cotton cloth and keep it somewhere warm. In a day or two you will see sprouts. Leave a few to grow in a cup of soil and, after some weeks, gently wash the roots to look for tiny round nodules.
