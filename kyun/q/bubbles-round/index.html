Why are bubbles round?

Blow a bubble through a square wand and it still comes out round. The bubble's skin is quietly solving a geometry problem.

The short answer

A bubble is a thin skin of soapy water wrapped around some air. That skin is always pulling inwards, trying to shrink, like a stretched balloon. Scientists call this pull surface tension.

The air inside pushes back, so the skin can't shrink to nothing. Instead, it settles into the shape that holds that amount of air using the smallest possible area of skin. That shape is a sphere. A cube or a pyramid with the same amount of air inside would need more skin, so the bubble pulls itself round.

Raindrops tend towards round shapes for the same reason, though air rushing past them as they fall squashes them a bit. Out in space, astronauts have watched blobs of water float as almost perfect spheres.

Surprising but true

When bubbles stick together in a cluster, their walls always meet in threes, at angles of 120 degrees, the same angle found in a honeycomb.

How soap helps

Plain water has strong surface tension, so strong that a skin of pure water snaps almost as soon as it forms. Soap weakens the pull and spreads it more evenly. Soap molecules have one end that likes water and one end that avoids it, the same trick that lets soap lift dirt off your hands. They line up on both sides of the film, making a sandwich: soap, a thin layer of water, then soap again.

The soap also slows down evaporation, so the water in the middle lasts longer. Adding sugar or glycerine to bubble mix helps even more, because it holds on to the water. In 1890, the British scientist Charles Vernon Boys gave famous lectures to children in London about bubbles and later published them as a book, Soap Bubbles, still in print today.

Where the colours come from

A bubble's film is thinner than a human hair, often less than a thousandth of a millimetre. When light hits it, some bounces off the outside surface and some off the inside surface. The two reflections overlap. Depending on how thick the film is, some colours add together and others cancel out.

As the water drains down and evaporates, the film gets thinner and the colours swirl and change. Just before a bubble pops, the top often goes dark, because the film has become too thin to reflect visible colours at all. The colours on an oil spill on a wet road come from the same effect. It's a different trick from the bending of light that makes a rainbow.

When bubbles meet

Blow lots of bubbles into a cluster and they stop being round. Where two bubbles touch, they share a flat wall. Where three meet, the walls always join at angles of 120 degrees, like the arms of a three-pointed star. The Belgian scientist Joseph Plateau worked out these rules in the 1800s by dipping wire frames into soapy water.

Now look at a honeycomb. Its cells are hexagons, and every corner has walls meeting at 120 degrees. Bees and bubbles are both finding a way to divide up space using the least material.

Why are honeycomb cells hexagons?Bees don't know geometry. But they build the best shape anyway.

Why bubbles pop

A bubble pops when its film breaks. Gravity drains the water downwards, so the top gets thinner. Water evaporates into the air. A speck of dust or a dry finger can puncture it in an instant.

That's why bubbles last longer on humid days, when the air is already damp and evaporation is slow. Touch a bubble with a finger wet with bubble mix, and it often survives. Bubble artists use this trick, along with special mixes, to make bubbles as big as a car or to put a child inside one.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A square bubble wand makes square bubbles.

Truth: The wand shape doesn't matter once the bubble is free. The film always pulls itself into a sphere, because that wraps the trapped air in the least possible skin.

Try this

Mix half a cup of washing-up liquid with about four cups of water and a spoon of sugar or glycerine, and let it rest for an hour. Bend a pipe cleaner into a square, a triangle and a star, and blow bubbles through each. Then blow two bubbles onto a wet plate so they touch, and look at the wall where they meet.
