Why is the sea level rising?

The sea has risen by more than 20 cm since 1880, and it is rising faster now than at any time in thousands of years.

The short answer

The sea is rising for two main reasons, both caused by a warming climate. First, the oceans are getting warmer, and warm water takes up a little more space than cold water. Second, ice on land is melting. Mountain glaciers and the huge ice sheets of Greenland and Antarctica are losing ice, and the meltwater runs into the sea.

Since 1880, the average sea level around the world has risen by more than 20 cm. Most of that rise has happened in the last few decades, and it is getting faster. Today the sea is rising by about 4 mm a year. That sounds tiny, but over a lifetime it adds up, and higher seas let storms and high tides push farther inland. Scientists link the warming to climate change caused mostly by burning coal, oil and gas.

Surprising but true

When an ice cube melts in a full glass of water, the glass doesn't overflow. In the same way, melting floating sea ice barely changes sea level. It is ice on land that matters.

Expanding water

Almost everything expands when it warms. Railway tracks have small gaps so they don't buckle on hot days. Water does the same. The ocean has soaked up most of the extra heat trapped by greenhouse gases, and as it warms, it swells.

Each bit of water expands only a little. But the ocean is very deep, so a tiny stretch in every metre adds up. Between 1971 and 2018, this expansion caused about half of the sea level rise, according to the IPCC, the world's main panel of climate scientists. In recent years, melting land ice has become the bigger part.

Ice on land and ice at sea

Not all melting ice raises the sea. Arctic sea ice is already floating, so when it melts, it hardly changes the level, like an ice cube in a glass of water. Ice on land is different. When a glacier in the Himalayas or Alaska melts, its water flows into rivers and then the sea, adding new water.

The largest stores of land ice are in Greenland and Antarctica. The Antarctic ice sheet is, on average, about 2 km thick. If all of it melted, which would take many centuries, the sea would rise by about 58 m. Nobody expects that soon. But even a small share of that ice slipping into the sea would make a big difference to coasts.

Measuring the sea

Sea level goes up and down every day with the tides, and waves make it bumpy. So how do scientists measure such a small change? For more than a century, tide gauges at harbours have recorded the height of the water every few minutes. Averaging over years cancels out the tides and waves.

Since 1992, satellites have measured the height of the sea from space. They bounce radar pulses off the water and time how long the echo takes. Together, gauges and satellites show the rise clearly. They also show that it is not the same everywhere. In some places, such as Jakarta in Indonesia, the land itself is sinking, which makes the sea seem to rise even faster.

Who is affected

Low-lying places feel rising seas first. Some islands in the Indian Sundarbans have been shrinking, partly because of rising water and erosion, and people have had to move. That area is also home to tigers and mangroves, as you can read in Sundarbans tigers. The Maldives, most of which lies less than 1 m above sea level, is planning for the future, and so are cities like Mumbai, Kolkata, Miami and New York.

Scientists expect the sea to rise somewhere between about 30 cm and 1 m more by 2100, depending on how much the world cuts its emissions. Coastal cities are building sea walls, raising roads and restoring mangroves and wetlands that soak up storm surges.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Melting sea ice in the Arctic is the main reason the sea is rising.

Truth: Sea ice already floats, so when it melts it hardly changes the sea level, just like ice in a glass. The rise comes from ice melting on land, in glaciers and ice sheets, and from warming water expanding.

Try this

Test melting ice. Put a few ice cubes in a glass and fill it with water right to the brim. Mark the level and wait for the ice to melt. Then place ice cubes on a small upturned cup inside a bowl of water, so they sit above the water, and mark the level. Which one raises the water when it melts?
