What makes the ground suddenly open up?

In 2014, the floor of a car museum in Kentucky fell into a hole, and eight sports cars went with it.

The short answer

A sinkhole is a hole that forms when the ground over an empty space underground collapses. Most sinkholes happen where the rock below is limestone, chalk, gypsum or salt. These rocks slowly dissolve in water, while hard rocks such as granite hardly dissolve at all.

Rainwater soaks down through the soil and picks up a little carbon dioxide, which makes it very weakly acidic. Over thousands of years, this water eats away the rock, widening cracks into hollows and caves. The soil above stays put for a while, like a thin crust over an empty pie. When it can no longer hold its own weight, it falls in.

Surprising but true

The deepest known sinkhole, Xiaozhai Tiankeng in China, is over 600 m deep. You could drop the Eiffel Tower into it and still not see the tip.

Swiss-cheese country

Landscapes full of dissolving rock are called karst. They are riddled with caves, underground streams and sinkholes, a bit like a slice of Swiss cheese. Rivers may vanish into the ground and pop out again kilometres away. Karst is where the water cycle goes underground in a big way: in some karst regions, people get almost all their drinking water from springs and wells fed by these hidden channels.

India has karst too. At the Belum Caves in Andhra Pradesh, visitors enter through holes in the ground that open into long limestone passages. In the hills of Meghalaya, rain has carved hundreds of caves, some of the longest in India.

Slow sinkholes and sudden ones

Some sinkholes form gently. As rock dissolves slowly, the ground sags into a bowl shape over many years, and sometimes the bowl fills with water to become a pond. Florida has thousands of round lakes made this way.

Others collapse all at once. In 1981, in Winter Park, Florida, a sinkhole opened up and kept growing for days. It swallowed a house, part of a swimming pool and several cars, and grew to about 100 m wide. The city later turned it into a lake.

In February 2014, a sinkhole opened under the National Corvette Museum in Bowling Green, Kentucky. Eight rare sports cars fell in. Kentucky sits on limestone full of caves, including Mammoth Cave, the longest known cave system in the world. Some of the damaged cars were restored, and the museum now tells the story to visitors.

When people cause them

Not every sinkhole is natural. Pumping too much groundwater can drain the water that was helping to hold up the rock and soil. Old mines can collapse. A leaking water pipe or drain can wash soil away under a road until it suddenly caves in, swallowing a car or a bus stop.

In 2010, a deep, round hole about 20 m across opened in the middle of a street in Guatemala City after heavy rain from a tropical storm. Engineers blamed leaking pipes and loose volcanic soil, not limestone. Cities now use radar that sees through the ground to spot hollows under busy roads.

The giants

In southern China, enormous sinkholes called tiankeng, meaning "heavenly pits", drop hundreds of metres. Xiaozhai Tiankeng is over 600 m deep. Some have whole forests growing at the bottom, with plants and insects that live nowhere else.

Underwater sinkholes are called blue holes. Dean's Blue Hole in the Bahamas plunges about 200 m. Free divers, who swim down on a single breath, train there because the water is calm and very deep.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Sinkholes appear out of nowhere, in an instant.

Truth: The collapse can be sudden, but the hollow underneath usually grows for years or even centuries before the surface gives way. Cracks in walls, tilting fences or small dips in the ground can be early warnings.

Try this

Build a small wall of sugar cubes in a tray and cover it with a layer of dry sand. Using a spoon, drip water slowly onto one spot. Watch the sand sag and then suddenly drop as the sugar dissolves underneath. Real sinkholes work the same way, only much more slowly.
