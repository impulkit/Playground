Why do we get spam?

The first spam email was sent in 1978 to about 400 people. Today, by most counts, nearly half of all the email sent in the world is spam.

The short answer

You get spam because sending it is almost free, and a tiny number of people fall for it. If a spammer sends 10 million emails and only a handful of people buy a fake product, click a bad link or hand over a password, the spammer can still profit.

Spam is unwanted email sent in bulk to huge numbers of people. Some is just annoying advertising. Some is dangerous: messages pretending to be from your bank, a delivery company or even a friend, trying to steal passwords or money. That kind is called phishing.

By most counts in the mid-2020s, close to half of all email sent worldwide is spam. Most of it never reaches you, because filters catch it first.

Surprising but true

Junk email is called spam because of a 1970 British comedy sketch, in which a café serves the tinned meat Spam with everything and Vikings sing "Spam" over and over.

The very first spam

On 3 May 1978, a marketing manager at an American computer company called DEC sent a message to about 400 people on ARPANET, the network that grew into the internet. It invited them to see a demonstration of new computers.

Many people were furious. ARPANET was meant for research, not adverts. But the company reportedly sold some computers as a result. A lesson was learned that has never gone away: bulk messages annoy people but sometimes pay.

Why it is called spam

The name comes from Spam, a brand of tinned meat first sold in the USA in 1937. In a 1970 sketch by the British comedy group Monty Python, a café serves Spam with nearly every dish. A group of Vikings then drown out the conversation by singing "Spam, Spam, Spam".

In the 1980s and 1990s, people on early online chat groups began calling floods of repeated junk messages "spam", because they drowned out everything else, just like the singing Vikings.

The maths of spam

Spam works because of very small chances multiplied by very large numbers. In 2008, researchers in California quietly took over part of a real spam network to study it. They found that out of about 350 million emails advertising fake medicine, only 28 led to a sale, about 1 in 12.5 million.

That sounds hopeless. But big spam networks were sending billions of messages a day, so those tiny chances could still add up to real money.

Much spam is sent from ordinary home computers that have been secretly taken over by viruses. Their owners usually have no idea. Thousands of these hijacked computers working together are called a botnet.

How filters fight back

Email services fight spam with filters that sort each message as it arrives. Early filters looked for words like "free money". Spammers simply misspelled them.

So in the early 2000s, programmers built filters that learn. They study millions of emails people have marked as spam or not spam, and work out patterns. The filters look at the words, the sender's address, the links, and whether thousands of identical messages are arriving at once. Each time you press "report spam", you help train them. These smart algorithms now catch the vast majority of spam before it lands in your inbox.

Email is just one front. In India, phones get waves of unwanted calls and text messages too, and the telecom regulator runs a Do Not Disturb list to block marketing calls.

Staying out of trouble

A few habits protect you. Don't click links or open attachments in messages you were not expecting, even if they look official. Real banks never ask for your password or PIN by email or text. If in doubt, ask an adult to check, or go to the company's website directly.

Messages that say "act now" or "you have won" are classic warning signs. Spammers try to make you hurry, because a person who stops to think is much harder to fool. That rush-and-panic trick is now being made even more convincing with AI, which can write polished fake messages and even copy the voices of people you know.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Clicking "unsubscribe" in any spam email will make it stop.

Truth: With real companies you signed up to, unsubscribing usually works. But in true spam, clicking anything can tell the sender your address is real and active, which can bring even more spam. It is safer to mark it as spam and delete it.

Try this

Look at the spam folder of a family email account with an adult. Make a tally of what each message is trying to get, such as money, passwords, clicks or sales. Then list the clues that gave each one away, like spelling mistakes, strange addresses or messages saying "act now".
