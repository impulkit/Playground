How do hearing aids and cochlear implants work?

A cochlear implant skips the ear's broken parts and talks straight to the nerve of hearing with tiny pulses of electricity.

The short answer

A hearing aid is a tiny amplifier you wear in or behind the ear. A microphone picks up sound, a small computer chip boosts the frequencies the person struggles to hear, and a tiny speaker plays the sound into the ear canal. It helps people whose ears still work but have become less sensitive.

A cochlear implant is for people whose inner ear is badly damaged. Instead of making sound louder, it changes sound into electrical pulses. A thin strip of electrodes, placed by a surgeon inside the spiral-shaped cochlea, sends those pulses straight to the hearing nerve. The brain learns to understand them as sound.

Surprising but true

When people first switch on a cochlear implant, voices can sound robotic or squeaky. Over weeks and months, the brain learns to turn the signals into speech that sounds natural.

From ear trumpets to chips

For centuries, people with hearing loss used ear trumpets: horns that collect sound and funnel it into the ear. Beethoven, who slowly went deaf, owned several. Electric hearing aids appeared in the early 1900s, but they were boxes as big as a radio, with heavy batteries.

How did Beethoven compose when he was deaf?By the time he wrote his most famous symphony, Beethoven could hardly hear an orchestra at all. He heard it in his head instead.

Transistors in the 1950s made hearing aids small enough to wear behind the ear. Today's are digital. They can tell speech from background noise, turn down the clatter of a restaurant and turn up a friend's voice. Many connect by Bluetooth to phones and TVs, sending calls and music straight into the ear.

How your ear normally hears

When sound enters your ear, it shakes the eardrum, which passes the vibration through three tiny bones to the cochlea. Inside the cochlea are thousands of tiny hair cells. Each responds to a different pitch: those at the wide end to high sounds, those deep in the spiral to low ones. When they bend, they fire signals along the hearing nerve to the brain.

Loud noise, ageing, some illnesses and some medicines can damage these hair cells. Once lost, they don't grow back in humans. A hearing aid helps the survivors work harder. When too few are left, a cochlear implant takes over their job.

Inside a cochlear implant

An implant has two parts. Outside, a sound processor sits behind the ear with a microphone. It turns sounds into a code and sends it through the skin by radio to the inside part, which is held in place by a magnet.

Inside, under the skin, a receiver passes the code to a strip of up to about two dozen electrodes curled through the cochlea. Each electrode stimulates nerve fibres for a different pitch, copying the way hair cells are laid out. The Australian surgeon Graeme Clark and his team implanted their first multichannel device in 1978. By 2019, the US National Institutes of Health estimated about 737,000 implants worldwide.

Learning to listen

At first, an implant can sound strange: beeps, buzzes or robot voices. With practice, the brain learns to make sense of it. Young children who get implants early, often around their first birthday, can learn to speak and understand speech well. In India, a government scheme helps pay for implants for young children from families who can't afford them.

A choice, not a cure

Not everyone who is deaf wants to hear. Many Deaf people, who often write "Deaf" with a capital D, see themselves as a community with its own culture and its own sign language, not as people with something to fix. Some have worried that implants push children away from signing. Others feel implants open doors. Today, many families choose both: an implant and sign language.

The World Health Organization says more than 1.5 billion people live with some hearing loss, and many could be helped by a hearing aid but don't have one. In many places, the biggest gap is not the technology but the cost and the lack of hearing tests.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Hearing aids give people back perfect, normal hearing, like glasses do for eyes.

Truth: Hearing aids help a lot, but they can't repair damaged parts of the ear. Sounds may seem different at first, and noisy places can still be hard. The brain needs time to get used to them.

Try this

Roll a sheet of stiff paper into a cone, like an old ear trumpet. Hold the narrow end near (not in) your ear and point the wide end at a quietly ticking clock or a friend whispering. Compare how loud it sounds with and without the cone.
