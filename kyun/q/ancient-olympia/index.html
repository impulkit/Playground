What was daily life like in ancient Greece?

A Greek boy walked to school with a slave to carry his things, learned the lyre and wrestled before lunch. His sister mostly stayed home.

The short answer

Daily life in ancient Greece depended on who you were and which city you lived in. In Athens, around 2,500 years ago, a citizen man might farm, trade or make things, then spend time in the agora, the open marketplace, talking politics and shopping. Citizen women mostly ran the household and rarely went out alone.

Many of the hardest jobs were done by enslaved people, captured in war or bought. Food was simple: barley bread or porridge, olives, figs, cheese, fish and wine mixed with water. Meat was usually eaten at religious festivals, after an animal had been offered to the gods.

Surprising but true

A Spartan princess called Kyniska won the four-horse chariot race at the Olympic Games in 396 BCE, even though married women were not allowed to watch.

A day at school

Boys from families who could pay started school at about seven. An enslaved servant called a paidagogos walked them there and kept an eye on them. From him we get the word "pedagogy", the art of teaching.

Pupils learned to read and write, often scratching letters on wax tablets with a pointed stick. They memorised long passages of Homer's poems, learned to play the lyre and sing, and spent hours at the gymnasium running and wrestling. Girls were usually taught at home, learning to spin, weave and manage a household. In Sparta things were different: girls there exercised outdoors too, which shocked visitors from Athens.

Home, food and clothes

Most houses were built of sun-dried mud brick around an open courtyard, which let in light and air. Rooms were sparsely furnished, with stools, chests and couches.

People wore a simple tunic called a chiton, made from a rectangle of wool or linen pinned at the shoulders. A cloak went over the top in cold weather. Wealthy men held drinking parties called symposia, where guests lay on couches, sang, played games and argued about ideas. Painted pottery shows these scenes in such detail that we can almost hear the music.

The festival at Olympia

Every four years, Greeks from rival cities travelled to Olympia, a sanctuary of Zeus in the west of Greece. Tradition says the first recorded Games took place in 776 BCE. A sacred truce let athletes and visitors travel safely.

The oldest event was the stadion, a sprint of about 190 metres, roughly two lengths of a football field. Later came wrestling, boxing, the pentathlon and chariot races. Athletes competed naked. Married women could not watch, but owners of winning horses counted as champions, so in 396 BCE the Spartan princess Kyniska became an Olympic victor through her chariot team. The Games carried on for over a thousand years, until about 393 CE.

Gods in everyday life

Religion was part of every day. Families kept a small altar at home and poured a little wine as an offering before meals. Cities held festivals with processions, sacrifices, music and plays. In Athens, these festivals gave birth to theatre, with actors in masks performing tragedies and comedies in vast open-air theatres.

People asked the gods for help with harvests, journeys, illnesses and love. Stories of Zeus, Athena and the other Greek gods were told at home long before children ever read them.

Lives we can barely see

Most of what we know comes from writers in Athens, nearly all wealthy men. Enslaved people, poor farmers and most women left few words of their own. Historians estimate that enslaved people may have made up a large share of Athens's population, but the true number is unknown. Archaeology helps fill the gap: a child's toy, a loom weight or a worn cooking pot speaks for people the books left out.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ancient Greek statues and temples were always plain white marble.

Truth: Traces of paint show that many statues and buildings were brightly coloured. Over the centuries the paint wore away, leaving the bare stone we see in museums.

Try this

Hold a Greek-style dinner. Serve bread, olives, cheese, figs or dates, and water. Lie on cushions on the floor to eat like guests at a Greek party, then take turns telling a short story or riddle, as Greek diners did.
