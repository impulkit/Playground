How was the length of a metre decided?

In the 1790s, two French astronomers spent seven years measuring a slice of the Earth, just to decide how long a metre should be.

The short answer

The metre was invented in France in the 1790s. Scientists wanted a unit that belonged to nobody, not to any king or town, and would never change. So they based it on the Earth itself.

They decided a metre would be one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator, measured along the line of longitude that runs through Paris. To find that distance, two astronomers spent seven years measuring part of it by hand.

The metre has been redefined several times since, each time more precisely. Today it is fixed using the speed of light.

Surprising but true

Today a metre is defined by light. It is the distance light travels in a vacuum in 1/299,792,458 of a second.

A country tangled in units

Before the French Revolution of 1789, France had a mess of measures. One estimate says there were about 250,000 local versions of units in use. A "pound" or a "foot" in one town could be different in the next. Traders cheated, and arguments were endless.

The new revolutionary government wanted fairness and reason. In 1791, the French Academy of Sciences recommended basing a new unit of length on the size of the Earth. It was named the mètre, from a Greek word for "measure". Everything else would be built from it in tens. A litre would be the volume of a box 10 cm on each side. A kilogram would be the mass of a litre of water.

Seven years of measuring

Two astronomers, Jean-Baptiste Delambre and Pierre Méchain, were sent to measure the distance from Dunkirk, on the northern coast of France, to Barcelona in Spain. Delambre started in the north, Méchain in the south.

They worked by triangulation. From hilltops and church towers, they measured the angles between distant landmarks to build a long chain of triangles. With careful geometry, the angles and one measured base line revealed the full distance.

It was slow and sometimes dangerous. France was at war, and the astronomers were arrested more than once by suspicious locals who thought their instruments were for spying. Méchain later found a small error in his own measurements and kept it secret, which troubled him for the rest of his life.

In 1799, the result was made into a bar of platinum, the "metre of the Archives".

Going round the world

Other countries were slow to join at first. In 1875, seventeen nations, including the United States, signed the Metre Convention in Paris and set up an international office to look after the units. In 1889, new metal bars of platinum and iridium were made and copies sent to each country.

Over the next century, most of the world adopted the system. India's metric law was passed in 1956. Today almost every country uses metric units for science, trade and daily life.

A metre made of light

A metal bar has problems. It can be scratched, it grows a tiny bit in heat, and there is only one original. So in 1960, scientists redefined the metre using the light given off by krypton atoms.

In 1983, they went further. Light in a vacuum always travels at the same speed, so they fixed that speed at exactly 299,792,458 metres per second. A metre became the distance light travels in 1/299,792,458 of a second. That is the same idea astronomers use when they measure space in light-years.

In 2019, the kilogram was also redefined using a constant of nature, so the last metal standard was retired. The GPS in your phone depends on measuring time and distance with this kind of accuracy.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The metre is defined by a metal bar kept in Paris.

Truth: It used to be. Since 1983, the metre has been defined using the speed of light, which is the same everywhere in the universe. Any well-equipped laboratory can now measure a metre without visiting France.

Try this

Cut a piece of string exactly one metre long using a ruler or tape measure. Use it to measure your height, your bed and the longest room in your home. Then lay it on the floor and take normal steps along it. How many of your steps make a metre?
