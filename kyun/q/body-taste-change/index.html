Why do vegetables taste better as you grow up?

Babies in the womb can taste what their mothers eat, and in one study, babies whose mothers drank carrot juice liked carrot-flavoured cereal more.

The short answer

Vegetables often taste better as you grow up for a mix of reasons. Young children are naturally more sensitive to bitterness and more cautious about new foods. Many vegetables, like karela, methi, broccoli and cabbage, contain bitter chemicals that put them off.

As you grow, you taste these foods again and again. Your brain learns they are safe, and you start to notice other flavours, like sweetness, crunch and the taste of spices. Your genes matter too. Some people taste bitterness far more strongly than others, all their lives.

Surprising but true

Brussels sprouts taste milder today than they did in the 1980s, because plant breeders found the chemicals that made them bitter and bred new varieties with less of them.

A safety system on your tongue

Your tongue has thousands of taste buds, each with tiny cells that detect five basic tastes: sweet, salty, sour, bitter and umami, a savoury taste found in tomatoes and cheese. Sweet usually means energy, and babies love it from birth. Bitter can be a warning, since many poisonous plants taste bitter.

Scientists think young children's strong dislike of bitterness helped keep them safe. A toddler who spat out bitter leaves was less likely to eat something harmful. The same instinct makes many children wrinkle their noses at karela, even though it is perfectly safe.

Practice makes it tasty

Researchers at University College London asked children to taste a vegetable they did not like, in tiny amounts, on eight days spread over about two weeks. Many children ended up liking it more. Other studies suggest children often need 8 to 15 tries before they accept a new food.

Forcing children to eat usually backfires. Tasting without pressure, and seeing parents and friends enjoy the food, works better. This is called "learning to like". It explains why foods that seemed awful at six can taste lovely at sixteen. Cooking helps too. Roasting cauliflower or frying bhindi until crisp brings out sweeter, nuttier flavours that hide some of the bitterness.

Learning flavours before birth

Taste learning starts very early. Flavours from a mother's food reach the fluid around a baby in the womb and later the breast milk. In a study at the Monell Chemical Senses Center in the USA, babies whose mothers drank carrot juice during pregnancy or breastfeeding made fewer disgusted faces when fed carrot-flavoured cereal.

That may be one reason children in different countries grow up loving different foods. A child in Andhra Pradesh may enjoy fiery chillies from early on. A child in Minnesota might prefer milder food. Both are learning from what their families eat.

Genes and changing bodies

About one in four people are so-called supertasters. They have a version of a gene that makes certain bitter chemicals taste extra strong. To them, broccoli, black coffee and some greens can taste harsh their whole lives. Others barely notice those flavours at all.

Taste cells are replaced every week or two, and the number of taste buds slowly falls as people get much older. Smell also changes. Since most flavour actually comes from smell, as the taste and smell link shows, older people may find food tastes milder.

The vegetables have changed too. In the 1990s, Dutch scientists identified the bitter chemicals in Brussels sprouts. Plant breeders then grew milder varieties, and sales went up. Some foods are bitter on purpose, though. Karela is loved across India for its sharpness, cooked with onions, jaggery or spices to balance it. And the tongue has one more surprise: the old map showing sweet at the tip and bitter at the back is wrong, since every part can taste every flavour. Umami, the savoury fifth taste hidden in tomatoes and cheese, has its own odd history in umami science.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If a child hates a vegetable the first time, they will hate it forever.

Truth: Tastes change a lot. Studies show many children start to like a food after tasting small amounts 8 to 15 times, especially without being forced.

Try this

Do a blind taste test. Pinch your nose and have someone feed you a small piece of apple and a small piece of raw potato or cucumber. Can you tell which is which? Let go of your nose and try again, and notice how much flavour really comes from smell.
