What crafts come from Kutch?

In one village in Kutch, artists paint cloth with boiled castor oil, using a metal stick and no brush at all.

The short answer

Kutch, in the far west of Gujarat, is the largest district in India. Much of it is the Rann, a flat salt desert that floods in the monsoon. Life there was always hard, and people turned what little they had into beautiful, useful things.

The result is one of the richest craft regions on Earth. Kutch is known for Ajrakh block printing, Rogan painting, mirror-studded embroidery, Bandhani tie-dye, mud-and-mirror wall art called lippan, woven shawls and blankets, and copper-coated bells. Many crafts belong to particular communities, and skills pass from parents to children.

Surprising but true

The round mud huts of Kutch, called bhungas, are famous for having stood up well in the 2001 earthquake while many concrete buildings fell.

Painting with oil and a stick

In the village of Nirona, a few artists practise Rogan painting. They boil castor oil for many hours until it turns thick and sticky, then mix in coloured powders. The artist puts a blob of paste in one palm and draws it out into thin threads with a metal stick, laying the thread on cloth. No brush touches the fabric.

Often only half the design is painted. The cloth is folded and pressed, printing a mirror copy on the other half. Rogan nearly died out in the last century. One family in Nirona kept it alive for generations, and they now train more learners.

Twenty days for one cloth

Ajrakh is a kind of block printing done with carved wooden blocks and natural dyes, mainly deep indigo blue and madder red. A single cloth can go through up to about 16 stages: soaking, printing a paste that resists dye, dyeing, washing in the river, drying in the sun, and printing again. It can take two or three weeks.

Ajrakh printers once worked in the village of Dhamadka. After the 2001 earthquake and years of water trouble, many families moved and set up a new village. They called it Ajrakhpur, which means "the town of Ajrakh".

Mirrors on mud walls

On 26 January 2001, a powerful earthquake struck Kutch. Thousands of people died and the city of Bhuj was badly damaged. But many of the region's round mud huts, called bhungas, stayed standing. Their circular shape and light roofs helped them sway instead of cracking.

Inside a bhunga, the walls are often covered in lippan kaam. Women shape raised patterns from clay mixed with dung, then press in small pieces of mirror. When a lamp is lit at night, the whole room twinkles. Lippan is now made on boards to hang in city homes too.

Threads that tell who you are

Kutch has more than a dozen styles of embroidery. Rabari, Ahir, Jat, Meghwal and other communities each use their own stitches, colours and motifs. Someone who knows the styles can often tell where a woman comes from just by looking at her blouse or her quilt. Tiny round mirrors, called abhla, are stitched into many designs to catch the light.

In Bhujodi, families of weavers make shawls and blankets on pit looms, some from wool of local sheep and camels. Nearby, Bandhani makers tie thousands of tiny knots in cloth before dyeing it, and a single fine sari can hold tens of thousands of dots.

Bells made in the mud

Back in Nirona and the nearby village of Zura, metalworkers make bells for cattle and camels. They fold a sheet of iron into shape, coat it in copper and brass powder, wrap it in mud and bake it in a fire. The coating melts and fuses to the bell. Each one is tuned by tapping, and herders say they can tell their own animals' bells apart in the dark.

Every winter, a tent city rises on the white salt of the Rann for the Rann Utsav festival, and visitors come to buy these crafts straight from the people who made them. The salt itself is a strange sight: under a full moon, the desert glows almost as white as snow.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Craft villages only make souvenirs for tourists.

Truth: Many Kutch crafts began as things people needed every day, such as clothes, quilts, bells for cattle and wall decoration. Embroidery patterns still show which community a woman belongs to.

Try this

Try mirror printing. Fold a sheet of paper in half, open it, and drop blobs of thick poster paint on one side only. Fold it shut, press firmly with your palm, then open it to find a matching pattern on both halves.
