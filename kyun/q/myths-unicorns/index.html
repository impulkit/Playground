Where did the idea of unicorns come from?

Around 400 BCE, a Greek doctor wrote that India was home to wild horse-like beasts with a single magic horn. He had never been to India.

The short answer

The unicorn began as a travellers' tale, and India sits right at the start of it. Around 400 BCE, a Greek doctor named Ctesias, working at the Persian royal court, wrote about wild animals in India the size of horses, with white bodies and a single long horn. He had never been there. He was probably passing on stories, and many historians think they grew from real animals, especially the one-horned rhinoceros.

Later writers repeated and decorated the tale. By the Middle Ages, the unicorn had become a pure white horse with a spiral horn and magical powers. Real objects, like narwhal tusks, made people believe it even more.

Surprising but true

For hundreds of years, rich Europeans paid more than its weight in gold for "unicorn horn", which was really the tusk of an Arctic whale called the narwhal.

A rhino in disguise?

Ctesias said the horn of the Indian beast could cure poisoning. Later, the Roman writer Pliny the Elder described a "monoceros", meaning one-horn, with a horse's body, an elephant's feet and a deep bellow. That sounds less like a fairy-tale pony and more like a rhino.

The Indian rhino really does have one horn. It lives in grasslands in Assam and Nepal today. Its horn is made of keratin, the same protein as your fingernails, not bone. A garbled description passed from trader to trader across Persia and Greece could easily turn a heavy grey animal into something more magical.

The one-horned animal on Indus seals

Long before Ctesias, people of the Indus Valley carved small stone seals, about 4,500 years ago. The most common animal on them looks like a bull with a single horn. Archaeologists often call it the "unicorn" seal.

Did they believe in a one-horned beast? Some think so. Others think it is simply a bull drawn side-on, with one horn hiding the other. Because nobody can read the Indus script yet, the real meaning is a mystery.

Why can't anyone read the Indus script yet?Over 4,000 years ago, people in the Indus cities carved tiny messages on stone seals. Nobody alive today can read a single one.

Whale tusks sold as magic

In medieval Europe, "unicorn horns" were among the most precious things a king could own. They were long, white and spiralled. They were believed to detect poison, and some were worth more than their weight in gold.

They were really tusks of the narwhal, a whale from the Arctic. A male narwhal's tusk is an overgrown tooth that can grow longer than a tall man is tall. Viking and later traders sold them without explaining their source. Denmark's royal throne, made in the 1600s, is built largely from narwhal tusks. By the 1600s, as more people saw narwhals, scientists began to see through the trick.

A unicorn on the royal arms

Scotland adopted the unicorn as a royal symbol in the 1400s, and it is still the country's national animal. In stories, the unicorn was proud and could not be tamed, which suited a kingdom that valued its freedom. When Scotland and England shared a king from 1603, the royal coat of arms showed a lion and a unicorn side by side.

Asia has its own one-horned creatures too. The Chinese qilin, sometimes called a unicorn in English, is a gentle beast whose arrival was said to signal a wise ruler.

Unicorns today

Today, unicorns are everywhere: on lunchboxes, cakes and cartoons. In business, a "unicorn" is a young company worth a billion US dollars, a name chosen because such companies were once very rare. India and the USA now have many of them. A unicorn face was added to the official emoji set in 2015, and it quickly became a favourite for birthday messages.

And the strangest part? A giant hairy rhino with a huge single horn, nicknamed the "Siberian unicorn", really did roam Asia until tens of thousands of years ago, at the same time as early humans. Stories of dragons may also have begun with real bones.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Unicorns were invented for fairy tales and children's toys.

Truth: For centuries, educated people in Europe and Asia believed unicorns were real animals living in faraway lands. Natural history books described them alongside lions and elephants.
