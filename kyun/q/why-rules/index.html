Why do we need rules?

Nearly 3,800 years ago, a king in Babylon had nearly 300 laws carved into a stone taller than a grown man, so everyone could see them.

The short answer

Rules let people who don't know each other trust what will happen next. When you stop at a red light, you are counting on the driver coming the other way to stop too. When you bat in a match, you trust that the bowler can't suddenly throw from two metres away.

Without rules, every shared thing would turn into an argument: roads, playgrounds, money, even a queue at the canteen. Groups of people, from families to whole countries, make rules so they can live and work together without fighting over every small decision. The best rules feel fair to most people, and can be changed when they stop working.

Surprising but true

Cricket's rules are officially called the Laws of Cricket, and they are still looked after by a London club, the MCC, which wrote its first code in 1788.

Rules carved in stone

Rulers have tried to write rules down for a very long time. Around 1750 BCE, King Hammurabi of Babylon had about 280 laws carved on a black stone pillar more than two metres tall. You can still see it in the Louvre in Paris. The Code of Hammurabi covered things like debts, farming and building houses safely.

In India, the emperor Ashoka had his messages carved on rocks and pillars across his empire in the 3rd century BCE. They asked people to respect parents, be kind to animals and treat other faiths with respect. Writing rules where everyone can read them means a ruler cannot simply change them in secret.

Who makes the rules

Today most countries make laws through elected lawmakers. India's Constitution, which came into force on 26 January 1950, is the rule book for how the country is run. It even sets out how the rules themselves can be changed. The United States Constitution was written in 1787 and came into force in 1789.

Smaller rules come from everywhere. Schools have them. Housing societies have them. Your family probably has a few about screen time or who washes the dishes. A rule made by the people it affects is usually easier to follow than one handed down with no explanation.

Rules that make games possible

A game is really a set of rules. The oldest known written laws of cricket date from 1744. The Marylebone Cricket Club in London drew up its own code in 1788, and the club still updates it. Football, chess and carrom all work because both sides agree on what counts.

Games also show why people need referees and umpires. Everyone might know the rules, but someone still has to decide, quickly and calmly, whether the ball crossed the line. In the same way, countries have courts and judges to settle arguments about laws.

Why people follow rules

Some people follow rules because they fear punishment. But most of the time, people follow them because they see the point. Researchers who study cooperation, including mathematicians working on game theory, find that groups do best when most people play fair and cheats are spotted.

Small children start to notice unfair rules very early. Ask any eight-year-old what happens when one sibling gets a bigger slice of cake. Our sense of fairness is one reason good rules last, and unfair ones get challenged.

When rules should change

Rules are made by people, so they can be wrong. For many years, laws in India and the United States treated people unequally because of caste, race or being a woman. People protested, argued in courts and voted, and many of those laws were changed. Some rules simply go out of date. Old laws in some places once banned things that nobody worries about now, and lawmakers sometimes spend whole sessions clearing away rules that no one has used in a century. Other rules arrive with new inventions. There were no laws about seat belts before cars, and none about online privacy before the internet. A good rule book includes a way to rewrite itself.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Rules are only there to stop you doing things.

Truth: Many rules make things possible. Traffic rules let millions of strangers share roads, and game rules are the reason a game exists at all. Without agreed rules, cricket is just people hitting a ball.

Try this

Play a simple game, like noughts and crosses or a card game, with a friend, but with no rules agreed in advance. Each player can make up a rule at any time. After a few minutes, stop and talk about what went wrong, then write down three rules you both agree on and play again.
