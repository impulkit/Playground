What is a comet's tail made of?

A comet is a dirty snowball the size of a city. Its tail can be longer than the distance to the Sun.

The short answer

A comet's tail is made of gas and dust that boil off its icy centre as it nears the Sun.

The centre, called the nucleus, is a lump of frozen water, frozen gases, dust and rock, usually a few kilometres wide. Far from the Sun it stays frozen and dark. As it swings closer, the Sun's heat turns some of its ice straight into gas, which drags dust out with it. This makes a glowing cloud around the nucleus and, often, two long tails. The tails can stretch for tens of millions of kilometres.

Surprising but true

A comet's tail always points away from the Sun, even when the comet is flying away from it.

Two tails, not one

Look closely at a bright comet and you may see two tails. The dust tail is broad and curved, and shines yellowish-white because the dust reflects sunlight. The gas tail is narrow and straight, and often glows blue.

They point slightly different ways because different things push them. Sunlight itself gently shoves the dust. The gas is blown straight back by the solar wind, a stream of charged particles that flows out from the Sun. Both always point away from the Sun.

Where comets come from

Most comets spend almost all their time in the cold, dark outer solar system. Some come from the Kuiper Belt, the icy zone beyond Neptune where Pluto lives. Others come from the Oort Cloud, a vast shell of icy bodies much farther out still.

Now and then, a nudge sends one falling inwards on a long, stretched orbit. Some comets return every few years. Others take thousands of years to come back. Old Indian texts call comets dhumketu, which means "smoke banner".

The comet that came back on time

In 1705, the English astronomer Edmond Halley noticed that comets seen in 1531, 1607 and 1682 followed almost the same path. He guessed they were one comet and predicted it would return in 1758. Halley died before then, but the comet appeared right on schedule, and it was named after him.

When Halley's Comet came back in 1910, Earth passed through its tail. Some people panicked and bought "comet pills" to protect themselves. Nothing happened, because the tail is spread so thin.

Landing on a comet

In 2014, the European Space Agency's Rosetta spacecraft reached a comet called 67P, after a ten-year journey. It released a small lander, Philae, which bounced twice before settling in a shadowy spot. Rosetta followed the comet for two years as it passed the Sun.

The comet turned out to be shaped like a rubber duck, with a surface darker than coal. Rosetta also measured its water. That water was different in one important way from the water in Earth's oceans. Scientists still argue about how much of Earth's water came from comets.

Leftovers from the beginning

Comets are some of the oldest, least changed objects in the solar system. They formed about 4.6 billion years ago from the same cloud as the planets, and have stayed in the deep freeze ever since. Studying one is a little like opening a freezer that has been shut since the solar system was born.

Comets can also meet a dramatic end. In 1994, astronomers watched Comet Shoemaker-Levy 9, which Jupiter's gravity had torn into more than 20 pieces, crash into Jupiter one piece at a time. The impacts left dark scars bigger than Earth in Jupiter's clouds.

When a comet passes the Sun it leaves a trail of dust. When Earth later ploughs through that trail, we see a meteor shower of shooting stars.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Comets streak across the sky in a flash, like shooting stars.

Truth: A comet is millions of kilometres away, so it seems to hang in the sky. It moves only a little against the stars from night to night and can stay visible for weeks.

Try this

Make a comet from a crumpled ball of paper with long strips of tissue or ribbon taped on. Stand in front of a table fan (the fan is the Sun) and walk slowly around it, holding your comet. Watch where the strips point as you move towards the fan, past it and away from it.
