How did people start counting?

Long before anyone wrote a number, people cut notches into bones, tied knots in string and pointed to parts of their own bodies to keep count.

The short answer

People began counting long before they could write. The earliest clues are bones with rows of scratched notches, perhaps used as tally marks. Later, farmers in the Middle East used small clay tokens to stand for sheep, jars of oil or bags of grain.

Around 5,000 years ago, people in Mesopotamia, in what is now Iraq, began pressing number signs into clay tablets. That was one of the first steps towards writing itself. Different cultures found many other ways to count, using fingers, body parts, knotted strings and pebbles. The number words you use today are the end of a very long story.

Surprising but true

The Oksapmin people of Papua New Guinea traditionally count by pointing to 27 places on their bodies, from one thumb, up the arm, over the head and down to the other hand.

Notches in bone

In 1950, a Belgian scientist digging near Ishango, close to Lake Edward in Central Africa, found a small bone with a piece of quartz fixed in one end. It is about 20,000 years old. Down its sides run groups of notches.

Some researchers think the marks are a tally, a simple record where one cut means one thing. Some see patterns in the numbers. Others say the marks might be decoration or a better grip. Nobody can be sure. But the idea of a tally is easy to understand. It is the same as a cricket scorer marking strokes on paper, or prisoners in old films counting days on a wall.

Clay tokens and the first writing

From about 8000 BCE, people in the Middle East shaped small tokens from clay: cones, spheres and discs. Archaeologist Denise Schmandt-Besserat argued that each shape stood for a type of goods, such as a measure of grain or one sheep.

To keep tokens safe, people sealed them inside hollow clay balls. Then someone had a clever idea. Why not press the tokens into the outside, so you could see what was inside without breaking it? Eventually, people skipped the tokens and drew the signs on flat tablets instead. In the city of Uruk in Mesopotamia, around 3200 BCE, these signs grew into one of the world's first writing systems.

Bodies, fingers and knots

Fingers are the most handy counting tool, which is why so many people count in tens. Some cultures went further. The Oksapmin of Papua New Guinea traditionally count using 27 body parts. In many parts of India and the Middle East, people count to 12 on one hand by touching the thumb to finger sections. Some historians think this may help explain the dozen.

In South America, the Inca kept records on khipu, bundles of coloured cords with knots. A knot's position showed ones, tens, hundreds and thousands. Trained record-keepers could read census numbers and tax records from them. Hundreds of khipu survive in museums today, and researchers are still trying to work out whether some of them also recorded words.

Sixty minutes, ten digits

The Babylonians of Mesopotamia counted in groups of 60. Sixty divides neatly by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20 and 30, which made sharing easy. We still use their system every day: 60 seconds in a minute, 60 minutes in an hour, 360 degrees in a circle.

Over many centuries, people found faster ways to calculate, from pebbles and counting boards to the abacus. The biggest leap came in India, with ten digits and a place-value system, where a digit's position tells you its size. To see why that worked so well, read about number systems.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Animals and babies have no sense of number until they are taught.

Truth: Experiments show that babies, crows, bees, fish and many other animals can tell small amounts apart, like two from three. Counting large numbers exactly, using words or symbols, is the part humans invented.

Try this

Count to 12 on one hand by touching your thumb to each section of your four fingers, three sections per finger. This method is still used by many people in India and the Middle East. Then use your other hand to keep track of each dozen, and see how high you can get.
