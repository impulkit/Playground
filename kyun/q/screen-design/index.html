Why do apps look the way they do?

The three little lines that open a menu on almost every app were first drawn in 1981, for a computer most people have never heard of.

The short answer

Every app you use has been carefully planned by designers. They decide where buttons go, how big they are, what colours to use and what happens when you tap. Their job is to make the app feel obvious, so that you can use it without reading instructions.

Many apps look alike because their designers have learned the same lessons. Fingers are fat and screens are small, so buttons must be big enough to hit. People's thumbs rest near the bottom of a phone, so important buttons move there. Users expect certain symbols, like a magnifying glass for search, so designers rarely change them. Apple and Google also publish detailed guides that most designers follow.

Surprising but true

Apple recommends that any button you tap should be at least 44 points across, roughly the width of a fingertip, because smaller ones get missed.

Buttons that ask to be pressed

In his book The Design of Everyday Things, the American scientist Don Norman wrote about objects that show you how to use them. A door handle you can grab says "pull". A flat metal plate says "push". The same idea works on screens.

A button with a shape, a colour or a shadow looks pressable. Plain grey text does not. Designers also give feedback: a button changes colour when tapped, or the phone gives a tiny buzz. Without that, people tap again and again, wondering whether anything happened.

From leather to flat

When the iPhone launched in 2007, many apps copied real objects. The notes app looked like a yellow notepad. The calendar had stitched leather edges. This style is called skeuomorphism, from Greek words meaning "tool" and "shape". It helped people who had never used a touchscreen guess what to do.

By the 2010s, people knew how phones worked, and the fake leather started to feel old-fashioned. Microsoft's Windows Phone, in 2010, used bold flat colours and simple tiles. Apple switched to a flat style with iOS 7 in 2013. Today most apps sit somewhere in between, with flat colours plus soft shadows to show what can be pressed.

Who designs everyday things?Someone decided the shape of your toothbrush, the buttons on a TV remote and the symbol ₹ on Indian money. Most of the time, you never notice them.

Symbols everyone knows

Some icons have outlived the things they show. The "save" button in many programs is still a floppy disk, though most children have never seen one. A phone handset means "call", even on phones shaped like slabs of glass.

The three-line menu icon, often called the hamburger, was drawn by designer Norm Cox for the Xerox Star computer in 1981. It vanished for years and came back with smartphones. Designers keep familiar symbols because changing them confuses millions of people at once. Road signs work the same way: their shapes and fonts change very slowly, because drivers must read them in a split second.

Designing for everyone

Good design works for people who see, hear or move differently. About one in 12 men has some colour blindness, usually trouble telling red from green. So designers don't rely on colour alone. An error message might be red and also carry a warning symbol.

Text must contrast strongly with its background so people with weak eyesight can read it. Apps should work with screen readers, which read out what is on the screen for blind users. In India, many apps offer several languages and scripts, and some use clear icons for people who are just learning to read.

Testing, and some tricks

Big companies test designs on real people. In an A/B test, half the users see one version and half see another, and the company measures which one people use more. Even the shade of a button can be tested.

Not every trick is kind. Some apps use "dark patterns": designs that push you into things you didn't mean to do, like a tiny "no thanks" link or an endless scroll that's hard to stop. Knowing how apps are made helps you spot when a design is working for you, and when it's working on you.

Myth or fact?

Myth: App designers just make things look pretty.

Truth: Looks matter, but most of the work is about making apps easy and safe to use. Designers study where people tap, what confuses them and how to make apps usable for people with poor eyesight or shaky hands.

Try this

Draw the home screen of an app you would like to exist on a sheet of paper the size of a phone. Ask someone to "tap" with their finger to do one task, such as finding a song, while you watch without helping. Where did they hesitate? Redraw it and test again.
