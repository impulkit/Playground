What changed in India in 1991?

In 1991, India had only enough foreign money to pay for about three weeks of imports, and it flew gold to London to borrow cash.

The short answer

In 1991, India changed the way its economy worked. For about 40 years, the government had controlled much of business. Companies needed licences to start, grow or make new products, and imports were tightly limited.

That summer, India faced a money crisis. It had almost run out of foreign currency to pay for oil and other imports. Prime Minister P. V. Narasimha Rao and Finance Minister Manmohan Singh responded with big reforms. They scrapped most licences, cut taxes on imports, welcomed foreign companies and lowered the value of the rupee. India's economy began to open up, and it grew faster in the decades that followed.

Surprising but true

Before 1991, a family in India could wait years for a new telephone line or a scooter, and a company often needed a government licence to make more of its own products.

The licence raj

After independence in 1947, India's leaders wanted to build industry quickly and keep it in Indian hands. The government ran steel plants, airlines, banks and much more. Private companies needed permission for almost everything, a system people nicknamed the "licence raj".

This protected some industries, but it also made shortages common. Waiting lists for a Bajaj scooter or a phone line could run for years. There were only a few car models, and the Ambassador and the Fiat looked the same year after year. Taxes on imports were among the highest in the world.

A country running out of money

By 1990, India was borrowing heavily abroad. Then the Gulf War sent oil prices soaring and cut off money sent home by Indians working in Kuwait and Iraq. Political trouble at home made foreign lenders nervous.

By June 1991, India's foreign reserves could pay for only about three weeks of imports. To get emergency loans, the government pledged gold. About 47 tonnes from the Reserve Bank of India were flown to London and pledged with the Bank of England and the Bank of Japan. It felt like a family pawning its jewellery, and many Indians were shocked.

The 1991 budget

The new government acted fast. In early July, the rupee was devalued by about 18–19%, making Indian exports cheaper abroad. On 24 July 1991, Manmohan Singh presented a budget that changed the rules. Most industrial licences were abolished. Import duties started to fall. Foreign companies could own bigger shares of businesses in India.

He ended his speech with a line from the French writer Victor Hugo, saying that no power on earth can stop an idea whose time has come. India borrowed from the IMF and the World Bank too, and agreed to further changes as part of the deal.

What changed for families

Over the next decade, daily life changed in ways people could see. New car brands arrived. Telephone lines became easier to get, and then mobile phones appeared. Private airlines started flying. Cable TV brought dozens of channels into homes that had only Doordarshan.

Indian software companies grew quickly, selling services to the US and Europe. Cities like Bengaluru and Hyderabad became technology hubs, and later a wave of Indian start-ups grew from that base. India's economy grew faster in the 2000s than in the 1970s and 80s.

Arguments that continue

The reforms are widely seen as a turning point, but not everyone agrees about their effects. Supporters point out that hundreds of millions of Indians have moved out of extreme poverty since 1991. Critics say the gains went mostly to cities and the better-off, while farmers and informal workers were left behind, and that the gap between rich and poor widened.

Some economists also note that India's growth had already started to pick up in the 1980s, so 1991 sped up a change that was beginning. What almost everyone agrees on is that the crisis of 1991 forced decisions that had been put off for years.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The 1991 reforms happened because India's leaders had always planned to open up the economy.

Truth: The reforms were largely forced by a crisis. India was close to running out of foreign money to pay for imports such as oil. The government had to act quickly, though some leaders had been arguing for changes for years.
