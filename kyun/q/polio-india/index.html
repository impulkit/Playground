How did India beat polio?

India once had more polio cases than any other country. Then, on a single day, millions of volunteers set out to give two drops to every child.

The short answer

Polio is a disease caused by a virus that spreads through dirty water and hands. Most infected people feel only mildly ill or nothing at all, but in about one in 200 cases it damages nerves and leaves a person unable to move their legs or arms. Most victims were children under five.

India beat it with the oral polio vaccine, two drops in the mouth, given again and again to every child under five. From 1995, the government's Pulse Polio campaign sent out millions of health workers and volunteers on vaccination days. The last case of wild polio in India was in January 2011.

Surprising but true

India's last case of wild polio was an 18-month-old girl named Rukhsar Khatoon in Howrah, West Bengal, in January 2011. She survived and later went to school.

A vaccine you swallow

In the 1950s, two American scientists created polio vaccines. Jonas Salk's vaccine was given as an injection and was tested on more than a million US schoolchildren in 1954. Albert Sabin's vaccine was taken as drops in the mouth.

India used Sabin's drops. They are cheap, need no needle, and can be given by volunteers after a little training. That made it possible to reach huge numbers of children on a single day.

Two drops, 17 crore children

On a national Pulse Polio day, about 23 lakh vaccinators fanned out across the country. They set up booths in villages and cities, then went door to door over the next days to find any child who had been missed. They marked houses with chalk to show which had been visited.

In one round, they could reach about 17 crore (170 million) children, more than half the population of the USA. If those numbers feel hard to picture, see our guide to big numbers.

The hardest places

Polio hung on longest in western Uttar Pradesh and Bihar. Many people lived close together, clean water was scarce, and young children had frequent stomach infections that made the vaccine work less well. In Bihar, floods on the Kosi river cut villages off.

Vaccinators found ways round. They boarded trains to give drops to children on the move. They visited brick kilns, building sites, nomadic camps and the Kumbh Mela. Scientists switched to newer versions of the vaccine aimed at the remaining types of polio. Film star Amitabh Bachchan appeared in advertisements to persuade worried parents.

The last case

On 13 January 2011, an 18-month-old girl named Rukhsar Khatoon fell ill with polio in Howrah district, near Kolkata. She had missed her polio drops. She survived.

After that, India watched and waited. Health workers kept testing sewage and checking every child who suddenly couldn't move a limb. No more wild cases appeared. In 2014, India and its neighbours in South-East Asia were officially declared polio-free.

What India's win means

Many experts had said India would be the hardest country in the world to clear of polio. Its success showed that it could be done anywhere.

The campaign was led by the Government of India together with the WHO, UNICEF, Rotary International and the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Rotary clubs across India raised money and sent volunteers on every vaccination day.

Health workers trained during the campaign later helped with measles vaccination and other programmes. The fight is not quite over. Polio is still found in Pakistan and Afghanistan, so Indian children still get their drops, and travellers arriving from those countries must show proof of polio vaccination.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Polio has been wiped out everywhere in the world.

Truth: Wild polio is gone from almost every country, but as of the mid-2020s it still spread in parts of Pakistan and Afghanistan. Until it is gone everywhere, children everywhere still need to be vaccinated.
