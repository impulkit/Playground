What would a day on the Moon be like?

One day on the Moon lasts about 29 and a half Earth days, so you would get two weeks of sunshine followed by two weeks of freezing night.

The short answer

A day on the Moon is very long, very bright and very quiet. The Moon turns slowly, so the Sun takes about two Earth weeks to cross its sky. Then comes two weeks of night.

There is no air, so the sky stays black even at noon. Stars would be faint in the glare, but the Sun would blaze white. In sunlight the ground heats to about 120°C, hotter than boiling water. At night it can fall to about −130°C. You would weigh only one-sixth of what you weigh at home, and you could not hear a single sound outside your helmet.

Surprising but true

From the side of the Moon that faces us, Earth never rises or sets. It hangs in almost the same spot in the black sky all month, slowly changing shape like the Moon does for us.

Sunrise takes all morning

On Earth, the Sun rises in a couple of minutes. On the Moon, it creeps up so slowly that sunrise takes about an hour. There is no colourful dawn, because colours in the sky come from air scattering light. The Sun just appears over the grey horizon like someone switched on a stadium floodlight.

Shadows are inky black and sharp. With no air to spread light around, a rock's shadow can be dark enough to hide your own boots.

Earth in the sky

From the near side, Earth hangs in nearly the same place all month. It looks about four times wider than the full Moon does from Earth. You would see it go through phases: full Earth, half Earth, thin crescent Earth.

Sometimes Earth passes in front of the Sun. Then the whole Earth glows with a thin red ring around its edge. That ring is every sunrise and sunset on Earth happening at once.

Why does the Moon change shape?It doesn't really change. We just see it from different angles.

Bouncing, not walking

The Moon's weak gravity changes everything. Apollo astronauts found walking awkward and often hopped like kangaroos instead. Dropped tools fell slowly. In 1971, Alan Shepard hit two golf balls on the Moon. His one-handed swing was poor, but the balls still sailed far.

A cricket ball thrown by a fast bowler on the Moon would fly much farther than on Earth. But you could not breathe, so every jump would happen inside a spacesuit that, with its backpack, weighed about 90 kg on Earth.

Dust, heat and cold

Moon dust is not soft like beach sand. Without wind or water to wear it smooth, each grain stays jagged like crushed glass. It stuck to Apollo suits, scratched visors and made astronauts sneeze when they took off their helmets.

A Moon base would need thick walls or a blanket of soil on top to block heat, cold and radiation from the Sun. Some craters near the poles never see sunlight at all. They are colder than −240°C and hold frozen water that future explorers might drink or turn into rocket fuel.

India's Moon day

On 23 August 2023, India's Chandrayaan-3 lander, Vikram, touched down near the Moon's south pole. It was timed to land just after local sunrise, so it would get a full lunar daytime of solar power. Its rover, Pragyan, rolled out and found sulfur in the soil.

After about 14 Earth days, sunset came and the long cold night began. Both were put to sleep, and they never woke up again.

Who goes next

In April 2026, NASA's Artemis II mission flew four astronauts around the Moon, the first crew to travel that far since 1972. NASA plans to land people near the south pole in the coming years. Countries including China and India have their own plans to send astronauts too.

The first people to spend a full Moon day there will have to survive two weeks of night. Planners are already looking past that, to an even harder target: building homes on Mars.

Myth or fact?

Myth: One side of the Moon is always dark, the "dark side of the Moon".

Truth: There is a far side that we never see from Earth, but it gets just as much sunlight as the near side. When we see a new Moon, the far side is actually in full daylight.

Try this

Measure a Moon jump. Jump as high as you can next to a wall and ask a friend to mark the height with a sticky note. Multiply that height by six. That is roughly how high the same jump would take you on the Moon, if your spacesuit let you bend your knees.
