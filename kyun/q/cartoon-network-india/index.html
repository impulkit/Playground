What cartoons did Indian kids grow up with?

The Jungle Book cartoon Indian children loved in the 1990s was made in Japan, dubbed in Hindi, with a theme song by Gulzar.

The short answer

Indian children of the 1980s and early 1990s mostly watched cartoons on Doordarshan, the government TV channel, often on Sunday mornings. Favourites included a Japanese-made Jungle Book and Disney's DuckTales and TaleSpin, all dubbed in Hindi.

From the mid-1990s, cable TV brought channels like Cartoon Network, first in English and later in Hindi and other Indian languages. Children grew up with Tom and Jerry, Scooby-Doo and Swat Kats, and then with Japanese shows like Doraemon, Shin-chan and Pokémon. In 2008, an Indian-made cartoon, Chhota Bheem, became a hit of its own.

Surprising but true

The famous Hindi theme song "Jungle Jungle Baat Chali Hai" was written by Gulzar for a Japanese anime version of Kipling's Jungle Book.

Sunday mornings on Doordarshan

When India had only one or two TV channels, a weekly cartoon was an event. In the early 1990s, Doordarshan showed The Jungle Book, an anime series made in Japan and based on Rudyard Kipling's stories set in Indian forests.

Its Hindi theme song, "Jungle Jungle Baat Chali Hai", had words by the poet Gulzar and music by Vishal Bhardwaj. Many adults can still sing it. Disney's DuckTales, with Scrooge McDuck and his nephews, and TaleSpin, with Baloo as a pilot, were also dubbed into Hindi with catchy theme songs.

Cable TV changes everything

In the 1990s, cable and satellite TV spread across Indian cities. Cartoon Network arrived in the mid-1990s, at first sharing time with a film channel and showing cartoons mostly in English.

Classic American cartoons like Tom and Jerry, The Flintstones and Scooby-Doo became daily viewing. So did newer shows like Dexter's Laboratory, Johnny Bravo and The Powerpuff Girls. Swat Kats, about two cat pilots fighting villains, was a huge favourite with Indian children, and many still ask for it to return.

Speaking our language

The big change came with dubbing. Channels began showing cartoons in Hindi, and later in Tamil, Telugu and other languages. Children who did not speak much English could now follow every joke.

Dubbing artists did more than translate. They rewrote jokes, added Indian expressions and gave characters a desi flavour. Shin-chan's Hindi voice became famous for its cheeky lines. The best dubs felt as if the cartoons had been made in India all along.

The Japanese wave

In the 2000s, more channels arrived, including Pogo and Hungama. They brought many Japanese cartoons, or anime. Doraemon, a blue robot cat from the future with a pocket full of gadgets, became one of the most-watched children's shows in India.

Shin-chan, a naughty five-year-old, was also hugely popular. Some parents complained about his rude behaviour, and in 2008 the show was taken off air for a while, then returned with edits. Pokémon, Beyblade and Dragon Ball also gathered big fan bases.

Cartoons made in India

For years, almost all cartoons on Indian TV came from abroad. Indian animation studios mostly did outsourced work for foreign companies. That began to change in 2008, when Chhota Bheem, made by Green Gold Animation in Hyderabad, started on Pogo.

Its village setting, Indian festivals and laddoo-loving hero made it a giant hit. Other Indian shows followed, such as Motu Patlu, based on characters from the comic magazine Lotpot.

Today children watch on phones and streaming apps as much as on TV, and can pick from thousands of shows at any hour. But many adults still remember rushing home to catch one show at one fixed time, with the whole family crowded around one screen.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Dubbing a cartoon just means translating the words exactly.

Truth: Dubbing writers often change jokes, names and references so they make sense to local viewers, and they must fit the lines to the characters' mouth movements. A good dub is closer to rewriting than to word-for-word translation.

Try this

Watch one minute of a cartoon with the sound off. Then write your own lines for the characters in Hindi, English or your home language, and perform them with a friend while the clip plays. Notice how hard it is to match the lips.
