How did flight go from Kitty Hawk to the Moon in 66 years?

The first aeroplane flight in 1903 was shorter than a cricket ground. Less than 66 years later, people walked on the Moon.

The short answer

On 17 December 1903, Orville Wright flew a wood-and-cloth aeroplane for 12 seconds near Kitty Hawk, North Carolina. It travelled about 37 m, less than the distance from the middle of a cricket ground to its boundary. On 20 July 1969, Neil Armstrong stepped onto the Moon.

The gap between those days was less than 66 years. One person could have watched both, and some did. The leap happened because many problems were solved in quick succession: better engines, metal planes, jets and finally rockets. Two world wars and a race between rival countries poured huge amounts of money and effort into each step.

Surprising but true

When Neil Armstrong landed on the Moon in 1969, he carried small pieces of cloth and wood from the Wright brothers' 1903 aeroplane.

Wood, cloth and a bicycle shop

The Wright brothers, Wilbur and Orville, built bicycles in Dayton, Ohio. Their real breakthrough was control. They learned to twist the wings slightly to tilt and steer, much as a bird changes the shape of its wings. They also built their own lightweight engine, because no company could supply one.

That first flight was so short that it would fit inside the wingspan of a Boeing 747. The brothers made three more flights the same day. The longest, with Wilbur at the controls, lasted 59 seconds and covered about 260 m.

Within ten years, planes were crossing seas. Louis Blériot flew across the English Channel in 1909. In 1919, John Alcock and Arthur Brown flew nonstop across the Atlantic. In 1927, Charles Lindbergh did it alone, staying awake for more than 33 hours.

Faster, higher, metal

The First World War turned planes from wooden toys into fighting machines. Afterwards, airlines began carrying mail and passengers. In 1932 J.R.D. Tata flew India's first airmail service, from Karachi to Bombay, in a small single-engine plane.

Planes got bigger and were built of aluminium instead of cloth. But propellers have a speed limit, and engineers wanted more. In 1939 a German plane, the Heinkel He 178, made the first flight powered by a jet engine. In 1947 the American pilot Chuck Yeager flew faster than sound in the rocket-powered Bell X-1.

How does a rocket work?A rocket doesn't push against the air. It throws hot gas out of the back so hard that the gas pushes it forward.

The rocket men

While aeroplanes improved, a few people dreamed of leaving the air altogether. Wings need air to push against, and space has none. Only rockets work there, because they push against their own exhaust.

In 1926, an American physics teacher named Robert Goddard launched the first rocket powered by liquid fuel. It flew for about 2.5 seconds and landed in a cabbage field. Newspapers mocked him, claiming a rocket could not work in empty space. He kept going anyway. During the Second World War, Germany built large military rockets, and after the war both the USA and the Soviet Union hired its rocket engineers.

The race to the Moon

In 1957 the Soviet Union launched Sputnik, the first satellite. In 1961 it sent Yuri Gagarin into orbit. The USA felt it was losing the space race, and President John F. Kennedy promised to land Americans on the Moon before 1970.

The rocket built for that job, the Saturn V, stood about 110 m tall, roughly as high as a 36-storey building. Its first stage burned fuel at a rate of about 13 tonnes every second. The flight computer on the Apollo spacecraft had less memory than a modern calculator.

Orville Wright lived until 1948, long enough to see jet planes and a pilot break the speed of sound. On the Moon in 1969, Armstrong carried a scrap of cloth from the Flyer's wing and a sliver of its propeller. Today that first Flyer hangs in the Smithsonian in Washington, D.C., not far from the Apollo 11 capsule that brought the astronauts home.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Wright brothers were the first people ever to fly.

Truth: People had flown in hot-air balloons since 1783 and in gliders before 1900. The Wrights made the first controlled, sustained flight in a powered machine heavier than air. That is what made modern aeroplanes possible.
