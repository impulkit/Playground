What is a reflex?

When you touch something hot, your hand jerks away before your brain has even worked out that it hurts.

The short answer

A reflex is an action your body does on its own, without you deciding to. When you blink at a speck of dust, sneeze at pepper or yank your hand off a hot pan, a reflex is at work.

Many reflexes take a shortcut. A nerve in your skin senses danger and sends a signal to your spinal cord, the thick cable of nerves running down your back. Instead of waiting for the brain, the spinal cord sends a message straight back out to your muscles. This loop is called a reflex arc. Because the signal travels a shorter path, the reaction is very fast. Your brain finds out a moment later.

Surprising but true

Newborn babies grip a finger so tightly with this reflex that some can briefly hold up their own weight. The reflex fades within a few months.

The tap on the knee

Doctors often test reflexes by tapping just below the knee with a small rubber hammer. The tap stretches a tendon, which tells the thigh muscle it's being pulled. The spinal cord answers by tightening the muscle, and your lower leg kicks forward.

This knee-jerk reflex normally helps you keep your balance. If your knee starts to buckle while you're standing, the same loop tightens the muscle and keeps you upright. If the kick is much too weak or much too strong, it can tell a doctor something about how the nerves are working.

Built-in protection

Many reflexes protect you. You blink when something flies at your face. You cough when food goes down the wrong way. You gag when something touches the back of your throat. You sneeze to clear your nose. In bright light, your pupils shrink to protect your eyes, and in the dark they open wide.

If you step on something sharp, you don't just lift that foot. Your other leg stiffens at the same time to hold you up. That's a reflex too, and it works without you thinking about which leg should do what. Your body runs hundreds of quieter reflexes all day too, adjusting your heartbeat, your breathing and the size of your blood vessels, without you ever noticing.

Baby reflexes

Newborn babies have some reflexes that older children don't. Stroke a newborn's palm, and their fingers curl tightly around yours. Touch their cheek, and they turn their head and open their mouth, searching for milk. A sudden noise makes them fling their arms out and then pull them in, which is the startle reflex.

These fade over the first months as the brain takes more control. Doctors check them at a baby's health check-ups because they show the nervous system is developing well. The grasp reflex may be left over from our primate relatives, whose babies cling to their mother's fur.

Reflex or reaction?

Some fast moves aren't reflexes at all. A cricket ball bowled at 150 km/h travels about 20 metres from the bowler's hand to the batter. Using speed and distance, that's under half a second. A batter has to see it, judge its line and swing, and decide on a shot, then move.

That's a reaction, not a reflex, because the brain has to choose. Most people take about a fifth of a second just to react to something they see. Top batters and baseball hitters manage because years of practice let them read the bowler's arm and grip early and guess where the ball will go. A simple reflex, like the knee jerk, can be several times faster than that.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Great batters and goalkeepers react so fast because they have super-fast reflexes.

Truth: Their simple reflexes are much like everyone else's. What experts have is years of practice that lets them read early clues, such as a bowler's wrist or a striker's hips, and start moving sooner.

Try this

Sit on a high table or chair so your legs dangle freely. Ask a friend to tap gently just below your kneecap with the side of their hand. If they find the right spot, your lower leg will kick out on its own. Then try to stop it from kicking, and notice how hard that is.
