Where did mermaid stories come from?

In January 1493, Christopher Columbus wrote that he saw three mermaids rise from the sea, and that they were "not as pretty as they are painted".

The short answer

Mermaid stories come from many places at once. People who live near the sea have long told tales of beings who are half human and half fish. Some of the oldest ideas come from ancient goddesses, like Atargatis in Syria, who was sometimes shown with a fish's tail.

Sailors added sightings of real animals. Gentle sea mammals like manatees and dugongs may have looked oddly human from far off, especially to tired sailors at sea for months. Writers such as Hans Christian Andersen turned these tales into stories still read today. Mermaids aren't real, but the reasons people believed in them are.

Surprising but true

The dugong, a gentle sea animal found off Tamil Nadu and the Andaman Islands, belongs to a group of animals named Sirenia, after the sirens of Greek myth.

Goddesses with tails

In ancient Syria, people worshipped a goddess named Atargatis. Some stories and carvings show her with a fish's tail. She was linked with water and fertility, and fish were sacred at her temples.

Many cultures have water spirits. India has Matsya, the fish form of Vishnu in Hindu stories, who saves the first man from a great flood. In the Thai and Cambodian versions of the Ramayana, a mermaid princess named Suvannamaccha falls in love with Hanuman. In West and Central Africa, people tell of Mami Wata, a powerful water spirit often shown as a mermaid. In Scotland and Ireland, old stories tell of selkies, seals that could shed their skins and walk on land as people.

Sirens with wings

In Greek myths, sirens sang so sweetly that sailors steered onto rocks. In Homer's Odyssey, the hero Odysseus plugs his crew's ears with wax and has himself tied to the mast so he can hear the song safely.

But the oldest Greek sirens weren't fish. Vase paintings show them as birds with women's heads. In medieval Europe, writers mixed sirens up with sea creatures, and they gained tails. In French, Spanish and Italian, the word for mermaid still comes from "siren".

Sailors and sea cows

On 9 January 1493, near today's Dominican Republic, Christopher Columbus wrote that he had seen three mermaids. He added that they were not as beautiful as in paintings though their faces looked a little human. Experts think he saw manatees.

Manatees and dugongs are slow, plant-eating sea mammals sometimes called sea cows. They surface to breathe, can hold their young close and have rounded heads. Seen at a distance, half-hidden in waves, they could seem human. Scientists named their group Sirenia, after the sirens. In India, a few hundred dugongs survive, mainly off Tamil Nadu, Gujarat and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands.

Fakes and a famous fairy tale

In 1842, the American showman P. T. Barnum displayed the "Fiji mermaid". Crowds paid to see it. It was really the top half of a monkey sewn onto a fish's tail.

In 1837, the Danish writer Hans Christian Andersen published The Little Mermaid, a sad fairy tale about a mermaid who gives up her voice to live on land. In 1913, a bronze statue of her was placed on a rock in Copenhagen harbour. Disney's cheerful film version came out in 1989.

Mermaids today

In 2012, a television channel aired a fake documentary claiming scientists had found mermaids. So many people asked questions that NOAA, the US government's ocean agency, put out a statement saying no evidence of mermaids has ever been found.

Mermaids still appear in films, books and dress-up boxes. At Weeki Wachee Springs in Florida, performers in mermaid tails have put on underwater shows since 1947, breathing through hidden air hoses between moves. And the strangest part? A one-horned beast went through the same journey, from travellers' tales of real animals to a fantasy creature, as the story of unicorns shows.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Scientists have found mermaid remains, but they keep it secret.

Truth: There is no evidence that mermaids ever existed. In 2012, after a television programme presented a fake mermaid documentary, the US ocean agency NOAA stated plainly that no such creatures have ever been found.
