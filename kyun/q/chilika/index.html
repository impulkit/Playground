What is Chilika Lake?

Chilika in Odisha is a giant lagoon where river water and sea water mix, and every winter it fills with more than ten lakh birds.

The short answer

Chilika is a huge shallow lagoon on the coast of Odisha. It is separated from the Bay of Bengal by a long, thin strip of sand, but a narrow mouth lets the sea flow in and out. Rivers pour fresh water in from the land side. The result is brackish water, partly fresh and partly salty.

It is the largest coastal lagoon in India, covering about 900 square kilometres in the dry season and more than 1,100 in the monsoon. Its waters hold dolphins, fish, crabs and prawns, and in winter it becomes one of the biggest bird gatherings in Asia.

Surprising but true

In 2000, engineers cut a new opening between Chilika and the sea, and the lake's fish catch and health improved so much that it became the first Asian wetland taken off a global danger list.

A meeting of waters

Because Chilika mixes river and sea, its water is not the same everywhere. Near the sea mouth it is quite salty. In the north, where rivers like the Daya flow in, it is almost fresh, especially after the monsoon.

That variety makes room for a lot of life. Scientists have recorded hundreds of kinds of fish, prawns and crabs in the lagoon. Many of them grow up in Chilika and swim out to sea as adults. The shallow water, rarely more than a few metres deep, warms quickly in the sun and grows plenty of tiny plants and animals for young fish to eat. Seagrass meadows on the lake floor give them places to hide. Around 2 lakh people in fishing villages around the shore depend on the lake for their living, some using methods passed down for generations, like the families described in India's fishing communities.

Ten lakh birds

Every winter, birds fly to Chilika from as far away as Siberia, Central Asia and the Himalayas, escaping the cold. Recent counts have often recorded more than 10 lakh birds in a single season, including ducks, geese, waders and flamingos.

The best place to see them is Nalabana, an island in the middle of the lake that becomes a bird sanctuary each winter. In the village of Mangalajodi, on the northern shore, some men who once hunted birds now work as guides and protect them instead, taking visitors through the reeds in small boats.

Dolphins in the lagoon

Chilika is home to Irrawaddy dolphins, grey, round-headed dolphins with no beak that live in coasts, lagoons and rivers in South and Southeast Asia. Their population worldwide is small, and Chilika holds one of the most important groups. In the 2025 census, 159 were counted in the lake.

Boats from Satapada, near the sea mouth, take tourists to watch them. Too many noisy motorboats can disturb the dolphins, so local rules try to limit how close boats go. Fishing nets are another danger, because a dolphin caught underwater can drown.

Saving Chilika

In the 1980s and 1990s, Chilika began to choke. Mud from deforested hills upstream filled the lake, and the sea mouth kept silting up. Less sea water came in, weeds spread, and fish catches fell sharply. In 1993 the lagoon was put on the Montreux Record, a list of wetlands in danger kept under the Ramsar Convention.

In 2000, the Chilika Development Authority dug a new mouth to the sea. Salt water flowed back in, weeds shrank and fish, prawns and dolphins recovered. In 2002, Chilika became the first Asian site removed from the Montreux Record.

Temples and islands

Chilika has several islands. On one stands the Kalijai temple, which pilgrims reach by boat, especially during the festival of Makar Sankranti. Another, Honeymoon Island, is popular with visitors for its clear water. In the early morning, fishers' boats spread across the lake, and the line between water and sky can be hard to see.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Chilika is a freshwater lake like any other.

Truth: Chilika is a lagoon. Rivers bring in fresh water from the land and tides bring in salt water from the sea, so its water is brackish, and it is saltier near the sea mouth than in the north.

Try this

Fill two glasses with water. Stir a teaspoon of salt into one. Gently drop a fresh egg into each with a spoon. The egg sinks in fresh water but may float or hover in the salty one. Chilika's water changes saltiness from place to place, which changes which creatures can live where.
