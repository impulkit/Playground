Why do colds make your nose run?

A runny nose isn't the cold virus making a mess. It's your body building a river to wash the virus away.

The short answer

A cold starts when a virus lands in your nose or throat and gets inside the cells that line it. Your body notices and reacts quickly. Blood vessels in the lining of your nose widen and leak fluid. Glands start making much more mucus than usual.

The result is a runny nose. All that liquid is a flood meant to trap the viruses and wash them out. Some goes down your throat, where stomach acid destroys the viruses. The rest ends up in a tissue. The stuffy feeling comes from the swollen lining, not from mucus alone. Most of what makes you feel rotten in a cold is your own body fighting back.

Surprising but true

More than 200 different viruses can cause a cold, which is why you can catch one after another.

Snot on duty

Your nose makes mucus every day, even when you are well. It's sticky so it can catch dust, pollen and germs before they reach your lungs. Tiny hairs sweep it backwards, and you swallow it without noticing.

During a cold, the mucus gets thicker and changes colour. Yellow or green snot comes from white blood cells that have rushed to fight. They contain an enzyme with a greenish colour. So green snot means the battle is well under way, not that something worse has started. Your sneezes help too, blasting out mucus and viruses in a burst.

One cold after another

The most common cold viruses are rhinoviruses. There are more than 160 kinds of rhinovirus alone, plus many other cold viruses. When your body beats one, it remembers that exact virus, but the next cold is often a different one. That's why there is no single cold vaccine.

Young children catch more colds than adults, sometimes six or more a year, because they haven't met as many viruses yet. Each cold teaches their immune system a little more.

Flu is a heavier hitter

Flu, short for influenza, is caused by a different family of viruses. It often comes on suddenly, with a high fever, aches all over and deep tiredness that can last a week or more. For most healthy children it passes, but it can be serious for babies, older people and people with other illnesses.

In 1918, near the end of the First World War, a flu pandemic swept around the world. India was hit very hard, with millions of deaths. Flu viruses change their outer coats a little every year. That is why doctors recommend a new flu vaccine each year, especially for people most at risk.

What helps, and what doesn't

There is no cure for a cold. Your body clears it, usually within a week to ten days. Rest, warm drinks and plenty of water can make you feel better. A spoon of honey in warm water can soothe a cough in children over one year old, though babies under one should never be given honey. Antibiotics don't help, because they kill bacteria, and colds are caused by viruses. Taking them when they're not needed helps germs learn to resist them.

The best way to avoid colds is to stop the virus reaching your nose. Cold viruses spread through droplets when people cough and sneeze, and on hands and surfaces. Washing your hands with soap, coughing into your elbow and not rubbing your eyes and nose all make a difference.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Going out in the cold or with wet hair gives you a cold.

Truth: Colds are caused by viruses, so you can only catch one from a person who has the virus. Colds are more common in winter partly because people crowd together indoors, and cold air may make the nose's defences a little weaker.

Try this

Rub a little cooking oil and a pinch of glitter on your hands. Then wash them with water only for 20 seconds and see how much is left. Try again with soap. The glitter is like germs, and soap is much better at shifting them.
