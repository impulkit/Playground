How do your eyes see colour?

Your eyes only have three kinds of colour detectors. Yet you can see millions of colours.

The short answer

Light enters your eye and lands on the retina at the back, where millions of light-sensing cells live.

Three kinds of cone cells respond to different colours of light. One kind reacts most to reddish light, one to green and one to blue. Your brain compares how strongly each kind is firing. A ripe mango might set off the red and green cones strongly and the blue ones hardly at all, and your brain reads that mix as orange-yellow.

That's also why screens can fool you with just red, green and blue dots.

Surprising but true

Deer have only two kinds of cones, so a tiger's orange looks greenish to them.

Three colours make millions

Because the brain compares three signals, it can tell apart a huge number of shades, perhaps a million or more. The idea that colour vision uses just three sensors was suggested by the English scientist Thomas Young around 1802, long before anyone could see a cone cell.

A phone or TV uses the same trick in reverse. Every pixel is made of tiny red, green and blue lights. Turn them up and down in different amounts, and your cones can't tell the mix from the real thing. Adding coloured light works differently from mixing paints, as you can see in mixing colours.

When cones work differently

In 1794, the English chemist John Dalton described his own odd colour vision to a society of scientists in Manchester. His paper was printed in 1798. Things others called red looked to him much like green. He had what we now call colour blindness, and for a while it was even called "Daltonism".

About 1 in 12 men of European ancestry, and fewer in many other groups, have red-green colour blindness. It's much rarer in women, because the genes for red and green cones sit on the X chromosome. Women have two X chromosomes, so a working copy on one usually makes up for the other.

Why a tiger is orange

Many animals see fewer colours than we do. Deer, like most mammals, have only two kinds of cones. To them, a tiger's orange coat blends into green leaves. That may be why tigers can be bright orange and still sneak up on their prey.

Bulls are similar. A matador's red cape doesn't make a bull angry because it's red. The bull charges at the movement. Dogs, too, see a world mostly of blues and yellows, which is why a red ball on green grass can be hard for them to spot.

Animals that see more

Many birds, fish and insects have four kinds of cones, including one that senses ultraviolet light, which we can't see at all. Bees spot ultraviolet patterns on flowers that guide them to the nectar, like landing lights on a runway.

The mantis shrimp has 12 or more kinds of colour detectors. Yet tests show it's worse than people at telling close colours apart. It seems to recognise colours quickly rather than compare them carefully, the way our brains do. Some octopuses are even stranger: they change colour brilliantly but seem to have only one kind of light sensor.

Colour in the dark

At night, your cones give up and your rods take over. Rods are very sensitive but can't tell colours apart. That's why a red rose and a green leaf both look grey by moonlight, and why it takes your eyes several minutes to adjust when you walk into a dark room. Myth or fact?

Myth: Colour-blind people see the world only in black and white.

Truth: Almost all colour-blind people see colours. Most just mix up certain ones, usually reds and greens, which can look similar. Seeing no colour at all is very rare.

Try this

Draw a bright red square on white paper and stare at its centre for 30 seconds without moving your eyes. Then look quickly at a plain white wall or sheet. You'll see a ghostly blue-green square. The cones that sense red got tired, so for a moment the others win.
