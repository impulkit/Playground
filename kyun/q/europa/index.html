Could Europa have an ocean?

Under a shell of ice on one of Jupiter's moons, there may be more liquid water than in all of Earth's oceans put together.

The short answer

Most scientists think yes. Europa, one of Jupiter's four largest moons, is covered in a shell of ice. Beneath it, there is very probably a deep ocean of salty liquid water. Estimates suggest it could hold about twice as much water as all of Earth's oceans.

Nobody has seen this ocean, but several clues point to it. Europa's surface is smooth, young and criss-crossed with cracks, as if the ice floats and shifts. Its magnetic signals behave as if a salty liquid lies underneath. And Jupiter's gravity squeezes Europa enough to keep water warm inside.

That makes Europa one of the best places in the solar system to search for life beyond Earth.

Surprising but true

Europa is a little smaller than our own Moon, yet its hidden ocean may hold about twice as much water as all of Earth's oceans.

Galileo's moons

In January 1610, Galileo pointed his small telescope at Jupiter and saw four little points of light that moved around it night after night. They were moons, the first ever found circling another planet. Europa is one of them, alongside Io, Ganymede and Callisto.

Europa is slightly smaller than our own Moon. Its surface is mostly water ice, brighter than fresh snow in places, and streaked with reddish-brown lines. Some of those streaks may contain salts that have come up from below.

Clues from a spacecraft

In the 1990s, NASA's Galileo spacecraft, named after the astronomer, flew close to Europa many times. It found that the ice is marked by long ridges and patches called chaos terrain. There, blocks of ice seem to have broken, drifted and frozen again, like sea ice breaking up in the Arctic.

The surface has very few craters. That means it is young, perhaps tens of millions of years old, so something keeps renewing it. Galileo also measured how Europa responds to Jupiter's powerful magnetic field. The results fit best with a layer of salty, electrically conducting water beneath the ice.

Kept warm by squeezing

Europa is about five times further from the Sun than Earth is. Its surface is around −160 °C at the equator and colder near the poles. So why isn't its ocean frozen?

The answer is tidal heating. Europa's orbit is slightly stretched, so its distance from Jupiter keeps changing. The giant planet's gravity pulls harder when Europa is close and less when it is far, flexing the moon over and over. On Earth, the Moon's pull raises tides in the sea. On Europa, Jupiter's pull bends the whole moon, and that bending makes heat.

Could anything live there?

Life on Earth needs liquid water, the right chemicals and a source of energy. Europa may have all three. Its ocean probably sits on a rocky sea floor. On Earth, hot springs on the ocean floor, called deep-sea vents, support whole communities of animals in total darkness. Something similar might happen on Europa.

That doesn't mean life is there, only that it is worth checking. In October 2024, NASA launched Europa Clipper, the largest spacecraft NASA has ever built for a planetary mission. It should reach Jupiter in 2030 and fly past Europa dozens of times, measuring the ice, the ocean and the chemistry. The European Space Agency's JUICE spacecraft, launched in 2023, will also fly past Europa on its way to study Ganymede.

Neither spacecraft will land. But if they find the right ingredients, a future lander might one day melt or drill its way down. Scientists already practise by drilling through the ice of Antarctica to reach the hidden lakes beneath.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Scientists have found life on Europa.

Truth: No life has been found on Europa, or anywhere beyond Earth. Europa is a top place to look because it seems to have water, chemicals and energy, but missions so far can only study it from a distance.

Try this

Squeeze and release a rubber ball or a ball of play dough quickly in your hand for a minute, then hold it to your cheek. It feels a little warmer. Jupiter squeezes Europa in a similar way, over and over, and that flexing helps keep its ocean liquid.
