How is paneer made?

You can make paneer with just two things from the kitchen, milk and a lemon, and watch it happen in a few minutes.

The short answer

To make paneer, you bring milk to the boil and squeeze in lemon juice or vinegar. Almost at once, the milk splits into soft white lumps, called curds, floating in a thin greenish-yellow liquid, called whey.

The curds are poured into a muslin cloth, rinsed and squeezed. Then a heavy weight, such as a pot of water, is placed on top for an hour or so to press them into a firm block. That block is paneer. It needs no ageing, no special mould and no rennet, which is why it can be made fresh at home on the same day.

Surprising but true

Many cheeses are set with rennet, a substance traditionally taken from a calf's stomach, so not every cheese is vegetarian. Paneer never uses it.

Why the milk splits

Milk is full of a protein called casein. Normally casein floats around in tiny clusters that repel one another, a little like magnets held the wrong way.

Acid changes that. It cancels out the charges that keep the clusters apart, so they crash into one another and clump. Heat speeds it up. The fat in the milk gets trapped inside the clumps. The water, along with lactose and other proteins, drains away as whey. Many cooks use the whey to knead roti dough or cook dal, so nothing is wasted.

Paneer and chhena

In Bengal, the same curds are kept soft and moist and called chhena. Kneaded smooth by hand, chhena becomes rasgulla, sandesh and other famous sweets of Kolkata and Odisha.

For paneer, the curds are pressed hard so they can be cut into cubes and cooked in curries like palak paneer or grilled as paneer tikka. Because paneer does not melt, it holds its shape in a hot pan, which makes it ideal for Indian cooking.

Where paneer came from

Old Indian texts mention many milk foods, such as curd, butter, ghee and khoa. Clear mentions of cheese made by splitting milk with acid are rarer. Some ancient writers seem to have thought deliberately splitting milk was wrong.

The food historian K.T. Achaya suggested that Portuguese settlers in Bengal in the 1600s may have taught local cooks to split milk with acid, leading to chhena. The word paneer comes from Persian, where panir simply means cheese, and some historians link paneer to cooks from Persia and Afghanistan. Both ideas could be partly true, and experts still disagree.

How most cheese is made

Paneer is one of the simplest cheeses. Most of the world's other cheeses, like cheddar or mozzarella, are set with rennet, which contains an enzyme that snips casein so it forms a gel. Helpful bacteria are added for flavour. Many cheeses are then salted, shaped and left to age for weeks or years.

People have been making cheese for a very long time. Scientists found traces of milk fat in pottery sieves in Poland that were used about 7,000 years ago, probably for cheesemaking. In 2018, researchers reported finding a lump of cheese more than 3,000 years old in a tomb in Egypt, as you can read in ancient Egypt.

Cheese in America

The USA makes more cheese than any other country. Wisconsin is famous for cheddar, and its football fans wear foam "cheesehead" hats. Indian grocery shops across the country now sell blocks of paneer, and some American dairies make it too.

Paneer is also high in protein, which matters in a country where many people are vegetarian. A plate of paneer and peas is one of the easiest ways to make dinner more filling without meat.

Myth or fact?

Myth: All cheese is vegetarian, because it is made from milk.

Truth: Many cheeses are set with rennet, which was traditionally taken from the stomachs of young calves. Today many use rennet made by microbes instead. Vegetarians often check labels, while paneer, set with acid, has no rennet at all.

Try this

Pour a glass of room-temperature milk into a bowl and stir in two spoons of lemon juice. Wait five minutes and watch small white clumps appear. Pour it through a clean cloth or tea strainer. The clumps are a tiny batch of paneer and the yellowish liquid is whey.
