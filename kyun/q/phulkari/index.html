What is Phulkari embroidery?

In old Punjab, a grandmother might start stitching a shawl the day a baby girl was born, to be finished in time for her wedding.

The short answer

Phulkari, meaning "flower work", is a kind of embroidery from Punjab. Women traditionally stitched it onto shawls and head-coverings called odhnis, using shiny silk thread on coarse, hand-spun cotton.

The patterns are mostly geometric: diamonds, triangles, stripes and stars. Some phulkaris have flowers scattered across the cloth. In the most splendid kind, called a bagh, which means garden, the stitches cover the cloth so completely that you can hardly see the fabric underneath. Phulkari belongs to the whole Punjab region, which today is split between India and Pakistan.

Surprising but true

Phulkari is stitched from the back of the cloth. The embroiderer works on the wrong side, counting threads, and the glowing pattern appears on the front.

Stitching from behind

Phulkari uses a simple stitch called a darn stitch: long straight stitches on top, with tiny picks of the needle underneath. The embroiderer works from the back of the cloth. She counts the threads of the cotton to place each stitch, usually without drawing anything first.

The silk thread, called pat, was soft and untwisted, so it spread out and caught the light. By changing the direction of the stitches, a woman could make one shape shine and the next look darker, even with the same colour. This play of light gives a bagh its shimmer, like sunlight on a field of mustard.

The pattern grows outward from the middle. Often the embroiderer starts in the centre of the cloth and works toward the edges, repeating the same shapes again and again. Many baghs have near-perfect symmetry. Some include one small motif in a different colour, which is often said to ward off the evil eye.

Cloth for a lifetime

Phulkari was part of family life. Girls learned from mothers, aunts and grandmothers, often stitching together in the afternoons. A family might begin a bagh soon after a daughter was born, adding to it over the years so it was ready for her wedding.

Different pieces had different jobs. A chope, embroidered along the edges, was traditionally given by the maternal grandmother at a wedding. Other phulkaris were worn every day or kept for festivals. The cotton cloth, khaddar, was usually dyed deep red, rusty brown or indigo, and often spun and woven at home, just like the khadi that Gandhi later made famous.

Split by a border

In 1947, Partition divided Punjab between India and Pakistan. Millions of families had to leave their homes. Some carried phulkaris with them as one of the few things they could save, and these pieces became precious links to lost villages.

The upheaval also broke the old chain of learning. With families scattered and machine-made cloth everywhere, fewer women made a bagh by hand. Many old pieces are now in museums such as the Victoria and Albert Museum in London. Collectors and families still treasure the ones that survived, and some have been passed down through four or five generations.

Phulkari today

Phulkari never vanished. Today women in Patiala, Amritsar and other towns, as well as across the border in Pakistani Punjab, still embroider it. Most modern pieces use cheaper threads and lighter cloth, and many patterns are printed onto the fabric first to speed things up.

In 2011, phulkari from parts of Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan received a GI tag, a label that protects crafts linked to a particular region. You will now see phulkari on dupattas, jackets, bags and shoes, and on bright costumes in bhangra performances, where it flashes as the dancers spin.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Phulkari is always bright yellow and orange on red.

Truth: Golden yellow on deep red is the best-known look, but old phulkaris also use white, green, pink, blue and more. Some styles, like sainchi, show whole scenes of village life, animals and people.
