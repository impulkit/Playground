What is mindfulness, and does it work?

It sounds too simple to matter, just noticing your breath, but it grew from 2,500-year-old Buddhist practice into a lesson in hospitals and schools.

The short answer

Mindfulness means paying attention to what is happening right now, on purpose, without judging it as good or bad. You might notice your breath going in and out, the feel of your feet on the floor or the sounds in a room.

Most of the time our minds jump around. We replay yesterday or worry about tomorrow. Mindfulness is practice in noticing where your attention has gone and bringing it back. Many adults find it helps them feel calmer and less stressed. You don't need to sit cross-legged or close your eyes. You can be mindful while eating a mango or waiting for a bus.

Does it work? For adults, studies suggest it helps a little, about as much as some other kinds of support. For children in classrooms, the evidence is less clear.

Surprising but true

When scientists tested mindfulness lessons in about 85 UK secondary schools, the pupils' mental health ended up no better on average than in schools that didn't use them.

An old idea from India

Mindfulness comes from Buddhist meditation, which began in India about 2,500 years ago. The Buddha taught a practice the old Pali language calls sati, often translated as mindfulness or awareness. Monks watched their breath, their bodies and their thoughts as a path to understanding the mind.

One tradition, Vipassana, became widely known in modern India through the teacher S. N. Goenka. From the 1970s, his silent ten-day courses spread from a centre at Igatpuri in Maharashtra to many countries. Yoga and meditation in Hindu traditions share some of the same ideas, such as close attention to breathing. Jain monks, too, have long practised careful awareness of every action, down to how they walk and eat.

From monastery to hospital

In 1979, an American scientist named Jon Kabat-Zinn started a programme at a medical school in Massachusetts. He took the attention exercises from Buddhist practice, left out the religion, and taught them to patients with long-lasting pain and stress. He called it Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction.

It spread fast. Today versions of it are used in hospitals, offices, sports teams and apps. In 2018, Delhi government schools began a Happiness Curriculum, with a few minutes of mindful breathing and attention at the start of lessons.

Who was the Buddha?A prince left his palace at 29 to find out why people suffer. What he taught now guides hundreds of millions of people.

What happens in the body

When you feel threatened, your body's alarm system speeds up your heart and breathing. This is the stress response. Slow, steady breathing with a long breath out is one of the simplest ways to calm it down, and it is often the first thing mindfulness teaches.

Brain scientists have also studied people who meditate a lot. Some studies report changes in areas linked to attention and emotion. These studies are often small, though, and experts warn against big claims. Mindfulness is best thought of as a skill, like learning to concentrate, and it gets easier with practice. A few people find that sitting quietly with their thoughts makes them feel worse, not better, and teachers are told to watch for this.

A careful look at the evidence

Between 2016 and 2022, researchers at the University of Oxford ran a huge study called MYRIAD. It involved about 85 schools and more than 8,000 pupils aged 11 to 16. Half the schools taught ten mindfulness lessons. The other half carried on as usual.

On average, the mindfulness pupils were no better off a year later. Many didn't practise outside class. The researchers said mindfulness may still help some young people, especially those who choose it themselves. That is a useful reminder: something that helps one person won't always help a whole class, and good science is how we find out.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Mindfulness means emptying your mind of all thoughts.

Truth: Nobody can switch off their thoughts. Mindfulness means noticing when your mind has wandered, without scolding yourself, and gently bringing it back. The wandering and coming back is the practice.

Try this

Try the 5-4-3-2-1 game when you feel restless. Quietly name five things you can see, four you can feel, three you can hear, two you can smell and one you can taste. It takes about a minute, and it pulls your attention back to where you are.
