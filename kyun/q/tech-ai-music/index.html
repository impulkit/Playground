Can computers write music?

In 1957, a computer at a university in Illinois wrote a piece for string quartet. Today an app can make a whole pop song in under a minute.

The short answer

Yes, computers can write music, and they have done it for almost 70 years. Today's AI programs can produce a full song with drums, melody, lyrics and a singing voice in less than a minute. All you type is a short description, like "a happy song about monsoon rain".

They do it by studying enormous amounts of recorded music and learning its patterns: which notes sound good together, how a chorus repeats, what a drum beat in hip hop sounds like. Then they generate new music that follows those patterns.

What they cannot do, as far as anyone can tell, is feel anything about it. Whether that makes them real composers is one of the big arguments of the 2020s.

Surprising but true

In 1843, long before any computer was built, Ada Lovelace wrote that a calculating machine might one day compose music.

Music with dice and early machines

The idea is older than computers. In 1843, the mathematician Ada Lovelace wrote that a calculating engine might "compose elaborate and scientific pieces of music of any degree of complexity". Nobody had built such a machine yet.

Even earlier, in the 1700s, people in Europe played "musical dice games". You rolled dice to choose which short bar of music came next, stitching together a new minuet each time. One of these games was published under Mozart's name in 1792, though experts are not certain he wrote it.

In 1957, two scientists at the University of Illinois, Lejaren Hiller and Leonard Isaacson, used a room-sized computer called ILLIAC I to write a piece for string quartet. They called it the Illiac Suite. The computer picked notes at random, then threw out any that broke rules of harmony. It was clunky, but it was a start. The music sounds a bit stiff today, yet it was performed by real string players and recorded, and people argued about whether a machine should be allowed to compose at all. That argument has never really stopped.

How are maths and music connected?Halve a guitar string and it plays the same note, only higher. That simple fact links music to maths, from ancient Greece to a tabla player's tihai.

How today's AI makes a song

Modern music AI works a bit like the chatbots that write sentences. A chatbot learns which word usually comes next. A music model learns which tiny slice of sound usually comes next, thousands of times every second.

To learn, it is fed huge collections of songs. Over time it picks up the shape of a ballad, the swing of jazz and the growl of rock guitar. Ask it for "a lullaby with a flute", and it builds one from scratch, one small step at a time.

Music is full of maths, such as repeating rhythms and note frequencies in neat ratios. That makes it a good puzzle for a pattern-learning machine.

The fights over fairness

The songs used to train these programs were written and recorded by real people, and most were never asked. In 2024, major record companies took two well-known AI song makers to court in the USA, saying they had copied music without permission. In late 2025, some of these companies agreed to settle and work on licensed versions instead, while other cases carried on.

Governments are still deciding the rules. The US Copyright Office has said that music made entirely by AI, with no real human creative choices, cannot be protected by copyright.

Helpers as well as rivals

Many musicians use AI as a tool. It can clean up old recordings, separate a singer's voice from the instruments, or suggest a chord when a songwriter is stuck. In 2023, technology like this helped pull John Lennon's voice out of an old, muffled tape, so the Beatles could finish one last song.

In India, composers have experimented with AI to recreate the voices of singers who have died, with their families' permission, which some listeners found moving and others found uneasy. That same trick of copying a voice is now one of the most powerful and worrying things AI can do with sound.

Myth or fact?

Myth: AI music programs just copy and paste bits of existing songs together.

Truth: Most build new sounds from patterns they learned, rather than gluing clips together. But because they learn from real songs, they can sometimes produce something very close to an existing tune, which is one reason for the court cases.

Try this

Make "dice music". Number six notes on a keyboard app or a set of glasses filled with different amounts of water. Roll a die eight times and play the notes in the order they come up. Does the tune sound like music, or just random? Try a rule, like "the last note must be note 1", and listen again.
