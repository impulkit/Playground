Is there life anywhere else?

Nobody has found life beyond Earth yet. But for the first time, we know exactly where to look and have machines that can search.

The short answer

We don't know yet. So far, the only place in the universe where we know life exists is Earth. But scientists are now searching more seriously than ever, in three main ways.

They are exploring places in our own solar system where tiny living things, like microbes, might survive, such as Mars and the icy moons of Jupiter and Saturn. They are studying the air of planets around other stars for gases that life might make. And some are listening for radio signals that an intelligent civilisation might send. Any one of these could one day change the answer from "we don't know" to "yes".

Surprising but true

A Saturn moon called Enceladus, only about 500 km wide, shoots fountains of salty water from an ocean under its ice. The Cassini spacecraft flew through them and tasted them.

What life needs

On Earth, every living thing needs liquid water, a source of energy and certain chemicals, especially ones built around carbon. Life also turns out to be tough. Microbes live in boiling hot springs, in rock kilometres underground and in Antarctic ice. Whole communities thrive around hot vents on the deep sea floor, far from any sunlight.

So scientists look for places with those ingredients, even if they look nothing like Earth's meadows and forests.

Close to home

Mars once had rivers, lakes and perhaps a shallow sea. Its surface is cold and dry today, but microbes could have lived there long ago. NASA's Mars rovers are searching. In 2024, the rover Perseverance found a rock with small speckled patterns that, on Earth, are often linked to microbes. Scientists say non-living chemistry could also explain them, and they hope one day to bring the sample home to check.

Europa, a moon of Jupiter, and Enceladus, a moon of Saturn, both have oceans of liquid water under their ice. Enceladus sprays some of its ocean into space, so a spacecraft can sample it without drilling. Saturn's largest moon, Titan, has lakes of liquid methane and a thick, hazy atmosphere. Any life there would have to be very strange. NASA's Dragonfly, a flying drone, is due to launch there later this decade.

Far away

There are more than 6,000 known planets around other stars, and probably hundreds of billions in our galaxy. Some are rocky and the right distance from their stars for liquid water.

Telescopes like the James Webb Space Telescope can now study the air of some of these planets. In 2025, one team reported a possible hint of a gas on a planet called K2-18b that, on Earth, is made mostly by living things. Many other astronomers think the evidence is too weak, and further studies have not settled it. Arguments like this are exactly how science tests big claims.

Listening for a signal

In 1960, the American astronomer Frank Drake pointed a radio telescope at two nearby stars for a few weeks, listening for messages. He heard nothing, but he started SETI, the Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence. A year later he wrote down an equation listing all the things you'd need to know to estimate how many civilisations might be out there.

In 1977, a radio telescope in Ohio picked up a strong, short signal that so excited the astronomer on duty that he circled it and wrote "Wow!" in the margin. It has never been detected again, and its source is still unknown. That is the point: one strange signal is not proof.

Where is everybody?

Around 1950, the physicist Enrico Fermi reportedly asked his colleagues at lunch: if the galaxy is so huge and old, where is everybody? Nobody has a settled answer. Perhaps life is rare. Perhaps it is common but intelligence is rare. Perhaps the distances are simply too vast.

Scientists think about this using probability, weighing what we know against what we don't. For now, the honest answer is that the universe hasn't told us yet.

Myth or fact?

Myth: UFO sightings prove that aliens are visiting Earth.

Truth: No sighting has ever produced solid evidence of alien visitors. Most turn out to be planes, balloons, satellites, the planet Venus or tricks of light. A 2023 NASA study panel found no evidence that any unexplained sightings came from beyond Earth.

Try this

With an adult, put a teaspoon of dried yeast and a teaspoon of sugar into a clean bottle with a little warm (not hot) water. Stretch a balloon over the neck and wait half an hour. The balloon slowly inflates with gas made by the living yeast. Scientists search other worlds for signs like this, gases that living things give off.
