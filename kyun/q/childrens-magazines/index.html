What magazines did Indian children read?

For decades, Indian children waited each month for a magazine where a stubborn king carried a talking ghost on his back.

The short answer

Indian children have had their own magazines for over a century. In Bengal, Sandesh began in 1913. After independence came a wave of others: Chandamama in 1947, Nandan in Hindi in 1964, Champak in the late 1960s, Balarama in Malayalam in 1972 and Tinkle in 1980.

They were cheap, colourful and came in many languages. Most mixed folk tales, puzzles, jokes, science snippets, comics and readers' letters. For millions of families, the day a new issue arrived was a small festival, and older siblings fought younger ones over who read it first.

Surprising but true

At its peak, Chandamama came out in 13 languages, including English and even Sanskrit, so children in Kerala and Punjab read the same stories in different scripts.

Sandesh and the Ray family

Sandesh, which means both "news" and a Bengali sweet, was started by Upendrakishore Ray Chowdhury, a writer, artist and printing expert. His son Sukumar Ray filled it with nonsense rhymes and drawings that Bengali children still recite.

The magazine closed in the 1920s. In 1961, Sukumar's son, the film director Satyajit Ray, revived it with a friend. Ray wrote detective stories and science fiction for it, including his first tales of the sleuth Feluda. He also drew many of the covers himself.

The moon uncle

Chandamama, meaning "moon uncle", was launched in Madras in July 1947, a month before independence, by the film producers B. Nagi Reddi and Chakrapani. It began in Telugu and Tamil and soon appeared in many other languages.

Its most loved series was about King Vikram and the Vetala, a spirit hanging from a tree. Each month Vikram carried it off on his shoulder, and each month it told him a story ending in a riddle. Chandamama drew on the Panchatantra, Jataka tales and old epics, with rich painted pictures. The artist K. C. Sivasankaran, known as Sankar, drew Vikram and the Vetala for decades, and his gloomy tree and grim-faced king became famous. It faded in the 2000s and stopped publishing around 2013.

Hindi, Malayalam and more

In north India, Nandan and Parag carried stories, poems and quizzes in Hindi. Champak, from the Delhi publisher Delhi Press, was full of talking animals in a forest town. It now appears in several languages, including English. In Delhi, the cartoonist K. Shankar Pillai, known simply as Shankar, started the English monthly Children's World in 1968.

In Kerala, Balarama became one of the most widely read children's magazines in India. It mixed home-grown comics with translated ones. Tamil children had Gokulam as well as Ambulimama, the Tamil edition of Chandamama. Marathi children had Kishor. Many of these magazines also printed readers' drawings and letters, so a child in a small town could see their own name in print.

Tinkle and its heroes

In 1980, Anant Pai, the man behind Amar Chitra Katha, started Tinkle. It brought India's best-loved comic characters: Suppandi, the servant who follows every order far too literally, and Shikari Shambu, the hunter who is terrified of animals and succeeds by luck. Readers sent in their own Suppandi stories, and the best ones were drawn and printed. Other regulars included Kalia the crow, who keeps rescuing smaller animals from a greedy jackal, and Tantri the Mantri, a minister whose plots to grab the throne always backfire.

Across the ocean

In the USA, Highlights for Children has been posted to homes since 1946, with its hidden-picture puzzles. An older magazine, St. Nicholas, ran a club for young writers in the early 1900s. One of them, an eleven-year-old named Rachel Carson, later wrote Silent Spring and helped start the modern environmental movement.

Some Indian magazines closed when phones and television took children's time. Nandan stopped printing in 2020. Others now sell digital editions next to the paper ones. Grandparents still keep bundles of old Chandamamas tied with string, and grandchildren still find them.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Children in India only started reading for fun after television and comics arrived.

Truth: Children's magazines in Indian languages go back more than a hundred years. Bengal's Sandesh was founded in 1913, and many families subscribed to magazines in Telugu, Tamil, Hindi, Malayalam and more long before television.

Try this

Make a one-page magazine for a younger cousin or class. Include a short story, a riddle, a joke and a "spot the difference" drawing. Fold it in half, give it a name, and ask your reader which part they liked best.
