Why do we have traffic rules?

On one Sunday morning in 1967, every driver in Sweden stopped, crossed to the other side of the road, and carried on driving on the right.

The short answer

Traffic rules exist because roads are shared by people moving at very different speeds. A truck, a cyclist, a cow and a child walking to school may all use the same street. Without rules, every crossing would turn into a guessing game.

Rules make other people predictable. If everyone keeps to the same side, stops at red and gives way at crossings, you can guess what the next driver will do. That saves lives. The World Health Organization estimates that road crashes killed about 1.2 million people around the world in 2021. They are the leading cause of death for children and young people aged 5 to 29.

Surprising but true

The world's first traffic light was installed outside the Houses of Parliament in London in 1868, decades before cars. It ran on gas and was meant for horse carriages.

Lights before cars

The first traffic light was set up in 1868 outside the Houses of Parliament in London, to help horse carriages and people crossing. It had red and green lamps lit by gas, and a police officer turned it by hand. A few weeks later it exploded, injuring the officer, and the idea was dropped for years.

Electric traffic lights appeared in American cities in the 1910s. In 1923, Garrett Morgan, a Black inventor from Cleveland, patented a signal with a third position that stopped traffic in all directions. Today's amber light does a similar job, a short warning before the red.

Left, right and Sweden's big day

India drives on the left, like Britain, Japan and Australia. The USA drives on the right. About a third of the world's people live in left-driving countries.

Sweden drove on the left for centuries, even though all its neighbours drove on the right. On 3 September 1967, the country switched. Traffic stopped early that morning, drivers carefully moved across, and then set off again on the right. The day is remembered in Sweden as Dagen H. Road accidents actually fell for a while afterwards, probably because everyone drove extra carefully.

Belts, helmets and speed

Some rules protect you even if you are the only one on the road. A seat belt holds you in place so you don't fly forward in a crash. WHO says wearing one cuts the risk of death for front-seat passengers by about half. A good helmet greatly lowers the chance of a serious head injury for someone on a two-wheeler.

Speed matters most of all. A faster car needs much more distance to stop, and a person hit at high speed is far less likely to survive. That's why speed limits drop near schools and hospitals. Using maths about speed and distance, you can work out that a car at 60 km/h covers more than 16 metres every second.

Roads in India and America

India has one of the highest numbers of road deaths in the world. In recent years, more than 1.6 lakh people have died each year, many of them walking, cycling or riding two-wheelers. In 2019, India raised its traffic fines sharply to push people to follow rules. The same law says that children older than four must wear a helmet when riding on a two-wheeler, just like adults.

The USA has around 40,000 road deaths a year. Many cities in both countries are now trying ideas like wider footpaths, raised crossings and bicycle lanes, so that safety is built into the roads themselves. A well-designed road makes the safe choice the easy one.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Wearing a helmet or seat belt only matters on long trips or highways.

Truth: Many crashes happen close to home and at low speeds. A seat belt or helmet protects you in any crash, which is why the law asks you to wear them on every trip.

Try this

Stand at a safe spot well back from a road with an adult, and count for five minutes. How many two-wheeler riders wear helmets? How many cars stop fully at a red light or zebra crossing? Make a tally chart and share what you notice with your class.
