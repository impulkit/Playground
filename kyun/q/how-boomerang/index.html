Why does a boomerang come back?

In 2008 an astronaut threw a boomerang inside the International Space Station, and even though everything there floats, it came back.

The short answer

A returning boomerang comes back because it does two things at once. It spins very fast, and each of its arms is shaped like a little aeroplane wing. As it whirls through the air, the arms make lift, a sideways push, just as wings do.

Here is the clever part. The arm at the top of the spin is moving forward through the air faster than the arm at the bottom, so it makes more lift. That uneven push tries to tip the boomerang over. But because it is spinning, it doesn't tip. Instead it slowly turns, like a bicycle leaning into a bend, and curves around in a big circle back towards you.

Surprising but true

Most traditional Aboriginal boomerangs were never meant to come back. They were heavy throwing sticks for hunting and fighting.

Wings that spin

Look at a boomerang's arms from the side. One edge is rounded and one is thin, and the top is more curved than the bottom. That is an aerofoil, the same shape as the wing of a plane. When air flows over it, it creates lift, which is explained in how aeroplanes fly.

A boomerang is thrown almost upright, so its lift pushes sideways rather than up. The thrower adds a hard flick of the wrist, giving it perhaps ten spins every second. Wind matters too. Throwers usually face the breeze and aim a little to the right of it, so the wind helps carry the boomerang round.

The spinning-top trick

Spinning objects behave strangely when you push them. Push the top of a spinning top and it doesn't fall, it swings around. Scientists call this precession.

The faster top arm of the boomerang always makes more lift than the slower bottom arm. That tries to tip the boomerang onto its side. Precession turns that tip into a steady turn. The boomerang curves left (for a right-handed thrower), circling round in a loop that can be 20–30 m wide, about the length of a cricket pitch.

Distance throwers use special boomerangs that loop out more than 100 m and still return, further than the length of a football field. Others are built to hover and drift down slowly, staying in the air for well over a minute.

Older than farming

Throwing sticks are among the oldest flying tools ever made. The oldest known boomerang was carved from a mammoth's tusk and found in Obłazowa Cave in southern Poland. In 2025, scientists dated it to about 40,000 years old. Like many ancient stone and bone tools, it was made long before anyone farmed or built a town.

Ancient Egyptians also used throwing sticks. Several were buried with the boy king Tutankhamun, including a few that may have been made to return.

Australia's famous tool

Aboriginal Australians have made boomerangs for many thousands of years. Most were heavy, nearly straight hunting sticks, sometimes called kylies, which flew straight and hit hard. Lighter, curved returning boomerangs were used for sport, for practice and to scare birds into nets.

India had throwing sticks too. In Tamil Nadu, the valari was a curved stick of wood or iron thrown in hunting and battle. Some people say certain valaris could return, but most were meant to hit and stay down.

Boomerangs in space

In March 2008, Japanese astronaut Takao Doi tested a small boomerang aboard the International Space Station. In the station everything floats, so nothing pulled it towards the floor, but there was air inside. The boomerang curved round and came back to his hand.

That shows gravity isn't what brings it home. Air and spin do all the work. A boomerang is really a spinning pair of wings. Its closest cousin may be the tilted wing of a kite, which needs a string to stay up, while a boomerang needs nothing but a good throw.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Boomerangs were invented in Australia, and every boomerang is designed to return.

Truth: Throwing sticks were made in many places, including ancient Egypt, Europe and India. Most were non-returning hunting tools. Returning boomerangs were a lighter, special kind, and Australia is where they became famous.

Try this

Make a mini card boomerang: cut a cross shape, about 8 cm across, from a cereal box and bend each arm's tip up slightly. Lay it on a book held at an angle and flick one arm hard with your finger. Watch it spin away and curve back.
