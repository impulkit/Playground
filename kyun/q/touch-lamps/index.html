How do touch lamps work?

A touch lamp doesn't feel your finger at all. It notices that your body, which is mostly salty water, can hold a little extra electric charge.

The short answer

A touch lamp has a small circuit inside that constantly measures how much electric charge its metal body can hold. That amount is called capacitance. On its own, the lamp holds a tiny, steady amount.

When you touch the metal, your body becomes part of it. You are a big bag of salty water, which conducts electricity well, so together you and the lamp can hold a bit more charge. The circuit notices the jump, decides someone has touched it, and switches the light on, off or to the next brightness. You don't feel anything, because the electrical signals are far too small.

Surprising but true

Hold a metal spoon in your bare hand and tap a touch lamp with it, and the lamp usually switches on. Tap it with a wooden spoon and nothing happens.

What capacitance means

Think of capacitance like the size of a bucket for electric charge. A small metal lamp base is a small bucket. Your body, much bigger and full of conducting water, is a much bigger one. Touch the lamp and you join the two buckets together.

The circuit inside keeps pouring tiny amounts of charge in and out, thousands of times a second, and times how long it takes to fill. With a bigger bucket it takes longer. That change in timing is the signal. It's a cousin of static electricity, the charge that makes balloons stick to walls, only far gentler.

Why gloves stop it

Wool, leather, plastic and wood don't conduct electricity well. They act like a wall between your body and the lamp, so the circuit can't sense the extra capacitance. That's why a touch lamp often ignores you when you wear thick winter gloves. Very thin gloves sometimes work anyway, because the circuit can still sense a hand through a thin enough layer.

Metal is different. A metal spoon or key conducts, so it acts like a longer finger. Even damp skin works better than very dry skin, because water helps charge move.

Dim, brighter, brightest

Most touch lamps have more than two settings. Each touch tells a tiny chip to move to the next step: dim, medium, bright, then off. The chip doesn't make the bulb weaker. Instead it switches the power on and off very fast, many times a second. The longer each "on" moment lasts, the brighter the light bulb looks. Your eyes blend the flicker into a steady glow.

Because the lamp is always sensing, it can sometimes switch on by itself. Electrical noise from a storm, a power surge or a nearby motor can fool the circuit into thinking someone touched it. Some lamps come with a small add-on to filter out that noise.

How does a touchscreen feel your finger?Your phone screen doesn't feel the push of your finger. It feels the tiny electric charge your body carries.

The same trick, everywhere

Once you know the idea, you see it all over the place. Most phone and tablet screens use capacitive sensing, with a fine grid of see-through wires that can tell exactly where your finger is. That's why a touchscreen ignores a plastic pen but responds to a finger, and why special gloves for phones have threads that conduct electricity.

Lift buttons with no moving parts, the glass buttons on induction stoves, and the touch controls on some earphones all work the same way. They're popular because there is nothing to press down or break, and the surface can be wiped clean easily. Many cars use the same idea in door handles that open the locks when you touch them.

Safe to touch

People sometimes worry about touching a lamp that is "full of electricity". In a touch lamp, the metal you touch is linked only to the low-power sensing circuit. The mains electricity that powers the bulb is kept safely inside. As with any lamp plugged into the wall, though, never open one up or poke inside, and ask an adult if a lamp behaves strangely.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Touch lamps work by sensing the heat of your hand.

Truth: They sense electrical charge, not heat. A warm cup won't switch one on, but a cold metal spoon held in your hand will. That is because your body conducts electricity and heat has nothing to do with it.

Try this

Test what a phone's touchscreen can feel. With an adult's permission, tap an app with a fingertip, a knuckle, a fingernail, a pencil eraser, a glove, a metal spoon held in your bare hand and a slice of carrot. Most phone screens use the same trick as a touch lamp, so the results should surprise you.
