How might we travel in 2050?

People in the 1950s were sure we'd all have flying cars by 2000. Here's what engineers are actually building for 2050, and what might still be a dream.

The short answer

In 2050, most people will probably still walk, cycle, take buses and trains, and drive cars. The big change is likely to be what powers them. Many countries plan for most new cars, scooters and buses to be electric well before then, and for faster trains to link big cities.

A few bolder ideas, such as flying taxis and self-driving cars, are being tested now. Some may become common. Others may turn out like the flying cars of 1950s magazines: always about ten years away. The honest answer is that engineers know what they're building, but nobody knows which ideas people will actually use.

Surprising but true

In 2023 Hyperloop One, the best-funded company trying to build super-fast tube trains, shut down without carrying a single passenger.

Why travel has to change

Transport burns a lot of fuel. Cars, trucks, ships and planes produce roughly a fifth of the world's energy-related carbon dioxide, the main gas behind climate change. Exhaust fumes also make city air harder to breathe, from Delhi to Los Angeles.

So most plans for 2050 aim at two things: cleaner power and fewer cars per person. That's why you'll hear about batteries, hydrogen, metro lines and cycle lanes all at once.

Long-distance trucks, ships and planes are harder. Batteries heavy enough to fly a jumbo jet across an ocean would weigh too much. Engineers are testing hydrogen, fuels made from plants or waste, and more efficient engines. These are likely to arrive more slowly than electric cars, and may still be rare in 2050.

Why is Earth getting warmer?In 1856, an American scientist showed that one gas in the air traps heat. That gas is now at its highest level for hundreds of thousands of years.

Faster trains

India's first bullet train line is being built between Mumbai and Ahmedabad, about 508 km. It is designed for speeds of up to 320 km/h, which would cut the trip to around two or three hours. In the US, California has been building a high-speed line for years, though it has faced long delays and rising costs.

Japan is going further with a maglev line, where trains float on magnets. A test train reached 603 km/h in 2015, faster than a small plane. The full line from Tokyo to Nagoya has been delayed, and won't open before the 2030s.

Ideas still on the drawing board

A hyperloop would shoot pods through tubes with most of the air pumped out, at airliner speeds. It got huge attention in the 2010s, and India even studied a Mumbai to Pune route. But building long, airtight tubes is very expensive, and in 2023 Hyperloop One, the best-funded company, closed down.

Flying taxis are small electric aircraft that take off straight up, like a helicopter, but quieter. Several companies are testing them, and a few cities hope to start short routes soon. They may end up as an expensive option for airports and traffic-jammed business districts, not something most families use daily. They also need new rules for crowded skies, and places to land on rooftops.

Self-driving cars already carry paying passengers in a few US cities, such as San Francisco and Phoenix. They still struggle with unusual roads, and Indian traffic, with its cows, handcarts and autorickshaws, is a tougher test than a neat American grid.

The quiet winners

Some of the biggest changes may look ordinary. Electric two-wheelers and three-wheelers are spreading fast in India. Cities are adding metro lines, bus lanes and bicycle paths. Parcels may arrive by small drones in some places.

The child who rides a metro to school in 2050 might not find it futuristic at all. Their grandparents, who rode a crowded bus on a dusty road, would see it very differently.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Electric cars cause no pollution at all.

Truth: Electric cars have no exhaust pipe, so they don't pollute the street. But making their batteries uses energy and mining, and the electricity they use may come from coal. Their tyres and brakes also shed dust.
