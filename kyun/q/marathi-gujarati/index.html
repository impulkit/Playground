How did Marathi and Gujarati grow?

In 1960, a single Indian state split in two along a line drawn by language, and Maharashtra and Gujarat were born on the same day.

The short answer

Marathi and Gujarati both grew out of older spoken languages of western India, called Prakrits and Apabhramsha, which themselves came from the same ancient roots as Sanskrit. Over hundreds of years, the way people spoke changed bit by bit, until by around a thousand years ago these had become recognisable early forms of Marathi and Gujarati.

They grew through poetry, devotional songs, trade and royal courts. Today Marathi is spoken by about 8.3 crore people and Gujarati by about 5.5 crore, according to India's 2011 Census. Both are official languages of their states, and both have large communities abroad.

Surprising but true

Gujarati is written in a script that looks like Devanagari with the top line taken off.

Poet-saints who wrote for everyone

In the 1200s, much serious writing in India was in Sanskrit, which most ordinary people could not read. In 1290, a young saint named Dnyaneshwar wrote the Dnyaneshwari, an explanation of the Bhagavad Gita in Marathi verse, so that everyone could understand it. Tradition says he was only about sixteen.

Other saints followed. Tukaram, in the 1600s, wrote abhangas, devotional songs still sung by pilgrims who walk to the temple town of Pandharpur. In Gujarat, the poet Narsinh Mehta wrote songs to Krishna in the 1400s. One of them, Vaishnava Jana To, describes a good person as one who feels the pain of others. It became one of Gandhi's favourite hymns.

These singers belonged to the bhakti movement, which spread devotion in everyday languages across India, much like the poems of Mirabai and Kabir in the north.

Kings, merchants and scripts

In the 1600s, Shivaji made Marathi a language of government in his kingdom and had a dictionary made to replace many Persian official words with Sanskrit and Marathi ones. For centuries, Marathi letters and accounts were written in a fast, flowing script called Modi, which let a writer keep the pen on the paper. Today Marathi uses Devanagari, the same script as Hindi and Sanskrit.

Gujarati became the language of traders. Gujarati merchants sailed to Arabia, East Africa and Southeast Asia for centuries, and their account books were often written in a quick script without the top line. That simple, flowing style became the Gujarati script used today.

One state becomes two

After independence, Marathi and Gujarati speakers shared a large state called Bombay State. Many people in both communities wanted their own state. The Samyukta Maharashtra movement campaigned for a Marathi state, and the Mahagujarat movement for a Gujarati one. There were long, sometimes violent protests, especially over which state should get the city of Mumbai.

On 1 May 1960, Bombay State was divided. Maharashtra, with Mumbai as its capital, and Gujarat were created on the same day. Both states still celebrate 1 May as their foundation day. It was part of a wider redrawing of India's states along language lines.

Words that travelled

Gandhi wrote his autobiography, The Story of My Experiments with Truth, in Gujarati, in the 1920s. It was later translated into many languages. Gandhi wanted Indians to value their own mother tongues. Alongside his English weekly, Young India, he ran a Gujarati weekly called Navajivan.

In 2024, the Government of India declared Marathi a classical language. Marathi has given India a huge theatre tradition, and Mumbai's film industry was born in a Marathi-speaking city. Gujarati is now heard in shops and temples from Leicester in England to Edison, New Jersey, where many Gujarati families have settled.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Marathi is a form of Hindi, because both use the same script.

Truth: Sharing a script does not make two languages the same. Marathi has its own grammar and vocabulary and a written history going back about a thousand years.
