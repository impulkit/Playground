Who invented Velcro, and how does it stick?

Velcro started with a dog covered in prickly seeds after a walk in the Swiss mountains.

The short answer

Velcro is made of two strips of fabric. One is covered in thousands of tiny stiff hooks. The other is covered in thousands of soft loops. Press them together and many hooks catch on many loops. Each hook is weak on its own, but together they hold surprisingly hard. A small strip on a shoe can easily take a day of running about. Wider strips, made of tougher plastic, can even hold pictures and tools up on walls.

To open it, you peel one strip back. That way you are only pulling a few hooks free at a time, so it is easy. The ripping sound is hundreds of hooks being dragged open, one after another. Pulling the whole strip straight apart at once is much harder, because every hook has to let go together.

The idea came from nature. In 1941, a Swiss engineer named George de Mestral noticed how burrs clung to his clothes and his dog's fur, and wondered why.

Surprising but true

NASA didn't invent Velcro, but it used plenty of it. Astronauts stuck pens, food packets and tools to the walls so they wouldn't float away.

A walk in the Alps

De Mestral had been out hunting in the Swiss countryside with his dog. Afterwards, both of them were covered in burrs, the spiky seed cases of the burdock plant. Instead of just picking them off, he looked at one under a microscope.

He saw that each spike ended in a tiny hook. The hooks caught on anything with loops, such as the threads in his trousers and the curly hair of his dog. Plants use this trick to spread their seeds, which hitch rides on passing animals and fall off somewhere new.

How do seeds fly?Plants can't walk, so many of them build their seeds parachutes, propellers and gliders, and let the wind do the rest.

Years of trial and error

Copying the burr turned out to be hard. De Mestral first tried cotton, but cotton hooks were too soft and wore out quickly. Eventually he found that nylon, a new plastic fabric, could be shaped into strong, springy hooks.

Making them was the next puzzle. He worked out a way to weave nylon into loops, toughen them with heat, and snip some of the loops so they became hooks. It took him about ten years. He received a patent in 1955 and named his invention Velcro, from the French words velours, meaning velvet, and crochet, meaning hook.

How NASA made it famous

At first, fashion designers weren't impressed. The fastener looked rough and cheap. Then, in the 1960s, it found a home in space. Inside a spacecraft, everything floats unless it is held down. Astronauts on the Apollo missions used patches of it to stick pens, food packets and equipment to the walls.

Astronauts on the International Space Station still do. Some have even stuck a small patch inside their spacesuit helmets to scratch an itchy nose. After the Moon missions, children's shoes, school bags and jackets began using it, and sales took off.

Velcro the name

Velcro is a company name, so the general name for the product is hook-and-loop fastener. The company has politely asked people not to call every hook-and-loop strip "velcro", much as some people say "Xerox" for any photocopy.

De Mestral's idea is now a classic example of what scientists call biomimicry, copying designs from living things. Engineers have studied the burr alongside other natural wonders, such as gecko feet that cling to walls and shark skin that slips through water. Next to a zip, Velcro is one of the simplest fasteners ever made, and its hooks still look almost exactly like the burdock's.

Myth or fact?

Myth: NASA invented Velcro for astronauts.

Truth: George de Mestral invented it in Switzerland and patented it in 1955. NASA made it famous by using it widely in spacecraft from the 1960s, which is probably where the myth comes from.

Try this

Look closely at both sides of a strip of hook-and-loop tape, using a magnifying glass if you can. Sketch what the hooks and loops look like. Then press the strips together and pull them apart slowly, then quickly, and listen. Try peeling from one corner and then pulling the whole strip straight apart. Which takes more force?
