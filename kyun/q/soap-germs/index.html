How does soap get rid of germs?

Each soap molecule has one end that loves water and one end that loves grease.

The short answer

Germs often stick to your hands in oily, greasy dirt, and water alone slides right off grease. Soap fixes this. Each soap molecule has a head that loves water and a tail that loves oil.

The oily tails dig into the grease, and the water-loving heads face outward. The grease gets wrapped up in tiny balls that rinse away, taking germs with them.

Soap doesn't need to kill a germ to get rid of it. Most of the work is lifting germs off your skin so the water can wash them down the drain.

Surprising but true

Soap can break apart some viruses by tearing open their fatty outer layer, like a pin popping a balloon.

Popping viruses

Some viruses have an outer coat made of fatty material. Soap tails can wedge into that coat and break it apart, which destroys the virus. The flu virus and the virus that causes COVID-19 both have this kind of coat, which is why handwashing was such important advice during the pandemic.

Many bacteria are tougher, but soap and scrubbing still loosen them from the skin and wash them away.

Twenty seconds

Scrubbing matters as much as soap. Wash for at least 20 seconds, about as long as humming "Happy Birthday" twice, including between fingers and under nails.

The rubbing helps soap reach every crease and pulls germs out of the tiny grooves in your skin. Rinsing carries them off, and drying your hands on a clean towel removes even more. Warm water feels nicer, but cold water cleans just as well.

How was soap invented?The first soap was made more than 4,000 years ago from fat and wood ash, and it was often used to clean wool and cloth rather than people.

A doctor nobody believed

In 1847, a doctor named Ignaz Semmelweis noticed something terrible at a hospital in Vienna. Many more mothers died after giving birth on the ward run by doctors than on the ward run by midwives. The doctors often came straight from examining dead bodies.

Semmelweis made them wash their hands in a chlorine solution, and deaths on that ward fell sharply. Many doctors laughed at him. Germs hadn't been discovered yet, and the idea that their own hands spread disease offended them. Today, washing hands with soap is one of the cheapest and best ways to stop many illnesses from spreading.

Soap through history

People have made soap-like cleaners for thousands of years, often by boiling fats or oils with ashes.

In India, families long washed hair and clothes with reetha, the soapnut, whose dried fruit makes a lather in water, and with shikakai pods. They work for a similar reason: they contain natural chemicals that behave a lot like soap.

Germs that help

Not every germ is an enemy. Your skin and gut are home to trillions of friendly microbes that help digest food and crowd out harmful ones. Washing your hands removes some of them for a while, but they quickly grow back.

Washing matters most at certain moments: before eating or cooking, after using the toilet, after blowing your nose, and after playing outside or with animals. The germs that do get past your skin then meet your immune system, the body's own defence force.

A day for washing

Global Handwashing Day is held every year on 15 October. Schools in India, the USA and many other countries use it to teach good handwashing. A bar of soap costs very little, yet health experts count it among the most useful tools in medicine.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Antibacterial soap is much better than ordinary soap for washing your hands at home.

Truth: For everyday handwashing, ordinary soap and water work just as well. In 2016, the US Food and Drug Administration found no proof that antibacterial chemicals in home soaps worked better, and banned several of them.

Try this

Rub a few drops of cooking oil over your hands, then sprinkle on a pinch of cinnamon powder to stand in for germs. Rinse with water alone and see how much stays stuck. Then wash with soap for 20 seconds, scrubbing between your fingers and around your nails, and compare.
