What are the Lakshadweep islands like?

India's smallest union territory is a scatter of coral islands so low and flat that the highest ground is only a few metres above the sea.

The short answer

Lakshadweep is a group of 36 small coral islands in the Arabian Sea, off the coast of Kerala. Only 10 of them have people living on them. They are flat and low, fringed with white coral sand and coconut palms, and surrounded by calm, clear lagoons of turquoise water. Beyond the lagoons, waves crash on the reefs.

It is India's smallest union territory. All the islands together cover only about 32 square km, and about 64,000 people lived there at the 2011 census. Most islanders fish, grow coconuts or work for the local government. Visitors need a special permit, which helps protect the islands and their way of life.

Surprising but true

All of Lakshadweep's islands put together cover only about 32 square km, smaller than many Indian cities, but its lagoons and reefs spread over a far bigger area of sea.

Built by coral

Most of the islands are atolls, rings of coral reef around a lagoon. Corals are tiny animals that build hard skeletons of limestone. Over thousands of years, those skeletons pile up into reefs.

Millions of years ago, there was volcanic rock here. As it slowly sank, corals kept building upwards towards the sunlight. The rock is now deep below, and what's left at the surface is a ring of reef with low islands of broken coral and sand. Nowhere on the islands is more than a few metres above the sea.

Life in the lagoon

The reefs are full of life: parrotfish, butterflyfish, octopuses, sea cucumbers and reef sharks. Green and hawksbill turtles graze on seagrass in the lagoons. The waters around the islands are also rich in tuna.

Lakshadweep fishers are known for catching skipjack tuna by pole and line. They throw small live bait fish into the water to draw a tuna shoal close, then pull the fish up one by one on hooks. It catches fewer unwanted animals than big nets do, and has been practised here for generations. Much of the catch is boiled, smoked and dried into a hard fish called masmin, sold in Kerala and beyond.

Islanders and their languages

Most islanders are Muslim, and Islam is thought to have reached the islands many centuries ago through traders and preachers sailing from Arabia and Kerala. The main language is a form of Malayalam called Jeseri. On Minicoy, the southernmost island, people speak Mahl, a language closely related to Dhivehi, which is spoken in the nearby Maldives.

Life follows the rhythm of the sea and the monsoon. Coconut is everywhere: in food, in rope made from its fibre, and in the thatch of older houses. Kavaratti, the capital, has a famous mosque with carved wooden ceilings. Minicoy has a tall lighthouse built by the British in 1885.

A fragile paradise

Low coral islands are some of the places most at risk from a warming planet. When the sea gets too hot, corals throw out the tiny algae living inside them and turn white. This is called coral bleaching, and it hit Lakshadweep's reefs hard in 1998, 2010 and 2016. Some reefs recovered. Others did not.

Rising sea levels are another worry, because the islands have no high ground. Healthy reefs act as natural breakwaters, taking the force of waves before they reach the shore. Protecting the corals helps protect the islands themselves. The government also limits tourism and fresh water use, because the islands depend on rain and on a thin layer of fresh groundwater floating above the salty sea water underground.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Lakshadweep is part of the Andaman and Nicobar Islands.

Truth: They are on opposite sides of India. Lakshadweep is in the Arabian Sea, west of Kerala. The Andaman and Nicobar Islands are far away in the Bay of Bengal, to the east.

Try this

Make a model atoll. Build a small mound of clay or play dough in a dish, then press a ring of small pebbles or shells around it near the top. Slowly add water until the mound disappears. The ring of "reef" that stays at the surface is the shape of an atoll.
