Why do mosquitoes bite some people more?

It isn't your "sweet blood". Mosquitoes choose who to bite mostly by smell, and some people's skin smells far more delicious to them.

The short answer

Mosquitoes find people mostly by smell. They follow the carbon dioxide you breathe out, then home in on your body heat and the scent of your skin. Your skin smell is a mix of chemicals made by your body and by the bacteria that live on it, and it's a little different for everyone.

Some people's mix is far more attractive to mosquitoes than others'. Scientists have found that people who make more of certain fatty acids on their skin get bitten more, and they stay that way for years. Other things matter too: people who breathe out more carbon dioxide, are hot from running, or are wearing dark clothes are easier for mosquitoes to find.

Surprising but true

In a 2022 experiment, the people most attractive to mosquitoes stayed that way for years. Their skin made more of certain fatty acids than other people's.

Only the mothers bite

Male mosquitoes never bite. Both males and females drink nectar from flowers for energy. But a female needs the protein in blood to make her eggs. After a meal of blood, she can lay a batch of 100 or more eggs in still water.

There are about 3,500 kinds of mosquito, and most don't bite people at all. A few, such as Aedes and Anopheles mosquitoes, are very good at finding humans. These are the ones that spread diseases like dengue and malaria.

Following the clues

A mosquito looking for blood picks up the carbon dioxide in your breath from several metres away. It flies upwind, zigzagging through the trail. As it gets closer, it senses your warmth and the moisture coming off your skin. At the last moment, your skin's smell helps it decide where to land.

That smell comes partly from sweat and partly from the bacteria that live on your skin, which turn sweat into smelly chemicals. Feet and ankles have lots of bacteria, which may be why some mosquitoes love to bite there.

Why are mosquitoes so dangerous?No shark, snake or tiger comes close. Through the diseases they carry, mosquitoes cause more human deaths than any other animal.

Mosquito magnets

In 2022, researchers at Rockefeller University in New York tested how attractive different people's smells were to mosquitoes. Volunteers wore nylon stockings on their arms for six hours to collect their skin scent. Then the stockings were placed in a box with mosquitoes.

Some people's stockings attracted many times more mosquitoes than others'. When the volunteers were tested again over months and years, the "magnets" stayed magnets. Their skin had higher levels of certain fatty acids. Nobody yet knows how to change your own natural scent.

What else matters

People who breathe out more carbon dioxide, like adults compared with small children, are often bitten more. Pregnant women tend to be bitten more too, probably because they breathe out more and are slightly warmer.

Colour makes a difference. A 2022 study found that once mosquitoes smelled carbon dioxide, they flew towards red, orange, black and cyan colours, and ignored green, blue and white. Blood type is less clear. Some studies suggest mosquitoes prefer people with type O blood, but others found no difference.

A discovery in Secunderabad

In 1897, a British army doctor named Ronald Ross, working in Secunderabad, near Hyderabad, found the malaria parasite in the stomach of an Anopheles mosquito. It proved that mosquitoes spread malaria. He won the Nobel Prize in 1902 for this work.

Malaria still kills about 6 lakh people a year, mostly young children in Africa, according to the World Health Organization's 2023 figures. That is why scientists care so much about what attracts mosquitoes. A better understanding of mosquito smell could lead to better repellents and traps.

Keeping them away

Repellents work by confusing the mosquito's sense of smell. Long sleeves, mosquito nets, window screens and getting rid of standing water in pots and buckets all help. Studies have found that eating garlic doesn't keep mosquitoes away, despite what many people believe. If a bite becomes very swollen or someone gets a fever after being bitten, tell a trusted adult, who can check with a doctor.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Mosquitoes bite some people more because they have sweet blood.

Truth: Mosquitoes can't taste your blood before they bite. They choose you by the carbon dioxide you breathe out, your warmth and the smell of your skin, which is shaped by the bacteria living on it.

Try this

Keep a family bite diary for one week. Each evening, everyone counts any new mosquito bites and notes what colour clothes they wore and whether they had been running around. At the end of the week, make a simple chart and see who the "mosquito magnet" is.
