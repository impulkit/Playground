What is Sesame Street?

Sesame Street was planned by researchers, not just entertainers, and they tested early episodes on real children to see which bits held their attention.

The short answer

Sesame Street is an American children's TV show that began in 1969. It mixes puppets, cartoons, songs and real people on a city street to teach young children letters, numbers, kindness and much more. Its most famous characters are Jim Henson's Muppets, such as Big Bird, Elmo, Cookie Monster, Oscar the Grouch, and Bert and Ernie.

What made it different was the planning. Educators and researchers helped write it and tested episodes on real children. The show has been made in dozens of countries, including India, where it was called Galli Galli Sim Sim.

Surprising but true

India had its own Sesame Street, called Galli Galli Sim Sim, with a girl Muppet named Chamki who loved school.

A question about TV

In 1966, a TV producer named Joan Ganz Cooney and a psychologist named Lloyd Morrisett asked a simple question. Young children already loved television and could sing advertising jingles. Could TV teach them something useful, especially children from poorer families who couldn't go to preschool?

Cooney studied the idea and set up a new organisation, the Children's Television Workshop, now called Sesame Workshop. The first episode aired on 10 November 1969.

Tested on children

Before it went on air, researchers showed test episodes to groups of young children. They noted the moments when the children looked away. Early on, scenes with only human actors didn't hold attention well, so the makers mixed in more Muppets.

The show was built like a TV advert, with short, punchy segments. One might teach the letter B, another the number 7, another how to share. Many episodes are "brought to you by" a letter and a number, a joke about real adverts.

The Muppets

Jim Henson and his team made the Muppets, a cross between a marionette and a hand puppet. Big Bird is a performer inside a giant yellow costume, while Elmo and Cookie Monster are operated from below the camera's view.

Each Muppet was designed to show a type of child. Elmo is a curious three-year-old. Oscar the Grouch shows that it's all right to feel grumpy. Cookie Monster can't control himself around cookies, which makes his lessons about patience funny.

Galli Galli Sim Sim

In 2006, an Indian version called Galli Galli Sim Sim began, made with Indian partners and shown on TV channels including Pogo and Doordarshan. Its stories were set in Indian neighbourhoods and spoke in Hindi.

Its Muppets included Chamki, a five-year-old girl who loves school, and Boombah, a big friendly lion who loves food. The show focused on things like going to school, washing hands, and treating girls and boys equally. Local versions have also been made in Mexico, South Africa, Bangladesh, Afghanistan and many other places.

Does it work?

Researchers have studied Sesame Street for decades. Several studies found that children who watched it learned more letters and numbers before starting school. One 2015 study looked at the 1970s, when some American areas could receive the show easily and others couldn't. Children in the areas with good reception did better in their early school years.

The show also tackled harder subjects, like divorce, illness and disaster, and used careful scripts to help children cope. Its research team helped show how young children's brains learn through repetition and play.

New friends

Sesame Street keeps adding Muppets to reflect real children. In 2017 it introduced Julia, a Muppet with autism, created with advice from autistic people and their families. Other new characters have been refugees or have a parent in prison.

After more than fifty years, the show still opens on the same street. Its songs about the alphabet, counting and friendship have been sung by millions of children, many of whom now watch with their own kids.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Cookie Monster gave up cookies and now eats only vegetables.

Truth: In the 2000s Cookie Monster began singing that cookies are a "sometimes food", which started the rumour. He still eats cookies, just alongside fruit and vegetables.

Try this

Make a sock puppet with buttons or paper for eyes. Then use it to teach a younger child or a toy one letter, number or word, with a short song or joke. Notice what makes your audience pay attention, and what makes them wander off.
