What was the Great Depression?

In October 1929 share prices in New York crashed. Within three years, about one in four American workers had no job, and the trouble had spread around the globe.

The short answer

The Great Depression was the worst economic slump of the modern world. It began in the United States in 1929 and lasted through most of the 1930s. Factories closed, banks failed, and millions of people lost their jobs, savings and homes.

Because countries traded and lent money to each other, the trouble spread fast. Farmers in Brazil, factory workers in Germany and jute growers in Bengal all felt it. Governments tried different cures. Some worked, some made things worse, and the hard times helped bring dangerous leaders to power.

Surprising but true

During the Depression, so much gold left India to pay debts and taxes that it helped Britain support its own currency.

The crash

In the 1920s many Americans bought shares in companies, often with borrowed money, betting that prices would keep rising. Prices on the stock market soared far beyond what the companies were really worth.

In late October 1929 the bubble burst. On 24 October, later called Black Thursday, panicked owners rushed to sell. Five days later came Black Tuesday, when prices fell even further. Fortunes vanished in days. The crash did not cause the whole Depression on its own, but it shook people's confidence and made them stop spending.

When banks ran out of money

A bank keeps only a small part of your money in its vaults. It lends the rest. That works as long as people don't all want their money back on the same day. In the early 1930s, frightened people did exactly that. Thousands of banks could not pay and shut their doors, wiping out families' savings.

With less money around, people bought less. Shops sold less, so factories made less and fired workers, who then bought even less. By 1933 about a quarter of American workers were jobless. Some lived in shacks made of crates and tin, in camps that people mockingly named "Hoovervilles", after President Herbert Hoover.

The Dust Bowl

On the Great Plains, farmers faced a second disaster. Years of ploughing had stripped away the grass that held the soil down. When drought struck in the 1930s, the wind lifted the dry topsoil into giant black clouds. Some dust storms rolled hundreds of kilometres and dropped dirt as far east as the Atlantic coast.

Hundreds of thousands of people left the Plains, many heading west to California to look for work. The writer John Steinbeck told their story in his novel The Grapes of Wrath.

The New Deal

In 1933 Franklin D. Roosevelt became president and promised a "New Deal". The government hired millions of people to build roads, dams, schools and parks. One programme, the Civilian Conservation Corps, gave jobs to young men who planted about three billion trees across the country. New rules protected bank savings. In 1935 the Social Security Act began paying pensions to older Americans, a system that still exists.

Unemployment fell, but did not fully recover until factories got busy again near the start of the Second World War.

Around the world

Countries tried to protect their own industries by putting big taxes on imports. That shrank world trade even more. In Germany, mass unemployment helped Adolf Hitler's Nazi party gain support, a step on the road to World War Two.

In India, prices for crops such as wheat, rice and jute fell by about half, but taxes and rents did not. Many farming families sold their gold jewellery to get by, and large amounts of gold flowed out of the country. Anger over these hardships fed into the freedom movement of the 1930s.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Great Depression only hurt the United States.

Truth: It spread worldwide. Germany, Britain, Australia, Latin America and India were all hit hard, because trade and loans tied countries together.
