How do kingfishers catch fish?

A Japanese engineer who loved birdwatching redesigned the nose of a bullet train to look like a kingfisher's beak, and the train became quieter and faster.

The short answer

A fishing kingfisher sits very still on a branch or wire above the water, watching. When it spots a small fish near the surface, it dives head first, wings folded back, and seizes the fish in its dagger-shaped beak. The whole dive takes about a second. It flies back to its perch, beats the fish against the branch to stun it, and swallows it head first so the fins and scales slide down easily.

To pull this off, a kingfisher must see through the glare on the water, aim for a fish that isn't quite where it looks, and hit the water without hurting itself. Its body is built for all three.

Surprising but true

The brilliant blue of a kingfisher's feathers isn't made by blue pigment. It comes from the way tiny structures in the feathers scatter light.

Seeing into the water

When light passes from water into air, it bends. This is called refraction, and it makes underwater things look shallower and in a different place than they really are. A kingfisher must allow for this, aiming below where the fish appears.

Young kingfishers miss often and get better with practice. They also tend to dive steeply from right above, where the bending is smallest. A see-through third eyelid, called a nictitating membrane, slides across each eye as it hits the water, like a pair of swimming goggles.

Built to dive

A kingfisher's long, sharp beak cuts into the water with hardly a splash. Its head and beak are shaped so that it slips from air into water, which is much thicker, without a big jolt. Its feathers are thick and waterproof, and after each dive it shakes off the water and preens.

The pied kingfisher, a black-and-white bird common over Indian rivers and lakes, can hunt without a perch. It hovers in one spot over open water, beating its wings fast, then plunges straight down. This lets it fish in the middle of wide lakes far from any branch.

A train with a bird's beak

In the 1990s, Japan's fast Shinkansen bullet trains had a problem. When they shot into tunnels at high speed, they pushed a wave of air ahead of them that burst out of the far end with a loud boom. Engineer Eiji Nakatsu, a keen birdwatcher, thought of how a kingfisher dives into water with barely a ripple.

His team gave the 500 Series train a long, pointed nose shaped like a kingfisher's beak. When it entered service in 1997, it was quieter in tunnels and used less electricity, and it ran faster. It is one of the best-known examples of engineers copying nature to solve problems, an approach called biomimicry.

Kingfishers in India and beyond

India has about 12 kinds of kingfisher. The common kingfisher, only about 16 cm long, flashes electric blue and orange along streams and ponds. The white-throated kingfisher, with its chocolate-brown head and loud rattling call, is often seen on wires in towns and fields, far from water. The big stork-billed kingfisher hunts in mangroves and forest rivers.

That flashing blue isn't paint. Kingfisher feathers have no blue pigment at all. Inside each feather are tiny structures, much smaller than a hair is wide, that scatter blue light back towards your eye, a bit like the way the sky looks blue. If you crush a blue feather, the structure breaks and the colour turns dull grey-brown. The orange on the bird's chest, by contrast, does come from pigment.

Kingfishers usually nest in tunnels they dig into earth banks along rivers, pecking and kicking out the soil. The tunnel is often more than half a metre long. The eggs are laid in a chamber at the end. In the USA, the belted kingfisher is the one most people see, rattling along rivers and lakes. To spot any of them, walk slowly near clean water in wetlands and watch the branches and wires just above the surface.

Myth or fact?

Myth: All kingfishers eat fish.

Truth: Many don't. India's white-throated kingfisher often hunts insects, lizards and even small snakes far from water, and Australia's kookaburra eats snakes, insects and small mammals. The name comes from the fishing kinds people noticed first.

Try this

Put a coin at the bottom of an empty steel mug and step back until the rim just hides it. Without moving your head, ask someone to slowly pour water into the mug. The coin seems to rise into view. That bending of light is the problem a kingfisher solves every time it dives.
