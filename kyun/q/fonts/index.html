What is a font?

We still say "upper case" and "lower case" because printers once kept capital letters in a wooden case above the case for small letters.

The short answer

A typeface is a design for a set of letters, numbers and symbols. Times New Roman, Arial and Comic Sans are typefaces. Strictly speaking, a font is one version of a typeface: a single size and style, such as 12-point bold. Today most people use the two words to mean the same thing.

Fonts change how words feel. The same sentence can look serious, friendly, old-fashioned or futuristic depending on the letters. Designers choose fonts for books, road signs, apps and food packets to make them easy to read and to give them the right mood. Every one of those letter shapes was drawn by someone.

Surprising but true

When a phone can't find a font for a letter, it shows an empty box. Designers call those boxes "tofu".

Fonts made of metal

Before computers, fonts were real objects. After Johannes Gutenberg developed his printing press in Germany around 1450, every letter was cast as a small block of metal. A complete set of one size and style was called a font, or fount in older British spelling, from a French word for something cast in metal.

Typesetters picked letters out of wooden trays called cases and lined them up by hand, backwards, to make each line. Capitals sat in the upper case and small letters in the lower case. That's where the names come from. Setting one page could take hours.

Feet or no feet

Many typefaces have serifs: small feet or flicks at the ends of the strokes. Look at a capital T in a newspaper and you may see tiny lines at the tips. Serifs go back to letters carved in stone in ancient Rome. Experts still argue about whether the carvers added them for beauty or because of the way their brushes and chisels worked.

Typefaces without these feet are called sans serif, from the French word for "without". They look cleaner and more modern. Many apps and road signs use sans serif fonts because they stay clear at small sizes and from far away.

Famous fonts and their stories

In 1932, The Times newspaper in London asked for a new typeface that was easy to read and saved space. The result was Times New Roman. In 1957, two Swiss designers made Helvetica, a plain, neat font that later appeared on signs, logos and subway maps around the world.

In 1994, Vincent Connare, a designer at Microsoft, saw a cartoon dog in a computer program talking in a stiff, serious font. He drew Comic Sans, based on the lettering in comic books, to look more relaxed. It became hugely popular. It also became one of the most mocked fonts ever, often used where it doesn't fit, such as on serious notices.

Fonts for Indian scripts

Printing in Indian scripts took longer, because letters join and change shape in many ways. In 1778, Charles Wilkins, an Englishman in Bengal, worked with a local craftsman named Panchanan Karmakar to cut the first Bengali printing type. Karmakar went on to cut type for other Indian scripts too.

Digital fonts for Indian scripts are still tricky. A Devanagari or Tamil font needs hundreds of shapes for joined letters and vowel signs. Unicode gives each letter a number, but the font must know how to draw each combination. When it can't, you see empty boxes, nicknamed "tofu". Google named its huge free font family Noto, from "no more tofu", with the aim of covering every script in use.

Choosing the right one

Good designers match the font to the job. A storybook for young children might use round, simple letters. A legal form might use a plain serif font. A horror film poster might use spiky, dripping letters. Text in apps must stay clear on small, bright screens.

Fonts are also linked to the history of writing tools. Styles from typewriters, where every letter took up the same width, still appear in coding programs today. Someone sat down and drew every letter you are reading right now, including this full stop.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Special fonts can cure dyslexia.

Truth: Some fonts are designed to be easier for people with dyslexia, and some readers like them. But careful studies have not found that these fonts make people read much faster or better. Larger text, more space between letters and lines, and good teaching seem to help more.

Try this

Write the same sentence, such as "The cat sat on the mat", in three different styles by hand. Make one tall and thin, one round and bubbly and one with little feet on every letter. Show them to someone and ask what kind of book or sign each one would suit.
