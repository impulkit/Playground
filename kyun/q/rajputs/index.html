Who were the Rajputs?

The walls of Kumbhalgarh fort in Rajasthan stretch for about 36 kilometres over the hills, built by a Rajput king in the 1400s.

The short answer

The Rajputs are a group of clans, or extended families, from north and central India, especially Rajasthan. The word comes from the Sanskrit rajaputra, "son of a king". From about the 7th and 8th centuries, Rajput families ruled kingdoms across the region, including Mewar, Marwar, Amber and Ajmer.

They became famous as warriors and fort-builders. Over the centuries, Rajput kingdoms fought the Delhi Sultans, the Mughals and each other. Many made alliances with the Mughals and served as their generals. Rajput rulers survived into British times as princes, and their forts and palaces still dominate the skyline of Rajasthan.

Surprising but true

Mirabai, one of India's best-loved poet-saints, was a Rajput princess who married into the royal family of Mewar.

Where the clans came from

Historians disagree about Rajput origins. A medieval legend says the four great clans were born from a sacred fire on Mount Abu. In the 1800s, some British writers argued that the Rajputs were descended from Central Asian invaders. Many modern historians think the clans formed from local landholding and warrior families who gained power and claimed ancient royal ancestors.

What is clear is that, by around 1000 CE, families like the Chauhans of Ajmer, the Gahadavalas of Kannauj and the Guhilas of Mewar were ruling kingdoms and building forts and temples.

Prithviraj and Tarain

The most famous early Rajput king is Prithviraj Chauhan, who ruled from Ajmer and Delhi. In 1191 he defeated the invading ruler Muhammad Ghori at the first battle of Tarain, near today's Karnal in Haryana.

Ghori returned the next year, in 1192, and won. Prithviraj was captured and killed. Ghori's general later became the first sultan of Delhi, and the Delhi Sultanate began. Prithviraj's life was retold in a long Hindi poem, the Prithviraj Raso, which mixes history with legend.

Forts on the hills

Rajput kings built their forts on hilltops and ridges. Chittorgarh, in Mewar, covers a plateau about 280 hectares in area. Kumbhalgarh, built by Rana Kumbha in the 1400s, has walls that wind over the hills for about 36 kilometres, among the longest fort walls anywhere after the Great Wall of China.

Chittorgarh was besieged three times, by Alauddin Khilji in 1303, by Bahadur Shah of Gujarat in 1535 and by Akbar in 1568. A famous story says Khilji attacked because he wanted Rani Padmini, the queen of Chittor. It comes from a poem called Padmavat written in 1540, more than two centuries later, and many historians doubt that Padmini was a real person.

Friends and enemies of the Mughals

When the Mughals arrived, the Rajputs had to choose. Rana Sanga of Mewar led a big alliance against the first Mughal emperor, Babur, but lost at Khanwa in 1527.

Akbar took a different path. He married Rajput princesses, gave Rajput kings high ranks and let them keep their lands. Raja Man Singh of Amber became one of his top generals. Mewar held out. In 1576 Maharana Pratap fought Akbar's army, led by Man Singh, at Haldighati. The battle was indecisive, but Pratap kept resisting from the hills for the rest of his life, and is remembered as a hero of independence.

Poets, painters and princes

Rajput courts were also centres of art. Artists painted bright miniatures of Krishna, court life and the seasons. Mirabai, a Rajput princess of the 1500s, gave up palace life to sing songs of love for Krishna that are still sung across India.

Under British rule, Rajput kingdoms became princely states such as Udaipur, Jodhpur and Jaipur. After 1947 they joined India. Today six Rajasthan hill forts, including Chittorgarh and Kumbhalgarh, form a single World Heritage Site.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Rajputs were always united against outside rulers.

Truth: Rajput clans often fought each other, and many made alliances with the Delhi Sultans and later the Mughals. Some, like Mewar, resisted for a long time, while others, like Amber, became close Mughal allies.
