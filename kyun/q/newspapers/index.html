How is a newspaper made?

A morning newspaper is written, checked, designed, printed and delivered to your door in less time than you spend asleep.

The short answer

A newspaper is made in a rush every day. Reporters find and check stories. Editors decide which ones matter most and fix the writing. Designers fit the stories, photos and adverts onto pages. Late at night, the pages are sent to a printing plant.

Giant presses print on rolls of paper, folding and cutting the pages as they go. Before dawn, vans carry bundles to distributors and newsstands. By breakfast, a delivery person has dropped the paper at your door.

Surprising but true

India's first newspaper, Hicky's Bengal Gazette, lasted only about two years. Its owner kept insulting the Governor-General, who had him jailed and his printing type seized.

A day in the newsroom

The day often starts with a meeting where editors plan the next morning's paper. Reporters go out to courts, police stations, government offices, stadiums and neighbourhoods. They interview people, read documents and take notes.

A good reporter checks each important fact, ideally with more than one source. Back in the office, sub-editors (copy editors in the US) tighten the writing, check names and numbers, and write headlines. A headline has to tell a story in a few words and fit into a fixed space.

As the deadline gets close, things move fast. If big news breaks late, a page may be redone at the last minute. The front page is usually the last to be finished.

Rolls, plates and speed

Once the pages are designed, each one is turned into a thin metal plate. Newspaper presses are huge machines, often several storeys tall. They use rolls of paper, called reels, that can weigh around a tonne each and unroll for kilometres.

The paper races through rollers that print both sides at once, in four ink colours: cyan, magenta, yellow and black. Further down the line, machines cut, fold and stack the papers. A big press can print tens of thousands of copies in an hour.

Newspaper paper, called newsprint, is thin and cheap, made from wood pulp or recycled paper. That keeps the price low enough to be thrown away tomorrow.

News before printing

In ancient Rome, from 59 BCE, the government posted handwritten notices called the Acta Diurna, "daily acts", in public places. China's imperial court sent out bulletins to officials for many centuries.

After the printing press came to Europe, printed news sheets followed. The World Association of News Publishers recognises Relation, printed in Strasbourg from 1605, as the first newspaper. In America, the Boston News-Letter began in 1704.

Newspapers in India

India's first newspaper, Hicky's Bengal Gazette, was started in Calcutta in 1780 by an Irishman, James Augustus Hicky. It was full of gossip and attacks on the officials of the East India Company, especially the Governor-General, Warren Hastings. In 1782 Hicky's printing press was seized and the paper closed.

Dozens of papers in Indian languages followed in the 1800s. During the freedom struggle, newspapers became powerful. Bal Gangadhar Tilak published Kesari in Marathi, and Mahatma Gandhi edited Young India and Harijan. The British sometimes shut papers down or jailed editors for what they printed.

Still worth printing

Today, most people read news on phones and websites, which are updated all day. Many papers sell fewer printed copies than they used to. In India, though, printed newspapers have stayed strong compared with many countries, and papers appear in dozens of languages.

The basic job hasn't changed. Find out what happened, check it, explain it clearly and make it news people can trust. A printed page can't be quietly edited later, which is one reason some readers still prefer it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If it's printed in a newspaper, it must be true.

Truth: Good newspapers check facts carefully and print corrections when they get things wrong, but mistakes still happen. Some papers also take sides. Reading more than one source is the best way to get close to the truth.

Try this

Make a one-page family newspaper. Interview two people at home about something that happened this week, write a short report with a headline, and add a weather forecast and a drawing. Before you "print" it, ask someone to check your facts, just like an editor would.
