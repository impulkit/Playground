What does your skin do?

Your skin is your largest organ, and you shed tens of thousands of its cells every minute without noticing.

The short answer

Your skin is a waterproof, stretchy coat that keeps your insides in and the outside world out. It blocks most germs, stops you drying out, and protects you from some of the Sun's harmful rays.

It also works as a thermostat and a sense organ. It sweats to cool you down, and it's packed with nerve endings that feel touch, heat, cold and pain.

Spread flat, an adult's skin would cover about 1.5 to 2 square metres, roughly the size of a single bed. That makes it the largest organ in your body.

Surprising but true

People of every skin colour have about the same number of colour-making cells. The difference is how much colour those cells make.

Three layers

The top layer, the epidermis, is thinner than a sheet of paper in most places. Its outer surface is made of flat, dead cells packed with a tough protein. They form a protective shield and flake away constantly. Fresh cells are made at the bottom of the epidermis and work their way up, so the outer layer is replaced roughly every month.

Below is the dermis. It holds blood vessels, nerve endings, hair roots and sweat glands. Its stretchy fibres let skin spring back when you pinch it. As people grow very old, these fibres weaken, which is one reason skin gets wrinkles.

At the bottom is a layer of fat that keeps you warm and cushions you when you fall.

Thick and thin

Skin is thickest on the soles of your feet and the palms of your hands, which take the most rubbing. It's thinnest on your eyelids. People who often walk barefoot grow even thicker soles for protection, a bit like a natural pair of chappals.

Your palms and soles also have ridges, which give you grip. On your fingertips they form your fingerprints, and no two people's are the same, not even identical twins'.

Keeping cool, keeping warm

On a hot day, your skin releases sweat. As it evaporates, it carries heat away. Blood vessels in the skin also widen, so more warm blood flows near the surface to lose heat, which is why your face goes red after running.

When you're cold, those blood vessels narrow to keep heat inside. Tiny muscles pull your hairs upright, giving you goosebumps.

The colour of skin

Skin colour comes mostly from melanin, a dark pigment made by special cells. Everyone has about the same number of these cells. People with darker skin have cells that make more melanin, or a darker kind of it.

Melanin works as a natural sunshade against ultraviolet light from the Sun. Scientists such as the American anthropologist Nina Jablonski think darker skin evolved where sunlight is strongest, near the equator, and lighter skin where it's weaker. Lighter skin may help people make enough vitamin D in places with little sun, because skin uses sunlight to make it. Tanning is your skin making more melanin after time in the sun.

An octopus goes much further. Its skin has colour sacs it can open and close in a fraction of a second.

Healing itself

When you cut yourself, your skin starts repairing right away. Blood clots seal the cut, and a scab forms on top like a temporary bandage. Underneath, new cells crawl in from the edges to close the gap. Small cuts often heal in about a week. Keeping a cut clean helps it heal well, and a doctor or trusted adult can check one that looks red, swollen or sore.

Myth or fact?

Myth: People with dark skin can't get sunburnt.

Truth: Darker skin has more melanin, which blocks more sunlight, so it burns less easily. But anyone can get sunburnt with enough strong sun. Shade, a hat and sunscreen help everyone.

Try this

Press a piece of clear sticky tape onto the back of your hand, peel it off and stick it onto dark paper. Look at it with a magnifying glass. The faint flakes you see are dead skin cells from the very top layer of your skin.
