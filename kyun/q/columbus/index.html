What did Columbus really find?

Christopher Columbus set out to reach India and China. He died still believing he had reached Asia.

The short answer

Christopher Columbus was a sailor from Genoa, in Italy. In 1492, sailing for the king and queen of Spain, he crossed the Atlantic Ocean, hoping to reach Asia by going west.

Instead, he reached islands in the Caribbean, landing first in the Bahamas on 12 October 1492. He went on to Cuba and Hispaniola, the island now shared by Haiti and the Dominican Republic. He made four voyages in all, and to the end of his life he insisted these lands were part of Asia. What he had actually reached were the Americas, two huge continents that Europeans didn't know about, though millions of people already lived there. His voyages connected the two halves of the world for good.

Surprising but true

Columbus thought Japan lay only about 4,400 km west of the Canary Islands. The real distance is closer to 20,000 km.

A sailor with a wrong number

Educated Europeans knew the Earth was round. The question was how big it was. Columbus used low estimates for the size of the Earth and high estimates for the width of Asia.

He concluded Japan was only about 4,400 km west of the Canary Islands. The real distance is more than four times that. Experts told the Spanish court his numbers were wrong, and they were right. If the Americas hadn't been in the way, his crew would have run out of food and water long before reaching Asia.

Three ships and a long wait

On 3 August 1492, Columbus left the port of Palos in Spain with three ships, the Niña, the Pinta and the Santa María, and about 90 men. The flagship, the Santa María, was probably about 18 metres long, shorter than a cricket pitch.

After a stop in the Canary Islands, they sailed west for more than a month without seeing land. The crew grew anxious. On 12 October, a lookout spotted an island the Taíno people called Guanahani. Columbus named it San Salvador and claimed it for Spain.

The people who were already there

The Taíno welcomed the strangers and traded with them. Columbus wrote that they would make good servants. That attitude shaped what followed.

As governor of Hispaniola, he forced Taíno people to search for gold and punished them harshly. Some were enslaved and shipped to Spain. Diseases from Europe, such as smallpox, killed huge numbers of people who had never met them before. Within a few decades, the Taíno population of Hispaniola had collapsed. Even the Spanish rulers grew unhappy with his rule, and in 1500 he was arrested and sent home in chains.

Swapping the world's plants and animals

Columbus's voyages started what historians call the Columbian Exchange. Maize, potatoes, tomatoes and chillies travelled from the Americas to Europe, Africa and Asia. Horses, cows, wheat and sugar cane went the other way.

That is why Indian food today is full of American plants. Before the 1500s, there were no chillies in Indian curries and no potatoes in samosas. Chillies probably reached India with Portuguese traders in the 1500s.

Remembering Columbus

The continents were named after another Italian, Amerigo Vespucci, who argued that they were a "New World". In 1498, the Portuguese explorer Vasco da Gama reached the real India, landing near Calicut in Kerala, by sailing around Africa.

In the USA, Columbus Day has been marked nationally since 1937. Many Americans, especially Italian Americans, see it as a celebration of courage and heritage. Others, including many Native Americans, see it as honouring the start of conquest, and some states and cities now mark Indigenous Peoples' Day instead or as well.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In Columbus's time, most educated people thought the Earth was flat.

Truth: Educated Europeans had known the Earth was round for about 2,000 years. The argument was about its size. Columbus thought it was much smaller than it is, and his critics were right.
