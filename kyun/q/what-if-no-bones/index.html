What if you had no bones?

Without a skeleton, you'd slump into a soft heap on the floor. Yet an octopus with no bones at all can squeeze through a gap the size of a coin.

The short answer

Without bones, your body couldn't hold its shape. Muscles need something firm to pull against, so you couldn't stand, walk or even sit up. You would slump into a soft heap, a bit like a jellyfish washed up on a beach.

You'd also lose protection for your brain, heart and lungs. And you'd lose a hidden blood factory, because the soft marrow inside many bones makes your blood cells. But nature shows there are other ways to build a body. Octopuses, worms and jellyfish have no bones at all, and do just fine.

Surprising but true

Your bones are factories. Their soft centres make about 2 million new red blood cells every second.

Your skeleton's day job

An adult has 206 bones. Babies are born with around 270 to 300, but many fuse together as they grow. The biggest is the thigh bone, or femur. The smallest is the stapes in your ear, about 3 mm long, smaller than a grain of rice.

Bones do three big jobs. They hold you up, like the steel frame inside a tall building. They protect soft parts: your skull guards your brain and your ribs form a cage around your heart and lungs. And they act as levers for muscles, which pull on them to move you.

A hidden factory

Inside many bones is soft, jelly-like marrow. It is busy. Every second, it makes about 2 million red blood cells, plus white blood cells that fight germs and platelets that help cuts heal.

Bones are also your body's calcium bank. About 99 per cent of your calcium is stored in your bones and teeth. When your blood needs more, for your heart to beat properly, the body takes some from your bones.

Without bones, you'd need another blood factory and another calcium store. Your body has neither.

The bendy octopus

An octopus has no bones. Its only hard part is a beak, like a parrot's, near the centre of its arms. That means it can pour itself through any gap bigger than its beak, sometimes as small as a coin.

Octopuses are famous for escaping from aquarium tanks. One octopus in New Zealand reportedly slipped through a gap in its tank, slid across the floor and escaped down a drainpipe to the sea. Its arms are made of muscles working against each other, and it can stiffen any part of an arm to make a temporary "elbow" wherever it likes.

Bodies built from water

Earthworms use a different trick. Their bodies are filled with fluid, divided into many segments. Squeezing muscles in one direction makes a segment long and thin; squeezing the other way makes it short and fat. Waves of this push the worm through soil. Scientists call this a hydrostatic skeleton, a skeleton made of water pressure.

You have some boneless parts that work this way too. Your tongue is a bundle of muscles with no bones, which is why it can curl, flatten and point. So is an elephant's trunk, which has many thousands of muscle bundles and not a single bone.

Sharks: bones made of something else

Sharks and rays don't have bones either. Their skeletons are made of cartilage, the bendy material in the tip of your nose and your ears. It is lighter than bone. Together with a big oily liver, that helps sharks stay afloat without the air-filled swim bladder most fish use.

Your own skeleton started as cartilage before you were born. Most of it slowly hardened into bone, a process that continues until you're about 25, as you'll find out in how bones grow.

When bones get too little use

Bones grow stronger when you use them. Running, jumping and carrying things make them thicker. Without that, they weaken.

Astronauts in space float, so their legs and spines don't carry weight. Their hips and spines can lose about 1 per cent of their bone mass every month. That's why astronauts on the space station exercise for around two hours a day.

Back on Earth, they can need months of exercise to rebuild. Bones turn out to be one of many surprises in what space does to the body, along with puffy faces and growing a little taller.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Bones are dry and dead, like sticks.

Truth: Living bones are wet, full of blood vessels and nerves, and constantly being rebuilt. Your skeleton replaces much of itself every ten years or so.

Try this

Fill a balloon with water and tie it. Try to make it stand up on its end. Then wrap it tightly in a few rubber bands and try again. Animals without bones, like worms, use pressure and muscle squeezing a body full of fluid in much the same way.
