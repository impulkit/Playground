Who is Viswanathan Anand?

When he became India's first chess grandmaster in 1988, the country had exactly one. By 2024 it had more than 80.

The short answer

Viswanathan Anand is a chess player from Chennai and one of the strongest players in history. In 1988 he became India's first grandmaster, the highest title in chess. He was world champion in 2000 and again from 2007 to 2013, winning the title in a knockout event, a tournament and three one-to-one matches.

Before Anand, chess in India was mostly a hobby. After him, it became a sport that children could dream about. India now has dozens of grandmasters, and many say they started playing because of "Vishy".

Surprising but true

In 2010 a volcano in Iceland shut Europe's airports, so Anand travelled about 40 hours by road from Germany to Bulgaria to defend his world title. He still won.

A quick young player

Anand learnt chess from his mother, Sushila, when he was about six. As a boy he lived for a time in Manila in the Philippines, where his father worked, and he and his mother solved chess puzzles from a TV programme together.

Back in Chennai he played at the Tal Chess Club and became famous for moving very fast. Opponents would still be thinking when he had already played. People called him the "Lightning Kid". In 1987 he became the first Indian to win the World Junior Championship. A year later he was a grandmaster.

World champion

Anand first became world champion in 2000, winning a knockout event that ended in Tehran. In 2007 he won the world championship tournament in Mexico City. Then he proved himself in head-to-head matches, beating Vladimir Kramnik of Russia in 2008, Veselin Topalov of Bulgaria in 2010 and Boris Gelfand of Israel in 2012.

He was known for deep preparation. His team would study openings, the first moves of a game, for months, and surprise opponents with new ideas. Anand was also one of the first top players to use computers heavily in his preparation. Chess programs could check his ideas for mistakes far faster than any human helper.

He is also known as one of the friendliest champions. In a sport where rivals sometimes refuse to shake hands, Anand stayed polite and calm, even after painful defeats.

The volcano journey

In April 2010, Anand was in Germany, about to fly to Sofia, Bulgaria, for his match against Topalov. Then the Eyjafjallajökull volcano in Iceland erupted. Its ash cloud closed airports across Europe.

Anand and his team set off by road, a journey of about 40 hours through several countries. He asked for a short delay, but the organisers allowed only one day. He lost the first game, tired from the trip, but won the match.

Losing to Carlsen

In 2013 Anand defended his title in his home city, Chennai, against the young Norwegian Magnus Carlsen. Carlsen won clearly and became champion. Anand challenged him again in 2014 and lost again. He said later that losing to Carlsen was painful, but that the younger man was simply better at that time.

Anand kept playing at the top level for years and won the World Rapid Championship in 2017, aged 48.

The Anand effect

India's chess boom is often called the Anand effect. India had just one grandmaster in 1988 and more than 80 by 2024. In December 2024 Gukesh Dommaraju of Chennai, then 18, became the youngest undisputed world champion in history. Anand had helped train him at his academy.

The same year India won both the open and women's gold medals at the Chess Olympiad in Budapest. Anand himself has become a coach, commentator and an official of FIDE, the world chess federation. He received the Padma Vibhushan, India's second-highest civilian award, in 2007.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Chess champions think through every possible move before they play.

Truth: Nobody can. There are more possible chess games than atoms in the known universe. Strong players recognise patterns from experience and look deeply only at a few promising moves.

Try this

Set up a chess or draughts board and play a game where each player has only ten seconds per move. Count aloud for each other. Then play a slow game with no time limit. Notice how speed changes the way you think. Anand was famous for playing well even when moving very fast.
