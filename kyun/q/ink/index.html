How is ink made?

The purple mark painted on your finger when you vote in India is made from a chemical that reacts with your skin and won't wash off for days.

The short answer

Ink is a coloured liquid or paste made of two main things. The first is a colouring: tiny solid particles, called pigments, or a dye that dissolves. The second is a liquid to carry it, often with a binder, like gum or glue, that helps it stick to the page.

The oldest inks were simple. Soot from lamps or fires was mixed with water and gum. Later people used plants, minerals, insects and metals. Indigo plants gave blue, and crushed lac insects gave red. Mixing new inks was a craft, and many scribes guarded their recipes. Today's inks are made in factories, with recipes for pens, printers, newspapers and even voting.

Surprising but true

For about 1,000 years, much of Europe wrote with ink made from oak galls, round lumps that grow on oak trees where wasps lay their eggs.

Black from soot

For thousands of years, the most common ink was carbon black. Burn oil or wood with too little air, and you get fine black soot. Mix that soot with water and a binder, and you have ink that stays black for centuries. Egyptian scribes used it on papyrus about 4,500 years ago.

In China, makers mixed soot from burnt pine wood or oil with animal glue and pressed it into hard ink sticks. A writer ground the stick with water on a stone dish called an inkstone. Ink sticks are still used for calligraphy and brush painting.

In India, scribes made an ink called masi from lampblack and other ingredients. Writers on palm leaves often scratched letters with a stylus and rubbed soot into the lines afterwards.

Ink from oak galls

Oak galls are round, nut-like lumps that grow on oak trees when certain wasps lay their eggs in the buds. Galls are full of a substance called tannin. When crushed galls are mixed with iron salts, a dark ink forms that soaks into paper or parchment.

This iron gall ink was used in Europe for well over 1,000 years. Leonardo da Vinci and Johann Sebastian Bach wrote with it. Many old documents in the Library of Congress are written in it. Over time it can eat into paper, so some old manuscripts now have holes where the letters were. Libraries use special methods to protect them.

The ink that proves you voted

In India, every voter's left forefinger is marked with indelible ink, meaning ink that can't be removed. It contains silver nitrate, which reacts with the skin and darkens in light. The mark stays for days or weeks, which helps stop anyone voting twice.

Since 1962, this ink has been made by one company, Mysore Paints and Varnish Limited, in Mysuru, Karnataka. It is also sent to many other countries for their elections.

Inks today

A ballpoint pen uses thick, oily ink that doesn't drip. A tiny ball at the tip rolls it onto the paper. Gel pens use a jelly-like ink with pigments. Felt-tip markers use runny ink that soaks along fibres.

Printers use different inks again. Newspaper presses use cheap, fast-drying oily inks in four colours: cyan, magenta, yellow and black. Together, these make almost any colour as tiny dots. The same four-colour idea is used in your home inkjet printer.

Animals make ink too. Octopuses and squid squirt a dark cloud of ink to confuse predators. Cuttlefish ink was once used by artists, and its brown colour is called sepia. Many old photographs have a brownish sepia tone, though that colour comes from chemicals, not from cuttlefish.

Myth or fact?

Myth: India ink comes from India.

Truth: The black ink sold as India ink was mostly made in China, and is also called Chinese ink. The English name may come from trade routes through India, or from Indian carbon inks, but nobody is certain.

Try this

Do ink chromatography. Draw a thick dot with a washable black felt-tip pen about 2 cm from the bottom of a strip of kitchen paper. Dip the bottom edge in a little water, keeping the dot above the waterline, and watch. The black splits into hidden colours as the water climbs.
