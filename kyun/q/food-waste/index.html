How much food do we waste?

About one fifth of the food that reaches shops, restaurants and homes is thrown away, much of it straight from home kitchens.

The short answer

A lot. The United Nations estimates that in 2022 about 1 billion tonnes of food were wasted by shops, restaurants and homes around the world. That is roughly one fifth of all the food available to shoppers. On top of that, more food is lost on farms, in storage and on the road before it ever reaches a market.

Most of the waste happens at home. On average, each person in the world wastes about 79 kg of food at home every year, about the weight of a grown-up man. Meanwhile, hundreds of millions of people do not get enough to eat.

Surprising but true

If food waste were a country, it would be one of the biggest producers of greenhouse gases on Earth.

Where food goes missing

Waste happens at every step of the food's journey. On farms, fruit may be left unpicked if prices are too low. On the road, tomatoes and bananas bruise and rot, especially in hot countries without cold storage. That is why better cold chains matter.

In shops, food is thrown away when it passes its date or looks less than perfect. In homes, people cook too much, forget leftovers at the back of the fridge, or throw away food that was still fine to eat.

Rich and poor countries

People once assumed food waste at home was mostly a problem in rich countries. The UN's newer data shows that is not true. Homes in middle-income countries, including India, waste similar amounts per person to homes in the United States and Europe.

What differs is where the rest is lost. In poorer places, more food spoils before it reaches the market. In richer places, more is wasted by shops and shoppers. In the US, confusion over labels like "best before" and "use by" leads many people to throw out perfectly safe food.

Why it warms the planet

Wasting food wastes everything used to grow it: water, land, fuel and fertiliser. Then, in a landfill, buried food rots without air and gives off methane, a strong greenhouse gas.

The UN estimates that food loss and waste together cause around 8–10% of all the greenhouse gases humans produce. That makes cutting food waste one of the simplest ways to slow climate change.

Feeding people, feeding soil

Some cities have found clever fixes. Food banks in the United States collect unsold food from shops and give it to families who need it. In India, many gurdwaras and temples run community kitchens that feed thousands of people a day, planning carefully so little is left over.

Food that really cannot be eaten can still be useful. Composting turns peels and scraps into rich soil, with help from earthworms and microbes.

Ugly but tasty

A surprising amount of food is thrown away just for its looks. Supermarkets in many countries reject carrots that are bent, apples with small marks and potatoes that are oddly shaped, even though they taste exactly the same. Some of this produce never leaves the farm.

A few shops in Europe and the United States now sell "wonky" fruit and vegetables at lower prices. In Indian markets, the sabziwala often sells slightly bruised tomatoes cheaply in the evening, so cooks can turn them into chutney or curry that night.

Countries have agreed on a goal to halve food waste in shops and homes by 2030. Most of the fixes are small. Buying only what you need, serving smaller portions, storing food properly and eating leftovers all help.

Grandparents in many Indian homes already knew these tricks. Yesterday's rice became lemon rice, stale rotis became a crunchy snack, and vegetable peels went to the cow at the gate.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Food thrown in a landfill is harmless because it is natural and just rots away.

Truth: Buried under rubbish with no air, rotting food gives off methane, a gas that traps heat far more strongly than carbon dioxide. Composting with air, or feeding scraps to animals, is much better.

Try this

For one week, scrape every bit of leftover food in your home into one bowl or bag after meals, and weigh it each night on a kitchen scale. At the end of the week, add up the total. Then talk with your family about one easy change, like smaller servings.
