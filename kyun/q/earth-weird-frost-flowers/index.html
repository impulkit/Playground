What are frost flowers?

On the first freezing mornings of autumn, some dead plant stems in the USA burst open and push out curling ribbons of ice that look like white petals.

The short answer

A frost flower is a delicate sculpture of ice that grows out of a plant's stem on a very cold morning. It can look like a tiny white rose, a curl of cotton candy or a ribbon of tissue paper. Some are no bigger than a coin. Others stand as tall as a coffee mug.

They form when the air drops below freezing but the soil is still warm enough to stay liquid. The plant's roots keep pulling up water. When that water reaches the frozen stem, it squeezes out through hair-thin cracks and freezes the moment it meets the air. More water pushes the ice outwards, layer by layer. By the time the Sun climbs high, the flower has melted away.

Surprising but true

The ice petals of a frost flower grow from the bottom, as the plant pushes out water through cracks in its stem, like toothpaste squeezed from a tube.

The plants that grow them

Only some plants do it. In the eastern and central USA, the champion is white crownbeard, a tall weed that farmers call frostweed for exactly this reason. Yellow ironweed and a small mint called dittany are also famous for it.

What these plants share is a stem that stays full of water after the leaves have died. The first hard frost splits the outer stem lengthwise. From then on, each cold night can squeeze out a new flower, though the stem gets more battered each time. Most plants manage only a handful before the ground freezes solid and the water supply stops.

Ice that grows like toothpaste

Normal frost grows from water vapour in the air, which turns directly into spiky ice crystals on cold surfaces. Frost flowers are different. They are built from liquid water pushed out from below.

Water expands as it freezes, which is also why ice floats. In the stem, freezing water forces the cracks wider, and the thin sheet of new ice gets shoved outwards by the water behind it. Each slit makes its own ribbon. Because the slits are slightly curved, the ribbons curl, sometimes into perfect spirals with fine lines running along them like the grain in wood.

Hair ice: grown by a fungus

In the forests of Europe, people sometimes find dead branches sprouting what looks like silky white hair. This is hair ice. Each strand is about a hundredth of a millimetre thick, much finer than a human hair, and can be up to 20 cm long.

In 1918, Alfred Wegener, the scientist famous for the idea of drifting continents, guessed that a fungus was involved. Nearly a hundred years later, in 2015, scientists in Germany and Switzerland proved him right. A fungus living in the wood, called Exidiopsis effusa, shapes the ice. Scrape the fungus away, or kill it, and the wood grows only a lumpy crust. Somehow, a living fungus sculpts strands of ice finer than cotton thread.

Flowers on the sea

There is a third kind, far from any garden. On calm, freezing nights in the Arctic and Antarctic, new sea ice can sprout fields of frost flowers a few centimetres high. They grow from vapour rising off the thin ice and are about three times saltier than seawater.

Scientists who studied them found the salty petals crowded with bacteria, far more than in the seawater underneath. A fresh field of sea frost flowers can stretch to the horizon, glittering like spilled salt.

A short-lived show

Frost flowers are hard to photograph because they barely last. Sunlight or a breath of warm wind turns them back to water within an hour or two. Some nature lovers walk the same patch of woodland every November, hoping for one perfect morning. And even the toughest frost flower is softer than a single grain of hail, which can fall as hard as a stone.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Frost flowers are just ordinary frost that has settled on a plant.

Truth: Ordinary frost grows from water vapour in the air. Frost flowers come from liquid water inside the plant, which is pushed out and freezes in sheets. That is why they curl outwards from the stem instead of coating it.

Try this

Watch ice grow from below. Fill a plastic tray with damp sand, push a drinking straw in so it stands upright, and put the tray in the freezer overnight. Next morning, look at the top of the straw and the sand around it with a magnifying glass. Draw any needles or crusts of ice you see and notice which way they point.
