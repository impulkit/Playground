Who wrote the first computer program?

In 1843, a young Englishwoman wrote step-by-step instructions for a computer that had never been built, and guessed it might one day make music.

The short answer

Ada Lovelace was an English mathematician who lived from 1815 to 1852. In 1843 she published a set of notes about the Analytical Engine, a mechanical computer designed by her friend Charles Babbage.

One of those notes set out, step by step, how the machine could calculate a difficult series of numbers. Because it is a precise set of instructions for a computer, many people call it the first published computer program, and Lovelace the first programmer.

Historians still argue about how much of it was her own work. But her notes went further than Babbage's in one big way: she saw that a computer could work with more than numbers.

Surprising but true

Ada Lovelace's father was the famous poet Lord Byron, but she never knew him. Her mother had her tutored in maths, partly to steer her away from poetry.

A poet's daughter who loved maths

Ada was the only legitimate child of the poet Lord Byron. Her parents separated when she was a baby, and she never saw her father again. Her mother, Annabella Milbanke, was a keen mathematician and insisted that Ada study maths and science.

As a child, Ada designed a flying machine and studied birds' wings to work out how it might fly. She was often ill, but she kept learning. At 17, she met Charles Babbage at a party in London and saw a working part of his Difference Engine. She was gripped by it, and they became lifelong friends.

The famous notes

In 1842, an Italian engineer, Luigi Menabrea, wrote an article in French about Babbage's Analytical Engine. Lovelace translated it into English. At Babbage's suggestion, she added her own notes, labelled A to G. They ended up about three times longer than the article.

Note G includes a table showing how the engine could calculate Bernoulli numbers, a series used in higher maths. It sets out each operation and which parts of the engine would hold each number. It is an algorithm written for a machine.

Babbage had sketched some smaller programs before. Some historians think he deserves more credit for Note G; others say the notes show Lovelace's deep understanding. The argument continues.

Beyond numbers

What stands out most is Lovelace's imagination. She wrote that the engine might work on things other than numbers, as long as they could be expressed as symbols. Musical notes, for example. She suggested it might one day compose elaborate pieces of music.

That is exactly what computers do now: they turn words, pictures and sounds into numbers and work with them. Lovelace called her way of thinking "poetical science", blending imagination and logic, perhaps a nod to the poetry of the father she never knew.

Lovelace's warning

She also wrote that the engine could only do what it was told. It could not, she said, come up with anything new by itself.

About a hundred years later, the computer pioneer Alan Turing discussed this idea in a famous 1950 paper on whether machines could think. He called it "Lady Lovelace's objection" and argued against it. People still debate the question today, as computers write stories and draw pictures.

Remembered at last

Lovelace died of cancer in 1852, aged 36, and was buried next to her father. For about a century, her notes were mostly forgotten.

When electronic computers arrived in the 1940s and 1950s, people rediscovered her work. In 1980, the US Department of Defense named a programming language Ada in her honour. Since 2009, Ada Lovelace Day, held each October, has celebrated women in science, technology, engineering and maths.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ada Lovelace built the first computer.

Truth: She never built a machine. She wrote about Charles Babbage's Analytical Engine, which was designed but never built, and explained how it could be programmed and what it might do.
