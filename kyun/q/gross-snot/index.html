Why do we have snot?

You swallow snot all day long without noticing, and that's exactly how your body wants it.

The short answer

Snot is your nose's dirt trap. Its proper name is mucus, a slippery, sticky layer that lines the inside of your nose. Every breath carries dust, pollen, smoke and germs. Mucus catches them before they can reach your lungs.

It also does another job. Air entering your nose can be cold and dry, while your lungs like air that's warm and damp. Mucus helps moisten each breath on its way in. Your body makes snot every day, even when you're healthy. You only notice it when there's extra, like during a cold.

Surprising but true

Alexander Fleming discovered one of the body's natural germ-killers in 1921 by dripping his own snot onto a dish of bacteria while he had a cold.

A conveyor belt in your nose

The inside of your nose is covered in microscopic hairs called cilia. They wave back and forth many times a second, in a steady rhythm, like a crowd doing a stadium wave. Together they push the layer of mucus slowly backwards towards your throat.

There, you swallow it, usually without noticing. It sounds gross, but it's clever. Your stomach is full of strong acid that destroys most of the germs caught in the mucus. The whole layer in your nose is replaced every 10 to 20 minutes or so.

Boogers are simply bits of mucus near your nostrils that dried out before the cilia could move them, full of trapped dust.

Snot fights back

Mucus is mostly water, mixed with sticky proteins called mucins that make it gloopy. It also carries antibodies and enzymes that attack germs, part of your immune system.

One of those enzymes was found by accident. In 1921 the Scottish scientist Alexander Fleming, who later discovered penicillin, had a cold. He dripped some of his own nasal mucus onto a dish of bacteria and noticed the bacteria near it died. He'd found lysozyme, a natural germ-killer that is also in tears and saliva.

Why it changes colour and flow

When germs get in, your body sends extra mucus to wash them out. That's why your nose runs during a cold. White blood cells arrive to fight, and they contain a green-tinted chemical that can turn mucus yellow or green. It's a sign of your body working, not proof of a dangerous infection.

Cold weather can make your nose drip even when you're well. Your nose adds moisture to freezing air, and some of that water condenses at the tip, like drops on a cold glass. Spicy food and crying make your nose run too.

To pick or not to pick

Almost everyone picks their nose sometimes. It isn't dangerous, but fingers carry germs into the nose and out again, and picking can scratch the delicate lining and cause nosebleeds. Blowing gently into a tissue, one nostril at a time, and then washing your hands is the cleaner choice.

Some people claim that eating boogers trains the immune system, but there's no good evidence for it. What scientists do know is that snot is being made all the time. Even when you feel completely fine, a fresh layer is sliding towards your throat as you read this.

Snot elsewhere

Your body makes mucus in other places as well. It lines your lungs, your stomach and your gut, where a thick layer stops your own stomach acid from digesting you. Plenty of animals depend on slime too. Snails glide on a carpet of it.

If you ever have a runny nose that lasts a long time, a fever, or pain around your eyes and cheeks, tell a parent or see a doctor. And the strangest part? A dog's nose is wet partly because of mucus, which catches smell particles. It helps give dogs a sense of smell thousands of times better than ours, as you'll find in the story of the dog's nose.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Green snot means you have a bacterial infection and need antibiotics.

Truth: Snot often turns yellow or green during an ordinary cold, which is caused by a virus. The colour comes from chemicals in white blood cells that rush to fight. Antibiotics don't work on viruses, so a doctor decides based on other signs.

Try this

See how a sticky layer traps dirt. Smear a thin layer of honey or oil on one plastic plate and leave a second plate clean. Sprinkle a pinch of pepper or flour over both, then tip them upright. The sticky plate holds on to the "dust" while the clean one lets it slide off. Your nose works the same way.
