Who were the Kushans?

A family of herders from the edge of China ended up ruling from Afghanistan to the Ganga, and put the Buddha on their gold coins.

The short answer

The Kushans were a ruling family descended from the Yuezhi, a nomadic people who herded horses in north-western China. Pushed west by enemies in the 2nd century BCE, the Yuezhi settled in Bactria, in today's northern Afghanistan. One of their clans, the Kushans, united the others.

By the 1st and 2nd centuries CE, Kushan kings ruled a huge stretch of land from Central Asia across Afghanistan and Pakistan to Mathura and beyond on the Ganga plains. They grew rich by sitting astride the Silk Road, the web of routes linking China, India, Persia and Rome. Their greatest king, Kanishka, is remembered as a patron of Buddhism and art.

Surprising but true

One of the earliest images of the Buddha with a name label appears on a gold coin of the Kushan king Kanishka, spelled in Greek letters as BODDO.

From the steppe to the Hindu Kush

Chinese historians tell the early story. The Yuezhi lost a war with the Xiongnu, another nomadic people, and moved thousands of kilometres west with their herds. A Chinese envoy named Zhang Qian visited them in Bactria around 128 BCE, hoping to make an alliance. They politely refused. They had settled down and liked their new home.

In the 1st century CE, a chief called Kujula Kadphises joined the five Yuezhi clans into one kingdom. His successors pushed south over the Hindu Kush into the plains of the Indus and the Ganga.

Kanishka, king of kings

Kanishka is the most famous Kushan ruler. His main capital was Purushapura, today's Peshawar, and Mathura was another great centre. A stone inscription found at Rabatak in Afghanistan in 1993 lists his ancestors and names cities he controlled in northern India.

Buddhist tradition says Kanishka called a great Buddhist council in Kashmir and built an enormous stupa at Peshawar. Chinese pilgrims centuries later described that stupa as one of the tallest buildings they had seen. At Mathura, a life-size stone statue of Kanishka stands in the museum. Its head is missing, but his heavy boots and long coat show he dressed like a rider from the steppe, not like a local king.

Coins in many languages

Kushan coins are one of the best sources about them. The kings issued plentiful gold coins, perhaps using gold that came in through trade with Rome. The coins use Greek letters to write Bactrian, an Iranian language, and some carry Indian scripts too.

On the back, they show a crowd of gods: Greek, Iranian and Indian. A coin of Kanishka shows a standing figure labelled BODDO, the Buddha. It was a clever message from rulers of many peoples. Merchants from Rome to China would handle these coins and see a powerful, welcoming empire.

Buddha gets a face

Under the Kushans, two great schools of sculpture grew up. In Gandhara, around Peshawar and Taxila, artists carved the Buddha in grey stone with wavy hair and heavy robes in folds, showing the influence of Greek styles that had lingered since Alexander. At Mathura, artists worked in spotted red sandstone and gave the Buddha a rounder, more Indian look.

Until about this time, artists had shown the Buddha only through symbols. Kushan-era sculptors made the human image of the Buddha common. Their ideas travelled along the Silk Road to Central Asia, China, Korea and Japan.

How the empire faded

In the 3rd century CE, the new Sasanian rulers of Persia took the western Kushan lands. Smaller Kushan kings held on in the Punjab for a while, but the empire broke apart. In northern India, the Guptas later rose in the space they left. The Kushans' stone Buddhas, scattered across museums from Mathura to Kabul to London, are the clearest trace of the riders who once crossed half of Asia.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Buddha has always been shown as a statue.

Truth: For several centuries after his death, artists showed the Buddha only through symbols, such as footprints, an empty throne or a tree. Human images of him became common in the Kushan period, in Gandhara and Mathura.
