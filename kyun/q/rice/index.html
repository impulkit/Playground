How does rice feed half the world?

More than half the people on Earth eat rice almost every day, and most of it grows in fields flooded like shallow lakes.

The short answer

Rice is the seed of a grass. Each plant makes a few hundred grains, and a single hectare of good paddy, about the size of a football field and a half, can produce several tonnes. It keeps for months once dried, cooks quickly and fills you up.

That is why rice became the main food for more than half the people on Earth, especially in Asia, where about 90% of it is grown and eaten. India and China grow the most. In the 2024–25 season India overtook China as the biggest grower, and India also sells more rice to other countries than anyone else.

Surprising but true

Rice plants have tiny air tubes in their stems that carry oxygen down to the roots, which is why they can stand in water that would drown most crops.

From wild grass to dinner plate

People in the Yangtze River valley in China began farming rice about 9,000 years ago. Over thousands of years they chose plants whose grains did not fall off before harvest. Rice reached India early, and Indian farmers grew their own kinds in the wet Ganga plains.

In West Africa, people tamed a separate species of rice on their own, about 3,000 years ago. Enslaved Africans later brought their rice-growing skills to South Carolina in the USA, where rice made plantation owners rich. Today the US state of Arkansas grows about half of America's rice.

Why the fields are flooded

Most crops die if their roots sit in water, because roots need oxygen. Rice has a trick: spongy air channels run down its stems, like straws carrying air to the roots.

So farmers flood their paddies, often with monsoon rain. Weeds drown, the rice does not. Planting by hand in knee-deep mud is hard work, and in many Indian villages it is still done in groups, with songs to keep the rhythm.

Why does the monsoon come?Every year, the wind turns around. And the rain arrives.

The miracle rice

In the 1960s, India was short of food and had to import grain. Scientists at the International Rice Research Institute in the Philippines bred a short, sturdy rice called IR8, released in 1966. It grew more grain and did not fall over when heavy with seed.

Along with new wheat, fertiliser and irrigation, this was part of the Green Revolution. Within a few decades India went from buying grain to selling it. The changes also used much more water and chemicals, a problem farmers are still dealing with.

Brown, white and fragrant

A rice grain is wrapped in a tough husk, which mills remove. Underneath is brown rice, still coated in bran. Polishing rubs away the bran and the germ, leaving white rice, which cooks faster and keeps longer but loses some vitamins.

There are tens of thousands of varieties. Long, fragrant basmati grows in the foothills of the Himalayas. Sticky rice holds together for eating with your fingers in Thailand and Laos. Short, round grains go into Japanese sushi and the payasam of Kerala.

Rice also travels well as a dry food. Flattened rice, called poha or aval, is made by soaking, roasting and pressing the grains. Puffed rice, or murmura, pops when grains are heated in hot sand. Both turn up in snacks from Kolkata's jhalmuri to Mumbai's bhel puri, and both keep for weeks in a tin.

Rice and a warming planet

Flooded paddies have a hidden side effect. Microbes in the airless mud make methane, a gas that traps heat in the air, so rice farming adds to climate change. Some farmers now let their fields dry out for a few days between floods. It saves water and cuts methane, and the rice still grows.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Rice has to grow underwater.

Truth: Rice can grow in ordinary wet soil. Flooding mainly stops weeds and makes water easy to control. Upland rice, grown on dry slopes, is still farmed in parts of India and Africa.

Try this

Put a few grains of brown rice and a few grains of white rice on separate wads of wet cotton in a saucer. Keep them damp for a week. The brown rice may sprout, but the white rice won't, because polishing removed the tiny part of the seed that grows into a plant.
