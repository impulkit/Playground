Can scientists bring back extinct animals?

In 2003, scientists brought an extinct wild goat back to life. It lived for about ten minutes.

The short answer

Sometimes, partly. If scientists have frozen cells from a recently extinct animal, they can try cloning it, putting the cell's DNA into an egg of a close relative. If the animal died out long ago, they can read its ancient DNA from bones or frozen remains. Then they edit a living relative's genes to make it look and act more like the lost animal.

But no one has yet brought back a lost species in a way that every scientist accepts. Clones can be unhealthy. Edited animals are new creatures, not exact copies. And for animals like dinosaurs, there is no DNA left to work with at all.

Surprising but true

DNA breaks down over time, and scientists estimate that even in ideal conditions it becomes unreadable after a few million years at most. Dinosaurs died out 66 million years ago.

The goat that lived ten minutes

The bucardo was a wild goat of the Pyrenees mountains between Spain and France. The last one, a female named Celia, died in 2000. Before she died, scientists took and froze some of her skin cells.

In 2003, they put her DNA into goat eggs and carried the embryos in other goats. One kid was born alive, the first extinct animal ever brought back. It died about ten minutes later because its lungs had not formed properly. The bucardo became the first animal to go extinct twice.

The dire wolf debate

In 2025, a US company, Colossal Biosciences, announced three white pups it called dire wolves. Dire wolves were big wolves that lived in the Americas until about 13,000 years ago. The company read ancient dire wolf DNA and made about 20 edits to the genes of grey wolf cells, then grew the pups in dog mothers.

Many scientists objected. A group of wolf experts working with the IUCN said the animals were gene-edited grey wolves, not dire wolves, because they share almost all their DNA with grey wolves. The three live on a large private reserve in the USA. Colossal says it is also working on the woolly mammoth, the dodo, the moa and the thylacine, or Tasmanian tiger.

Why not dinosaurs

DNA starts to break apart soon after an animal dies. Heat and water speed this up, and cold slows it down. The oldest DNA ever read came from soil in northern Greenland and is about 2 million years old. Mammoth DNA has been read from teeth more than a million years old, preserved in Siberian permafrost.

Dinosaurs died out 66 million years ago, more than 30 times longer ago than that. Their bones have turned to stone. Any DNA has long since fallen apart.

Saving animals before they vanish

The same tools can help animals that are still alive. In 2020, a black-footed ferret named Elizabeth Ann was born in the USA, cloned from cells frozen in 1988. She brought back genes that had been lost from the small surviving population. In 2024, another cloned ferret gave birth to healthy babies.

Only two northern white rhinos are left in the world, both females living in Kenya. Scientists have made embryos in the lab from stored eggs and frozen sperm and hope to carry them in southern white rhino mothers.

Is it a good idea

Supporters say de-extinction could restore lost habitats, give us new science and inspire people to care about nature. Critics say it costs a lot of money that could protect living animals, that revived animals might suffer, and that a lab-made mammoth would have no herd to learn from.

Most scientists agree on one thing. It is far easier to keep a species alive than to bring it back. Thousands of kinds of animals are at risk today, and many could still be saved.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Scientists could clone a dinosaur from DNA in amber, like in the films.

Truth: DNA does not last anywhere near 66 million years. Insects in amber have been tested, and no real dinosaur DNA has ever been found.

Try this

Write one sentence, about ten words long, on a piece of paper. Ask a friend to copy it quickly, then ask a second friend to copy the copy, and so on, five times. Compare the last version with the first. DNA copies and old DNA collect mistakes and gaps too, which is one reason ancient genes are so hard to read.
