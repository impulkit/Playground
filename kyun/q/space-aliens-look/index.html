What might aliens look like?

Nobody has ever found an alien, but scientists can make clever guesses, and some of their best clues come from octopuses, birds and the bottom of the sea.

The short answer

Nobody knows, because we haven't found any aliens yet. But scientists aren't completely in the dark. Life on Earth has evolved for about 4 billion years, and it keeps solving the same problems in similar ways. Many scientists think alien life, if it exists, would follow some of the same rules.

That means aliens might have features that work well anywhere: ways to sense light, move, find food and avoid danger. On the other hand, a planet with stronger gravity, thicker air or a different star would shape life in its own way. And the most common aliens are probably tiny microbes, not creatures you could shake hands with.

Surprising but true

Eyes have evolved separately dozens of times on Earth, in insects, octopuses, vertebrates and more, which makes some scientists think aliens on a sunny planet would have eyes too.

Nature keeps repeating itself

One of the best clues is something biologists call convergent evolution. Animals that aren't closely related often end up with similar bodies because they face similar problems. Sharks, dolphins and extinct ichthyosaurs all evolved the same streamlined shape for swimming fast.

Wings evolved separately in insects, birds, bats and pterosaurs. Eyes have evolved dozens of times, in many different ways. An octopus eye looks a lot like ours, even though our last shared ancestor was a tiny worm-like creature over 500 million years ago.

Gravity, air and light

The planet itself would shape aliens. On a world with strong gravity, animals might be short, wide and sturdy, with thick legs. On a low-gravity world, they could grow tall and spindly, and flying would be easier.

Thick air would make flying and gliding simpler, and sound would carry well. A planet around a dim, red star might have plants that look very dark, even black, to soak up as much light as possible. NASA scientists have modelled this using what we know about photosynthesis on Earth.

Life without sunlight

Aliens might not live on the surface at all. Moons like Europa and Enceladus have huge oceans hidden under ice. No sunlight reaches them, but life might feed on chemicals from the seabed, as it does around deep-sea vents on Earth. Creatures there could look like the tube worms, pale crabs and glowing fish of our own deep oceans.

Saturn's moon Titan is stranger still. Its lakes are filled with liquid methane, at about minus 180 °C. Some scientists wonder whether life could use methane instead of water, with chemistry completely unlike ours. Nobody knows if that's possible.

Why films get it wrong

The classic "grey alien", with a big head, large black eyes and a small body, comes mostly from science fiction and stories from the 1960s onwards. Film aliens are often human-shaped because actors had to wear the costumes.

Real aliens would have no reason to resemble humans. Even on Earth, most animals don't. More than a million insect species, sponges, jellyfish and fungi show how many different shapes life can take.

Smart aliens?

Intelligent aliens, ones that could build radio telescopes, would need some way to handle objects, like hands, tentacles or trunks. They would need senses to understand the world and a way to communicate, perhaps with sound, light, colour patterns or chemicals.

On Earth, intelligence evolved separately in apes, dolphins, crows and octopuses, so it may not be unique. But it took about 4 billion years to produce a species that builds rockets. We don't know if that's typical or very rare.

Saying hello

In 1977, NASA's two Voyager probes set off carrying a message for any aliens who might find them, with greetings, music and pictures of people. No one knows if anyone will ever see that golden record. If they do, they may find our shape just as strange as theirs would seem to us.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Aliens probably look like humans, with two arms, two legs and big heads.

Truth: Human-like aliens are mostly an idea from films, partly because actors in costumes are human-shaped. Life on other worlds would evolve to suit its own conditions, and could look very different from us.
