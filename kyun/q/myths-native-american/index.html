What stories do Native Americans tell?

In one Native American story, the whole of North America sits on the back of a giant turtle. Many Native people still call the continent Turtle Island.

The short answer

Native American stories explain how the world was made, why animals look the way they do, and how people should treat each other and the land. They star tricksters, heroes, spirits and talking animals.

But there is no single set of "Native American myths". Before Europeans arrived, North America was home to hundreds of nations, each with its own language and its own stories. Today the USA recognises 574 tribal nations (as of 2025), and Canada has hundreds more First Nations. Many stories are still told, often by elders, and some are sacred and shared only within a community.

Surprising but true

Among the Navajo and several other nations, Coyote stories are traditionally told only in winter, after the first frost and before the snakes wake up in spring.

A world on a turtle's back

The Haudenosaunee (sometimes called the Iroquois) tell of Sky Woman, who fell through a hole in the sky world. Below her was only water. Birds caught her on their wings, and a great turtle offered its back as a resting place.

The animals dived to find mud from the bottom. Many failed. At last, a small muskrat came up nearly drowned, with a pawful of mud. Sky Woman spread it on the turtle's shell, and it grew into the land. That is why many Native people call North America Turtle Island.

The Cherokee have a different start: a little water beetle dives down and brings up the mud that becomes the Earth.

Tricksters and troublemakers

Almost every region has a trickster. In much of the West, it is Coyote: greedy, boastful and always getting into trouble. Sometimes his mistakes make the world better by accident. In some stories, he scatters the stars across the sky in a careless hurry, which is why they don't form neat patterns.

On the Pacific coast, the trickster is Raven, who stole daylight from a selfish chief. Among the Lakota, it is Iktomi, a shape-shifting spider. In the Southeast, it is Rabbit, a clever animal much like Br'er Rabbit of African American folk tales.

Tricksters will remind Indian readers of the clever jackals and crows of the Panchatantra. Stories everywhere seem to love a small animal outwitting a big one.

Why do Panchatantra stories use animals?A clever hare tricks a lion into jumping down a well. Stories like this were first told to teach princes how to rule.

Stories with rules

Native storytelling often has rules about when and how stories are shared. Among the Navajo, Coyote stories are told only in winter. One reason given is that animals and spirits are asleep then and won't overhear.

Some stories belong to a particular family or clan. Others are sacred and are told only during ceremonies. That is why many Native communities ask outsiders to treat their stories with care, and why some well-known retellings in books were written down without permission.

Stars, spiders and corn

Many nations read stories in the night sky. The Mi'kmaq of the northeast see the bowl of the Big Dipper as a bear, chased across the sky all year by seven hunter birds. Several peoples tell of seven sisters or seven children who became the Pleiades, the same cluster Indian astronomers call the Krittika. You can see how different peoples drew different pictures among the stars in constellations.

Grandmother Spider appears in Cherokee, Hopi and other traditions. In one Cherokee story, she carries fire to the people in a little clay pot on her back. In the Southwest, stories describe Corn Mother, who gave people maize, the crop that fed much of the Americas.

Words that are still spoken

Many of these stories live in their own languages, and more than 150 Native languages are still spoken in the USA. Several are in danger of disappearing, and tribes now run schools and apps to teach them to children.

One of those languages once helped win a war. During the Second World War, Navajo speakers sent military messages in their own language, and enemy codebreakers never cracked it. The story of the code talkers is one of the most surprising of the 20th century.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Native Americans were one people with one language and one set of stories.

Truth: There are hundreds of distinct nations, with very different languages, homes and traditions. A Hopi story from the Arizona desert can be as different from a Lakota story of the Great Plains as a Tamil folk tale is from a Norwegian one.

Try this

Tell a trickster story the old way, out loud with no book. Pick an animal you know well, like a crow or a squirrel, and make up a tale where it tricks someone bigger and explains something, like why the crow is black. Tell it to your family, then ask someone to retell it tomorrow and see what changes.
