What happened at Ellis Island?

For more than 60 years, a small island in New York harbour was the front door to America. About 12 million people walked through it to start new lives.

The short answer

Ellis Island is a small island in New York harbour, close to the Statue of Liberty. From 1892 to 1954 it was the United States' busiest immigration station. Ships from Europe docked in New York, and passengers in third class and steerage, the cheapest part of the ship, were ferried to the island to be checked.

Inspectors examined their health and asked questions. Most people were allowed in within a few hours. About 12 million immigrants passed through. Today a large share of Americans, possibly tens of millions, have at least one ancestor who stepped onto that island.

Surprising but true

Officials at Ellis Island did not change people's names. Passenger lists were written up before the ships left Europe, and inspectors simply checked names against them.

The first arrival

The station opened on 1 January 1892. The first person to be registered was Annie Moore, a teenage girl from County Cork in Ireland, who had come with her two younger brothers to join their parents in New York. She was given a ten-dollar gold coin to mark the day.

The original wooden buildings burned down in 1897. The grand brick main building that stands today opened in 1900, with a huge hall where thousands of people waited each day.

The six-second exam

New arrivals carried their bags up a long staircase into the Great Hall. As they climbed, doctors watched. Those who seemed ill were marked with chalk and taken aside. Doctors checked eyes for an infection called trachoma, which could cause blindness, by flipping up eyelids with a small hook.

Then came questions. What is your name? Where are you going? Do you have work or family waiting? Do you have money? There were about 29 questions in all. Interpreters helped, because people spoke dozens of languages.

Who came, and why

Most immigrants came from Europe: Italy, Ireland, Germany, Russia, Poland, Greece and many more. Some were escaping poverty or hunger. Others fled persecution, including many Jewish families from eastern Europe. Most simply hoped for work and a better life for their children.

The crossing by steamship took around one to two weeks. Steerage passengers slept in crowded bunks below deck, often with everything they owned packed into a single trunk or cloth bundle. Many had relatives already in America who had sent money for the ticket.

The busiest year was 1907, when more than a million immigrants entered the country. On 17 April that year, 11,747 people were processed at Ellis Island in a single day.

Doors begin to close

In the 1920s, new laws set strict limits on how many people could come from each country. Southern and eastern Europeans got small quotas. People from Asia were almost completely shut out.

That hit Indians too. A 1917 law barred most immigrants from a large "Asiatic Barred Zone" that included India. In 1923 the US Supreme Court ruled in a case brought by Bhagat Singh Thind, an Indian Sikh army veteran, that Indians could not become citizens. These limits eased only in the 1940s and 1960s, which is why most of today's large Indian American community arrived after 1965.

A museum of journeys

After 1924, most checks happened abroad, and Ellis Island became quiet. It closed in 1954 and slowly fell into ruin. After a major restoration, it reopened as a museum in 1990.

Visitors can now search ship records for their own ancestors, and stand in the Great Hall where so many new New Yorkers once waited, clutching their papers and wondering what came next.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Most people who arrived at Ellis Island were sent back.

Truth: Only about 2% were refused entry, usually because of serious illness or because officials thought they could not support themselves. For most people, the whole visit took a few hours.
