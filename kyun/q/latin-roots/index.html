How did Latin give us so many English words?

English is a Germanic language, a cousin of German and Dutch, yet more than half the words in a big English dictionary come from Latin.

The short answer

English got its Latin words in waves, over about 1,500 years. Churchmen brought Latin to England in the early Middle Ages. Then, after 1066, French-speaking rulers brought thousands of French words, and French itself had grown out of Latin. Later still, during the Renaissance, scholars borrowed Latin words directly for science, law and learning.

The result is that about 60% of the words in a large English dictionary come from Latin, either directly or through French. English is still a Germanic language at heart. Its grammar and its most common short words come from Old English, the language of the Anglo-Saxons. But its vocabulary is a mixture.

Oddly, the Romans themselves left very few words behind. They ruled Britain from about 43 CE to 410 CE, but the people there spoke Celtic languages. English only arrived after the Romans left, when Angles, Saxons and other Germanic peoples crossed the North Sea.

Surprising but true

Sanskrit and Latin are distant cousins. Sanskrit pitr and Latin pater both mean father, and matr and mater both mean mother.

The Normans arrive

In 1066, William, Duke of Normandy, crossed from France and won the Battle of Hastings. For about 300 years afterwards, the kings and nobles of England spoke French, while ordinary people spoke English.

This split still shows in English. Farmers kept English words for living animals: cow, pig, sheep. The lords who ate the meat used French words: beef (boeuf), pork (porc) and mutton (mouton). Words for law and power, such as court, judge, jury, government and crown, also arrived from French.

Eventually English became the language of the court again, but it kept thousands of these borrowed words. Often English ended up with two words for nearly the same thing: a plain Old English one and a fancier French one, such as "begin" and "commence", or "help" and "aid".

Words for science and learning

From the 1500s, scholars across Europe still wrote in Latin. When English writers needed words for new ideas, they often built them from Latin parts. That is how English got words such as "species", "formula", "atmosphere" and "spectacles".

Many roots repeat across dozens of words. Scrib means write, giving describe and scribble. Dict means say, giving dictionary and predict. Port means carry, giving transport and portable. Learn a few roots and you can often guess a word you have never seen.

Science still names living things in Latin. A tiger is Panthera tigris, and humans are Homo sapiens, "wise human".

Hiding in plain sight

Latin turns up in everyday English without people noticing. "a.m." and "p.m." stand for ante meridiem and post meridiem, before and after midday. "etc." is et cetera, "and the rest". "e.g." is exempli gratia, "for example". Roman numerals still mark clock faces, film sequels and the Super Bowl.

The USA has a Latin motto on its money and seal: E pluribus unum, "out of many, one". Latin is also the official language of the Holy See, which runs the Catholic Church from Vatican City.

Cousins from India

In 1786, a British judge in Calcutta named William Jones gave a famous talk. He had been studying Sanskrit and noticed it looked strangely similar to Latin and Greek. Sanskrit trayas and Latin tres both mean three. Nava and novus both mean new.

Jones suggested that all three languages came from a common ancestor that no longer existed. He was right. Scholars now call this family Indo-European. It includes Hindi, Bengali, Persian, Latin, Greek, English, German and many more. So when you say "mother" in English and mata in Hindi, you are using two branches of the same very old word.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Most of the everyday words we use in English come from Latin.

Truth: Latin-based words fill the dictionary, but the words we use most, like "the", "is", "water", "house" and "go", mostly come from Old English. Latin words tend to be the longer, more formal ones.
