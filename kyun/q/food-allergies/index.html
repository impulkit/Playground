Why do some people get sick from nuts?

For some people, a single peanut is dangerous, because their immune system mistakes a harmless food for an invader.

The short answer

Your immune system is your body's defence force. It hunts down germs and attacks them. In someone with a food allergy, it makes a mistake. It treats a harmless protein in a food, such as peanut, milk or egg, as if it were a dangerous invader.

The next time that person eats the food, the immune system sounds the alarm. Cells release chemicals such as histamine, which can cause itchy skin, swelling, stomach pain or vomiting. In a severe reaction called anaphylaxis, the throat can swell and breathing can become hard. That is a medical emergency.

Surprising but true

A peanut is not a nut at all. It is a legume, a close cousin of chana and moong.

How the mistake happens

The first time someone with an allergy meets the food, nothing much may happen. But the body makes special antibodies that remember the protein. These antibodies sit on immune cells like tripwires.

When the food arrives again, the protein trips the wire and the cells burst open, flooding the body with alarm chemicals. A reaction can begin within minutes. Even a tiny crumb can be enough for some people, which is why they must check every label.

Allergy or intolerance?

Not every bad reaction to food is an allergy. Many people cannot digest lactose, the sugar in milk, and get a tummy ache or gas after drinking it. That is lactose intolerance. It is uncomfortable, but it does not involve the immune system and is not dangerous in the same way.

A true allergy can be serious, so it is important to find out which is which. If a food seems to make you unwell, tell a trusted adult. A doctor can test for allergies safely.

Ready for an emergency

People with serious food allergies often carry an adrenaline auto-injector. It is a pen-shaped device that pushes medicine into the thigh. Adrenaline, also called epinephrine, quickly opens the airways and keeps blood pressure up. After using it, the person still needs to go to hospital.

Many schools in the United States keep these pens and train staff. Knowing the warning signs, like swelling lips or trouble breathing, is a useful part of first aid for anyone.

Eat peanuts early?

For years, parents of babies at risk of allergies were told to avoid peanuts. In 2015 a large study in London, called LEAP, tested the opposite. Babies at high risk who were given small amounts of peanut food from early in life were about 80% less likely to develop a peanut allergy by age five.

That changed advice in the US and UK. Doctors now often suggest introducing peanut foods early, under medical guidance for high-risk babies. It is a reminder that common sense is not always right, and careful tests matter.

Living with an allergy

Children with serious food allergies learn to live carefully, but they can still do almost everything their friends do. They read labels, ask how food was cooked and sometimes carry their own snacks to parties. Packaged foods in the US must clearly list major allergens, and sesame was added to that list in 2023.

Friends can help too. Not sharing food without asking and knowing who to tell in an emergency make a real difference. Some children grow out of milk and egg allergies as they get older, while peanut allergies more often last.

Food allergies appear to have become more common in many countries over recent decades. Scientists are not sure why. One idea is that children today meet fewer of the microbes that once trained the immune system. Others point to changes in diet, vitamin D or how early babies try foods.

India has less research. Peanuts are a common snack there, and some doctors think peanut allergy is less frequent than in the US, but there is not enough data to be sure.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Peanuts are nuts.

Truth: Peanuts are legumes, related to beans, peas and lentils. Almonds, cashews and walnuts are called tree nuts. Doctors treat the two as separate allergies.

Try this

Collect five packets of food from your kitchen. Find the ingredients list and look for words like "contains" or "may contain". Make a chart of which packets mention milk, nuts, wheat or soy. You might be surprised where they turn up.
