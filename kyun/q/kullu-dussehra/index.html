Why does Kullu's Dussehra start when everyone else's ends?

In most of India, Dussehra ends with a giant Ravana going up in flames. In one Himalayan valley, that is the day the real festival begins.

The short answer

In much of India, Dussehra ends on Vijayadashami, when huge models of Ravana are set on fire. In Kullu, a valley in Himachal Pradesh, that is when the celebration starts.

For seven days, hundreds of village gods and goddesses, carried in decorated palanquins, gather in the town to honour Lord Raghunath, a form of Lord Rama. There is no burning of Ravana. Instead, the festival ends with a ceremony called Lanka Dahan, which stands for good winning over evil.

Surprising but true

At Kullu Dussehra the gods themselves are the guests. Hundreds of village deities are carried down from the hills on people's shoulders, some from many kilometres away, to visit Lord Raghunath.

A king, a statue and a valley

The tradition goes back to the 17th century. According to local folklore, Raja Jagat Singh of Kullu had a statue of Lord Raghunath brought from Ayodhya and placed in his palace temple. He then invited all the deities of the valley to come and pay their respects. They have come every year since.

Rama is the hero of the Ramayana, the great epic that Dussehra celebrates across India.

What is the Ramayana?A prince, a princess, a demon king and an army of monkeys. The Ramayana has been retold for more than 2,000 years, from Indian villages to Thai palaces.

When gods go visiting

In the Kullu valley, almost every village has its own deity, called a devta. For the festival, villagers lift the devta's palanquin onto their shoulders and walk it down the mountain paths, with drums and long horns playing. It is a procession that can last for hours.

The opening day begins with Lord Raghunath's chariot being pulled through the crowds with thick ropes. Each deity first visits Lord Raghunath, then sets up camp at Dhalpur Maidan, a large open ground, for the week. Every step follows an old ritual. The festival was given international status in 1972, and visitors now come from around the world.

One festival, many faces

Other places mark these days in their own way. Bengal says goodbye to Goddess Durga at the end of Durga Puja, and Mysuru holds a grand procession of elephants from its palace. In Kullu, when the deities finally set off for home, they carry the festival back to hundreds of villages across the valley. It is one reason India has so many festivals: each place tells the same story its own way.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Dussehra is celebrated the same way all over India.

Truth: The idea of good winning over evil is shared, but each place celebrates differently. North India burns Ravana, Bengal says goodbye to Goddess Durga, Mysuru has a royal elephant procession, and Kullu holds a week-long gathering of gods.
