How do cats always land on their feet?

A falling cat can flip itself the right way up in mid-air without touching anything, a puzzle that kept scientists arguing for about 75 years.

The short answer

When a cat starts falling the wrong way up, its inner ear senses which way is down within a split second. The cat turns its head first, then twists its front half round while its back half stays almost still. A moment later it twists the back half to match. By the time it lands, its feet are underneath it, its back is arched and its legs are ready to soak up the bump.

The clever part is that it does all this in mid-air, with nothing to push against. Cats can manage it because their spines are very bendy and their collarbones float loose in muscle instead of locking the shoulders in place.

Surprising but true

Kittens aren't born able to do it. The righting reflex starts at about three to four weeks old and is polished by about seven weeks.

A puzzle for physicists

For a long time, scientists said this should be impossible. A basic rule of physics, the conservation of angular momentum, says a spinning object with nothing to push on can't start spinning on its own. Yet cats seemed to do it.

In 1894 a French scientist, Étienne-Jules Marey, used a camera that took many pictures a second to film a falling cat. The photos, among the first ever to freeze such a quick movement, showed the twist clearly. Some members of the French Academy of Sciences still thought there must be a trick. It took until 1969 for two American engineers, Thomas Kane and M. P. Scher, to build a proper mathematical model.

The bending trick

Here is how a cat cheats the rule. It bends in the middle, so it becomes two halves. It pulls its front legs in tight, which makes the front half easy to spin, like a skater pulling in their arms. At the same time it sticks its back legs out, which makes the back half hard to spin. The front swings round a long way, and the back turns only slightly the other way.

Then the cat swaps. Front legs out, back legs in. Now the rear half catches up. The total spin still adds up to zero, so physics is happy, but the cat ends up the right way round. Astronauts floating inside a space station have used a version of this to turn without grabbing anything.

Why higher can be safer

In 1987, two New York vets studied 132 cats that had fallen from tall buildings, on average about five and a half storeys. Around 90 percent survived with treatment. Oddly, cats that fell from above seven storeys seemed to have fewer broken legs than those from lower down.

One idea is that a cat only reaches its top speed, about 95 km/h, after falling a few storeys. Once it stops speeding up, it relaxes and spreads its legs like a furry parachute, which slows it slightly and spreads out the landing. Other scientists point out a catch: cats that died in the fall were never taken to the vet, so they weren't counted. The truth is probably a mix of both.

Built for climbing

The righting reflex makes sense for an animal that hunts in trees and on walls. Leopards, snow leopards and many wild cats do the same. Squirrels and some lizards can twist in the air as well.

A cat's light body helps a lot. The smaller you are, the slower you fall compared with your weight, which is why a mouse can survive a fall that would hurt a cat. This is part of the story of how gravity makes things fall.

And the strangest part? A cat knows which way is down thanks to tiny fluid-filled chambers and loops in its inner ear. You have almost exactly the same thing inside your own head.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Cats always land safely, so falls can't hurt them.

Truth: Cats are good at landing on their feet, but falls still injure many of them every year. Vets see so many cats hurt in falls from flats that they gave it a name, "high-rise syndrome". Windows and balconies need screens.

Try this

Sit on a spinning office chair with your feet off the floor and arms out wide, holding a heavy book in each hand. Have someone give you a gentle spin, then pull the books in to your chest. You speed up. Cats use the same rule, pulling legs in to twist faster.
