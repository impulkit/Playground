How do wells and borewells get water?

The water under your feet sits in tiny gaps between grains of sand and cracks in rock, and a well is simply a hole that lets it gather.

The short answer

When rain falls, some of it runs into rivers and some evaporates. The rest soaks down into the soil. It trickles through sand, gravel and cracks in rock until it reaches a layer where every gap is already full of water. The top of that wet layer is called the water table. A layer of rock or sand that holds plenty of water is an aquifer.

A well is a hole that reaches below the water table. Water seeps sideways out of the wet ground and collects at the bottom of the hole. Then you lift it with a bucket, a hand pump or an electric pump. A borewell is the same idea, drilled much narrower and much deeper.

Surprising but true

Archaeologists have found hundreds of brick-lined wells in Mohenjo-daro, a city built more than 4,500 years ago.

The old dug well

For most of history, wells were dug by hand. Workers used spades and baskets to make a round shaft a metre or more wide. They lined the walls with stone or brick so the sides wouldn't fall in. A rope, a bucket and a wooden pulley did the rest.

For farms, one bucket was never enough. In north India and Pakistan, farmers used the Persian wheel, or rahat: a chain of clay or metal pots looped over a wheel. A pair of bullocks or a camel walked in circles to turn it, and the pots came up full and tipped their water into a channel.

India has dug wells for thousands of years. In Mohenjo-daro, one of the great Indus cities, archaeologists found hundreds of wells lined with wedge-shaped bricks. Many homes seem to have had their own. In dry parts of western India, people later built grand stepwells, with flights of stairs leading down to the water as it rose and fell through the year.

Drilling a borewell

A borewell is drilled by a truck-mounted rig. A spinning drill bit, often with hard tips, grinds through soil and rock, while air or water flushes the broken bits up to the surface. The hole may be only 15 centimetres wide. It can go down 100, 200 or even 400 metres in places where the water table has dropped.

Workers fit a pipe called a casing into the top of the hole to stop soil falling in. Then they lower a submersible pump, a long thin electric pump that sits underwater at the bottom. It pushes water up instead of sucking it. That matters, because suction alone can only lift water about 7 or 8 metres.

The hand pump

Across Indian villages you can still see sturdy iron hand pumps. The India Mark II design, developed in the 1970s with help from UNICEF, has its pumping cylinder down inside the well rather than at the top. Each push on the handle lifts a column of water, so it can work from much deeper than an old suction pump.

Running low

India pumps more groundwater than any other country. Most of it waters farms, and many cities depend on it too. In parts of Punjab, Rajasthan and Bengaluru, wells that once struck water at 10 metres now go down hundreds of metres. When people pump out more than rain puts back, the water table falls year after year.

The fix is to help rain soak back in. Every monsoon, water caught in ponds, check dams and pits can seep down and refill the aquifers. Rainwater harvesting from roofs and recharge wells does the same thing in cities.

A safety rule worth knowing

Old borewells that are left uncovered are dangerous, because their openings are small and easy to miss. India's Supreme Court has ordered that unused borewells must be capped or filled. If you ever see an open hole in a field, keep away and tell an adult straight away.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Wells tap into underground rivers or lakes.

Truth: Most groundwater is held in the tiny spaces between grains of sand or gravel, or in cracks in rock. A well fills because water seeps slowly from these gaps into the hole. Large underground streams exist only in some limestone caves.

Try this

Fill a clear glass with dry sand, then slowly pour in water and watch it fill the gaps. Push a straw into the sand and gently suck. The water you draw up was hiding between the grains, just like groundwater in the earth.
