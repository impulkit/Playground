What does your liver do?

Your liver does hundreds of jobs at once, weighs about as much as a big bottle of water, and can grow back after surgeons remove part of it.

The short answer

Your liver is a large, reddish-brown organ tucked under your ribs on the right side. It works like a factory, a warehouse and a cleaning plant all in one.

Blood coming from your gut, full of everything you've just digested, goes straight to the liver first. The liver sorts it. It stores some sugar for later, packs away iron and vitamins, and builds proteins your blood needs, including the ones that make it clot when you cut yourself. It also breaks down things the body doesn't want, such as old blood cells and medicines that have done their job. And it makes bile, a green-yellow liquid that helps you digest fat.

Surprising but true

A healthy person can give part of their liver to someone who needs it. Within a few months, both pieces grow to close to full size.

The body's warehouse

After a meal, there is more sugar in your blood than you need right away. The liver packs the extra into a starch-like store called glycogen. Hours later, when you're running around or asleep, it turns that store back into sugar to keep your brain fed.

It also holds stocks of vitamin A, vitamin D, vitamin B12 and iron. Some of these stores can last for months. This is one reason a person doesn't fall ill the day they miss a few vitamins.

The cleaning plant

Many substances that enter the body need to be made safe before they can leave. The liver changes them, using enzymes, into forms that can go out in bile or be passed to the kidneys and leave in urine.

When red blood cells wear out, after about four months, the liver helps handle the yellow pigment left over. If this pigment builds up, the skin and the whites of the eyes turn yellow. This is called jaundice. Many newborn babies get mild jaundice in their first week, because their livers are still getting started. Doctors watch it, and it usually clears by itself or with a simple light treatment.

The liver's work also matters for medicines. It breaks most of them down, which is why a dose wears off after a few hours. Children's livers handle some medicines differently from adults', so doctors and pharmacists choose doses by age and weight. All that chemical work also gives off heat. Resting, the liver is one of the body's main sources of warmth.

The organ that grows back

Most organs can't replace large lost pieces. The liver can. If part of it is removed, the cells that remain start to divide until the liver reaches close to its old size. This is what makes living donor transplants possible: a healthy adult gives part of their liver to someone whose liver is failing, often a family member.

The first liver transplant was tried by surgeon Thomas Starzl in the USA in 1963. Today, hospitals around the world, including many in India, do thousands of these operations every year. A good share of them use living donors.

Bile and the gall bladder

Bile drips out of the liver all the time. Between meals it is stored in a small pouch under the liver called the gall bladder. When fatty food arrives in your gut, the gall bladder squeezes bile into the small intestine. Bile breaks fat into tiny droplets, the way washing-up liquid breaks up grease on a plate. That gives digestive enzymes more surface to work on.

Bile is also what gives poo its brown colour. The pigments from old blood cells travel out in bile and change colour on their way through the gut.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The liver can regrow, so nothing can really harm it.

Truth: The liver can heal from a lot, but long-lasting damage, for example from heavy drinking of alcohol or some viruses, can leave scars. Scarred liver can't do its work, and it doesn't grow back the way healthy liver does.
