Why do we sweat?

Humans are among the sweatiest animals on Earth, and that watery talent may be why our ancestors could outrun antelopes in the heat.

The short answer

Your body works best at about 37 °C. When you get hot, from running, from a fever or from a summer afternoon, your brain switches on millions of tiny sweat glands in your skin. Each one is a coiled tube that pumps out water with a little salt.

Sweat itself doesn't cool you. What cools you is sweat turning into vapour and floating away. Changing from liquid to gas takes heat, and that heat is pulled from your skin. It's the same reason a wet cloth on your forehead feels cool, or why a clay pot, a matka, keeps water cool as it slowly seeps and evaporates from the outside.

Surprising but true

Fresh sweat has almost no smell. The smell comes from bacteria on your skin feeding on it.

Two kinds of sweat glands

Most of your sweat glands are the small kind, spread over almost your whole body. They are packed most tightly on your palms, the soles of your feet and your forehead. They make thin, watery sweat for cooling.

The second kind lives mainly in your armpits and groin. These glands start working at puberty. Their sweat is thicker and contains fats and proteins. Fresh, it has hardly any smell. But bacteria living on the skin feed on it and give off smelly substances. That's what body odour is, and why washing and clean clothes make such a difference.

The great sweaty runner

Most mammals don't sweat much. Dogs pant with their tongues out. Cats lick their fur. Elephants flap their big ears. Humans, though, can sweat over their whole bodies and keep going in the heat for hours.

Some scientists think this helped early humans hunt. In a method called persistence hunting, people chased animals like antelopes through the midday heat. The animals were faster but had to stop to pant and cool down. The humans kept going, sweating, until the animal was too hot to run. A few hunters in southern Africa used this method into recent times. It may also be why people can run a marathon at all.

Why is a marathon 42.195 km?The marathon's odd distance was fixed in 1908, so that a race in London could finish in front of the royal family's seats.

Sticky days

Sweat only works if it can evaporate. On a dry, hot day in Rajasthan or Arizona, sweat vanishes almost as soon as it appears, and you may not even feel wet. On a humid monsoon day in Mumbai, Kolkata or Chennai, or a summer day in Florida, the air is already full of water. Sweat can't escape, so it drips off, and you stay hot.

That's why humid heat feels worse than dry heat at the same temperature, and why it can be more dangerous. Weather forecasters use a heat index to describe how hot it really feels. In very hot places, the most important thing is to drink plenty of water and rest in the shade.

Nervous sweat

Palms often get damp before a test or a stage show. That's emotional sweating. When you're nervous, the same alarm system that speeds up your heart switches on the sweat glands in your palms, soles and armpits, even if you aren't hot.

Scientists think sweaty palms may once have helped our ancestors grip branches or tools better when in danger. Today it mostly just makes holding a pen tricky. Some people sweat much more than others, and a small number sweat so much that it gets in the way of daily life. Doctors can help with that.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pigs sweat a lot, as in the saying "sweating like a pig".

Truth: Pigs have very few working sweat glands and can't cool down by sweating. That's why they roll in mud and water on hot days, letting the wet mud cool them instead.

Try this

Wet a cloth and wrap it around the bulb of a thermometer, then fan it gently for a few minutes. Watch the reading fall. Compare it with a dry thermometer next to it. The drop shows how evaporating water pulls heat away, just as sweat does on your skin.
