What can fossil poo tell us?

Scientists study dinosaur poo. Seriously. And it solved a mystery in India.

The short answer

Fossil poo is called a coprolite. It's poo that turned to stone.

Coprolites show what ancient animals ate, which is hard to learn any other way. Inside them, scientists find crushed bones, bits of plants, seeds, fish scales and even tiny parasites.

In 2005, coprolites from Maharashtra and Madhya Pradesh showed that dinosaurs ate grass. That changed the history of grass. A giant T. rex coprolite from Canada is full of crushed bone, proof of its bone-crunching bite.

Surprising but true

Dinosaur poo from India rewrote the history of grass.

Dung stones

The first person to notice fossil poo was probably Mary Anning, the fossil hunter of Lyme Regis in England. She found strange stones inside the skeletons of ichthyosaurs, where their stomachs would have been. When broken open, the stones held fish bones and scales.

In 1829, the geologist William Buckland studied them and gave them their name, coprolite, from Greek words for "dung" and "stone". Buckland was famous for being eccentric. He even had a table made with polished coprolites set into its top.

How poo becomes stone

Most poo rots away within days or is eaten by insects. To fossilise, it has to be buried quickly, in mud or sand, before it falls apart. Over time, water carries minerals into it, and they slowly replace the original material, just as with bones.

Meat-eaters' poo fossilises more often than plant-eaters'. It is rich in calcium phosphate from the bones they ate, which helps it harden. Plant-eaters' poo was softer and more often broke up before it could be buried. Some coprolites are found far from any skeleton, so working out which animal made them takes detective work too.

Grass in the dinosaur age

For a long time, scientists believed grass had appeared after the dinosaurs died out. Then, in 2005, a team led by the Indian scientist Vandana Prasad studied coprolites from Pisdura in Maharashtra. They had probably been left by titanosaurs, about 67 million years ago.

Inside were tiny pieces of silica called phytoliths. Grasses make them in their leaves, and each kind of plant makes its own shapes. Some of the phytoliths came from grasses. So grass existed in India while dinosaurs were alive, and the giants were eating it.

In 2011, the same team reported phytoliths from plants related to rice in these coprolites. That pushed the history of the rice family back too. The dinosaurs ate a wide mix, including conifers, palms and other plants.

A king-sized dropping

In 1998, the American scientist Karen Chin described a coprolite found in Saskatchewan, Canada. It was about 44 cm long, longer than a school ruler and a half, and much of it was crushed bone. Only one meat-eater big enough lived there at the time: T. rex.

The bone fragments showed that T. rex did not just tear flesh. It crunched through bone and swallowed it, something few animals can do. It is strong evidence for the powerful bite that its skull and teeth also suggest.

Rotten wood and crabs

Chin has also studied coprolites from plant-eating dinosaurs in Montana and Utah. Many were full of rotting wood, and some from Utah held bits of crab or crayfish shell. The dinosaurs may have eaten rotting logs at certain times of year, perhaps for the fungi and small animals living inside them. Like a detective with a magnifying glass, a scientist with a slice of coprolite can find a dinosaur's last meal.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Fossil poo still smells.

Truth: A coprolite is a rock. The original dung was replaced by minerals over millions of years, so it has no smell at all. Some are even polished and sold as curiosities.

Try this

Look closely at a handful of soil or compost with a magnifying glass. Can you spot bits of leaves, seeds or tiny shells? Scientists do the same with coprolites, breaking them into thin slices to find what the animal had eaten.
