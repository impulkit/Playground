How do octopuses change colour?

An octopus can match the colour of a rock in less than a second, even though scientists think it is colour-blind.

The short answer

An octopus's skin is packed with tiny colour cells called chromatophores. Each is a small, stretchy sac full of pigment, yellow, orange, red, brown or black, with little muscles all around it. When the brain sends a signal along a nerve, the muscles pull, the sac stretches into a coloured disc, and that patch of skin changes colour. When the muscles relax, the sac shrinks back to a dot.

Because these cells are controlled directly by nerves, they work very fast, often in less than a second. Below them sit other layers of cells that reflect and scatter light, adding shimmering blues, greens and bright whites.

Surprising but true

Octopuses change colour in their sleep. Scientists filmed them flickering through patterns during a stage of active sleep, and some wonder whether it is a little like dreaming.

Three layers of colour

The top layer is the chromatophores, which add warm colours. Under them are iridophores, which reflect light like the inside of a shell or a soap bubble, giving blues and greens. At the bottom are leucophores, white cells that scatter whatever light falls on them, so they look the colour of the surroundings.

By opening some chromatophores and closing others, an octopus can make stripes, spots, blotches or a smooth wash of colour. It can even make dark bands ripple across its body, like clouds passing over a field.

Changing shape too

Colour is only half the trick. An octopus can also change the texture of its skin. Small bumps called papillae rise up into spikes or ridges, so it can look like rough rock, seaweed or coral. Because it has no bones, it can flatten itself against a reef or squeeze into a crack the width of its beak.

Put together, colour, pattern and shape make it one of the best disguise artists in the sea. Divers have swum right past octopuses sitting in the open.

In 2023, scientists in Japan filmed octopuses sleeping in tanks. Most of the time they were pale and still. But roughly every hour, they went into a short "active" sleep, with their skin flickering through the patterns they use when awake. The researchers suggested the brain might be replaying the day, a little like dreaming, though nobody can ask an octopus.

The colour-blind puzzle

Here is the strange part. Most octopuses have only one type of light-sensing cell in their eyes. Animals like that usually can't tell colours apart. Yet octopuses match their background beautifully.

Scientists are testing a few ideas. In 2015, researchers in California found that octopus skin itself contains light-sensitive proteins called opsins, the same kind used in eyes. A patch of skin cut off from the brain still reacted to light. So the skin may "see" brightness in its own simple way. Another idea is that the octopus's oddly shaped pupils might help it pick up colour clues by focusing different colours slightly differently. Neither idea is fully proven.

More than hiding

Octopuses also use colour to talk and to bluff. An angry or frightened octopus may suddenly turn dark or flash pale. The small blue-ringed octopus of the Pacific and Indian Oceans shows bright blue rings as a warning that it is extremely venomous.

The mimic octopus, discovered in 1998 off the coast of Sulawesi in Indonesia, goes further. It can change its colour and posture to imitate a striped lionfish, a sea snake or a flatfish, all animals that predators tend to avoid. And an octopus's brain doesn't do all the work. It has about 500 million nerve cells, but most of them are in its eight arms, which help control the skin nearby.

Other animals change colour too, but usually more slowly. Chameleons mostly do it by changing the spacing of tiny crystals in their skin, which can take seconds or minutes. Octopuses, squid and cuttlefish are among the fastest colour-changers known. And when a disguise fails, an octopus has a backup plan: a dark cloud of ink to cover its escape.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Octopuses change colour only to hide.

Truth: Camouflage is a big reason, but not the only one. Octopuses also flash colours to warn predators, to scare prey, to signal to other octopuses, and their colours shift with their mood. Some even change colour while asleep.

Try this

Draw lots of small dots with a felt pen on a deflated balloon. Then blow it up slowly and watch the dots spread into big patches of colour. Let the air out and they shrink. An octopus's colour cells work in a similar way, except muscles do the stretching.
