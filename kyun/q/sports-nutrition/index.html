What should athletes eat?

Marathon runners talk about "hitting the wall", a sudden heavy-legged slump that often comes when the fuel stored in their muscles starts running out.

The short answer

Athletes need the same healthy foods as everyone else, just more of them, at the right times. The main fuel for running, jumping and hitting is carbohydrate, found in rice, roti, millets, potatoes, bananas and other fruit. Protein from dal, eggs, milk, paneer, fish, meat and beans helps repair muscles after training. Vegetables and fruit add vitamins and minerals. Water keeps everything working.

Timing matters too. A proper meal two or three hours before a game, a small snack if needed, and food plus water afterwards help the body recover. There is no single magic food. Plain, varied meals do most of the work. Sleep matters too. Muscles repair and fuel stores refill mostly while you rest, so a tired athlete who skips sleep can't fully recover, however well they eat.

Surprising but true

In hot weather, a hard-working athlete can sweat out a litre or more of water in an hour, about the same as a big water bottle.

Fuel in the tank

When you eat carbohydrates, your body breaks them into sugar, called glucose. What it doesn't use straight away it stores as glycogen, packed into your muscles and liver like fuel in a tank.

During hard exercise, muscles burn glycogen quickly. After about 90 minutes of hard running, the tank can run low. Marathon runners call this hitting the wall: legs feel like lead and every step is a struggle. That's why long-distance athletes eat plenty of carbohydrates the days before a race, and often take small snacks or sports drinks while running.

Building and repairing

Training makes tiny tears in muscle fibres. Protein provides the building blocks to repair them, and the muscles grow back a little stronger.

Young athletes need some protein at every meal, but not huge amounts. A bowl of dal with rice, an egg, a glass of milk or a cup of curd adds up fast. Many vegetarian athletes in India eat paneer, soya, sprouts and pulses. Some cricketers and wrestlers swear by traditional foods, and some now add millets like ragi, which also give calcium for bones.

Two minerals matter a lot for young athletes. Iron carries oxygen in the blood, and running low can make you tired and breathless. It comes from leafy greens, beans, jaggery, eggs and meat, and foods with vitamin C, like lemon or amla, help the body absorb it. Calcium builds bones, which store most of their calcium during childhood and the teenage years. Milk, curd, ragi and sesame are good sources.

Water and sweat

Your body cools itself by sweating. On a hot afternoon in Chennai or Phoenix, an athlete can lose a litre or more of sweat in an hour. Losing too much water makes you tired and dizzy and slows your thinking.

The simple fix is to drink water before, during and after exercise, in small sips rather than gulping a whole bottle at the end. For most school sport, plain water is enough. Sports drinks, with sugar and salts, are meant for long, hard sessions in heat. Energy drinks are different: they contain caffeine, and doctors' groups such as the American Academy of Pediatrics say children shouldn't have them.

Real athletes, real plates

Elite athletes work with nutritionists who measure exactly what they need. Scientists in sports science test sweat, weigh players before and after training and plan meals around matches.

For most young players, the advice is simpler. Eat regular meals with a mix of grains, protein foods, fruit and vegetables. Don't skip breakfast before morning practice. Have a snack such as a banana, poha or a peanut chikki if there's a long gap before a game. If you're worried about your weight, energy or diet, talk to a parent, coach or doctor rather than trying supplements. A champion's plate often looks a lot like a good home-cooked thali.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Young athletes need protein shakes to build muscle.

Truth: Most children and teenagers already get enough protein from foods like dal, eggs, milk, curd, paneer, chicken or beans. Extra protein isn't stored as muscle, and training builds strength far more than powders do.

Try this

Keep a one-week sports plate diary. After each game or PE class, draw what you ate before and after it on a paper plate outline, colouring grains, protein foods, fruit and vegetables differently. At the end of the week, see which colour was missing most, and talk it over with a parent.
