How do tsunami warnings work?

In 2004 a ten-year-old girl on a beach in Thailand saw the sea suddenly pull back, remembered a geography lesson and helped clear the beach before the wave hit.

The short answer

A tsunami warning starts with an earthquake. Seismometers, instruments that feel the ground shaking, pick up a big quake under the sea within a minute or two. Computers work out where it struck and how strong it was.

If the quake is big and shallow enough to lift the sea floor, warning centres send an alert. Sensors on the sea floor and tide gauges along the coast then check whether a wave is really on its way and how large it is.

The warning goes out by text message, radio, TV, sirens and loudspeakers. People along the coast are told to move to high ground before the waves arrive.

Surprising but true

In the deep ocean a tsunami can travel at about 800 km/h, as fast as a jet plane, yet ships far out at sea often don't notice it pass beneath them.

Why there is time to warn

A tsunami is a series of waves made when a large patch of sea floor jerks up or down, usually in an earthquake. The whole depth of the ocean moves, not just the surface.

In deep water these waves are fast, about 800 km/h, but only about a metre high. Ships hardly feel them. As they reach shallow water near shore, they slow down and pile up, sometimes into walls of water many metres tall.

Earthquake signals travel through rock in seconds or minutes, far faster than the waves. A coast hundreds of kilometres away may have an hour or more. A coast right next to the quake may have only minutes, so people there are taught to move as soon as they feel a strong shake.

Listening to the sea floor

Earthquakes don't always make tsunamis. To be sure, scientists use deep-sea sensors called DART buoys, developed by NOAA in the USA. A pressure sensor sits on the ocean floor, sometimes 6,000 m down. When a tsunami passes over, the extra water above it pushes a little harder.

The sensor sends a sound signal up to a buoy floating on the surface. The buoy sends the news by satellite to warning centres. Tide gauges near the coast add more clues. Today dozens of these buoys sit in the Pacific, Atlantic and Indian Oceans, and India runs its own in the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea.

The wave that changed everything

On 26 December 2004, a huge earthquake struck under the sea off Sumatra, in Indonesia. It set off a tsunami that crossed the Indian Ocean and killed about 230,000 people in 14 countries. In India, the coasts of Tamil Nadu and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands were badly hit.

At the time, the Pacific Ocean had a warning system, started in Hawaii in 1949. The Indian Ocean had none.

On a beach in Phuket, Thailand, a ten-year-old British girl named Tilly Smith saw the sea froth and draw back. She had learned about tsunamis at school just two weeks before. She told her parents, who warned others, and the beach was cleared before the waves arrived.

India's watchtower in Hyderabad

After 2004, countries around the Indian Ocean built a warning system together. India's part is the Indian Tsunami Early Warning Centre at INCOIS in Hyderabad, which began work in 2007.

It watches for earthquakes around the clock using seismic stations, sea-floor pressure sensors and tide gauges. It aims to send an alert within about ten minutes of a big undersea quake. It also warns other countries around the Indian Ocean.

Warnings only work if people act on them. So coastal towns hold drills, put up evacuation signs and mark safe routes uphill. Many coastal villages in Odisha and Tamil Nadu now practise what to do. Schoolchildren learn the signs Tilly Smith knew: a strong earthquake, a sudden roar, or the sea rushing away from the shore.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A tsunami is one giant wave that curls over like a surfing wave.

Truth: A tsunami is usually a series of waves, often arriving minutes apart, and the first is not always the biggest. Near shore it often looks like a fast, rising flood rather than a curling breaker.

Try this

Fill a long baking tray with a couple of centimetres of water. Lift one end a little and drop it gently. Watch the wave race to the other end and pile up against the side. Then put a sloping "beach" of modelling clay at one end and see how the wave grows taller as it reaches shallow water.
