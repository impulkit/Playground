How do muscles move you?

Every muscle in your body can only pull. Not one of them can push.

The short answer

A muscle is a bundle of long, thin cells called fibres. When your brain sends a signal down a nerve, the fibres get shorter. As the muscle shortens, it pulls on whatever it's attached to.

Most of the muscles you use to move are joined to bones by tough cords called tendons. When a muscle pulls on a bone, the bone swings at a joint, like a door turning on its hinge. Because muscles can only pull, they come in pairs. One pulls a bone one way, and its partner pulls it back.

You have more than 600 of these muscles, and you use dozens of them just to stand up.

Surprising but true

Your tongue isn't one muscle. It's a bundle of eight muscles working together, with no bone inside.

Three kinds

Skeletal muscles move your bones. You control them when you want to, which is why you can wave or kick a football.

Smooth muscles line your stomach, intestines and blood vessels. They squeeze food along and narrow or widen blood vessels without you ever thinking about it.

Heart muscle is found only in your heart. It contracts about 1 lakh times a day for your whole life and never needs a rest.

Tiny sliding ropes

Inside each fibre are millions of even thinner threads made of two proteins, actin and myosin. In 1954, scientists in Britain found that these threads don't shrink. Instead, they slide past each other. Myosin has tiny heads that grab the actin, pull, let go and grab again, like a team pulling a rope hand over hand in a tug of war.

Each grab uses a little energy from your food. That's why muscles need a steady supply of oxygen and sugar from your blood, and why your heart beats faster when you exercise.

Your arm is a lever

Your elbow works like a lever. The biceps is attached just below the elbow, close to the joint. That means it has to pull hard, but a small shortening swings your hand a long way, fast. It trades strength for speed, which is handy for throwing a ball.

Big, small and busy

The biggest muscle is the gluteus maximus in your bottom. It keeps you upright and powers climbing and running. The smallest, the stapedius, is in your ear and is only about a millimetre long. It tightens when loud sounds arrive, to protect your hearing.

The muscles that move your eyes are among the busiest. Six small muscles hold each eyeball and swivel it in every direction. They make tiny flicks many times a second as you read, and you hardly notice them.

Getting stronger

When you lift heavy things, your muscle fibres get thicker over time. They don't grow in number much. In 2021, the weightlifter Mirabai Chanu won a silver medal for India at the Tokyo Olympics. She lifted a total of 202 kg in two lifts, more than four times her own body weight. Years of training had built muscles that could pull that hard.

Muscles also make heat. When you're cold, they twitch quickly without moving you anywhere. That's shivering, and it's your body's way of warming up.

Machines that copy muscles

Engineers have tried to copy muscles for years. Most robots move with electric motors, which spin rather than pull. Some new robot "muscles" are made from special plastics or tubes that shorten when air is pumped in, getting closer to the way a real muscle works.

Myth or fact?

Myth: It takes more muscles to frown than to smile.

Truth: This saying is popular but nobody has a reliable count. Faces vary, and both smiles and frowns can use different sets of muscles depending on the expression. It's a nice idea, not a scientific fact.

Try this

Rest one hand on the front of your upper arm and slowly bend that elbow, then straighten it. Feel the biceps bulge and harden. Now move your hand to the back of your arm and do it again. You can feel the two muscles taking turns.
