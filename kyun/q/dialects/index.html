Why do people from the same country speak differently?

An old Hindi saying goes "kos kos par badle paani, chaar kos par baani". Every few kilometres the water changes, and so does the way people speak.

The short answer

People in the same country speak differently because languages are always changing, and they change in different ways in different places. Mountains, rivers, distance and old borders kept groups apart for centuries. Each group's speech slowly drifted in its own direction.

These local versions of a language are called dialects. They have their own words, sounds and sometimes grammar. A person from Bihar and a person from Haryana might both speak Hindi but choose different words for the same things. A Texan and a New Yorker can tell where the other grew up within a sentence.

Surprising but true

In much of the American South, people call every fizzy soft drink a "coke", even if it's lemon-flavoured. In parts of the Midwest, it's "pop", and in the Northeast, "soda".

How dialects form

Picture a group of people who all speak the same way. Some move over a mountain range and lose touch with the rest. Every generation, small changes creep in: a vowel shifts, a new word is borrowed, an old one is forgotten. After a few hundred years, the two groups sound noticeably different.

Contact with other languages matters too. Near borders, people borrow words from their neighbours. In US cities, immigrants from Ireland, Italy, Germany, Mexico and elsewhere left traces in local English. That is part of the story of immigration in America.

Before cars, trains and television, most people spent their whole lives within a small area. So speech could change from one cluster of villages to the next, just as the Hindi saying about every four kos says. A kos is an old unit of distance of roughly 3 km.

Dialect or language?

Linguists use "accent" for the way words sound, and "dialect" for a whole package of sounds, words and grammar. Everyone has an accent, even people who think they don't.

The line between a dialect and a separate language is blurry. Spoken Hindi and Urdu are so close that speakers understand each other easily, but they use different scripts and literary words, and are counted as separate languages. A linguist called Max Weinreich made a joke famous: a language is a dialect with an army and a navy. He meant that power and history, not just grammar, decide what gets called a language.

Bhojpuri is a good example. Many people call it a dialect of Hindi. But it has tens of millions of speakers and its own films and songs, and many linguists treat it as a language in its own right.

Pop, soda or coke

The United States is huge, and its English varies a lot. Many southerners say "y'all" for "you all". Traditional Boston speakers often drop the "r" in words like "car". New Yorkers stand "on line" where most Americans stand "in line". The soft-drink map, pop versus soda versus coke, is a favourite of American geography quizzes.

American and British English are dialects too. In 1828, Noah Webster's dictionary pushed American spellings like "color" and "center", partly to show the new country's independence. English spread around the world and grew new dialects, including Indian English, with its own words like "prepone" and "co-brother".

Which version is "correct"?

Most countries have a standard version of the language used in schools, news and government. Standard Hindi grew from Khari Boli, spoken around Delhi. Standard English grew mainly from the speech of London and southeast England.

The standard is useful because everyone can learn it. But it is not more logical than other dialects. Linguists have shown that every dialect follows consistent rules, and dictionaries increasingly record regional words. Many people switch between their home dialect and the standard, depending on who they're talking to. That skill is so common, you probably do it without noticing.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Dialects are broken or lazy versions of the "proper" language.

Truth: Every dialect has its own regular rules for sounds, words and grammar. The standard version of a language usually began as just one dialect, often the one spoken by powerful people in the capital.

Try this

Make a dialect map of your family or class. Ask everyone what they call a few everyday things, like a small bag for school lunch, a tap, or the evening meal. Mark where each person grew up. You may find words change with the places people come from.
