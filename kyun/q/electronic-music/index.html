What is electronic music?

In 1982 a Bombay film musician recorded ten ragas on a Roland bass machine that had flopped in shops. Today some call it early acid house.

The short answer

Electronic music is music where the sounds are made or shaped by electricity. Older instruments rely on strings, drum skins or air in a tube. The main tool is the synthesizer. It builds a sound from an electric signal, which a loudspeaker then turns into vibrations in the air.

Today almost every song you hear has some electronic part, from a pop chorus to a Bollywood dance number. But it started a hundred years ago with odd machines that most people thought were toys.

Surprising but true

The theremin, one of the first electronic instruments, is played without touching it. The player moves their hands in the air near two metal antennas.

An instrument you never touch

Around 1920 a Russian scientist called Leon Theremin built a box with two metal antennas. Moving one hand near the upright antenna raised or lowered the note. The other hand, near a loop, made it louder or softer. The sound was eerie and wobbly, like a singer humming through a pipe.

The theremin became the sound of flying saucers in 1950s science fiction films. It is hard to play well, because there are no keys or frets to tell you where a note is. Players learn to hold their fingers very still, since a tiny twitch bends the note out of tune. Clara Rockmore, a violinist who moved to the United States, became famous in the 1930s for playing classical pieces on it with real precision.

Scissors, tape and a time machine

In the 1950s and 60s, people found they could make music by recording sounds on magnetic tape and then cutting, slowing down or looping it. In Britain, Delia Derbyshire of the BBC Radiophonic Workshop turned a written melody into the theme for Doctor Who in 1963. She built it note by note from tape, test-tone machines and careful splicing, without a synthesizer.

The synthesizer arrives

In 1964 the American engineer Robert Moog showed a synthesizer that musicians could play from a keyboard. Inside, electric circuits made simple waves. Knobs changed their shape and frequency, which is how fast the wave wobbles and how high the note sounds. A smooth wave sounds soft, like a flute. A jagged one sounds buzzy, like a bee in a jar.

By the 1970s the German band Kraftwerk was making whole albums this way. Their robotic songs about trains and computers shaped pop, dance music and early hip-hop.

A raga to a disco beat

In 1982 Charanjit Singh, a session musician who played for Hindi film recordings in Bombay, made an album called Synthesizing: Ten Ragas to a Disco Beat. He used a Roland TB-303 bass machine, a drum machine and a synthesizer to play Indian ragas over a thumping beat.

Hardly anyone bought it. A few years later, musicians in Chicago used the same squelchy TB-303 sound to invent acid house. When the album was reissued in 2010, listeners were startled to hear that sound had already been tried in India.

A studio on a laptop

Today a teenager with a laptop can do what once needed a room full of machines. Music software lets you record, loop and stack sounds on a screen. Producers also sample: they borrow a short clip of an old recording and build a new song around it.

Indian film music changed in the 1990s when composers like A.R. Rahman mixed synthesizers with folk singers, strings and tabla. Dance music festivals in Goa, Mumbai and Las Vegas now draw huge crowds, and the person on stage is often standing behind a table of glowing buttons.

Some of the newest tools even listen back. Drum machines can shuffle a beat slightly so it feels human, and software can tune a wobbly voice in an instant. Musicians still argue about how much of that is fair, which is the same argument people had about the first synthesizers sixty years ago.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Electronic music isn't real music because a computer plays it.

Truth: People still choose every sound, rhythm and change. Many electronic musicians train for years, and plenty play keyboards, drum pads or controllers live on stage.

Try this

Use a free music app or website that lets you make a beat on a grid. Start with four squares for a drum, then add a sound on every third square and listen to how the pattern shifts. Swap one sound at a time and notice what changes the mood most.
