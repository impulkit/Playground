What does the Election Commission do?

The purple ink painted on Indian voters' fingers comes from one factory in Mysuru, and it has been made there for every general election since 1962.

The short answer

The Election Commission of India is the independent body that runs elections for the Lok Sabha, the Rajya Sabha, state assemblies, and the offices of President and Vice-President. It keeps the list of voters, sets the dates, supervises every polling station and announces the results.

Article 324 of the Constitution gives it its powers. Until 1989 it had just one member. Today it has a Chief Election Commissioner and two Election Commissioners. During an election, crores of government employees, teachers and police officers work under its orders. Its job is to make sure every eligible citizen can vote freely and that every vote counts the same. Elections to village councils and town bodies are run separately by each state's own election commission.

Surprising but true

India's Election Commission was set up on 25 January 1950, one day before the Constitution came into force, so the new republic could start preparing its first election straight away.

Starting from scratch

The Commission was set up on 25 January 1950, a day before India became a republic. That date is now marked as National Voters' Day. Its first chief, Sukumar Sen, a mathematician by training, had to organise the first general election for about 17 crore voters, most of whom had never voted before.

His team made voters' lists by going house to house, designed ballot boxes and party symbols, and trained lakhs of officials. When many women gave their names only as someone's wife or mother, he insisted they be recorded with their own names. Sen later helped run Sudan's first elections too.

The ink that won't wash off

Since 1962, the ink painted on voters' left index fingers has come from Mysore Paints and Varnish, a company in Mysuru once owned by the Maharaja of Mysore. The ink contains silver nitrate, which reacts with skin and light to leave a dark mark that lasts for days or weeks. It stops anyone voting twice. The company also sends the ink to elections in many other countries.

Rules for the campaign

Once elections are announced, a Model Code of Conduct comes into force. Governments can't announce new schemes to win votes, ministers can't use official cars for campaigning, and parties shouldn't appeal for votes on the basis of religion or caste. Campaigning must stop 48 hours before polling.

The code was rarely enforced strictly until T. N. Seshan became Chief Election Commissioner in 1990. He cracked down on illegal posters, overspending and the misuse of government machinery, and became famous across India for it. Politicians complained that he was too strict. Many voters loved him for exactly the same reason, and later commissioners kept many of his rules.

From paper to machines

For decades, Indians voted by stamping paper ballots, which then had to be counted by hand for days. Electronic voting machines were tried in 1982 in Kerala, and in 2004 every Lok Sabha seat used them. Each one runs on a battery, so it works in villages without electricity. The machines are not connected to the internet, and they are stored under guard between polling day and counting day.

Since 2019, every machine has been paired with a VVPAT printer, which shows the voter a paper slip of their choice. Some parties and activists have questioned whether the machines can be trusted. The Commission and the Supreme Court have examined these concerns. So far the courts have upheld the system, while ordering paper slips to be checked against machine counts at some polling stations in every seat.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Election Commission is part of the government in power and takes orders from it.

Truth: The Constitution makes it an independent body, and the Chief Election Commissioner can only be removed in the same difficult way as a Supreme Court judge. Debates do continue about how its members are chosen.
