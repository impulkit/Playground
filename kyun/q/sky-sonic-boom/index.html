What is a sonic boom?

The crack of a whip is a tiny sonic boom. Its tip moves faster than sound, more than 1,200 km/h.

The short answer

Sound moves through air as waves of squeezed and stretched air, spreading out like ripples on a pond. At sea level, sound travels at about 343 metres a second, around 1,235 km/h. That's fast, but jets can go faster.

When a plane flies slower than sound, its pressure waves race ahead of it. When it flies faster than sound, the waves can't get out of the way. They pile up into a cone-shaped shock wave trailing behind the plane. When that cone sweeps over you, the air pressure jumps suddenly and then drops. Your ears hear a loud double bang: a sonic boom.

Surprising but true

A supersonic plane doesn't make a single boom when it "breaks the sound barrier". It drags a boom behind it along its whole path, like a boat's wake.

Breaking the barrier

In the 1940s, many pilots feared that planes would shake apart near the speed of sound. Some test planes had wobbled badly as they got close.

On 14 October 1947, the American pilot Chuck Yeager flew a rocket plane called the Bell X-1 over the Mojave Desert in California. It was dropped from under a big bomber and lit its rocket engines. Yeager reached about 1.06 times the speed of sound, now called Mach 1.06. The plane didn't shake apart. People on the ground heard a boom, the first from a plane flying level faster than sound. The orange X-1 now hangs in the Smithsonian in Washington, DC.

A crack you can make yourself

Sonic booms aren't only made by jets. When a whip cracks, its tip travels faster than sound for a split second, making a mini boom. Bullets from many rifles fly faster than sound too, making a crack as they pass.

In 2012, the skydiver Felix Baumgartner jumped from a balloon about 39 km up and fell faster than sound in the thin air. Thunder works in a related way: lightning heats air so fast that it expands with a shock wave, which is why thunder can crack so sharply nearby.

Why does thunder rumble?Thunder is the sound of air being heated, in a split second, to several times hotter than the surface of the Sun.

The boom that grounded Concorde

From 1976 to 2003, the British-French Concorde carried passengers at about twice the speed of sound. It could fly from London to New York in about three and a half hours, roughly half the usual time.

But it was loud. In 1973, the USA banned civil planes from flying faster than sound over land, because booms rattled windows and startled people and animals. Many other countries made similar rules. So Concorde could only go supersonic over the ocean. It was costly to run, and after a crash in 2000 and a slump in air travel, it was retired in 2003.

The cloud that isn't a boom

You may have seen photos of a jet wrapped in a white cone of cloud. It looks like the moment of breaking the sound barrier, but it isn't quite. The cloud is water vapour condensing as air pressure drops around the plane. It can happen just below the speed of sound, in damp air.

Fighter jets of the Indian Air Force, such as the Rafale and Sukhoi Su-30MKI, can fly at close to twice the speed of sound. Pilots usually do this high up or over the sea, so booms don't disturb towns.

A quieter future

NASA has built a strange-looking jet called the X-59, with a nose about a third of its length. Its shape is designed to spread out the shock waves so that people on the ground hear a soft "thump" rather than a boom. It made its first flight on 28 October 2025.

If tests go well, rules might change and supersonic travel over land could return. Meanwhile, look up after a jet passes and you might see long white lines across the sky. They have nothing to do with booms at all. They're contrails, made of ice crystals.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A sonic boom happens only once, at the moment a plane breaks the sound barrier.

Truth: The boom travels with the plane the whole time it flies faster than sound. People on the ground hear it when the shock wave passes over them, wherever they are along the route.

Try this

Drag your finger slowly through still water in a basin, then quickly. At high speed, the ripples pile up into a V-shaped wake behind your finger. A sonic boom is a 3D version of that V, made of squashed air instead of water.
