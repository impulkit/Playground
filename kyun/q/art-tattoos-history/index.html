How old are tattoos?

A man frozen in the Alps for about 5,300 years was found with 61 tattoos, made by pushing soot or charcoal into the skin.

The short answer

Tattoos are at least about 5,300 years old. We know because of Ötzi, a man who died high in the Alps between Italy and Austria and was frozen in ice until hikers found him in 1991. His skin carries 61 tattoos: groups of short lines and small crosses, made with charcoal or soot. Experts once thought it was rubbed into tiny cuts, but a 2024 study suggests it was more likely poked in with a sharp point.

Since then, tattoos have turned up on ancient bodies from Egypt, Siberia and South America. People have tattooed for beauty, for healing, to show bravery or adulthood, to mark which group they belonged to, and to remember someone they loved.

Surprising but true

The English word "tattoo" comes from the Tahitian word "tatau", picked up by Captain Cook's crew in 1769.

The Iceman's mystery marks

Ötzi's tattoos aren't pictures. They're simple lines on his lower back, knees, ankles and wrists. Here's the strange part: many of them sit over joints where scans show he had wear and pain. Some researchers think the marks were part of a treatment, a little like acupuncture. Others say we can't be sure.

Ötzi was about 45, carried a copper axe and had eaten a meal of meat and grain shortly before he died. Scientists have studied him so closely that we know more about his body than about many people alive today.

Egypt's hidden pictures

For years, people thought ancient tattoos were only dots and lines. Then, in 2018, scientists at the British Museum used infrared light to look at two mummies from Gebelein in Egypt, about 5,000 years old. A man had a wild bull and a sheep tattooed on his arm. A woman had S-shaped marks on her shoulder.

These are the oldest picture tattoos known. In later ancient Egypt, several female mummies carry patterns of dots and lines, and one from about 3,000 years ago has more than 30 tattoos, including eyes, flowers and cows.

How were mummies made?Egyptian embalmers pulled out the brain through the nose with a hook, but left the heart exactly where it was.

Tatau and moko

The word "tattoo" came to English from Tahiti. Sailors on Captain James Cook's ship in 1769 wrote down the Tahitian word "tatau". Many sailors returned home with tattoos of their own.

Across the Pacific, Polynesian tattooing is ancient and sacred. In Samoa, the pe'a covers a man from the waist to the knees and can take weeks to finish, using combs of bone tapped with a stick. Māori tā moko in New Zealand carves lines into the skin of the face, showing a person's family and history.

Tattoos in India

India has long tattoo traditions, especially among tribal communities. In central and eastern India, women of communities like the Baiga and Gond wear godna, patterns of dots and lines. Many believed godna were the only ornaments that stayed with you after death.

In Arunachal Pradesh, older Apatani women have facial tattoos, a custom that stopped in the 1970s. Among the Konyak Nagas, face tattoos once showed a warrior's deeds. Today these traditions are fading, but young artists are reviving the old patterns. A gentler cousin, mehendi, stains only the top layer and fades in a couple of weeks.

Ink that won't wash away

In 1891, a New York tattoo artist named Samuel O'Reilly patented an electric tattoo machine, adapted from an electric pen designed by Thomas Edison. It made tattooing much faster.

A tattoo needle puts ink into the dermis, the layer under the outer skin. Your body treats the ink as an invader. Special cleaning cells of the immune system, called macrophages, swallow the ink but can't break it down. When those cells die, new ones swallow the same ink again, so the picture stays put for decades.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Tattoos are a modern fashion that started with sailors.

Truth: Sailors helped spread tattoos in Europe and America in the 1700s and 1800s, but people in Asia, Africa, the Americas and the Pacific had been tattooing for thousands of years before that.

Try this

Draw a design on paper first, then copy it onto your hand with a washable, skin-safe marker or a little mehendi with an adult's help. Check it each day and see how many days it lasts as your skin's outer layer slowly sheds.
