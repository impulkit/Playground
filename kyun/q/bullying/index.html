What should you do about bullying?

In a Canadian playground study, when another child stepped in, bullying often stopped within about ten seconds.

The short answer

Bullying is when someone keeps hurting another person on purpose, and the person being hurt finds it hard to make it stop. It can be hitting or pushing, name-calling, leaving someone out, spreading rumours, or sending nasty messages online.

If it is happening to you, the most useful step is to tell an adult you trust: a parent, a teacher, a school counsellor or an older relative. If it keeps going, tell another adult, and another. If you see it happening to someone else, there are small, safe things you can do that make a real difference.

Surprising but true

Researchers who filmed school playgrounds found that other children were watching in most bullying episodes, which means bystanders hold a lot of power.

What makes it bullying

Friends sometimes argue, and people sometimes say mean things once and feel sorry later. Researchers usually call something bullying when three things are true. It is on purpose. It happens again and again. And one side has more power, because they are older, stronger, more popular or part of a group.

That power gap is why "just sort it out yourselves" so rarely works. The person being bullied is not on equal ground. One of the first people to study this carefully was a Norwegian psychologist, Dan Olweus, who began surveying schoolchildren in the 1970s. His work helped schools across the world see bullying as a problem for the whole school, not just two children.

If it is happening to you

Bullying is never your fault, whatever the bully says. It can feel embarrassing to tell, but adults who work with children hear about it often. These steps help:

  • - Tell someone. Say what happened, when and where, and who saw it. Adults can do things children cannot, like changing seating or talking to parents.
  • - Keep a record. Write down dates. For cyberbullying, take screenshots before blocking or reporting.
  • - Stay near friends in the places where it tends to happen, like corridors, buses or the lunch queue.
  • - Try not to hit back. It is a fair feeling, but it often gets the wrong person into trouble.

Bullying can cause real stress: stomach aches, trouble sleeping, not wanting to go to school. Those are signals worth sharing with a parent or doctor too.

The power of the people watching

When researchers in Toronto filmed playgrounds, they found other children were watching in most bullying episodes. Usually they did nothing, or even laughed. But when someone did step in, the bullying often stopped within about ten seconds.

You don't have to be a hero. You can refuse to laugh, walk away with the person being picked on, say "Come on, let's go," or tell a teacher later. Sitting next to someone who has been left out is a quiet way of standing up for them. All of this comes from empathy, the ability to sense how a situation feels from the inside.

What schools and governments do

In India, the CBSE has asked its schools to set up anti-bullying committees, and colleges have strict rules against ragging, the hazing of new students. In the United States, every state has an anti-bullying law or policy. Schools that do best tend to have clear rules, adults who watch the playground and lunchroom, and children who know exactly whom to tell.

Many schools now train older students to look out for younger ones, and some use a box or an app where children can report bullying without giving their name. Finland's KiVa programme, developed at the University of Turku, is used in schools in many countries.

Plenty of grown-ups were bullied as children, and many of them say the moment that changed things was when one person, a friend or a teacher, simply noticed.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Bullying is a normal part of growing up, and it makes children tougher.

Truth: Studies in many countries link being bullied to worry, poor sleep, headaches and falling marks, and the effects can last for years. It is common, but it is not harmless, and adults are right to take it seriously.

Try this

With a friend or family member, think of three short, calm sentences you could say if you saw someone being picked on, such as "Leave it, let's go" or "That's not funny." Practise saying them out loud in an ordinary voice. Having words ready makes it much easier to speak up when it matters.
