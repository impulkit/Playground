Why are the Galápagos Islands special?

On these volcanic islands on the equator, lizards swim in the sea, penguins live in the tropics and tortoises outweigh motorbikes.

The short answer

The Galápagos Islands are a group of volcanic islands in the Pacific Ocean, on the equator, about 1,000 kilometres west of Ecuador, the country they belong to. They have never been joined to any continent. Every plant and animal that lives there arrived by flying, swimming, floating or being blown across the sea.

Once they arrived, the newcomers were cut off. Over thousands and millions of years, they changed to suit each island, and many became species found nowhere else. The islands became famous after Charles Darwin visited in 1835, because their animals helped him think about how living things change over time, the idea of evolution. Today they are a national park and a living laboratory for scientists.

Surprising but true

The Galápagos penguin is the only penguin that lives wild north of the equator, kept cool by cold ocean currents.

Born from volcanoes

The Galápagos sit above a hotspot, a place where hot rock rises from deep inside the Earth and punches up through the sea floor as volcanoes. The ocean floor slowly slides over the hotspot, carrying older islands away, much like the Hawaiian islands.

The youngest islands, Fernandina and Isabela in the west, are still active. Their black lava fields look almost bare. The older islands in the east have had time to wear down and grow forests. One recent eruption on Fernandina sent lava flowing into the sea in 2020.

Swimming lizards and tropical penguins

The marine iguana is the only lizard in the world that feeds in the sea. It dives to graze on seaweed, then lies on black rocks to warm up in the sun. It sneezes salt out through its nose, which leaves white crusts on its head. It is one of the oddest ocean reptiles alive.

Galápagos penguins live farther north than any other penguins. Cold currents from the south and from deep water keep the sea around the western islands chilly enough for them. Nearby swim sea lions, and on land live flightless cormorants, whose wings have shrunk to stubs because they had no predators to fly from.

What happens when animals move to new places?In 1935, about a hundred toads were shipped to Australia to eat beetles. Today there are many millions, spread across a huge part of the country.

Giant tortoises

The islands were named after their giant tortoises. In old Spanish, galápago was a word for tortoise. The biggest can weigh over 200 kilograms and live for more than a hundred years. Tortoises on wet, grassy islands have dome-shaped shells. On dry islands, where food grows higher up, some have saddle-shaped shells that let them stretch their necks up to reach cactus pads.

Sailors once took thousands of tortoises onto ships as fresh meat, because they could survive months without food or water. The last tortoise from Pinta Island, nicknamed Lonesome George, died in 2012.

Watching evolution happen

When Darwin visited, he noticed that mockingbirds were different on different islands, and the islanders told him they could tell which island a tortoise came from by its shell. Back in England, an expert told him that his collection of small brown birds were all finches, closely related but with very different beaks.

In 1973, the scientists Peter and Rosemary Grant began measuring finches on the tiny island of Daphne Major, and they kept going for about forty years. After a drought in 1977, they saw the average beak size grow in a single generation.

The islands' biggest threats today are newcomers. Goats, rats and cats brought by people ate plants, eggs and chicks. Park rangers have removed goats from several islands, one of the largest efforts against invasive species ever attempted. Visitors are limited and must stay on marked paths with a trained guide.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Darwin worked out his theory of evolution while looking at finches on the Galápagos.

Truth: Darwin collected finches there but didn't label well which island each came from, and he didn't realise at first that they were closely related. His ideas grew over years back in England, with help from other scientists who studied his specimens.
