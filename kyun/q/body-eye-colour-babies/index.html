Why do some babies' eyes change colour?

Many babies born with blue or grey eyes have brown or green eyes by their first birthday, and there is no blue paint in a blue eye at all.

The short answer

The coloured ring in your eye is called the iris. Its colour comes mainly from melanin, the same brown pigment that colours skin and hair. Lots of melanin makes brown eyes. Less makes hazel or green. Very little makes blue or grey.

Many babies are born before their irises have made much melanin. Over the first months of life, special cells in the iris start making more. So a baby born with blue-grey eyes may end up with green, hazel or brown eyes by around their first birthday. Sometimes the change carries on slowly until age three.

In India and across much of Asia and Africa, most babies are born with dark eyes, and they stay dark.

Surprising but true

Blue eyes contain no blue pigment. They look blue for the same reason the sky does, because of the way light scatters.

Blue without blue paint

Here is the strange part. Blue eyes have no blue pigment at all. The front layer of a blue iris has very little melanin. When light enters it, tiny fibres scatter short, blue wavelengths more than long, red ones. Some of that blue light bounces back out to someone looking at you.

It is the same effect that makes the sky blue: air scatters blue sunlight more than red. Green eyes are a mix, with a little yellowish pigment plus scattered blue. Brown eyes have so much melanin that it soaks up most of the light, so they look brown in every lighting.

That's also why light eyes can seem to change shade with clothes, weather or lighting. The structure stays the same, but the light around them changes.

Why babies start light

Melanin is made by cells called melanocytes. Before birth, in the darkness of the womb, these cells in the iris have little reason to work hard. After birth, light and the baby's growing body switch them on.

How much melanin they end up making depends on genes from both parents. If the genes say "make a lot", the eyes darken quickly, maybe within weeks. If they say "make only a little", the eyes stay blue. Doctors usually say a baby's eye colour is fairly settled by about a year old.

Babies' eyes are changing in other ways too. A newborn sees mostly blurry shapes, best at about 20 to 30 cm, roughly the distance to a parent's face during feeding.

Many genes, many shades

At school, you might hear that eye colour follows a simple rule: brown beats blue. Real life is messier. Scientists have found more than a dozen genes that affect eye colour, and some research points to many more.

Two of them, called OCA2 and HERC2, matter most. In 2008, scientists in Copenhagen reported that blue-eyed people seem to share one small change near the OCA2 gene. They suggested it first appeared in a single person who lived roughly 6,000 to 10,000 years ago.

About 70 to 80 per cent of people in the world have brown eyes. Blue eyes are common mainly in northern Europe. Green eyes are among the rarest, found in only a small slice of people worldwide.

Two colours at once

A few people have eyes of two different colours, or one iris with two colours in it. This is called heterochromia. It is usually harmless and simply means melanin is spread unevenly. Huskies, cats and some horses often have it too.

If an older child or adult's eye colour suddenly changes, though, it is worth seeing a doctor, because it can sometimes be a sign of an eye problem. For babies, a slow change is normal and expected.

Melanin does more than colour eyes. It also decides the shade of your skin and hair, and it shields your body from the Sun's ultraviolet light. That's why people whose ancestors lived in sunny places tend to have darker skin and eyes.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Eye colour is decided by one gene, and brown always beats blue.

Truth: Many genes work together to set eye colour. That's why two blue-eyed parents can, rarely, have a child with brown or green eyes, and why there are so many in-between shades.

Try this

Fill a clear glass with water and add a few drops of milk. Shine a torch through the side in a dark room and look at the glass from the side. It looks slightly blue, while the light coming out the far end looks orange. Tiny particles scatter blue light most, the same trick that makes some eyes look blue.
