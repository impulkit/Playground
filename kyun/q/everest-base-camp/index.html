What is Everest Base Camp like?

Everest Base Camp in Nepal sits on a moving glacier, so the ground under climbers' tents creaks, cracks and shifts all season.

The short answer

Everest Base Camp is a seasonal tent village high in the Himalaya, where climbers prepare to scale the world's highest mountain. There are two. The busier South Base Camp in Nepal sits at about 5,364 metres on the Khumbu Glacier. The North Base Camp in Tibet, reached by road, is at about 5,150 metres.

In spring, hundreds of colourful tents spread across the rocky ice. It is cold, windy and dusty, with thin air that leaves visitors puffing after a few steps. Yet it also has kitchens, medical tents, satellite internet and prayer flags snapping in the wind. For tens of thousands of trekkers every year, simply reaching base camp is the adventure.

Surprising but true

You can't see the summit of Mount Everest from the Nepal base camp itself. Other peaks block the view, so trekkers climb a nearby hill called Kala Patthar to see it.

The trek in

Most people reach the Nepal side on foot. The trip usually starts with a short flight to Lukla, a village with a famously short, sloping runway on a mountainside. From there, it takes about eight or nine days to walk up, with rest days built in.

The trail follows the Dudh Kosi river valley through pine forests and over swaying suspension bridges. It passes through Namche Bazaar, a Sherpa town built in a horseshoe on a hillside, with bakeries, gear shops and a weekly market. Higher up, trees disappear, and so do most plants. Yaks and porters carry supplies, as there are no roads. Some porters carry loads heavier than a large suitcase on their backs, held by a strap across the forehead.

Life on the glacier

Base camp sits on the Khumbu Glacier, a slow river of ice covered in rocks. Because the ice moves, tent platforms have to be rebuilt every season. At night, climbers hear the ice creak and the rumble of distant avalanches.

Expeditions arrive in April and stay for six weeks or more. Cooks prepare dal bhat, noodles, eggs and soup in big tents. Water comes from melted snow and ice. Before climbing, many teams hold a puja, a ceremony led by a Buddhist lama, asking the mountain for safe passage. Climbers and Sherpas throw rice, hang prayer flags and share sweets.

Thin air

At base camp, each breath holds roughly half the oxygen it would at sea level. Visitors get headaches, sleep badly and lose their appetite. That is why treks build in rest days, so the body can adjust to the altitude.

Climbers heading for the top spend weeks going up and down between higher camps to get used to it. Above base camp lies the Khumbu Icefall, a jumbled maze of ice blocks and deep cracks. Sherpa specialists called "icefall doctors" fix ladders and ropes across it every season.

The Sherpa story

Many of the people who make Everest climbs possible are Sherpas, a mountain people of eastern Nepal whose ancestors came from Tibet. Their bodies are well suited to high altitude, and their skills on the mountain are essential.

In 1953, Tenzing Norgay, a Sherpa who had grown up in the Khumbu region, reached the summit with Edmund Hillary of New Zealand. Today Sherpa guides hold many Everest records, and several have climbed it more than 20 times.

Too many visitors?

Base camp now faces big problems. Crowds leave rubbish and human waste that does not rot in the cold. Nepal now asks climbers to bring rubbish back down, and clean-up teams carry tonnes of waste off the mountain each year.

The glacier itself is thinning as the climate warms, and Nepal's government has talked about moving base camp lower in future. The area is part of Sagarmatha National Park, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1979. Sagarmatha is the Nepali name for Everest. In Tibet, it is called Chomolungma.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Everest Base Camp is near the top of Everest.

Truth: The Nepal base camp is about 3.5 km lower than the summit. Climbers still need several weeks of going up and down between higher camps before trying for the top.

Try this

Count how many breaths you take in one minute while sitting quietly. Then walk briskly up and down some stairs for a minute and count again. At base camp, even walking slowly can feel like that stair climb, because every breath brings in only about half the oxygen you're used to.
