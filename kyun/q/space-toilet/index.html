How do astronauts go to the toilet?

On the International Space Station, yesterday's urine becomes today's drinking water, and the machines now recycle about 98% of it.

The short answer

Astronauts use toilets that work with air instead of gravity. On Earth, waste falls into the bowl and water carries it away. In space, nothing falls. So a space toilet has fans that pull air, and everything with it, into the toilet.

For urine, astronauts use a hose with a funnel on the end. The fan sucks the liquid away. For solid waste, there is a small seat with a hole, and the astronaut holds on with foot straps and thigh bars so they don't drift off. The waste goes into a bag inside a can, which is sealed and swapped when full. It is all much more complicated than pressing a flush, and new astronauts practise on a training toilet on the ground, which has a camera inside to help them line up correctly.

Surprising but true

The toilet NASA sent to the space station in 2020 cost about 23 million dollars.

Nothing falls up there

The International Space Station is falling around the Earth all the time, at about 28,000 km an hour. Everything inside falls at the same speed, so it all seems to float. This is called microgravity.

That makes a toilet tricky. Liquid forms wobbly balls that drift off. Solid waste doesn't drop away from the body. An unlucky astronaut could end up chasing an escaped blob around the cabin with a towel. So engineers design every part to keep waste moving in one direction: into the machine, carried by a stream of air.

From toilet to tap

Water is heavy and costly to launch. Sending a single litre to the space station costs thousands of dollars. So almost nothing is wasted.

The station's urine processor boils urine at low pressure, collects the steam and cleans it through filters and chemical steps. Sweat and the moisture in astronauts' breath are also caught from the air. In 2023, NASA announced that the system was recovering about 98% of all the water. What comes out is cleaner than most tap water on Earth. Astronauts like to joke that today's coffee is yesterday's coffee.

How it used to be done

The first American in space, Alan Shepard, had a problem in 1961. His flight was only 15 minutes long, so nobody planned a toilet. Then the launch was delayed for hours. He was given permission to wet his spacesuit.

On the Apollo missions to the Moon, astronauts used plastic bags with a sticky rim, taped to the body. Urine went out through a tube into space, where it froze into a cloud of glittering crystals. Astronauts said the frozen droplets glittered like stars in the sunlight. The bags were among the least liked parts of the mission.

A toilet worth millions

In 2020, NASA sent a new toilet to the station, called the Universal Waste Management System. It cost about 23 million dollars to develop. It is smaller and lighter than the old one, works better for women astronauts and starts its fan automatically when the lid is lifted.

Waste cans are packed into cargo spacecraft. When those ships are full, they are sent back down to burn up in the atmosphere, like a shooting star made of rubbish.

Going further

On a trip to Mars, lasting perhaps two or three years, there will be no cargo ships to take waste away. Scientists are testing ways to recycle even solid waste, using it to grow plants or turn it into useful materials. India's Gaganyaan crew module will also need its own toilet system for longer missions. And the next problem for astronauts, once they have gone to the toilet, is getting some rest while floating, which is why they sleep in bags strapped to the wall.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Astronauts just let their waste float out into space.

Truth: Almost nothing is thrown out that way. Urine is cleaned and recycled into drinking water, and solid waste is packed into containers that are later loaded onto cargo ships, which burn up when they re-enter Earth's atmosphere.

Try this

See how air can carry things. Put some small pieces of tissue paper on a table, then hold the nozzle of a vacuum cleaner a few centimetres above them, with an adult's help. Notice how the airflow pulls them up without touching them. A space toilet uses the same idea with a gentler, steady stream of air.
