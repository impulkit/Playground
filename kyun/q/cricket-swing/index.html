Why does a cricket ball swing in the air?

Bowlers polish one side of the ball on their trousers. That's not just a habit. It's physics.

The short answer

Air flowing past the ball behaves differently on the shiny side and the rough, seamed side.

That difference in airflow creates a sideways push, so the ball curves. A good swing bowler can make it move tens of centimetres in the air, enough to beat the edge of a bat.

Reverse swing was mastered by Pakistani fast bowlers in the 1980s and 1990s, and confused batters for years.

Surprising but true

The same airflow science helps engineers design aeroplane wings and racing cars.

The air around the ball

As a ball flies, a very thin layer of air, called the boundary layer, clings to its surface. At some point this layer peels away, leaving a messy wake behind the ball.

When the seam is tilted, it trips the air on one side into swirling, turbulent flow. Strangely, turbulent air clings to the ball for longer before peeling off. On the other, smoother side, the air peels off sooner. So the wake behind the ball is pushed to one side, and the ball is pushed the opposite way, towards the seam side. The same ideas about air clinging to curved surfaces help engineers design aeroplane wings.

Why polish one side?

Bowlers rub one side of the ball on their trousers to keep it smooth and shiny, and let the other side get rough. With an older ball, the difference between the two sides helps it swing even when the seam is not doing all the work.

For many years, bowlers used sweat and saliva to help polish the ball. In 2020, because of COVID-19, the ICC stopped players using saliva, and in 2022 it made the ban permanent. Sweat is still allowed. Anything else, such as sandpaper or bottle tops, counts as ball-tampering and is against the rules.

Reverse swing

With an old, scuffed ball bowled very fast, something odd happens. The air becomes turbulent on both sides, and the ball swings the opposite way, away from the seam and towards the shiny side. This is reverse swing.

It was developed by Pakistani bowlers such as Sarfraz Nawaz and Imran Khan, and perfected in the 1990s by Wasim Akram and Waqar Younis. They used it to bowl late-swinging yorkers at the batter's toes. Because it comes late in the ball's flight, it is very hard to play. Scientists still argue about exactly what speeds and ball conditions make it happen.

Why weather matters

Many players believe cloudy, humid days help swing. Scientists have tested this, and the evidence is mixed. It's still being argued about. Some think the effect is real but small. Others think cloudy days simply arrive with other helpful conditions, or that bowlers remember the days it worked. In England, where cloud is common, swing bowlers have long had a reputation for thriving.

The bowler's skills

Swing bowlers hold the seam upright and tilted, and release the ball with a steady wrist. A small change in angle changes everything. Too fast, and an ordinary new ball swings less. Too slow, and the airflow never becomes turbulent enough on the seam side.

India has produced fine swing bowlers, from Kapil Dev in the 1980s to Zaheer Khan and Bhuvneshwar Kumar more recently. England's James Anderson took over 700 wickets in Test cricket, more than any other fast bowler. Like the spinners, they turn a tiny change in airflow into a ball the batter never quite reaches.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Swing happens because the ball is spinning, like a curveball.

Truth: Conventional swing is not caused by spin. A swing bowler keeps the seam steady and tilted, and the difference in airflow on the two sides of the ball pushes it sideways. Spin is a separate trick.

Try this

Make a gully-cricket "tape ball" by wrapping electrical tape tightly around half of a tennis ball, leaving the other half fuzzy. Bowl it gently to a friend in an open space, holding the line between taped and fuzzy sides upright. Watch which way it moves. Many famous bowlers in South Asia first learned swing this way.
