What happened to Amelia Earhart?

In July 1937, the world's most famous woman pilot sent a last radio call over the Pacific Ocean. Then she vanished, and nobody has found her plane since.

The short answer

It was the morning of 2 July 1937. Amelia Earhart radioed one of her last messages: "We must be on you, but cannot see you." Then silence. She and her navigator, Fred Noonan, were hunting for Howland Island, a tiny dot in the Pacific. They never arrived.

Nobody knows exactly what happened. The most widely accepted idea is that their plane ran out of fuel and sank into water more than 5 km deep. A second idea says they landed on a reef at another island, Nikumaroro, and lived there for a while as castaways. Almost ninety years of searching have not settled it.

Surprising but true

On her last flight, Amelia Earhart landed in Karachi and Calcutta, and spent nights in both cities before heading on towards the Pacific.

The most famous pilot alive

By 1937, Earhart was a superstar. In 1932 she became the first woman to fly solo across the Atlantic, in about 15 hours. She wrote books, gave talks and designed her own range of clothes.

Then she set out to fly around the world along its fattest part, close to the equator. That meant about 47,000 km. Her silver Lockheed Electra left Miami on 1 June 1937 and flew east across South America, Africa and Asia.

On the way she landed in Karachi, then in Calcutta (now Kolkata), where monsoon rain soaked the airfield. Less than two weeks later she reached Lae, in New Guinea. Only about 11,000 km remained, but the hardest leg came first.

A speck in a very big ocean

From Lae to Howland is about 4,100 km of open water. There were no satellites and no GPS. Noonan used the stars, a compass, a clock and maths to guess their position.

Howland was so small that a navy ship, the Itasca, waited beside it to guide her by radio. The ship could hear Earhart, but she seemed unable to hear it. Her radio direction finder never locked on. Her calls grew louder, which meant she was close, and then they stopped.

Some radio listeners far away, from Florida to Canada, later said they had picked up faint calls from a woman in the days after. Nobody could prove the calls were real.

The US Navy and Coast Guard searched about 650,000 square kilometres of ocean. That is an area about the size of Texas. They found nothing.

Theory one: down in the deep

Most experts think the Electra ran low on fuel, dropped into the sea near Howland and sank. The water there is deep and dark. Finding a small plane on that seabed is like finding a car on the floor of a sea as wide as a country.

In 2024, a company called Deep Sea Vision released a sonar picture that looked like a plane. A closer look later that year showed it was a rock.

Theory two: castaways on a reef

Nikumaroro is a coral atoll about 650 km southeast of Howland. In 1940, a British officer found part of a skeleton there, along with a woman's shoe sole and a box made for a sextant, a tool for navigating by the stars. The bones were measured by a doctor, then lost.

A group called TIGHAR has led more than a dozen trips to the island since 1989. In October 2026, a team from Purdue University checked a plane-shaped blur in the lagoon. It turned out to be a pile of old tree trunks.

The search never stops

Earhart's plane is one of the most hunted objects on Earth. Every new satellite photo, sonar scan or rusty piece of metal brings a fresh wave of hope. Strange disappearances at sea have always gripped people. The oddest of all may be the ship found drifting in 1872 with its crew gone and their belongings still aboard: the Mary Celeste.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Amelia Earhart was captured by Japanese soldiers, and a photo proves it.

Truth: A photo said to show her on a Pacific dock in 1937 was later found printed in a Japanese travel book from 1935, two years before she disappeared. There is no good evidence that she was captured.
