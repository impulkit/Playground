What were the first living things?

For most of Earth's history, the only living things were far too small to see, and they built rocks.

The short answer

The first living things were single cells, far too small to see without a microscope. They lived in water, at least 3.5 billion years ago, when Earth was a very different planet, with no oxygen in the air and no plants on land. They were probably similar in some ways to today's bacteria and other microbes.

The oldest widely accepted fossils of life are stromatolites, layered rocks built by communities of microbes, found in Australia and about 3.5 billion years old. Some scientists think life began even earlier, over 4 billion years ago. Where and how life first appeared is one of the great open questions in science.

Surprising but true

Living stromatolites, rocky mounds built by microbes in layers, still grow today in Shark Bay, Western Australia, much as they did over 3 billion years ago.

A young, strange Earth

Earth formed about 4.5 billion years ago. At first it was battered by asteroids and covered in hot, molten rock. Over time it cooled, oceans formed, and the air filled with gases from volcanoes, including carbon dioxide, water vapour and nitrogen, but almost no oxygen.

Somewhere in this world, simple chemicals began joining into more complex ones. At some point, a few of these were able to copy themselves. Many scientists think an early form of RNA, a close cousin of DNA, could both carry information and do chemical work, and may have come first. This idea is called the "RNA world".

Rocks built by microbes

Stromatolites form when sticky mats of microbes trap sand and mud. The microbes grow up through each layer to reach sunlight, and over years they build mounds, layer on layer, like a pile of pancakes.

Fossil stromatolites about 3.5 billion years old have been found in the Pilbara region of Western Australia. Older signs of life have been claimed from rocks in Greenland and Canada, but scientists still argue about them, because similar patterns can form without life.

India has ancient stromatolites too. Rocks near Udaipur and Chittorgarh in Rajasthan contain stromatolites well over a billion years old. Living ones still grow in Shark Bay, Australia, where the water is too salty for snails that would graze them away.

Where did life begin?

There are two leading ideas. Some scientists think life began at hydrothermal vents on the sea floor, where hot, mineral-rich water provides energy and chemicals. Others favour shallow pools on land that dried out and filled again, concentrating chemicals each time. In 1871, Charles Darwin wondered in a letter whether life might have started in a "warm little pond".

In 1952, Stanley Miller, a student working with Harold Urey at the University of Chicago, tried to copy early Earth in a glass flask. He used water, gases and electric sparks to act like lightning. Within days, the water contained amino acids, building blocks of proteins. It was a major step, but no living thing appeared.

Microbes that changed the air

For billions of years, life stayed small. Some of those microbes, called cyanobacteria, learned to use sunlight to make food by photosynthesis, giving off oxygen as waste. Around 2.4 billion years ago, oxygen began building up in the air, an event called the Great Oxidation.

That oxygen later made larger, more active life possible, including animals. It also changed the rocks. Iron dissolved in the oceans reacted with oxygen and settled on the sea floor as red, rust-coloured layers. Many of the world's big iron ore deposits, including some in India, are ancient banded rocks like these. Every breath you take depends on what those tiny cells started.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Miller-Urey experiment created life in a test tube.

Truth: It made amino acids, simple chemicals that are building blocks of life, but no living thing. No experiment has yet made life from non-living chemicals.

Try this

Take a long strip of paper, such as a roll of receipt paper or taped-together sheets, and mark it as a timeline of 4.5 metres, one metre for each billion years. Mark where the first signs of life appear, about 1 m from the start of Earth, and where animals appear, nearly 4 m in. Dinosaurs and people squeeze into the last few centimetres.
