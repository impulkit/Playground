Who was Claude Monet?

In 1874 a critic mocked Claude Monet's painting Impression, Sunrise, calling the whole group "Impressionists". The insult became the name of an art movement.

The short answer

Claude Monet was a French painter and a leader of Impressionism, a style of art that tried to catch a fleeting moment of light and colour. Instead of smooth, detailed pictures, he used quick, visible brushstrokes. Up close they look like dabs of paint. Step back, and they become shimmering water, fog or sunlight.

He painted outdoors whenever he could and often painted the same scene many times to show how it changed. His water lily paintings, made in his garden at Giverny, are among the most loved artworks in the world.

Surprising but true

Monet painted about 250 pictures of the water lilies in his own garden pond, which he had dug specially, partly so he would have something to paint.

A boy by the sea

Monet was born in Paris in 1840 but grew up in Le Havre, a port on the coast of Normandy. As a teenager he drew funny caricatures of local people and sold them. A landscape painter, Eugène Boudin, saw his work and encouraged him to paint outside, in the open air.

Painting outside had just become easier, thanks to a change in art materials. In 1841 an American painter, John Goffe Rand, invented the squeezable metal paint tube. Before that, artists ground and mixed their paints in the studio and stored them in pig bladders that leaked and dried out. Tubes let Monet carry his colours to beaches, fields and riverbanks.

The insult that stuck

In 1874 Monet and his friends, including Pierre-Auguste Renoir, Camille Pissarro and Berthe Morisot, held their own exhibition in Paris. The official art world had often rejected their work.

One of Monet's paintings showed the harbour at Le Havre at dawn, with an orange sun over hazy blue water. He called it Impression, Sunrise. A critic named Louis Leroy wrote a mocking review and called the painters "Impressionists". They kept the name, and Impressionism became one of the best-known movements in art.

Why is Paris called the City of Light?Paris was one of the first big cities to light its streets at night, in the 1600s. It was also a centre of bold new ideas, and both may have earned it the name City of Light.

Chasing the light

Monet was obsessed with how light changes. In the early 1890s he painted haystacks in a field near his home again and again: in morning mist, at noon, at sunset and under snow. Then he painted the front of Rouen Cathedral more than 30 times at different hours.

He sometimes worked on several canvases at once, swapping them as the sun moved. Up close, he placed colours side by side rather than mixing them, letting your eyes blend them from a distance. A shadow might be blue or purple, not grey, because that's what he saw. That idea fits with how colours mix in light and paint.

The garden at Giverny

In 1883 Monet moved to Giverny, a village west of Paris. He built a large flower garden and later dug a pond, filled it with water lilies and crossed it with a Japanese-style bridge. He loved Japanese woodblock prints and collected many. They still hang in his house, and their flat colours and bold views from above or the side helped shape how he framed his own pictures.

For the last 30 years of his life, the pond was his favourite subject. He made about 250 water lily paintings, some of them huge. Eight giant panels now curve around two oval rooms in the Musée de l'Orangerie in Paris.

Fading eyes, bold colours

In his seventies, Monet developed cataracts, a clouding of the lens inside the eye. Colours looked muddy and yellowish to him, and his paintings became redder and rougher. In 1923 he had surgery, an early version of the operations that now fix eyesight in minutes.

He died at Giverny in 1926, aged 86. His house and garden are now open to visitors. Painters like Van Gogh learned from Impressionism, and in 2019 one of Monet's haystack paintings sold at auction for about 110 million US dollars.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Monet painted blurry pictures because he had bad eyesight.

Truth: He painted in loose brushstrokes for decades before his eyes got worse. Cataracts later in life did change the colours he saw, and his late paintings turned redder and darker, but his style came from choice, not poor vision.
