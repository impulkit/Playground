Who was Aesop?

The man behind the tortoise, the hare and the boy who cried wolf may have been an enslaved storyteller, and he may never have existed.

The short answer

Aesop is the name attached to hundreds of short animal stories, called fables, that end with a lesson. The Tortoise and the Hare, The Fox and the Grapes, and The Boy Who Cried Wolf are all said to be his.

Ancient Greek writers believed Aesop was a real man who lived around 600 BCE. The historian Herodotus, writing more than a century later, says he was enslaved on the island of Samos. Beyond that, his life is a mix of history and legend. Some scholars think "Aesop" became a handy name for any old fable, the way many Indian jokes get pinned on Birbal.

Surprising but true

Scientists have tested the fable of the thirsty crow that drops pebbles into a jug. Some real rooks and crows worked out the same trick.

A storyteller without a voice of his own

According to the stories told about him, Aesop was born into slavery and won his freedom through his wit. In ancient Greece, many households and workshops depended on enslaved people, who had few rights. A clever story was a safe way to make a point about the powerful without naming them.

A later "Life of Aesop", written hundreds of years after his death, describes him as ugly but brilliant, outwitting his master and arguing with kings. Historians treat this book as entertainment rather than fact. It does show what people wanted Aesop to be: proof that wisdom could come from someone with no power at all.

How a fable works

A fable is short. It usually has animals that talk and act like people, a single problem, and a twist. The fox can't reach the grapes, so he decides they were sour anyway. That is where the phrase "sour grapes" comes from.

The lesson, or moral, is often added as a final line. In many cases that line was written by later collectors, not by the original teller. Different versions sometimes draw different morals from the same tale. In The Boy Who Cried Wolf, a shepherd boy keeps shouting "Wolf!" for fun. When a real wolf comes, nobody believes him. The lesson, that liars are not believed even when they tell the truth, needs no explaining.

Animal stories with lessons appear in many cultures. India's Panchatantra and the Buddhist Jataka tales are full of them, and a few plots look very similar to Aesop's. Scholars still debate whether stories travelled along trade routes or grew up separately in different places.

From Greece to the printing press

The first known collection of Aesop's fables was made around 300 BCE by Demetrius of Phalerum, but it is lost. The Roman poet Phaedrus later turned many fables into Latin verse. Copies were made by hand for over a thousand years.

When the printing press arrived in Europe, Aesop became one of the first bestsellers. William Caxton printed an English translation in 1484. Since then, Aesop has been used in schools around the world to teach reading. Indian children often meet him in the same books as the Panchatantra. The tales are so short that teachers can read one aloud in a minute, and so clear that a six-year-old can explain the lesson back.

The crow was right

Some fables describe real animal behaviour. In the 2000s, scientists in England gave rooks a tube of water with a worm floating out of reach. The birds dropped stones in to raise the level. Later studies found crows doing the same. A story told in Greece 2,500 years ago turned out to be a fair test of bird intelligence.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Aesop wrote down all the fables we call Aesop's Fables.

Truth: Nothing written by Aesop survives, and he may have told his stories only aloud. The fables were collected centuries after his time, and later writers added hundreds more.

Try this

Fill a narrow glass halfway with water and float a small piece of cork or a dry leaf on top. Drop in pebbles or marbles one at a time and count how many it takes to bring the cork near the rim. Then you will know how hard the crow had to work.
