How do stunt performers stay safe?

In 1928, the front wall of a house came crashing down over actor Buster Keaton. He survived because he stood exactly where an open window would land, with only a few centimetres to spare.

The short answer

Stunt performers stay safe through planning, practice and clever equipment. Before a big stunt, a team works out every step: distances, speeds, landing spots and what could go wrong. Then they rehearse, often many times, starting slowly.

On the day, they use hidden helpers like airbags, padded mats, wires, harnesses and breakaway props. Cameras are placed to make a short fall look huge, or a near miss look like a hit. Computers can then erase the safety gear. The best stunts look dangerous on screen but are tightly controlled.

Surprising but true

Film windows that shatter in fights used to be made of sugar. Today most "breakaway glass" is a special soft plastic that breaks into harmless chunks.

The stunt coordinator

Every stunt is run by a stunt coordinator. This person designs the action, chooses the performers, checks equipment and has the power to stop filming if anything looks unsafe.

Many stunt performers start in sports like gymnastics, martial arts, diving or horse riding. They learn to fall, roll and take a fake punch without getting hurt. A stunt double who looks like the main actor often performs the riskiest moments.

How to fall

The secret of a safe fall is slowing down gently. When you land on something hard, you stop in an instant, and that sudden stop causes injuries. When you land on a giant airbag or a stack of cardboard boxes, you sink in and slow down over a longer time.

That's the same idea behind car airbags and crash mats in gymnastics. For very high falls, performers use special rigs with cables that slow them down just before they hit the bag. From a 10-storey building, a falling person reaches about 80 km/h, so every detail counts.

The house that fell

One of the most famous stunts ever happened in 1928, in the silent film Steamboat Bill, Jr. The front wall of a house, weighing around two tonnes, fell towards actor Buster Keaton.

He stood exactly where an open attic window would land. The window frame passed around him, with only a few centimetres of space on either side. If he had stood a little out of place, he would have been crushed. Today, no film would allow such a risk. Modern crews would test with dummies, lightweight sets and computer effects.

Fire, glass and wires

For fire scenes, performers wear fireproof suits under their costumes and cover exposed skin with a cold protective gel. Safety teams with extinguishers stand just off camera.

Windows and bottles in fights are made of breakaway materials. Crews once used boiled sugar, which looked like glass and crumbled easily. Today most are a light resin that breaks into blunt pieces.

Wires and harnesses let performers fly, leap between buildings or get thrown across a room. The wires are painted to blend in, or removed later on a computer. Many superhero scenes mix real wire work with special effects.

Stunt stars of India

India has its own long stunt history. In the 1930s, an actress known as Fearless Nadia became a star by doing her own stunts. In the 1935 film Hunterwali, she cracked whips, swung from chandeliers and fought villains, which was rare for a woman on screen at the time.

Today, Bollywood and south Indian film industries hire large teams of action directors and stuntmen. Car chases, motorbike jumps and fight scenes are often filmed with experts from around the world.

Finally, an Oscar

For decades, stunt performers complained that their work went unrewarded at the Academy Awards. In April 2025, the Academy announced a new Oscar for Stunt Design. It will be given for the first time in 2028, at the 100th Academy Awards.

Even so, the best stunts are often the ones you don't notice. A good fall looks easy because of weeks of work you never see, and that hidden effort is one small piece of how a film is made.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Stunt performers are daredevils who take wild risks for the camera.

Truth: Good stunt work is careful planning. Every fall, crash and fight is measured, practised slowly and tested with safety gear many times before the cameras roll. The goal is to look dangerous while being as safe as possible.

Try this

Drop a small toy from table height onto a hard floor, then onto a pillow, then onto a pile of cushions. Notice how the softer landings slow it more gently. Stunt airbags work the same way, stretching out the moment of stopping so the body isn't jolted.
