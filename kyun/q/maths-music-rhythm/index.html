How is rhythm a kind of maths?

Indian poets counting long and short beats discovered the Fibonacci numbers centuries before Fibonacci was born.

The short answer

Rhythm is the pattern of sounds and silences in time. To make a rhythm, you split time into equal beats, then group those beats and divide them into smaller pieces. That is counting, adding and making fractions, all in your ears and feet.

Every music tradition does this. A Hindustani tabla player counts a 16-beat cycle called teentaal. A Carnatic singer keeps an 8-beat cycle with hand claps and finger taps. A rock drummer counts "1, 2, 3, 4". The patterns repeat like times tables, and musicians play with them the way mathematicians play with numbers.

Surprising but true

The scholar Virahanka worked out, sometime around 600 to 800 CE, that counting rhythms of short and long beats gives the sequence 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, now called the Fibonacci numbers.

Cycles that come back home

Indian classical music is built on the tala, a cycle of beats that repeats again and again. In teentaal, the 16 beats are grouped 4 + 4 + 4 + 4. The first beat, called sam, is the "home". Musicians wander away into complicated patterns, and everyone waits to see them land back exactly on sam.

One favourite trick is the tihai: a phrase played three times so that its last note falls on sam. To make it land, the player has to calculate. If the phrase plus a gap is 5 beats long, three of them fill 15 beats, so it must start one beat after sam. Good tabla players do this kind of maths at full speed, without stopping to think.

Fibonacci before Fibonacci

Sanskrit poetry has long and short syllables. A short syllable takes one count, a long one takes two. Poets wanted to know how many patterns could fill a line of a certain length.

Try it. For 1 count, there's one pattern. For 2 counts, two (short-short or long). For 3 counts, three. For 4 counts, five. Then 8, 13, 21. Each number is the sum of the two before it. The scholar Virahanka described this, sometime around 600 to 800 CE. Gopala and Hemachandra wrote about it later. The Italian mathematician Fibonacci wrote about the same numbers in 1202, which is how they got his name. These numbers also turn up in pine cones and sunflowers, as the story of Fibonacci in nature shows.

Even earlier, the scholar Pingala listed rhythm patterns with a method that works like counting in binary, with short and long standing in for 0 and 1.

Fractions you can dance to

In Western music, a note can last a whole beat, half a beat or a quarter of a beat. That's fractions. A "time signature" at the start of a piece, like 3/4, tells players there are three beats in a bar, each a quarter note. A waltz goes ONE-two-three, ONE-two-three. Most pop songs are in 4/4.

Some music uses odd numbers. Folk music from the Balkans often has 7 or 11 beats, grouped as 2 + 2 + 3 or similar. Carnatic music has talas of 5, 7 and 9 beats too.

Spreading beats evenly

In 2005, the computer scientist Godfried Toussaint noticed something surprising. Take a number of beats, like 8, and try to spread 3 strong beats among them as evenly as possible. You get a pattern of 3 + 3 + 2. That is the Cuban tresillo, found in rhythms all over the world. Many famous rhythms turn out to follow the same simple maths rule, one also used inside computers.

Rhythm in your body

Your body keeps rhythms too. Your heart beats about 70 to 100 times a minute at rest in children. You breathe and walk in steady patterns. When a song's beat matches your walking pace, around 100 to 120 beats a minute, you naturally want to tap along.

Doctors listen to the rhythm of the heart for clues about health. Run up a flight of stairs and that drumbeat in your chest speeds up at once, for a reason explained in the story of your heartbeat.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Rhythm is pure feeling, and has nothing to do with maths.

Truth: Feeling matters, but rhythm is built on counting, fractions and repeating patterns. Indian musicians count talas on their fingers, and Western musicians divide each beat into halves, quarters and thirds.

Try this

Clap a pattern of 3 against 2. With your left hand, tap twice evenly while your right hand taps three times evenly in the same time. Say "pass the gol-den but-ter" to help. It feels tricky, but it is just the maths of fitting 3 and 2 into the same space, called a polyrhythm.
