Why do a triangle's angles always add up to 180 degrees?

Tear the three corners off any paper triangle, line them up, and they always make a perfectly straight line.

The short answer

An angle measures how much something turns. A full turn is 360 degrees, so a straight line, which is half a turn, is 180 degrees.

Here is the trick. Take any triangle and draw a line through its top corner that runs parallel to the bottom edge. The three angles at the top now sit side by side along that straight line. Two of them are copies of the bottom two corners, because a line crossing two parallel lines makes matching angles. So the three corners of the triangle together make a straight line: 180 degrees.

It works for every triangle on a flat surface, fat or thin, tiny or huge.

Surprising but true

On a ball-shaped surface like the Earth, you can draw a triangle with three right angles, adding up to 270 degrees.

Euclid's famous proof

About 2,300 years ago, a Greek teacher in Alexandria named Euclid collected the geometry of his time into a set of books called the Elements. Proposition 32 of the first book proves the triangle rule using the parallel-line trick above.

What made Euclid special was his method. He started with a few simple statements everyone agreed on, then built every new fact from the ones before, step by step. The Elements was copied, translated and taught for more than 2,000 years. It reached India, the Arab world and Europe, and parts of it are still in school textbooks today.

Euclid was not the only one working with triangles. In India, the Sulba Sutras were rules for building fire altars, written down well over 2,000 years ago. They already used the geometry of right-angled triangles to lay out exact shapes with ropes and pegs.

The mystery of 360

Nobody knows for sure why a full turn is 360. The idea comes from ancient Babylon, in what is now Iraq. Babylonian astronomers counted in sixties, and 360 is six sixties. It is also close to the number of days in a year, so the Sun moves about one degree a day against the stars.

The number is handy too. It divides evenly by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10 and 12, which makes it easy to split a circle into equal slices.

When the rule breaks

The 180-degree rule only works on a flat surface. On a curved one, things change.

Start at the North Pole and walk straight down to the equator. Turn left by 90 degrees and walk a quarter of the way around the Earth. Turn left by 90 degrees again and walk back to the Pole. You have made a triangle, and every corner is a right angle. The angles add up to 270 degrees.

The bigger a triangle on a ball, the more its angles add up to. A triangle chalked on a school playground is so nearly flat that the extra is far too tiny to measure.

This is why pilots and ship captains use special maths for long journeys. It is also why no flat map of the round Earth can get every shape and distance right.

Triangles everywhere

Once you know the rule, you can use it. If two corners of a triangle are 60 and 70 degrees, the third must be 50. Builders and surveyors have used this for thousands of years to work out angles they can't easily measure.

Triangles are also the strongest simple shape. Push on a square frame and it squashes into a diamond. Push on a triangle and it holds, because its three sides lock the angles in place. That is why railway bridges, electricity towers and bicycle frames are full of them.

The rule also tells you about other shapes. Any flat shape with straight sides can be cut into triangles from one corner. A square makes two triangles, so its angles add to 360 degrees. A pentagon makes three, so its angles add to 540.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A bigger triangle has bigger angles that add up to more than 180 degrees.

Truth: The size of a triangle has nothing to do with its angles. A triangle drawn on a stamp and one drawn across a cricket ground can have exactly the same corners.

Try this

Cut any triangle out of paper and colour each corner a different colour. Tear off the three corners and push their points together on a table. They fit side by side to make a straight line, which is 180 degrees. Try it with a long thin triangle too.
