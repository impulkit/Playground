How do scientists rebuild an animal from its bones?

Bones are only the start. Then comes detective work.

The short answer

Scientists compare dinosaur bones with their closest living relatives: birds and crocodiles.

Bumps on bones show where muscles were. Skin prints and feather fossils show the outside. Footprints show how the animal stood and walked.

Good scientists say clearly which parts are evidence and which parts are guesses. A skeleton is usually solid evidence. The colour of the skin, the shape of soft parts and how the animal behaved are much harder to know.

Surprising but true

Old pictures showed dinosaurs dragging their tails. Footprints showed tails didn't drag, so the pictures changed.

Starting with the skeleton

First, the bones must be cleaned, measured and put in the right order. That is harder than it sounds. Skeletons are rarely complete, and bones from different animals can get mixed up in the rock. Missing parts are often filled in from related animals, or from the opposite side of the same body.

Joints show how bones fitted and moved, a bit like hinges. Scientists check how far each joint could bend, so they can pose the skeleton in a way the living animal could really have stood. Your own bones carry similar clues about how you move.

Adding muscles

Muscles attach to bones with tough tendons, and they leave marks: rough patches, ridges and bumps. By finding these scars on fossil bones, scientists can work out where the big muscles were and roughly how large they were.

Here the living relatives help most. Birds and crocodiles sit on either side of dinosaurs in the family tree. If both have a particular muscle in the same place, dinosaurs very likely had it too. This idea is called the extant phylogenetic bracket. "Extant" just means "still living".

Famous mistakes

Early reconstructions got a lot wrong. Gideon Mantell put Iguanodon's thumb spike on its nose. In 1854, life-size models of Iguanodon and Megalosaurus were built in Crystal Palace Park in London, looking like giant, heavy lizards. They still stand there today. The truth about Iguanodon came in 1878, when coal miners in Bernissart, Belgium, found dozens of skeletons deep underground. The spikes were on the hands. Iguanodon had a pointed thumb, and it walked on all fours as well as on two legs.

For most of the 1900s, T. rex was shown standing upright like a kangaroo, with its tail on the ground. Footprints with no tail drag marks, and studies of its hips and spine, showed it actually held its body level and its tail in the air. Museums have rebuilt their skeletons to match.

New tools

Today, scientists use CT scanners to look inside fossils without breaking them. They can make digital copies, fix crushed bones on a computer and print them in 3D. 3D printing also lets museums share exact copies of rare fossils.

Computer models can test how an animal moved. Scientists add virtual muscles to a scanned skeleton and see whether it can walk, run or swim. That is how teams argue about questions like whether Spinosaurus swam.

The outside layer

Skin is the hardest part. A few dinosaurs left skin impressions, and some feathered ones preserved colour clues. For most, artists must decide how much fat, skin and feather to add. Many older drawings were "shrink-wrapped", with skin stretched tightly over the bones. Real animals have fat, cheeks and fluffy coats. A shrink-wrapped elephant or bird would look very strange.

That is why modern palaeoartists work closely with scientists, and why dinosaur pictures keep changing as new evidence comes in.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Dinosaur models in museums show exactly what the animals looked like.

Truth: The skeleton is usually accurate, but much of the outside is informed guesswork. Colours, fat, frills and feathers on many models are best guesses, and they change when new fossils are found.

Try this

Take a photo of a friend's arm, then draw just the outline of the bones inside (use a picture of a skeleton to help). Now add muscles, skin and clothes on separate sheets of tracing paper laid on top. Which layers did you have to guess? Palaeontologists face the same question with every dinosaur.
