How are giant stadiums built?

The world's biggest cricket ground, in Ahmedabad, can seat about 1,32,000 people, more than live in many Indian towns.

The short answer

A giant stadium is a bowl of seats wrapped around a field. Almost every part of it is designed for one job: letting tens of thousands of people see clearly and get in and out safely.

Builders start with the ground. Huge foundations, often concrete piles driven deep into the soil, carry the weight. Then come the stands, built from steel frames or concrete sections, often made in factories and lifted into place by cranes. The seats rise steeply so every row can see over the one in front.

Last comes the roof, the hardest part. It has to cover the crowd without a single pillar in the way.

Surprising but true

Many top football and cricket pitches aren't pure grass. Real grass is woven together with millions of plastic fibres to make it tougher.

Older than you'd think

People have built stadiums for thousands of years. The ancient Greeks raced in stadiums at Olympia. In Rome, the Colosseum opened in 80 CE and held perhaps 50,000 people. It had numbered entrances so crowds could find their seats quickly, and a huge canvas awning, pulled across by sailors, to shade spectators from the sun.

Many of its ideas survive. Modern stadiums still use numbered gates, steep banks of seats and passages under the stands, called vomitories, that let crowds pour in and out.

What happened in the Colosseum?Under the Colosseum floor was a maze of tunnels and lifts that could raise a lion straight up into the arena, as if by magic.

The roof problem

A roof over a crowd is like a huge lid. Pillars would hold it up easily, but they'd block the view. So engineers use cantilevers: roofs held down firmly at the back of the stand that stick out over the seats like a diving board. Cables or steel trusses carry the weight back to strong supports behind the crowd.

London's Wembley Stadium hangs part of its roof from a steel arch 133 metres high, which you can see from miles away. Some stadiums in the USA have roofs that slide open and shut in good or bad weather.

Biggest of all

The Narendra Modi Stadium in Ahmedabad, rebuilt and reopened in 2021, can seat about 1,32,000 people, making it the largest cricket stadium in the world. Its roof is held up so that no pillars stand in front of the seats.

In the USA, the biggest is Michigan Stadium, known as "The Big House", with over 1,07,000 seats. Kolkata's Eden Gardens, one of India's oldest grounds, has hosted cricket since the 1860s.

Grass under a roof

Grass needs sunlight, air and water, and a stadium's walls and roof block much of that. Groundstaff use giant grow lamps on wheels to light the turf at night, and fans under the field to move air through the soil.

Many big grounds now use hybrid grass. Machines stitch millions of plastic fibres deep into the soil, and the grass roots wrap around them. The pitch looks and plays like natural grass but doesn't tear up as easily when players slide or stop suddenly. At a few stadiums, like the one in Glendale, Arizona, the whole grass pitch sits on a giant tray that rolls outside to soak up the sunshine.

Getting everyone out

Engineers plan exits as carefully as seats. In many countries, safety rules say a full stand should be able to empty within a few minutes in an emergency. That means wide stairways, many gates and clear signs. Ramps, lifts and wheelchair spaces with clear views help disabled fans and older visitors too. After big events, it can still take an hour for trains and roads to clear.

Building a stadium takes years and teams of engineers, architects and thousands of workers. The best ones feel simple when you're inside: you find your seat, look out, and the whole field is right in front of you.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The grass at big stadiums is always fake plastic turf.

Truth: Most top football and cricket grounds use real grass. Many reinforce it with plastic fibres stitched into the soil, so the roots grip tighter, but it is still living grass that has to be mowed and watered.

Try this

Make a cantilever with a ruler and a stack of books. Hold one end of the ruler under the books and let the rest stick out over the edge of a table. Hang a small bag of coins from the free end and see how far out you can push the ruler before it tips. Add more books and try again.
