Why are stars different colours?

Blue stars are the hottest and red stars the coolest, the opposite of the red-hot and cold-blue colours we use on taps.

The short answer

A star's colour tells you how hot its surface is. Everything that is hot enough glows, and the colour of that glow changes with temperature. Cooler stars glow red or orange. Medium ones, like our Sun, look white or yellowish. The hottest stars glow blue.

You can see the same thing on Earth. An iron rod in a blacksmith's fire first glows dull red, then bright orange, then yellow-white as it heats up. A star is a giant ball of glowing gas, and the same rule applies. Astronomers can look at a star's colour and work out its temperature without ever going near it.

Surprising but true

Seen from space, the Sun is white, not yellow. Earth's air scatters some of its blue light away, which tints it yellowish from the ground.

Hot things glow

Everything gives off light, even you. At body temperature, that light is infrared, which our eyes can't see but heat cameras can. Heat something to about 500 °C and it starts to glow a faint red. Keep going and the glow shifts to orange, yellow, white and finally blue-white.

The reason is that hotter objects give off more energetic light, with shorter wavelengths. Red light has long waves. Blue light has short ones. A cool star pours out mostly red and infrared light. A very hot star pours out more blue and ultraviolet.

The same shift happens at home. The coil of an electric heater glows dull red when it is warm and brighter orange as it gets hotter. A gas flame burns blue at its hottest point and yellow where it is cooler and sootier.

Reading the colours

Red stars have surfaces of around 2,500 to 3,500 °C. That is still hot enough to melt iron, but cool for a star. The Sun's surface is about 5,500 °C. Blue giants can be 10,000 to more than 40,000 °C.

In the early 1900s, astronomers at Harvard sorted hundreds of thousands of stars by the patterns in their light. Annie Jump Cannon, who was partly deaf, could classify a star in seconds, and she sorted around 350,000 in her lifetime. Her system put stars into classes O, B, A, F, G, K and M, from hottest to coolest. Students remember the order with the line "Oh Be A Fine Girl, Kiss Me" or "Oh Be A Fine Guy, Kiss Me". The Sun is a G star.

Why no green stars?

Every star shines across a whole rainbow of colours at once. Its colour is just the shade that's strongest. A star whose light peaks in the green part of the spectrum still gives off plenty of red and blue too. The mix looks white to our eyes, which is why the Sun, whose light peaks in the blue-green, looks white from space.

So you won't see a green or purple star. Some double stars look greenish next to a strongly orange partner, but that's a trick of contrast, the way a grey square looks bluish beside a bright orange one.

Colours you can spot tonight

Most stars look white to the naked eye. In dim light, our eyes use cells that can't see colour well. The brightest stars, though, show their colours clearly.

In the constellation of Orion, reddish Betelgeuse marks one shoulder, and blue-white Rigel marks the opposite knee. Indian astronomers knew Betelgeuse as Ardra, one of the 27 nakshatras. Orange Aldebaran, in Taurus, is Rohini. In the summer sky, red Antares shines in Scorpius. Its Greek name means "rival of Mars" because its colour matches the red planet.

Colour also tells a star's fate. Blue stars burn through their fuel in a few million years, while small red ones can shine for far longer than the universe has existed so far. Their whole life cycle is written partly in their colour.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Sun is a yellow star.

Truth: The Sun gives off all colours of light, and together they look white. It only looks yellow, orange or red from Earth because our atmosphere scatters away some of the blue, especially near sunrise and sunset.

Try this

On a clear night, find Orion, the hunter, with its three stars in a row for a belt. Look at the bright star at his top left shoulder and the one at his bottom right knee. Without a telescope, try to see which looks orange and which looks blue-white. A pair of binoculars makes the colours stronger.
