How did Niagara Falls form?

Niagara Falls is moving. Over about 12,000 years it has eaten its way about 11 km upriver, and in 1969 engineers even switched part of it off.

The short answer

Niagara Falls formed at the end of the last ice age, about 12,000 years ago. Thick ice sheets had covered the area and carved out the Great Lakes. As the ice melted, water from Lake Erie began flowing north to Lake Ontario through the Niagara River. On the way, it tumbled over a steep cliff of rock called the Niagara Escarpment.

At first the falls were near where the towns of Lewiston and Queenston stand today. Since then, the water has worn away the rock and pushed the falls about 11 km upriver, leaving a deep gorge behind.

Surprising but true

In 1901, a 63-year-old teacher named Annie Edson Taylor became the first person to go over Niagara Falls in a barrel and survive.

A hard lid on soft rock

The secret of Niagara lies in its layers of rock. At the top is a hard rock called dolostone. Underneath are softer layers of shale and sandstone. The falling water churns at the bottom and scours away the soft rock, leaving the hard top layer hanging out over empty space. In time, the overhang cracks and falls, and the edge moves back.

This used to happen fast, about a metre a year. Today much of the river's water is taken out before it reaches the falls, to make electricity, so the erosion is much slower.

Three falls, two countries

Niagara is really three waterfalls on the border between Canada and the USA. The biggest is the Horseshoe Falls, mostly in Canada, about 51 m high and curved like a horseshoe. The American Falls and the small Bridal Veil Falls lie on the US side, separated by Goat Island.

In the tourist season, up to about 2,800 cubic metres of water pour over the falls every second during the day. That would fill an Olympic swimming pool in about one second. At night, a treaty between Canada and the USA lets power companies take even more water.

Lighting up a city

Water falling that far carries a huge amount of energy. In 1895, a power station opened at Niagara using alternating current, a system developed by Nikola Tesla and built with the Westinghouse company. In 1896, it sent electricity about 40 km to the city of Buffalo. It was one of the first big hydroelectric plants in the world, and it showed how falling water could power whole cities.

Who was Nikola Tesla?The electricity flowing into your home right now travels as alternating current, a system Nikola Tesla helped design in the 1880s.

Switching it off

By the 1960s, piles of fallen rock were building up at the foot of the American Falls. In 1969, the US Army Corps of Engineers built a temporary dam upstream and turned the American Falls off for about five months. Visitors walked on the dry riverbed. Engineers and geologists studied the rocks and cracks. In the end, they decided to let nature take its course and reopened the falls.

Daredevils and boats

Niagara has always drawn daredevils. In 1859, Charles Blondin walked across the gorge on a tightrope. In 1901, Annie Edson Taylor, a 63-year-old teacher, went over the Horseshoe Falls in a wooden barrel and survived. Stunts like hers are now against the law.

Since 1846, boats have carried visitors close to the bottom of the falls, into the thundering spray. Passengers wear raincoats, and still come back soaked.

Ice, mist and rainbows

In very cold winters, spray freezes onto rocks, trees and railings, coating them in thick ice. Huge mounds of ice, called ice bridges, can form at the bottom. People once walked across them, until a deadly collapse in 1912 led to a ban. On sunny days the mist rising from the falls catches the light, and rainbows hang over the gorge for hours.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Niagara Falls freezes solid in very cold winters.

Truth: Ice can build up into huge sheets and mounds, but water keeps flowing underneath. The falls only stopped once, in March 1848, when an ice jam upstream on the river blocked the water for about a day.

Try this

Make a waterfall that moves. In a tray tilted on a book, build a step of hard clay on top of a layer of soft sand. Pour water slowly over the edge and watch. The sand washes out under the clay, the overhang breaks off, and the "waterfall" moves backwards.
