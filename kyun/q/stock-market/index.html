What is the stock market?

Asia's oldest stock exchange began in the 1850s with a few traders meeting under a banyan tree in Bombay.

The short answer

A stock market is a place where people buy and sell shares in companies. A share is a tiny piece of a company. If a company is split into a crore shares and you own one, you own a crore-th of it.

Companies sell shares to raise money to grow, build factories or develop new products. After that, investors trade the shares with each other on a stock exchange. Prices go up when more people want to buy a share than sell it, and down when more want to sell. Today almost all of this happens on computers, in fractions of a second.

Surprising but true

The New York Stock Exchange traces its start to 1792, when 24 brokers signed an agreement under a buttonwood tree on Wall Street.

From ships to shares

Selling shares is a way to share risk. In the 1600s, sending a ship to Asia for spices was hugely expensive and dangerous. Ships sank or were captured. So merchants pooled their money.

In 1602, the Dutch East India Company began selling shares to the public in Amsterdam. Anyone could buy a small part, and people traded these shares with each other. Many historians see this as the start of the modern stock market. Britain's East India Company, which later ruled much of India, was also owned by shareholders.

Under the trees

In the 1850s, a handful of brokers in Bombay began meeting under a banyan tree near the Town Hall to trade shares. As the group grew, they moved from street to street. In 1875, they formed an association that became the Bombay Stock Exchange, the oldest in Asia. It now stands in a tall tower on Dalal Street in Mumbai.

New York's story is similar. In 1792, 24 brokers signed an agreement under a buttonwood tree on Wall Street, setting rules for trading. That group became the New York Stock Exchange.

For a long time, trading meant brokers shouting and waving on a crowded floor. India's National Stock Exchange, set up in 1992, brought trading by computer screen to the whole country. Since 2020, the number of Indians with share-trading accounts has more than quadrupled, as buying shares on a phone app became easy.

How did a trading company end up ruling India?A group of London merchants set out to buy pepper. Within 200 years, their company ran its own army and governed most of India.

Bulls, bears and indexes

A rising market is called a bull market, and a falling one a bear market. People say a bull attacks by tossing its horns up, while a bear swipes down.

To show how the whole market is doing, people use an index, an average of the prices of a group of big companies. India's best known are the Sensex, which tracks 30 companies on the BSE, and the Nifty 50 on the National Stock Exchange. In the USA, people follow the Dow Jones and the S&P 500.

Booms and crashes

Share prices can rise and fall sharply. In October 1929, the US stock market crashed, and the Great Depression followed. In 1992, India was shaken by a scandal in which a broker used money borrowed illegally from banks to push prices up.

After that scandal, India gave stronger powers to the Securities and Exchange Board of India, or SEBI, which polices the market. In the USA, a similar body, the Securities and Exchange Commission, was set up in 1934, after the 1929 crash.

Over many years, shares in good companies have usually grown more than money kept in a savings account, but there is no guarantee. Most experts advise putting money in for the long term, spreading it across many companies, and never investing money you might need soon.

Myth or fact?

Myth: When you buy shares, you are lending money to the company.

Truth: Buying a share makes you a part-owner. Unlike a lender, you are not promised your money back. If the company does well, your share can grow; if it fails, you can lose what you paid.

Try this

Pick three companies whose products you know, such as a biscuit maker, a car maker and a phone company. With an adult, look up their share prices in a newspaper or app every day for two weeks and draw a line graph for each. Notice how often they rise and fall for no obvious reason.
