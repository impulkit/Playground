Can a jellyfish live forever?

One tiny jellyfish can turn back into a baby.

The short answer

Most animals are born, grow up, grow old and die. One small jellyfish, Turritopsis dohrnii, can skip the last part. When it is hurt, starving or stressed, it can turn its adult body back into its baby form and grow up all over again.

Scientists call it biologically immortal. That doesn't mean it can never die. It can still be eaten by a fish or killed by disease, and most of them are. But it doesn't have to die of old age.

Jellyfish in general are survivors. They have no brain, no heart and no bones, and their bodies are about 95% water. Yet they have been drifting through the oceans for more than 500 million years.

Surprising but true

Jellyfish are older than dinosaurs, sharks and trees.

Two lives in one

Most jellyfish live two very different lives. They start as a tiny polyp stuck to a rock, like a little flower on a stalk. The polyp buds off baby jellyfish, which swim away and grow into the floating bell shape we know, called a medusa.

Normally, a medusa lays eggs and dies. Turritopsis can run the film backwards. Its bell shrinks into a blob, sinks, sticks to the seabed and becomes a polyp again. That polyp then buds off fresh young jellyfish, with the same genes as the old one.

In 1988 a German student called Christian Sommer was keeping these jellyfish in dishes in Italy. He noticed that they kept disappearing and polyps kept appearing in their place. A few years later, scientists confirmed what was happening.

Older than dinosaurs

Jellyfish have soft bodies, so they hardly ever turn into fossils. But in rocks in Utah, USA, scientists have found prints of jellyfish about 505 million years old. Some even show faint traces of tentacles.

That makes jellyfish older than sharks, older than trees and much older than the dinosaurs. Their close cousins, the corals, have been building reefs for hundreds of millions of years too, as you can read in coral reefs.

Simple, but not so simple

Instead of a brain, a jellyfish has a net of nerves spread through its body. It still manages to swim, hunt and dodge danger. Box jellyfish have 24 eyes, arranged in four clusters around the bell. Some of those eyes can form rough pictures.

The tentacles carry thousands of stinging cells. Each one holds a coiled thread with a tiny harpoon. When something brushes it, the harpoon fires out in less than a millionth of a second, one of the fastest movements in nature. Most stings feel like a nettle. A few, like those of some Australian box jellyfish, can be very dangerous.

Jellyfish in space

In 1991, the US space shuttle Columbia carried more than 2,000 moon jellyfish polyps into orbit. In space they multiplied into tens of thousands of baby jellyfish. Back on Earth, some of the space-born jellies had trouble telling which way was down. Jellyfish sense gravity with tiny crystals, and in space those crystals had nothing to fall towards. Astronauts' bodies face a similar puzzle, which you can explore in space and the body.

Today, jellyfish numbers seem to be rising in some seas, perhaps helped by warmer water and overfishing. Huge swarms have clogged fishing nets in Japan and blocked the water pipes of power stations. Sea turtles help keep them in check. A big leatherback turtle can gulp down hundreds of kilograms of jellyfish in a day, which is a lot of jelly for very little food.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Peeing on a jellyfish sting makes it better.

Truth: It doesn't help and can make the sting worse. Lifeguards usually rinse a sting with seawater and remove any tentacles carefully, and a bad sting needs a doctor.

Try this

Fill a bucket with water and drop in a clear plastic bag. Watch it drift and billow. From above it almost disappears, and it looks a lot like a jellyfish. That is why sea turtles, which love to eat jellyfish, sometimes swallow plastic bags by mistake.
