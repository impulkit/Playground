How is a song recorded?

The song you hear in three minutes may be built from more than a hundred separate recordings, made weeks apart, stacked like layers of a cake.

The short answer

Recording a song usually happens in four stages. First, musicians record their parts, often one instrument at a time, into microphones or straight into a computer. Next comes editing: picking the best takes and tidying mistakes. Then mixing, where an engineer balances all the parts so each can be heard. Finally mastering, a last polish that makes the song sound good on everything from phone speakers to cinema systems.

Each part sits on its own track. A simple song might have 20 tracks. A big pop song can have more than a hundred, including many stacked layers of the same voice, to make it sound richer.

Surprising but true

The oldest known recording of a human voice was made in 1860, but nobody could hear it until scientists turned its squiggly lines back into sound in 2008.

The first recorded sounds

In 1860 a French inventor named Édouard-Léon Scott de Martinville recorded someone singing a French folk song, Au clair de la lune. His machine, the phonautograph, scratched sound waves as wiggly lines on paper blackened with soot. It could record, but not play back.

In 2008 scientists in the USA scanned those lines and used computers to turn them back into sound. For the first time in nearly 150 years, a ghostly voice could be heard. Meanwhile, in 1877, Thomas Edison had built the phonograph, the first machine that could record a voice and play it back, starting with "Mary had a little lamb".

One part at a time

For decades, a band recorded all together, live in one room. In old Bombay film studios, singers like Lata Mangeshkar stood at a microphone in front of a whole orchestra. If anyone slipped, everyone started again.

From the late 1940s, the American guitarist Les Paul pioneered recording on top of earlier recordings, called overdubbing. Tape machines that could record several separate tracks followed. In 1967 the Beatles made Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band using four-track tape machines, copying finished tracks together to free up space for more. Today a laptop can hold hundreds of tracks.

Inside the studio

A recording studio has two main rooms. In the live room, musicians play, often wearing headphones so they hear a steady beat called a click track. Walls are covered in soft panels so the sound doesn't bounce around. In the control room, engineers watch the sound as moving waves on a screen.

Singers usually record in a quiet booth. A round mesh screen, called a pop filter, sits in front of the microphone. It stops the puff of air from letters like "p" and "b" thumping into the recording. A singer may sing the same verse ten times, and the engineer stitches together the best lines.

Mixing and mastering

Mixing is a little like cooking. The engineer decides how loud each part should be and places instruments to the left or right of your headphones. Effects like echo can make a voice sound as if it's in a big hall. Small changes in tone help the drums, bass and voice each find their own space.

Computers have changed everything. In 1997 Andy Hildebrand, an engineer who had used maths to search for oil underground, invented Auto-Tune, which nudges off-key notes into tune. In 1998 the singer Cher turned it up high on purpose, making a robotic sound that's now heard everywhere.

From bedroom to the world

Big studios aren't essential any more. A. R. Rahman built his own studio behind his house in Chennai, where he worked on the music for Roja in 1992. In the USA, Billie Eilish and her brother Finneas recorded their first album in his small bedroom, and it won major awards in 2020.

And the strangest part? The microphone, the headphones and the speaker all do the same trick in different directions. They turn wobbles in air into electricity and back, as you'll hear in the story of how sound is recorded.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Singers in recorded songs sing the whole song perfectly in one go.

Truth: Most recorded vocals are put together from several takes. Engineers choose the best version of each line, and sometimes even single words, then join them smoothly. Live concerts are where singers do it all in one go.

Try this

Make a two-layer recording with two phones or a phone and a laptop. Record yourself clapping a steady beat. Play it back through a speaker while a second device records you singing or humming over the top. Listen to the result. You've just done overdubbing, the trick behind almost every song on the radio.
