What is the caste system, and how has India tried to end discrimination?

In 1927, thousands of people walked to a water tank in Mahad and drank from it. It was an act of protest, because they had been forbidden to touch it.

The short answer

Caste is an old system in South Asia that sorted people into groups by birth. For centuries, a person's caste often decided what work they could do, whom they could marry and even where they could draw water.

There are two ideas mixed together. The varnas were four broad classes described in ancient texts: priests, warriors, traders and labourers. The jatis are thousands of smaller, local communities, often linked to a trade. Below or outside them were people treated as "untouchable", who today mostly call themselves Dalits.

Since 1950, India's Constitution has banned discrimination by caste and abolished untouchability. Turning that law into everyday life has taken much longer.

Surprising but true

India's Constitution does not just discourage untouchability. Article 17 abolishes it outright and makes practising it a crime.

How old is caste?

The four varnas appear in some of the oldest Hindu texts, from the Vedic age. How strict the system was in practice changed from place to place and century to century. Some historians argue it was more flexible in the past. They say British rulers in the 1800s made it rigid by recording everyone's caste in the census and using caste in laws and army recruitment. Others point to much older evidence of exclusion. Both views have serious scholars behind them.

Caste-like divisions are also found among some Muslim, Sikh and Christian communities in South Asia, even though their faiths teach equality.

Voices against caste

People questioned caste long before modern times. The Buddha welcomed followers of every background. In the 12th century, Basavanna in Karnataka rejected caste rules. Poets like Kabir and Ravidas sang that God did not care about birth, and their verses are still sung today.

In 1848, Jyotirao and Savitribai Phule opened a school for girls in Pune. They later taught Dalit children too, which angered many in the city. In Kerala, Narayana Guru built temples open to all, and the Vaikom Satyagraha of 1924–25 demanded the right to walk on roads near a temple.

Ambedkar and the Constitution

B. R. Ambedkar was born in 1891 into a family treated as untouchable. As a schoolboy he had to sit apart and was not allowed to touch the water tap. He went on to earn doctorates from Columbia University in New York and the London School of Economics.

In 1927, he led the Mahad Satyagraha, where thousands drank from a public tank they had been barred from. Later, as chair of the committee that drafted India's Constitution, he made sure it outlawed untouchability and allowed special help for groups that had been kept down.

Ambedkar and Gandhi both opposed untouchability but disagreed on how to end it. Gandhi called Dalits Harijan, "people of God", a word many Dalits later rejected as patronising.

Change since 1950

Laws passed in 1955 and 1989 made caste discrimination and violence punishable crimes. Reservation set aside seats in colleges, jobs and Parliament. In 1997, K. R. Narayanan became the first Dalit President of India. He had grown up in a poor family in Kerala and walked kilometres to school.

Cities have mixed people in schools, trains and offices in ways that would have been rare a century ago. Many young people now choose friends, and sometimes partners, without asking about caste. Yet surveys still find that most marriages happen within the same caste, and cases of discrimination are reported every year. The fight Ambedkar started is still going on.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Caste discrimination disappeared when the Constitution banned it in 1950.

Truth: The law changed quickly, but habits changed more slowly. Courts, newspapers and government crime records still report cases of caste discrimination and violence, and campaigners continue to work against them.
