What is brain freeze?

Gulp a cold kulfi too fast and a sharp pain stabs your forehead, even though nothing cold ever touched your head.

The short answer

Brain freeze is a short, sharp headache you get when something very cold touches the roof of your mouth or the back of your throat. Think of a gola, a slush drink or a big bite of ice cream. The pain usually hits the forehead or behind the eyes within seconds, and it fades in about 20 to 30 seconds.

Your brain does not freeze. The cold sets off nerves and blood vessels in the roof of your mouth. Those nerves share wiring with nerves in your forehead, so your brain gets confused about where the trouble is. Doctors call this kind of mistake referred pain.

Surprising but true

Your brain itself cannot feel pain at all. Brain freeze is a pain your brain wrongly places in your forehead.

A pain with a very long name

The medical name is sphenopalatine ganglioneuralgia. That is 30 letters to describe a headache that lasts half a minute. The sphenopalatine ganglion is a small bundle of nerves sitting behind your nose, close to the roof of the mouth.

Most doctors just call it an ice cream headache. It's one of the most common headaches there is. Surveys suggest that roughly a third of people get it, and children seem to get it more than adults.

What the cold does

When something icy hits the roof of your mouth, the tiny blood vessels there tighten quickly to save heat. Then they suddenly widen again to rush warm blood back in. Many scientists think this fast squeeze and swell is what sends the alarm.

The message travels along the trigeminal nerve, a big nerve that carries feeling from your face, teeth and forehead. Your brain gets a strong signal from that nerve and decides the pain must be in your forehead, because that's where such signals usually come from. It is a wiring shortcut in your nervous system, and most of the time it works perfectly well.

A study with straws

In 2012, a team of researchers in the US asked 13 adults to sip ice-cold water through a straw pressed against the roof of the mouth. They measured blood flow in the head. When brain freeze struck, a big artery near the front of the brain widened and blood flow rose. When it narrowed again, the pain went away.

That's a neat clue. But 13 people is a small group, and the study has not been repeated on a large scale, so scientists are still careful about what it proves.

Why the brain itself feels nothing

Here's a strange fact: the brain has no pain sensors of its own. Surgeons can operate on parts of it while a patient is awake, after numbing the scalp. Every headache you ever feel comes from tissues around the brain, such as blood vessels, the coverings of the brain and the muscles of the head.

So pain is not a direct report from the place that hurts. It is the brain's best guess, and brain freeze shows that the guess can be wrong.

How to stop it

The quickest fix is to warm the roof of your mouth. Press your tongue flat against it, or sip some lukewarm water. Eating cold food slowly, and letting it melt at the front of your tongue, helps stop brain freeze from starting at all.

It is harmless. But a headache that comes with no cold food, lasts a long time or keeps returning is worth telling a parent or doctor about.

Cold isn't the only thing that sets off pain in the wrong place. Bump one particular spot on your elbow and your little finger buzzes, thanks to a nerve with almost no padding over it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Brain freeze means your brain is actually getting cold.

Truth: Your brain stays at the same warm temperature. The pain comes from nerves and blood vessels in your mouth and head reacting to sudden cold.

Try this

Next summer, try a fair test with a grown-up. Eat two spoons of ice cream, one quickly pressed against the roof of your mouth and one slowly from the front of your tongue. Rate any pain from 0 to 5. Then try warming the roof of your mouth with your tongue and see how fast it fades.
