What was the Bloop?

In 1997, underwater microphones across the Pacific picked up a deep, rising sound, some of them more than 5,000 km from where it started. For years, people wondered if it came from a giant sea monster.

The short answer

The Bloop was a very loud, very low underwater sound recorded in 1997 by NOAA, the American agency that studies the oceans and the air. It lasted about a minute and rose in pitch, a bit like a giant "bloooop". Several underwater microphones picked it up, some more than 5,000 km from its source.

Because it was so loud and so mysterious, many people guessed it came from an unknown sea creature. NOAA scientists later concluded it was almost certainly an icequake: the sound of a huge iceberg cracking or breaking off Antarctica.

Surprising but true

Sound can travel thousands of kilometres through the ocean in a hidden layer of water, the SOFAR channel, that works like a whispering tunnel.

Listening to the ocean

During the Cold War, the US Navy placed underwater microphones, called hydrophones, across the oceans to track enemy submarines. Later, scientists at NOAA used some of these and their own hydrophones to listen for natural sounds, like undersea volcanoes, earthquakes and whales.

The ocean is surprisingly noisy. Whales sing, shrimps snap, ships rumble and the sea floor creaks. Scientists turn these sounds into pictures called spectrograms, which show pitch and loudness over time.

A sound with no owner

In 1997, the hydrophones caught a sound they couldn't match to anything known. It came from the remote South Pacific, somewhere off the southern tip of South America.

The Bloop was so powerful that microphones thousands of kilometres apart heard it. Its pitch rose quickly, a pattern some animals use. That made people wonder if a living creature, far bigger than any known whale, was swimming in the deep. Scientists nicknamed it the Bloop and posted the recording online.

The whispering layer

How can a sound travel so far? The ocean has a hidden layer, often about 1,000 metres down, called the SOFAR channel. In this layer, the temperature and pressure work together to bend sound waves back towards the middle, so they don't escape up or down.

Sound can bounce along this layer for thousands of kilometres, losing very little energy. Some whales may use it to call to each other across ocean basins. It was this layer that carried the Bloop so far.

The US Navy discovered the channel during the Second World War. In tests, a small explosive set off in the channel could be heard thousands of kilometres away. Sailors even planned to use it to find airmen lost at sea, who would drop a small charge that sank to the right depth and went bang.

Icequakes

Over the next years, NOAA put hydrophones closer to Antarctica. They recorded many sounds made by ice. When huge icebergs crack, scrape the sea floor or break away from ice shelves, they create long, rising sounds called icequakes.

On spectrograms, many of these looked just like the Bloop. By the mid-2000s, NOAA scientists had concluded that the Bloop was most likely an icequake from Antarctic ice. Glaciers and ice shelves breaking up can release huge amounts of energy, easily enough to be heard across an ocean.

Monster stories

The Bloop's location became famous for another reason. It was fairly close to a spot in the Pacific where the American writer H. P. Lovecraft had placed the sunken city of his made-up sea monster, Cthulhu, in a 1928 story. Fans loved the coincidence.

But an animal loud enough to make the Bloop would have to be far bigger than a blue whale, the largest animal ever known. Real deep-sea giants, like the giant squid, don't make loud calls at all.

More strange sounds

The Bloop isn't the only odd noise. Others have nicknames like "Upsweep", "Julia" and "Slow Down". Upsweep, heard since 1991, may come from volcanic activity. Julia and Slow Down seem to come from ice too.

Sounds from space have puzzled scientists in the same way. In 1977, a radio telescope in the USA picked up a strong signal nobody could explain, a mystery still debated as the Wow! signal.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Bloop proved there is a gigantic unknown animal in the deep sea.

Truth: The sound was far too powerful for any known animal, even a blue whale. The best explanation is ice cracking near Antarctica, which makes similar sounds that scientists have recorded many times since.

Try this

Fill a sink or bucket with water. Put one ear against the side of the bucket while a friend taps two spoons together under the water, then taps them in the air. Which sounds louder and clearer? Sound travels about four times faster in water than in air.
