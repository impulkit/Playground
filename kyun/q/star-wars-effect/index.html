Why did Star Wars change movies?

The scream of the TIE fighters in Star Wars was partly an elephant's call, mixed with a car driving on a wet road.

The short answer

Star Wars, released in 1977, changed movies in several ways at once. Its special effects looked more real than anything audiences had seen. Its sound and music filled cinemas in a new way. And it earned so much money from tickets and toys that Hollywood studios began chasing giant "blockbuster" films with sequels and merchandise.

To make it, George Lucas had to start his own effects company, Industrial Light & Magic, which went on to work on hundreds of films. A small computer team at his company later became Pixar. Much of how big films are made today traces back to that one space adventure.

Surprising but true

The famous hum of a lightsaber was made by mixing the sound of an old film projector's motor with buzzing picked up from a television set.

A film nobody expected

In the mid-1970s, space films were out of fashion and many people thought Lucas's script was too strange. The studio gave him a modest budget. On its opening day in May 1977 it played in only a few dozen American cinemas.

Within weeks, queues stretched around the block. People watched it again and again. Star Wars became the highest-earning film released up to that time.

Making spaceships fly

Lucas couldn't find anyone who could make the space battles he imagined, so in 1975 he set up Industrial Light & Magic (ILM) in California. Its team built detailed model spaceships, some only as long as your arm.

They also built a computer-controlled camera, called the Dykstraflex after its designer John Dykstra. It could repeat exactly the same move over and over. That let the team film each model separately and combine them, so dozens of ships seemed to zoom past together. ILM later created special effects for films from Jurassic Park to the Marvel series.

The sound of a galaxy

The sound designer Ben Burtt spent months collecting noises. The lightsaber hum mixed an old film projector's motor with buzz from a television set. The scream of a TIE fighter used an elephant's call blended with a car driving on a wet road. Chewbacca's roar mixed sounds from bears and other animals.

In real space, none of these sounds could be heard. Sound needs air or another material to travel through. Lucas added them anyway, because a silent battle didn't feel exciting.

John Williams wrote a full orchestral score, at a time when many films used pop songs. His Star Wars theme is one of the most recognisable pieces of film music ever written.

Toys, sequels and money

Before the film came out, Lucas agreed to take a smaller fee as director. In return, he kept the rights to sequels and to merchandise like toys. Few people thought those rights were worth much.

They turned out to be worth a fortune. Star Wars action figures sold in huge numbers, and Lucas made more films. Studios learned that one hit could sell lunchboxes, games and books for decades. In 2012 Disney bought Lucasfilm for about US$4 billion.

Old stories, new galaxy

Lucas borrowed from many places. He was inspired by old adventure serials, Westerns, and the Japanese director Akira Kurosawa's samurai films. He also read the writer Joseph Campbell, who studied hero myths from around the world.

That mix is part of why Star Wars travels so well. A farm boy who leaves home, a wise teacher, a princess who fights back and a villain in a black mask feel familiar to people everywhere. Many cultures have their own versions of these characters, which may be why the films feel like old stories even with spaceships in them.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You would hear explosions and laser blasts in space, just like in the films.

Truth: Space is nearly a vacuum, so sound cannot travel through it. A battle in space would be completely silent to anyone outside the ships.
