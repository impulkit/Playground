Who invented zero?

Nothing was one of the most important inventions ever.

The short answer

Many ancient peoples counted, but treating zero as a number of its own was a big leap.

Indian mathematicians, especially Brahmagupta, wrote down rules for calculating with zero. In his book of 628 CE, he explained that a number minus itself gives zero, and that anything multiplied by zero is zero.

The digits you use, 0 to 9, are called Hindu-Arabic numerals, because they came from India through the Arab world. No single person invented zero. It grew in India over centuries, and Indian scholars were the first to treat it as a full number.

Surprising but true

The numbers you write every day were carried from India across the world by traders and scholars.

Why zero is clever

With zero as a placeholder, ten symbols can write any number: 5, 50, 500. The zero's job is to say "nothing in this place", so the other digits stay in the right columns. Roman numerals had no zero, which made big sums much harder. Try multiplying CCCXLVII by XXIX.

The Babylonians, in today's Iraq, used a mark for an empty place about 2,300 years ago, but never as a number by itself. On the other side of the world, the Maya of Central America invented their own zero for their calendars, entirely separately.

Rules for nothing

Brahmagupta was an astronomer in Rajasthan. Alongside his rules for adding and multiplying with zero, he treated negative numbers as "debts" and positive numbers as "fortunes". A debt minus zero is a debt, he wrote.

Even he wasn't sure about dividing by zero. He said zero divided by zero was zero, which mathematicians no longer accept. Today we say dividing by zero has no answer at all.

Carved in stone, written on birch bark

Inside Gwalior Fort, a small temple carries an inscription from 876 CE. It lists gifts to the temple, including a garden 270 measures long and 50 garlands of flowers a day. Both numbers end in a clear round 0, one of the oldest dated zeros anyone can still visit.

The Bakhshali manuscript is a maths text written on birch bark, dug up by a farmer near Peshawar in 1881. It uses a dot for zero. In 2017, carbon dating suggested some pages might be very old indeed, but scholars still argue about what the results mean.

How zero travelled west

Around 825 CE, the Persian mathematician al-Khwarizmi wrote a book in Baghdad explaining calculation with the Indian numerals. Translated into Latin, it carried his name to Europe, and our word "algorithm" comes from it.

Merchants in North Africa used the new digits for trade. A young Italian named Leonardo of Pisa, later called Fibonacci, learned them there while helping his father, a trade official. In 1202 he published a book showing how much easier they made sums of money, weights and interest. Slowly, over the next few hundred years, Europe gave up its counting boards and Roman numerals.

From shunya to zero

The Sanskrit word for zero is shunya, meaning empty. Arab scholars translated it as sifr. In Latin it became zephirum, then zero in Italian. The same Arabic word gave English "cipher", which once meant zero and later came to mean a secret code, as in secret codes.

Not everyone welcomed the new numbers. In 1299, the city of Florence banned them in official account books, partly because a 0 could easily be changed into a 6 or a 9. But merchants found the Indian digits far quicker, and they spread along the trade routes. Every computer now runs on zeros and ones. Count like a computer.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Arabs invented the numbers we call Arabic numerals.

Truth: The digits 0 to 9 began in India. Scholars in the Arab world, such as al-Khwarizmi, learned them, wrote about them and passed them on. Europeans called them Arabic because that is where they got them.
