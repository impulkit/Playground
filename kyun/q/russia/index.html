How big is Russia?

Russia is so wide that when children in its far east are finishing school for the day, children in Moscow are only just waking up.

The short answer

Russia is the biggest country in the world by far. It covers about 17 million square kilometres, roughly one-ninth of all the land on Earth. It is more than five times the size of India and almost twice the size of the United States.

It stretches across two continents. The Ural Mountains divide European Russia, in the west, from Asian Russia, called Siberia, in the east. Most Russians live in the European part, around cities like Moscow and Saint Petersburg. Siberia is enormous but thinly populated, with vast forests, frozen ground and rivers longer than the Ganga.

Surprising but true

Lake Baikal in Siberia holds about a fifth of all the unfrozen fresh water on Earth's surface, more than all five of North America's Great Lakes together.

Eleven time zones

From the Baltic Sea in the west to the Pacific Ocean in the east, Russia is about 9,000 km wide. When the Sun rises over the Pacific coast, it is still the middle of the previous evening in the west.

So Russia uses eleven time zones. If you could speak to friends in Kaliningrad, in the far west, and Kamchatka, in the far east, at the same moment, one would be having breakfast and the other dinner.

A week on one train

The Trans-Siberian Railway links Moscow with Vladivostok, on the Pacific coast. The main route is over 9,000 km long, the longest railway line in the world. A non-stop journey takes about a week and crosses seven or eight time zones, depending on the route.

Passengers sleep in bunks, drink tea from hot-water boilers at the end of each carriage, and buy snacks from people selling food on station platforms. Outside the window, the birch and pine forests of Siberia, called the taiga, roll past for days.

The deepest lake

Lake Baikal, in southern Siberia, is the deepest lake on Earth, at about 1,640 m. It is also among the oldest, around 25 million years old. It holds so much water that if every river on Earth emptied into it, they would take more than half a year to fill it.

Baikal is home to animals found nowhere else, including the nerpa, one of the few seals in the world that live only in fresh water. In winter, the lake freezes so thick that people drive across it on marked ice roads.

Frozen ground and frozen mammoths

Much of Siberia sits on permafrost, ground that has stayed frozen for thousands of years. Buildings in some cities stand on stilts, so the heat from inside does not melt the ground and make them sink.

Permafrost is also a natural freezer. Woolly mammoths, woolly rhinos and even ancient wolves and lions have been found there with skin, hair and sometimes stomach contents still preserved. As the climate warms and the ground thaws, more of these frozen animals are turning up.

Rockets, ballet and books

In 1957, the Soviet Union, of which Russia was the largest part, launched Sputnik, the first artificial satellite. In 1961, Yuri Gagarin became the first person in space, a story told in the first person in space. In 1984, Rakesh Sharma became the first Indian in space, flying aboard a Soviet Soyuz spacecraft.

Russia is famous for its writers, such as Leo Tolstoy, and for composers like Tchaikovsky, who wrote the music for the ballets Swan Lake and The Nutcracker. Moscow's Red Square, with the colourful onion domes of Saint Basil's Cathedral, is one of the best-known sights on Earth.

Myth or fact?

Myth: It is freezing cold everywhere in Russia, all year round.

Truth: Winters are long and cold, especially in Siberia, but Moscow often reaches 25 to 30 °C in summer. The southern resort of Sochi, on the Black Sea, has palm trees and warm beaches.
