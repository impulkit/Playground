How do singers hit high notes?

Your voice comes from two small folds of tissue in your throat, only one or two centimetres long, buzzing hundreds of times a second.

The short answer

Singing starts in your voice box, or larynx, the bump at the front of your throat. Inside are two small bands of tissue called vocal folds. When you breathe out and start to sing, the folds come together and air from your lungs makes them buzz, very fast.

To sing higher, small muscles stretch the folds longer, thinner and tighter. Tight, thin folds vibrate faster, and faster vibrations make a higher note. It works like a rubber band: stretch it more and pluck it, and the note rises. Trained singers learn to control these tiny muscles very finely, while keeping a steady flow of air.

Surprising but true

For the highest note in one famous Mozart song, a soprano's vocal folds vibrate about 1,400 times every second.

Air is the fuel

Every sung note needs air, supplied by your lungs. Singers practise breathing low and deep, using the dome-shaped muscle under the lungs, the diaphragm, along with the muscles of the belly and ribs.

The aim is a steady, controlled stream of breath, not a big gust. Long notes need careful saving of air. A singer holding a note for fifteen seconds is like a cyclist pedalling steadily uphill, not sprinting.

Why voices are different

Adult men usually have longer, thicker vocal folds than women, so their voices are lower. Children's folds are shorter still. During the teenage years, a boy's larynx grows quickly and his folds get longer, so his voice drops, sometimes cracking along the way. Girls' voices change too, but less.

The sound from the folds is only a thin buzz. Your throat, mouth and nose shape it into a rich voice, like the body of a guitar shaping a string's sound. Moving your tongue and lips changes the vowels.

The highest notes

Very high notes need the folds to be stretched tight and to vibrate in only part of their length. A soprano's high C vibrates about 1,000 times a second.

In Mozart's opera The Magic Flute, the Queen of the Night sings a furious song that climbs to an F above that, about 1,400 vibrations a second. Few singers can perform it well. Above the normal range, some singers can reach a thin, piercing sound called the whistle register.

Trained opera singers also learn to make a strong ring in their voice, which helps them be heard over a whole orchestra without a microphone.

Practice, Indian style

Indian classical singers do riyaz, daily practice, often early in the morning. One famous exercise is kharaj practice: singing very low, steady notes to build breath and control. Singers also practise sliding smoothly between notes.

Playback singers in Hindi films have sung in huge ranges. Lata Mangeshkar was known for a light, clear, high voice that she kept for decades. Singers in Carnatic music practise fast runs of notes called brigas, which need very quick, precise control of the vocal folds.

Looking after a voice

Vocal folds are delicate. Shouting for a long time, or singing hard when you have a cold, can make them sore and swollen, and your voice goes croaky. Most of the time, they recover with rest.

Singers drink water, warm up gently and avoid straining to reach notes. If someone's voice stays hoarse for weeks, it is a good idea to see a doctor. A well-cared-for voice can last a lifetime. Some singers keep performing into their eighties.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Opera singers can easily shatter glasses with their voices.

Truth: It is possible but very hard. It needs a thin crystal glass, a very loud voice, and the exact note at which that glass naturally rings. A singer did it on an American TV science show in 2005, but it is not something singers do every day.

Try this

Rest your fingers gently on the front of your throat and hum a low note. Slowly slide up to a high note and feel the buzzing change. Then hum while you pinch your nose shut. The hum stops, because humming sends air out through your nose.
