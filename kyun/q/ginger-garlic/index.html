Why are ginger and garlic used together so often?

A whole garlic clove barely smells. Crush it and, within seconds, a chemical reaction fills the kitchen with that famous sharp scent.

The short answer

Ginger-garlic paste starts thousands of Indian dishes, from chicken curry to chole. It is so common that shops sell it ready-made in jars. Cooks in China, Korea, Thailand and the Caribbean pair the two as well.

The main reason is flavour. Garlic brings a deep, savoury, slightly sulphur smell. Ginger brings a bright, lemony heat. Together they cover each other's weak spots and make food taste fuller. Both also tame strong smells in meat and fish, both keep well for weeks and both are cheap. Once a pairing works for centuries in kitchens, it gets passed down and becomes "how food is made".

Surprising but true

Garlic doesn't contain much of its famous smell until you cut it. Crushing the clove mixes two chemicals that were kept apart in different parts of its cells, and they react to make the smell.

Two very different plants

Ginger and garlic both grow underground, but they're not related. Ginger is a rhizome, a swollen sideways stem, from a leafy tropical plant related to turmeric and cardamom. Nobody has ever found ginger growing wild, away from farms and gardens. It was probably first grown somewhere in tropical South or Southeast Asia, thousands of years ago.

Garlic is a bulb, a cluster of fleshy leaf bases, and a cousin of onions. Its wild ancestors came from the mountains of Central Asia. Ancient Egyptians grew it, and bulbs of garlic were found in Tutankhamun's tomb.

Today India grows more ginger than any other country, much of it in Madhya Pradesh, Karnataka and the Northeast. China grows about three-quarters of the world's garlic, far ahead of India in second place.

The chemistry of a crush

Garlic plays a clever trick. A whole clove keeps a chemical called alliin in one part of its cells and an enzyme in another. When you crush or chop it, the two mix and make allicin, which smells sharp and stings the tongue. That's probably how the plant defends itself against hungry animals.

Heat changes allicin into gentler, sweeter compounds. That's why raw garlic bites, while slow-cooked garlic tastes mellow and nutty.

Fresh ginger gets its heat from gingerol. When ginger dries or cooks, some gingerol turns into shogaol, which is even more pungent. Dried ginger powder, called sonth, is fiercer than the fresh root.

Why the pair works

Flavour is mostly smell. Your tongue picks up sweet, salty, sour, bitter and savoury, but your nose does the rest. Ginger and garlic hit your nose with very different scents at once, so a dish smells complex rather than flat.

In Indian kitchens the paste usually goes into hot oil after the onions have browned. Frying for a minute or two cooks out the raw edge. Then come tomatoes and powdered spices such as turmeric. Skipping that frying step leaves a curry tasting harsh and raw. Many cooks judge the moment by smell alone: the sharp, green scent fades and a sweeter, toasty one takes over. In South India, the paste often joins curry leaves and mustard seeds, while in the north it meets cumin, cardamom and garam masala.

In Chinese cooking, ginger, garlic and spring onion form a classic trio, tossed into a hot wok before everything else. This is one reason Indo-Chinese food like chilli chicken feels familiar to Indian tastes.

Not everyone's favourite

Some traditions leave the pair out. Many Jains avoid garlic, onions, fresh ginger and other roots, because pulling them up harms the whole plant and the tiny creatures in the soil. Some Hindu communities also cook without garlic and onion, especially on fasting days and in temple kitchens.

Ginger has a long place in traditional medicine. Ayurveda calls it vishwabheshaj, "the universal medicine", and ginger tea is a common home remedy for an upset stomach. Some studies suggest ginger can ease mild nausea. Bigger claims, about either ginger or garlic curing serious illness, aren't backed by good evidence. If someone is unwell, the best plan is still to see a doctor.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Eating lots of garlic will stop you catching colds.

Truth: Garlic is healthy food, but careful scientific reviews have found no good evidence that it prevents or cures colds. Rest, handwashing and time do more.

Try this

With an adult, smell a whole peeled garlic clove, then smell it again a minute after an adult crushes it. Do the same with a slice of fresh ginger and some grated ginger. Write down which changes more, and try describing each smell in three words.
