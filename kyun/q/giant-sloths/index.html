Were there sloths as big as elephants?

A future US president once studied a giant sloth's claw and decided it belonged to an enormous lion.

The short answer

Yes. Until about 10,000 years ago, the Americas were home to giant ground sloths. The biggest, Megatherium of South America and its close cousin Eremotherium, were up to about 6 m long and weighed around 4 tonnes, about as much as an elephant.

They were plant-eaters with huge curved claws. Instead of hanging in trees, they lived on the ground. They could rear up on their back legs and tail to tear down branches from tall trees. Today's small tree sloths are their distant relatives.

Surprising but true

Dry caves in the American Southwest still hold heaps of giant sloth dung more than 10,000 years old.

A skeleton for a king

In 1788, a huge skeleton was dug up near the river Luján in Argentina, then ruled by Spain. It was shipped to Madrid and put together for the king's natural history collection. In 1796, the French scientist Georges Cuvier studied drawings of it and named it Megatherium, "great beast".

Cuvier saw that it was related to living sloths and anteaters, yet nothing like it was alive anywhere. This helped convince him that some animals had gone extinct, an idea many people found hard to accept then.

The president's claw

In 1797, Thomas Jefferson, who later became the third US president, described some giant claws found in a cave in what is now West Virginia. He thought they came from an enormous lion and named it Megalonyx, "great claw". He hoped explorers might find one alive in the unexplored West.

It was soon shown to be a ground sloth. Scientists later named it Megalonyx jeffersonii in his honour, and it is now the state fossil of West Virginia. A few decades later, Charles Darwin dug up giant sloth bones in Argentina during his voyage on the Beagle.

How they lived

Giant sloths had peg-like teeth with no hard enamel, which kept growing all their lives, good for grinding tough leaves. Their claws were so big that they could not walk flat-handed. Instead they walked on their knuckles and on the outer edges of their feet.

Their bones show strong hips and a thick tail, which formed a tripod when they stood up. Some may also have dug burrows. In Brazil and Argentina, large tunnels in the rock, wide enough for a person to walk through, have scratch marks thought to be from giant sloths or giant armadillo relatives.

Dung in the desert

Some of the best clues come from a smaller species, the Shasta ground sloth. In dry caves in Nevada and Arizona, including Rampart Cave near the Grand Canyon, its dung piled up for thousands of years. The desert air dried it out.

Scientists picked through the dung and found what the sloths ate: desert globemallow, saltbush and other plants that still grow nearby. In 1976, a fire started in Rampart Cave and smouldered for months, burning much of the dung.

The end of the giants

Giant ground sloths vanished from the mainland around 10,000 years ago, along with mammoths, sabre-toothed cats and many other big animals of the ice ages. The climate was changing, and humans had recently arrived. Some sloth bones carry cut marks from human tools.

On a few Caribbean islands, which people reached much later, smaller ground sloths survived for thousands of years longer. Their story is one clue that people played a big part in the giants' disappearance. Today's tree sloths, which weigh only a few kilograms, are all that is left of the family.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Giant sloths hung in trees like today's sloths.

Truth: Giant ground sloths lived on the ground. They were far too heavy for trees. They reared up on their back legs, propped on their thick tails, to pull down branches.

Try this

Make a fist and try to walk on all fours on your knuckles across a soft carpet, the way a giant sloth's hands met the ground. Then try with flat hands. Notice which way would protect long claws, if you had them. Go slowly and stop if your wrists feel sore.
