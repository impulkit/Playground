Why did an emperor build a clay army?

In 1974, farmers digging a well in China struck a clay head, and found an army of about 8,000 life-size soldiers guarding a dead emperor.

The short answer

Qin Shi Huang was the first emperor to unite China, in 221 BCE. He was powerful and fearful of death. He built a vast tomb near the modern city of Xi'an and filled pits nearby with an army of about 8,000 clay soldiers, with horses, chariots and real bronze weapons.

Many people in ancient China believed that life continued after death, and that a ruler would need servants, officials and protectors there too. The clay army was meant to guard the emperor and help him rule in the afterlife. It was forgotten for more than 2,000 years, until farmers found it by accident in 1974.

Surprising but true

The clay soldiers were once painted in bright colours, but the paint can curl and flake off within minutes of being exposed to dry air.

A lucky well

In March 1974, farmers in a village near Xi'an were digging a well during a drought. They found pieces of clay and a head. Archaeologists soon realised it was part of something huge.

So far, four main pits have been found. Pit 1, the biggest, is about as long as two football fields and holds rows of infantry soldiers. Pit 2 has archers, cavalry and chariots. Pit 3 seems to be a command post. Pit 4 is empty, perhaps unfinished. Only a part of Pit 1 has been fully dug out.

Every soldier is different

The soldiers stand about 1.8 m tall on average, and each is unique in some way. They have different faces, hairstyles, moustaches and expressions. Generals are taller, with fancy headgear. Archers kneel or stand ready to shoot.

How was this possible? Workers used moulds for the main body parts, then added clay by hand for faces, hair and details. Some figures carry names of workshop leaders stamped into the clay, a kind of quality control. Scientists studying the ears of soldiers in 2014 found no two pairs exactly the same, suggesting real people may have modelled for some faces.

How do archaeologists know what happened long ago?Old rubbish heaps, burnt seeds and a farmer's well have told us more about the past than many kings' proud claims.

Colours that vanish

When new, the army was a riot of colour. Soldiers wore red, green, purple and blue robes, with pink faces and black hair. Among the paints was a human-made pigment now called Han purple.

Most of the paint has flaked away underground. When archaeologists dig up a soldier, the remaining paint can curl and fall off within minutes as the damp clay dries in the air. Chinese and German scientists have worked on chemicals to hold the colours in place, but most figures are left grey. That's one reason digging is slow.

The tomb nobody has opened

The emperor's own tomb lies under a large mound, about 1.5 km from the army pits, and has never been opened. About 100 years after his death, the historian Sima Qian described what was inside. He wrote of palaces, treasure and rivers of mercury that flowed by machinery, guarded by crossbows set to fire at intruders.

Scientists testing the soil above the mound have found unusually high levels of mercury, which suggests Sima Qian's story may hold some truth. Sima Qian also said about 700,000 workers built the complex, though historians think that number may be exaggerated. China has decided not to open the tomb until it can protect what's inside.

A ruler of extremes

Qin Shi Huang united China's warring states, standardised its writing, coins and weights, and linked earlier walls into an early version of the Great Wall. He was also harsh, ordering books burned and scholars punished. He searched for potions to live forever. Some of those potions may have contained mercury, which possibly harmed him.

He died in 210 BCE, and his dynasty collapsed within four years. His clay army, made of fired terracotta like Indian pots and temple tiles, has lasted far longer. Today millions visit the site, one of the great discoveries of ancient China.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Terracotta Army was always grey or brown, like it looks today.

Truth: The soldiers were brightly painted in reds, greens, blues, purples and pinks. Most of the paint flaked off over time, and when figures are dug up, remaining paint can peel away within minutes as it dries.

Try this

Make a small clay or dough figure of a soldier or a friend. Give it a unique hairstyle, face and pose. Line up several figures made by different people and see how the same basic body can look completely different with small details, just like the Terracotta Army.
