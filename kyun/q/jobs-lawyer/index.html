What does a lawyer do?

Mahatma Gandhi, B.R. Ambedkar, Jawaharlal Nehru and Abraham Lincoln all have one thing in common. Before they led nations, they were lawyers.

The short answer

A lawyer is someone trained in the law who helps people understand their rights and solve legal problems. Lawyers give advice, write documents like contracts and wills, and speak for their clients in court. In India, a lawyer who argues cases in court is called an advocate.

Some lawyers defend people accused of crimes. Others help families with property, companies with deals or citizens who think the government has treated them unfairly. Whatever the case, a lawyer's job is to explain the law clearly and to stand up for their client within the rules.

Surprising but true

Most lawyers spend far more time reading and writing than arguing in court. Many lawyers never stand in front of a judge at all.

Advice, paper and court

Much of a lawyer's day is spent reading. Laws can be long and tricky, and judges' earlier decisions shape how they apply. A lawyer has to find the rules that fit a client's problem.

Writing comes next. Lawyers draft contracts, letters and the papers that start or answer a court case. A single word can change what a contract means, so they write carefully. Only some cases reach a courtroom. There, a lawyer presents evidence, questions witnesses and argues before a judge. In some American trials, a jury of ordinary citizens decides the facts.

Many kinds of lawyers

Criminal lawyers either prosecute people accused of crimes, on behalf of the government, or defend them. Family lawyers help with divorce and the care of children. Corporate lawyers advise businesses. Others specialise in the environment, human rights, sports, patents or the internet.

Some lawyers work for free for people who can't afford to pay. India's Constitution says the state should provide free legal aid to people who need it, and legal services authorities run free help desks in many courts.

Lawyers who led

Mahatma Gandhi trained as a barrister in London and went to South Africa in 1893 to work on a legal case. There he saw how Indians were treated and began his fight for equal rights. Back in India, lawyers such as Jawaharlal Nehru, Sardar Patel and B.R. Ambedkar led the independence movement and helped write the Constitution.

In the United States, Abraham Lincoln taught himself law by reading borrowed books and became a well-known lawyer in Illinois before he became president. Many American presidents and judges started as lawyers.

Breaking barriers

For a long time, women weren't allowed to work as lawyers. Cornelia Sorabji, who grew up in Pune, became the first woman to study law at Oxford University, starting in 1889. Back in India she worked for years to help women whose property rights were ignored. In 1924, after the rules changed, she became one of the first women allowed to practise as a lawyer in India.

Today women make up a growing share of lawyers in India and the US, and several have served as judges in the Supreme Court of each country.

How to become one

In India, students can study a five-year law course after Class 12, often after taking an entrance test such as CLAT, or a three-year LLB after college. They then enrol with a State Bar Council and pass the All India Bar Examination.

In the United States, students first finish a college degree, then spend three years at law school and pass a bar exam in the state where they want to work. Good lawyers read a lot, write clearly and can see a problem from both sides. That's why debate clubs and mock trials, where students act out a pretend court case, are a popular start. Lawyers also need patience. A big case can take years. In India millions of cases are waiting to be heard at any time, which is one reason people keep arguing about how laws are made and how courts could work faster.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Lawyers spend most of their time arguing dramatic cases in court, like on TV.

Truth: Most legal work happens at a desk. Lawyers read laws, write contracts and letters, research earlier cases and advise clients. Many cases are settled without ever going to trial.
