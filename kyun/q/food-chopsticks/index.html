Why do people eat with chopsticks?

The oldest chopsticks ever found are made of bronze and are over 3,000 years old, and they were probably used for cooking, not eating.

The short answer

About a fifth of the world's people eat with chopsticks, mostly in China, Japan, Korea and Vietnam. They began in China more than 3,000 years ago, first as cooking tools for stirring pots and fishing food out of boiling water.

Over time they became eating tools too. Many East Asian dishes are cut into small pieces in the kitchen, so diners don't need knives at the table. Noodles, dumplings, rice and small bits of vegetables and meat are easy to pick up with two sticks. And once chopsticks became part of good manners, people kept using them for everything.

Surprising but true

The Chinese word for chopsticks, kuaizi, sounds like "quick ones", and some historians think the name was changed long ago because the older word sounded like "stop" to boatmen.

Older than you might think

Archaeologists digging at Yinxu, the old capital of the Shang dynasty in central China, found bronze chopsticks dating to about 1200 BCE. Bronze would have been expensive, so most people probably used bamboo or wood, which rotted away long ago.

For centuries, the Chinese ate many grains, such as millet, with spoons. Chopsticks became the main eating tool around the time of the Han dynasty, about 2,000 years ago, as noodles and dumplings grew popular. By around the year 1400, they were the normal way to eat in China.

Who made the first noodles?The oldest noodles ever found were made from millet about 4,000 years ago, and they were discovered still sitting in an upturned bowl.

The theories behind it

One popular idea is about fuel. Wood for cooking fires was scarce in parts of ancient China, so cooks chopped food small to cook it quickly. Small pieces suit chopsticks.

Another idea links them to Confucius, the teacher whose ideas shaped Chinese manners. A saying linked to his followers suggests that a gentleman keeps knives, tools of the slaughterhouse, away from the table. Historians aren't sure how much this mattered, but it shows how eating tools were tied to ideas of politeness.

Different sticks, different countries

Chopsticks spread from China to Vietnam, Korea and Japan, and each changed them. Chinese chopsticks are long, often around 25 cm, with blunt ends, good for reaching dishes shared in the middle of the table. Japanese hashi are shorter and pointed. Korean chopsticks are usually flat and made of stainless steel, and Koreans eat rice and soup with a spoon.

Vietnamese chopsticks are long like Chinese ones. Thailand is the surprise. People there mostly eat with a fork and spoon, keeping chopsticks for noodles.

A simple lever

Chopsticks are a clever piece of physics. The bottom stick stays still, resting against your fingers. The top stick pivots near your thumb like a pair of pincers. That makes chopsticks a kind of lever, turning a small movement of your fingertips into a precise pinch at the far end.

Skilled users can pick up a single grain of rice or a slippery peanut. Some studies suggest that children who learn chopsticks early develop good finger control, though it is hard to prove.

Manners and mistakes

Chopsticks come with rules. In Japan and China, sticking them upright in a bowl of rice looks like the incense sticks placed at funerals, so it's considered very rude. In Japan, passing food from one pair of chopsticks to another is avoided because it echoes a funeral ritual. Pointing or waving with chopsticks is impolite almost everywhere.

Disposable wooden chopsticks are a modern problem. China alone is estimated to use tens of billions of pairs a year, which means cutting down many trees. Some people now carry their own reusable pair.

Chopsticks in India

In India, chopsticks arrived with Chinese migrants in Kolkata and later spread with the craze for Indo-Chinese food. Many Indians happily eat hakka noodles with chopsticks and dal-chawal with their fingers. That second habit has science of its own, explored in eating with your hands.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Chopsticks are used the same way everywhere in East Asia.

Truth: Every country has its own shapes and manners. Koreans use flat metal chopsticks and eat rice with a spoon. In Thailand, most meals are eaten with a fork and spoon, and chopsticks are mostly used for noodle dishes.

Try this

Hold two pencils as chopsticks. Keep the bottom one still, resting against your ring finger, and move only the top one with your thumb, index and middle fingers. Try picking up a cotton ball, a dry pea and a small piece of paper. Which is hardest, and why?
