What is Thrissur Pooram?

At Thrissur Pooram in Kerala, two teams of decorated elephants face each other and flip through dozens of glittering umbrellas in a contest of colour.

The short answer

Thrissur Pooram is one of Kerala's most spectacular temple festivals, held each year in Thrissur in April or May. It brings together about ten temples from around the city. Their processions of decorated elephants, drummers and musicians gather on the vast grounds around the Vadakkunnathan temple, dedicated to Shiva.

The highlights are huge drum and horn performances, the famous umbrella contest called kudamattam and a giant fireworks display in the early morning. Lakhs of people from across Kerala and beyond come to watch, and the whole city becomes one big stage for a day and a half.

Surprising but true

The festival was started in 1798 by a ruler of Kochi, after rain kept local temples from reaching a nearby festival and they were turned away.

A festival born from a rainy day

Kerala has many poorams, temple festivals held in the month of Medam. Before 1798, temples from Thrissur went to a big pooram in nearby Arattupuzha. That year, heavy rain delayed them, and they arrived late. They were not allowed to join.

The ruler of Kochi, Raja Rama Varma, known as Sakthan Thampuran, decided Thrissur should have its own festival. He invited the temples to gather at Vadakkunnathan instead. He organised them into two groups, led by the Paramekkavu and Thiruvambady temples. The two sides still try to outdo each other in every part of the festival.

Hundreds of musicians under one tree

One of the most loved parts is the Elanjithara melam, a drum concert held under an old elanji tree in the temple grounds. Around 250 musicians play together, led by the chenda, a cylindrical drum beaten with sticks. Cymbals, horns and pipes join in.

The music begins slow and steady, then builds for hours, faster and faster. People in the crowd throw their arms in the air and keep time. The rhythm follows strict patterns that musicians spend years learning, and some performers have played at the festival for decades.

The day starts much earlier. From dawn, processions from the smaller temples arrive one after another, each with its own elephant and drummers, to pay respects at Vadakkunnathan. The one from Kanimangalam traditionally comes first. By the time the big melam begins, the city has been drumming for hours.

Umbrellas atop elephants

In the late afternoon, the two teams line up facing each other. About 15 elephants stand on each side, decorated with gold-plated face coverings called nettipattam. On top of each sit riders holding fans, plumes and silk umbrellas.

Then the kudamattam, the exchange of umbrellas, begins. Each side raises a set of matching umbrellas, then swaps them for another, then another. Some are covered with sequins, some with lights, some with images of gods. Teams keep their designs secret for months. The crowd roars at each surprise.

Fireworks and a promise to meet again

Before dawn, the night sky blazes with fireworks, known locally as vedikettu. The sound can be heard kilometres away. On the final day, the two teams' deities bid each other farewell, promising to meet again at next year's Pooram.

The festival also raises serious questions. Animal welfare groups worry about elephants standing in heat and crowds and about fireworks safety. Courts and the Kerala government have set rules on how long elephants can stand, how far crowds must stay back and how fireworks are stored. Festival organisers say they follow these rules carefully, and the debate continues.

People of many faiths join in, and Thrissur's shops and homes open their doors to visitors. Each elephant has a mahout who has often spent years with that one animal. The big drums of Pooram, chenda, maddalam and more, belong to an ancient family of instruments, the story of drums. And the bursts of colour in the morning sky come from metals burning in a clever way, explained in how fireworks work.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Thrissur Pooram is just one temple's festival.

Truth: It brings together about ten temples from around the city. Their deities are carried to the Vadakkunnathan temple grounds, where two big teams lead the main events.

Try this

Make a mini kudamattam. Decorate three or four paper plates or small umbrellas with bright colours and shiny paper. With a friend on the other side of the room, take turns holding up a new design on a clap, and let your family judge which is more surprising.
