Who are some modern Indian mathematicians?

In 1959, two Indian mathematicians and an American friend proved the great Leonhard Euler wrong, and newspapers nicknamed them "Euler's spoilers".

The short answer

India's mathematical story didn't end with Aryabhata or Ramanujan. In the last hundred years, Indian mathematicians have built new fields, solved old puzzles and won the world's top prizes. Some worked in India, at places like the Indian Statistical Institute in Kolkata or the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research in Mumbai. Others did their work in the USA, the UK or Australia.

Here are a few of them, from a statistician who helped plan India's economy to a schoolteacher with a magic number.

Surprising but true

A schoolteacher from Devlali in Maharashtra, D. R. Kaprekar, found that almost any four-digit number leads to 6174 if you keep rearranging and subtracting its digits.

Counting a country

Prasanta Chandra Mahalanobis was a physicist who fell in love with statistics, the maths of data. In 1931, he founded the Indian Statistical Institute in Kolkata. He invented a way of measuring how different two groups are, now called the Mahalanobis distance, which is used in fields from medicine to face recognition. It helps a computer decide whether a new measurement looks more like one group or another.

He also designed huge sample surveys to understand how Indians lived, worked and spent money. The National Sample Survey, which he helped begin in 1950, still runs today. His student C. R. Rao became one of the most important statisticians in the world and, aged 102, received the International Prize in Statistics in 2023.

Euler's spoilers

In 1782, the Swiss mathematician Leonhard Euler made a guess about arranging items in square grids so that no row or column repeats, a puzzle related to Sudoku. He believed certain sizes of grid were impossible. For 177 years, nobody proved him wrong.

Then, in 1959, R. C. Bose and S. S. Shrikhande, working in the USA, together with the American mathematician E. T. Parker, showed that Euler was wrong for every size he had doubted except the two smallest. The discovery made the front page of The New York Times, and the three became known as "Euler's spoilers". Their work later helped in designing experiments and computer codes.

The teacher and his numbers

Not every mathematician works at a university. Dattatreya Ramchandra Kaprekar taught at a school in Devlali, near Nashik, and played with numbers in his spare time. In 1949 he found that almost any four-digit number, put through a simple routine of rearranging and subtracting, ends up at 6174. It is now called Kaprekar's constant.

He found many other number patterns too. For years, professional mathematicians ignored him, and he had to publish many of his ideas in small journals or at his own cost. Today, puzzle books and maths clubs all over the world use his discoveries.

The highest prizes

Harish-Chandra, born in Kanpur in 1923, did deep work on symmetry in mathematics at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton. A research institute in Prayagraj is named after him.

S. R. Srinivasa Varadhan, who grew up in Chennai, won the Abel Prize in 2007 for his work on probability, the maths of chance. Manjul Bhargava, an American mathematician whose family comes from India, won the Fields Medal in 2014. He has said that Sanskrit poetry and tabla rhythms helped spark his love of patterns. Akshay Venkatesh, born in New Delhi and raised in Australia, won the Fields Medal in 2018.

New voices

The story continues. Neena Gupta of the Indian Statistical Institute solved a famous problem in algebra and won the Ramanujan Prize for young mathematicians in 2021. Raman Parimala, known for her work in algebra, has been honoured around the world.

Each year, Indian students also compete in the International Mathematical Olympiad, and many bring back medals. Some of them may be the names in an article like this one, fifty years from now.

Myth or fact?

Myth: India's great mathematicians all lived long ago, like Aryabhata, Brahmagupta and Ramanujan.

Truth: Indian and Indian-born mathematicians have won the field's highest prizes in the 21st century, including the Abel Prize in 2007 and Fields Medals in 2014 and 2018.

Try this

Pick any four-digit number whose digits are not all the same, such as your house number or a year. Follow Kaprekar's steps, writing each subtraction down. Count how many steps it takes to reach 6174. Compare with a friend's number. Kaprekar showed it never takes more than seven steps.
