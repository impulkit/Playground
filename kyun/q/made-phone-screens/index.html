How are phone screens made?

The glass on your phone was bathed in molten salt hotter than a kitchen oven, which squeezed its surface and made it far harder to crack.

The short answer

A phone screen is a sandwich of very thin layers. On top is a sheet of toughened glass. Under it sits a touch layer that senses your fingers. Below that is the display itself, made of millions of tiny dots called pixels, each built from red, green and blue parts that light up in different mixes. At the bottom is a layer of tiny electronic switches that tell each pixel what to do.

These layers are made in spotless factories called cleanrooms, mostly in South Korea, China, Japan and Taiwan. Machines lay down materials thinner than a hair, one layer at a time, on huge glass sheets that are later cut into many screens.

Surprising but true

Screens are made several at a time on giant sheets of glass that can be bigger than a car parking space, then cut apart.

Glass that never touches anything

The cover glass on many phones is made by a method called fusion draw. Melted glass overflows from both sides of a long trough, like water spilling over a bathtub. The two streams meet underneath and fuse into one sheet, which is pulled down and cools. Because the surface never touches a roller, it comes out smooth and flat without polishing.

Then the glass is strengthened. Cut pieces are dipped in a bath of molten potassium salt at about 400 °C. Large potassium atoms push into the surface and take the place of smaller sodium atoms. Crammed in, they squeeze the surface tight, so small cracks find it much harder to spread.

Millions of tiny lights

A typical phone screen has about 2 to 3 million pixels. Each pixel has three sub-pixels: red, green and blue. Turn all three on fully and your eye sees white. Turn on red and green only and you see yellow. Our eyes mix the tiny dots into a smooth picture because they are too small to see one by one.

There are two main screen types. In an LCD, a white backlight shines through liquid crystals that twist to let more or less light through coloured filters. In an OLED, each sub-pixel is a tiny light made of special carbon-based materials that glows by itself. OLED pixels can switch off completely, which gives deep blacks and saves battery.

How do your eyes see?The picture at the back of your eye is upside down. Your brain flips it the right way up without you ever noticing.

Building screens in a cleanroom

Screens are built in factories far cleaner than a hospital operating theatre. Workers wear suits that cover everything but their eyes. A single speck of dust could create a dead pixel.

Screens start on enormous sheets of glass, some of them bigger than a car parking space. Machines lay down a layer of tiny electronic switches called thin-film transistors, one or more for every sub-pixel. For OLED screens, the glowing materials are heated in a vacuum so they turn into vapour and settle through a metal stencil with millions of tiny holes. Afterwards, the sheet is sealed, tested and cut into dozens of phone-sized screens.

How the screen feels your finger

Above the display is a touchscreen layer. It is a grid of see-through lines made from indium tin oxide, a material that conducts electricity yet lets light pass. The grid holds a small electric field.

Your skin conducts electricity. When your finger comes close, it disturbs the field at that spot. A tiny chip checks the grid many times a second and works out exactly where you touched, even with several fingers at once.

Screens that fold

Folding phones need a screen that can bend thousands of times. Their top layer is a tough plastic film or glass so thin, sometimes around 30 micrometres, that it flexes like paper. The OLED layers underneath have no backlight, which helps them bend.

All of this has to be assembled, tested and fitted into a phone. And the strangest part? The switches behind every pixel are made with the same kind of technology as computer chips, which is why countries like India are now racing to build chip factories of their own.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Phone screens are made of plastic.

Truth: Most phone screens have a top layer of special toughened glass. Underneath are many thin layers of glass, plastic films and electronics. Some folding phones do use plastic or ultra-thin bendable glass on top.

Try this

Ask an adult if you can open a plain white page on a phone, tablet or TV and look at it through a magnifying glass. You may see tiny red, green and blue dots. Then look at a yellow area and check which colours are lit, and which are dark.
