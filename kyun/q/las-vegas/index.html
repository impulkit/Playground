How did a desert town become Las Vegas?

Las Vegas gets about 100 mm of rain in a year, less than Mumbai can get in a single stormy day, yet more than 2 million people live in its valley.

The short answer

Las Vegas began as a patch of green in the Mojave Desert, fed by natural springs. Travellers stopped there for water on the long, dry journey to California. In 1905, a railway company sold land beside its new tracks, and a small town grew up around the station.

Three things turned that railway stop into one of the most famous cities in the world. A giant dam brought workers and then water and electricity. Nevada allowed gambling, which most other states banned. And businesses learned to sell a fantasy: hotels shaped like castles, pyramids and Italian cities, all glowing with lights in the middle of the desert.

Surprising but true

Most of the famous Las Vegas Strip is not actually inside the city of Las Vegas. It lies in neighbouring areas such as Paradise.

Springs in the sand

The Mojave is one of the driest places in North America. Las Vegas gets only about 100 mm of rain in an average year. Mumbai gets more than 20 times as much. Yet underground water once bubbled up in springs here, feeding grass and trees.

Spanish-speaking traders travelling between New Mexico and California around 1830 called the spot Las Vegas, "the meadows". Later, Mormon settlers built a small fort here in 1855, though they left after a few years. Its remains still stand and are among the oldest buildings in Nevada.

The railway town

On 15 May 1905, a railway company auctioned plots of land beside its tracks. Hundreds of buyers turned up in the heat, and that day is counted as the city's birthday. Trains stopped here to take on water, just as wagons once had. Like many towns along America's railroads, it lived by serving travellers.

A dam changes everything

In 1931, work began on a huge dam across the Colorado River, about 50 kilometres away. Thousands of workers came looking for jobs during hard times. The same year, Nevada made gambling legal. Workers with wages in their pockets, and tourists who came to see the dam, found casinos and shows waiting in town.

The Hoover Dam was finished in 1936. Behind it, a lake called Lake Mead filled up. The dam also produced electricity, which helped power the city's lights and air conditioners.

How big is the Hoover Dam?The Hoover Dam holds enough concrete to pave a two-lane road from San Francisco to New York, and it was finished in 1936, two years early.

The Strip

From the 1940s, big hotels and casinos were built along a highway heading south out of town. This became the Strip. Some early hotels were paid for by gangsters, and the city spent years cleaning up that reputation. Big companies took over, and hotels grew into enormous resorts, some with more than 5,000 rooms.

Glowing signs made of neon tubes and light bulbs competed to catch drivers' eyes. Today many old signs rest in a "Neon Boneyard" museum. Newer attractions include the Sphere, which opened in 2023: a ball-shaped arena whose outside is covered in LED lights, making it one huge round screen. In 2024 about 4.2 crore visitors came to Las Vegas, many for concerts, sports and big conventions rather than casinos.

Running out of water

All this needs water. About 90% of the valley's supply comes from Lake Mead. During long droughts in the 2000s and 2020s, the lake dropped so low that old boats and even a bathtub ring of white minerals appeared on its rocky walls.

Las Vegas has responded. Almost all water used indoors is cleaned and returned to the lake. Many homes have swapped lawns for desert plants, and the water authority has paid people to rip out thirsty grass. Under a Nevada law passed in 2021, purely decorative grass in places like road verges and office parks can't be watered from the Colorado River after 2026.

The city has also built a deeper intake pipe, nicknamed the "third straw", so it can keep drawing water even if the lake falls much further. A city that grew by ignoring the desert is now learning to live with it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Las Vegas Strip is in the middle of the city of Las Vegas.

Truth: Most of the Strip's giant hotels sit just outside the city limits, in unincorporated towns such as Paradise and Winchester. The old downtown, around Fremont Street, is the part inside the city.
