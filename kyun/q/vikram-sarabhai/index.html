Who was Vikram Sarabhai?

India's space programme began in a fishing village in Kerala, with rocket parts carried on bicycles and a church used as an office.

The short answer

Vikram Sarabhai was the scientist who started India's space programme. In the 1960s he persuaded the government that a poor, newly independent country should still build rockets and satellites, because they could help with farming, weather, communication and education.

He founded the group that became ISRO, the Indian Space Research Organisation, and set up its first launch site at Thumba in Kerala. He also started research laboratories, a famous business school and textile research centres. He died young, at 52, but the space agency he planned has since sent missions to the Moon and Mars.

Surprising but true

The lander that India sent towards the Moon on Chandrayaan-2 in 2019 was named Vikram, after him.

A family of builders

Sarabhai was born in 1919 into a wealthy family of mill owners in Ahmedabad. The house was full of visitors, including leaders of the freedom movement and the poet Rabindranath Tagore.

He studied in Gujarat and at Cambridge. During the Second World War he returned to India and worked in Bangalore with C. V. Raman, studying cosmic rays, the fast particles that rain down on the Earth from space. There he met Homi Bhabha, who would become a lifelong friend. Back at Cambridge he completed his doctorate in 1947.

That same year, aged 28, he founded the Physical Research Laboratory in Ahmedabad. It began in a few rooms of a college and is now one of India's leading space science centres.

A church and a bicycle

After the Soviet Union launched Sputnik in 1957, Sarabhai argued that India needed a space programme of its own. In 1962 the government set up a space research committee, with him in charge.

He chose Thumba, a fishing village near Thiruvananthapuram, because it lies almost on the Earth's magnetic equator. The local bishop and the village community agreed to give up the land, including the Church of St Mary Magdalene. The church became an office and workshop. Rocket parts were carried to the launch pad on bicycles and bullock carts. A photograph of a young technician cycling with a rocket nose cone on his carrier is now one of the best-known pictures in Indian science.

On 21 November 1963, a small American rocket lifted off from Thumba. Sarabhai gathered a team of young engineers around him, and one of those who grew up in his programme was A. P. J. Abdul Kalam. The old church is now a space museum.

How does a rocket work?A rocket doesn't push against the air. It throws hot gas out of the back so hard that the gas pushes it forward.

Space for people

Sarabhai believed space technology should solve problems on the ground. He planned a satellite to beam educational television to villages that had never had TV. This became the Satellite Instructional Television Experiment of 1975–76, which used an American satellite to reach about 2,400 villages with programmes on farming, health and hygiene. It changed the story of television in India.

He also pushed India to build its own satellites. The first, Aryabhata, went up in 1975, four years after his death.

Many hats

Sarabhai co-founded the Indian Institute of Management in Ahmedabad and set up a research group to help the textile mills of Gujarat. His wife, Mrinalini Sarabhai, was a classical dancer who founded the Darpana Academy of Performing Arts.

He died in his sleep at Kovalam, near Thumba, in December 1971. The rocket centre at Thiruvananthapuram is now the Vikram Sarabhai Space Centre. When ISRO sent its lander towards the Moon on Chandrayaan-2 in 2019, it named the lander Vikram. The lander that touched down safely near the Moon's south pole in 2023 carried the same name.

Myth or fact?

Myth: India's first space rocket in 1963 was built in India.

Truth: The first rocket launched from Thumba was an American-made Nike-Apache. Countries including the United States, France and the Soviet Union helped India start out. Indian engineers learned quickly and soon built their own sounding rockets, called Rohini.

Try this

Make a balloon rocket. Thread a long piece of string through a drinking straw and tie the string between two chairs. Blow up a balloon, hold its neck shut, and tape it to the straw. Let go and watch it zoom along. Try a bigger and a smaller puff of air and measure how far it goes.
