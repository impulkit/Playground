Why do magnets stick to some things but not others?

A magnet grabs a paperclip but ignores a gold ring. Why so picky?

The short answer

Many atoms act like teeny magnets, because of the way their tiny electrons spin and move. In most materials, these atomic magnets point every which way and cancel out.

In iron, the tiny magnets can line up in the same direction, so the whole thing becomes magnetic. Nickel and cobalt can do this too, and so can a few rarer metals. Gold, aluminium, copper and silver can't, so a magnet ignores them.

Earth itself is a giant magnet, which is why a compass works.

Surprising but true

Heat a magnet hot enough and it stops being magnetic. Its tiny magnets get jumbled up.

Tiny teams inside iron

Inside a piece of iron, atoms form small groups called domains. In each domain, millions of atomic magnets point the same way. In an ordinary iron nail, the domains point in different directions, so the nail isn't a magnet on its own.

Bring a magnet close, and the domains swing round to line up with it. Now the nail is a magnet too, and it's pulled in. Take the magnet away and most domains drift back, which is why a paperclip only stays magnetic for a short while. In a permanent magnet, the domains stay lined up.

Heat jiggles atoms about. Get iron hotter than about 770°C and the domains fall apart, so the magnetism vanishes.

North and south

Every magnet has two ends, called poles: north and south. Opposite poles pull together and matching poles push apart. Try pushing two north poles together and you can feel the invisible cushion between them.

Snap a magnet in half and you don't get a lone north and a lone south. You get two smaller magnets, each with both poles.

Stones that point north

People first met magnets as a dark rock called lodestone, a natural magnet made of an iron mineral. The ancient Greeks found it near a place called Magnesia, which may be where the word "magnet" comes from.

In China, people discovered that a sliver of lodestone, free to turn, always points north and south. By about 900 years ago, Chinese sailors were using compasses to navigate. The idea spread to Arab, Indian and European sailors, and changed how people made maps and crossed oceans.

Why does a compass point north?A tiny needle that knows the way, without batteries.

A planet-sized magnet

Compasses work because Earth behaves like a giant magnet. Its outer core is liquid iron, swirling as the planet turns, and that moving metal makes a magnetic field. The field also shields us from harmful particles streaming from the Sun.

Some animals sense this field. Young sea turtles seem to use it to find their way across whole oceans, and many birds use it on long migrations.

Magnets and electricity

Magnets and electricity are two sides of the same force. Run a current through a coil of wire and it becomes a magnet, called an electromagnet. Scrapyard cranes use giant ones to lift cars, then switch them off to drop them.

The reverse works too. Moving a magnet near a wire makes electricity flow. Every power station, every electric motor and every speaker uses this link.

Floating trains

The strongest everyday magnets, made with a metal called neodymium, were invented in the 1980s. A small one can lift many times its own weight, and they hide inside phones, earbuds and electric cars.

Big magnets can even lift trains. Maglev trains float just above the track, held up and pushed along by magnets, so there's no wheel rubbing on rail. The Shanghai maglev in China has carried passengers at around 430 km/h.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If you break a magnet in half, you get a north piece and a south piece.

Truth: Each piece becomes a whole new magnet with its own north and south pole. However many times you break it, you never get a magnet with only one pole.

Try this

Unbend a steel paperclip into a straight piece. Stroke it along a fridge magnet about 30 times, always in the same direction. Lay it on a small leaf or a scrap of paper floating in a bowl of water. It will slowly turn to point north and south, just like a compass.
