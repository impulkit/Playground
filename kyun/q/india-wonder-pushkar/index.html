What happens at the Pushkar fair?

Every November, a small desert town in Rajasthan fills with camels in necklaces, moustache contests, and pilgrims bathing in a sacred lake at dawn.

The short answer

The Pushkar fair is two things at once. It is one of the oldest animal markets in India, where herders from across Rajasthan trade camels, horses and cattle on the sand dunes. It is also a Hindu pilgrimage, held around Kartik Purnima, the full moon that usually falls in November.

For about a week, the quiet lakeside town of Pushkar turns into a giant camp. There are tents, food stalls, Ferris wheels, folk musicians and dancers. Foreign photographers jostle with farmers from nearby villages. Pilgrims take a holy dip in Pushkar Lake, which is ringed by 52 bathing steps called ghats.

Surprising but true

The camels at Pushkar get makeovers. Herders shave patterns into their fur, a little like a fancy haircut, so they stand out to buyers.

A market of painted camels

Herders begin walking in days before the fair, some from hundreds of kilometres away. They camp on the dunes with their camels and spend long evenings talking prices around small fires. Buyers check an animal's teeth to judge its age, feel its legs and watch how it walks.

To catch a buyer's eye, herders dress camels up. They hang beaded necklaces and anklets on them, and some shave neat patterns into the camel's fur. Horses and cattle are traded too. In recent years a few prize horses and buffaloes have been sold for prices that make headlines, often more than a new car.

Moustaches, matkas and camel races

Between the trading, the fair holds contests that sound made up. There is a competition for the longest moustache, with entrants twirling whiskers that reach their ears. There is matka phod, where teams try to break a clay pot hung high in the air. Camels race across the sand, and sometimes dance to drums.

Visitors from abroad join a tug of war against local teams. Folk singers from the Thar perform late into the night, their songs older than any stage they stand on.

At night the dunes glow with cooking fires. A camel can kneel, fold its long legs and settle onto the cold sand like a parked car. Herders sleep beside their animals, partly for warmth and partly to guard them. By morning, the air smells of woodsmoke, chai and a few thousand camels, and the bargaining starts again before the sun is fully up.

The holy lake

For pilgrims, the market is the side show. Hindu tradition says Brahma, the creator god, dropped a lotus flower here and a lake sprang up where it landed. Pushkar's Brahma temple is one of very few in India dedicated to him. On the night of the full moon, crowds bathe in the lake and float small lamps on the water.

The small town is packed with hundreds of temples. Meat and alcohol are not allowed in Pushkar, so the food stalls sell malpua, kachori and sweets by the tray.

Fewer camels every year

The fair has a worry. Fewer camels arrive than in the past. Rajasthan had about 3.3 lakh camels in 2012 and about 2.1 lakh by 2019, according to India's livestock census. Trucks and tractors now do the work camels once did. Grazing land has shrunk, and a 2015 Rajasthan law to protect camels also made it harder to sell them outside the state.

Herders at recent fairs say they now bring only a fraction of the animals their fathers did. The government made the camel Rajasthan's state animal in 2014, hoping to help. Some herding families, such as the Raika, now sell camel milk and cheese to earn more from their herds. Their way of life, moving with animals across the desert, is shared by many of India's travelling communities. And there is a gathering even bigger than Pushkar, where crowds so vast they show up in satellite photos meet at the Kumbh Mela.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Pushkar fair is just a show put on for tourists.

Truth: It is a real livestock market and a religious festival that is centuries old. Herders still come to buy and sell animals, and pilgrims come to bathe in the lake, even though tourists now arrive in large numbers too.

Try this

Hold a mini fair at home. Give each family member a pretend herd of toy animals and a pile of paper money, then take turns bargaining to buy and sell. The best traders, like Pushkar's herders, never accept the first price.
