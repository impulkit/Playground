Why do we cry?

Your eyes make tears all day, every day, even when you're perfectly happy. Crying is just the moment there are too many to drain away.

The short answer

Tears do three jobs. All the time, your eyes make a thin film of tears that keeps them wet, clean and smooth, so you can see clearly. When dust, smoke or onion fumes get in, your eyes make a flood of tears to wash them out. And when you feel very sad, very happy, hurt or moved, you may cry emotional tears.

Tears are made by the lacrimal glands, which sit under the bone above the outer corner of each eye. Every blink spreads the tears across the eye like a windscreen wiper.

Usually tears drain away into your nose. When you cry, the glands make more than the drains can handle, so tears spill over onto your cheeks.

Surprising but true

When you cry hard, your nose runs because tears drain into it through tiny holes in the inner corners of your eyelids.

A thin shield for your eyes

The tear film on your eye is thinner than a sheet of paper, but it has three layers. An oily outer layer stops it drying out. A watery middle layer carries salt, oxygen and germ-fighting proteins. A sticky inner layer helps the tears cling to the eye.

Without this film your eyes would feel gritty and sore, and you couldn't see well. People with dry eyes often use eye drops, a kind of artificial tears.

Tears for washing

If a speck of dust lands in your eye, nerves in the eye send a message to your brain. The brain tells the lacrimal glands to make lots of tears fast. These are reflex tears. They rinse the speck away.

Cutting an onion works the same way. When you slice it, the onion releases a gas that drifts up and irritates the nerves on the surface of your eye. Your eyes sting, and reflex tears rush in to wash it out.

Tears of feeling

Emotional tears are the puzzle. Humans cry when they are sad, but also when they are proud, relieved or laughing hard. Many animals make tears to clean their eyes, but scientists know of no other animal that clearly cries because of its feelings.

So why do we do it? One popular idea is that tears are a signal. They show others that we are upset or need help, without words. Babies start this way: crying brings an adult running. Later in life, a friend's tears can make us want to comfort them, a sign of empathy.

Researchers like Ad Vingerhoets in the Netherlands, who has studied crying for decades, think this social signal is the main reason we evolved to weep. Other ideas are still being tested.

What are feelings, and where do they come from?A feeling is your brain and body teaming up in a split second to tell you whether something matters, and what to do about it.

When babies cry

Newborn babies cry a lot, but often without tears. Their tear glands are still developing, so emotional tears usually appear only after a few weeks, sometimes a couple of months. Their eyes still make enough basal tears to stay moist.

As children grow, they usually cry less often. Adults cry too, men as well as women, though people in some families and cultures are taught to hide it.

Crocodile tears

People say someone is crying "crocodile tears" when they pretend to be sad. The phrase comes from an old belief that crocodiles weep while eating their prey.

Oddly, it's partly true. In 2007 scientists filmed caimans and alligators eating, and several of them did shed tears. The tears were probably caused by the hissing and huffing as the animals ate, which pushed air through their sinuses and squeezed their tear glands. The tears had nothing to do with sorrow.

So whether you are chopping onions, standing in the wind or watching a sad film, your tears are doing a job. If your eyes are often sore, red or watery for no clear reason, it is worth telling a parent or seeing a doctor.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Crying gets rid of stress chemicals, so you always feel better afterwards.

Truth: Some people do feel better after a cry, especially if someone comforts them. But studies have not shown that tears carry away enough stress chemicals to matter, and some people feel worse after crying.

Try this

Look closely in a mirror at the inner corner of your lower eyelid, near your nose. You should see a tiny dot, like a pinprick. That is a tear drain, called a punctum. There is another on the upper lid. Don't poke them, just look.
