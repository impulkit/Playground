How much rice would fill a chessboard?

Put one grain of rice on the first square of a chessboard, two on the next, four on the next. By the last square, you need more rice than the world grows in 800 years.

The short answer

Start with one grain on the first square, then keep doubling: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16. By the 64th and last square, the total comes to 18,446,744,073,709,551,615 grains. That is about 18 billion billion.

A grain of rice weighs roughly 25 milligrams. So the full board would hold about 460 billion tonnes, a pile far bigger than any mountain range of grain anyone has seen. The whole world grows about 540 million tonnes of rice a year. You would need to save every grain for around 850 years to pay up.

Surprising but true

The first row of the chessboard needs only 255 grains, about a pinch. The whole board needs more than 18 billion billion.

The king and the clever player

The puzzle comes from an old legend. A wise man invents the game of chess and shows it to a king. The delighted king offers any reward. The inventor asks only for rice: one grain on the first square, doubling on each square after. The king laughs at such a small request, until his treasurers try to count it out.

Chess itself grew from an Indian game called chaturanga, played about 1,500 years ago. The story was told by medieval writers in Persia and the Arab world. In Kerala, there is an Indian version. Lord Krishna, disguised as a sage, plays chess with a king and asks for rice. When the king cannot pay, Krishna says the debt can be paid slowly, by serving rice pudding to pilgrims. People say that is why the Ambalappuzha temple serves sweet paal payasam to this day.

Small, small, then enormous

The odd thing about doubling is how gentle it looks at first. The whole first row of eight squares needs only 255 grains, about a pinch, weighing less than 7 grams. After two rows, you need 65,535 grains, still less than 2 kg, a small bag from the shop.

Then it speeds up. Square 21 alone holds about a million grains, a 25 kg sack. After 32 squares, halfway across the board, the running total is over 4 billion grains, about 107 tonnes, enough to fill several lorries.

The last square holds as much as all the other 63 squares put together, plus one grain. Laid end to end, the board's grains would stretch more than 10 light-years, far past the nearest star beyond the Sun.

Powers of two

Mathematicians write doubling with exponents. Two multiplied by itself 10 times is 2 to the power 10, which equals 1,024. Square number n holds 2 to the power (n minus 1) grains. The total for the board is 2 to the power 64, minus 1.

Computers love this number. They count in binary, using only 0s and 1s, and a 64-bit computer can store any whole number up to 2 to the power 64, minus 1. That is exactly the chessboard total. So the phone in your pocket can hold the king's debt in one tiny slot of its memory.

Doubling in the real world

Doubling is also called exponential growth, and it shows up in surprising places. Bacteria in a warm dish can double every 20 minutes or so, which is why food left out spoils so quickly. Money left in a bank grows in a similar way, as interest earns more interest. Bankers call it compound interest.

Our brains are bad at guessing doubling, because almost nothing in daily life grows that way. We expect things to add up, not multiply. The king made exactly that mistake. And doubling has an even stranger trick: fold a sheet of paper in half again and again, and in theory the folds would reach the Moon faster than you would ever guess.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Doubling only gets big when you start with a big number.

Truth: Starting from a single grain, doubling reaches about a million by the 21st square and over 18 billion billion by the 64th. The speed comes from doubling again and again, not from the starting size.

Try this

Use dry rice or small lentils to fill the first row of a chessboard or a drawn grid of 8 squares, doubling each time. You need 255 grains. Now try the first square of the second row, which needs 256 on its own. Count how long it takes and guess how long the next square would take.
