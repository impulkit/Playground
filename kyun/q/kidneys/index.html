What do kidneys do?

Two organs the size of your fists clean about 180 litres of fluid from your blood every day, enough to fill a bathtub.

The short answer

Your kidneys are two bean-shaped organs at the back of your belly. Their main job is to clean your blood. Blood flows into each kidney through a big artery, gets filtered, and flows out again cleaner.

The waste, along with any extra water and salt, becomes urine. It trickles down two thin tubes into your bladder, which stores it until you go to the toilet. Kidneys also decide how much water to keep, help control blood pressure, and send out a signal that tells your bones to make more red blood cells. They are small, but they get about a fifth of all the blood your heart pumps.

Surprising but true

The first artificial kidney was built during the Second World War from sausage skins, tin cans and parts of a washing machine.

A million tiny sieves

Each kidney contains about a million filtering units called nephrons. Each nephron starts with a tiny knot of blood vessels inside a cup. Water, salt, sugar and waste are pushed out of the blood into the cup, leaving blood cells and big proteins behind.

That fluid then flows along a long, looping tube. Along the way, the kidney takes back almost everything useful, including nearly all the water and sugar. Of the 180 litres or so filtered each day, only 1 or 2 litres leave as urine. The kidney is like a person who empties their whole school bag onto the floor every few minutes, then puts back everything except the rubbish.

Saving water, spending water

When you haven't had enough to drink, your brain sends a hormone to the kidneys telling them to save water. Your urine becomes darker and smaller in amount. When you drink lots of water, the kidneys let more go, and your urine turns pale.

Some animals are champions at this. Desert creatures like camels and the kangaroo rat of the American Southwest make very concentrated urine, so they lose very little water. Our kidneys can't match them, which is why people in hot places like Rajasthan or Arizona need to drink often.

How do camels survive the desert?A thirsty camel can drink about 100 litres of water in a quarter of an hour, about five big buckets' worth.

A machine made from sausage skins

Kidneys can be damaged by illness, injury or long-lasting high blood pressure or diabetes. When both stop working, waste builds up in the blood and makes a person very ill.

In 1943, in the occupied Netherlands during the Second World War, a young doctor named Willem Kolff built a machine to do the kidneys' job. He used sausage casings as filters, wrapped around a drum that turned in a tub of cleaning fluid, with parts from orange juice cans and a washing machine. His first patients didn't survive, but in 1945 a woman did. Today's version is called dialysis, and millions of people around the world rely on it.

Twin brothers and a new kidney

The other answer is a transplant: a new kidney from a donor. The trouble is that the body attacks tissue that isn't its own. In 1954, in Boston, a doctor named Joseph Murray found a way around this. Richard Herrick's kidneys were failing, and his identical twin Ronald offered one of his. Because identical twins have the same genes, Richard's body accepted it.

It was the first successful organ transplant in history. Murray later won a Nobel Prize. Ronald lived for decades more with one kidney, which shows how much spare capacity a healthy kidney has. Today, medicines that calm the immune system let people receive kidneys from donors who aren't twins.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Your kidneys are low down in your lower back, near your waist.

Truth: They are higher than most people think, tucked under the back of the ribcage on either side of the spine. Pain low in the back is usually from muscles, not kidneys.

Try this

For a day, check the colour of your wee each time you go and compare it to a pale straw or a lemonade colour. Write down what you drank in the hours before. You will see your kidneys making more water when you drink more and saving water when you drink less.
