How does a market decide prices?

In July 2023, tomatoes in some Indian cities cost more than ₹200 a kilo. Nobody in charge decided that. The market did.

The short answer

In a market, prices are set by a tug-of-war between two things: supply, how much of something is available, and demand, how much people want it. When something is scarce and many people want it, its price rises. When there is plenty and few want it, its price falls.

No single person decides this. Thousands of sellers and buyers each make their own choices, and the price settles where the amount people want to buy roughly matches the amount on offer. The Scottish thinker Adam Smith described this in 1776, in a book called The Wealth of Nations.

Surprising but true

In 2019, a single bluefin tuna sold at Tokyo's New Year fish auction for about 333 million yen, or about 3.1 million US dollars.

The tomato test

Indian kitchens use tomatoes almost every day. In July 2023, heavy rain and heat damaged crops in several growing regions. Fewer tomatoes reached markets, but families still wanted them. Prices in some cities shot past ₹200 a kilo, and some restaurants left tomatoes off their menus. A few months later, fresh harvests arrived and prices dropped back.

Onions behave the same way. Because they matter so much to everyday cooking, sudden jumps in onion prices often make national news in India.

Is a tomato a fruit?In 1893, the highest court in the USA had to decide whether a tomato was a fruit or a vegetable, and the answer came down to taxes.

Haggling and price tags

For most of history, shoppers haggled. Buyer and seller argued back and forth until they agreed. In many Indian bazaars, that still happens. A seller starts high, the buyer offers less, and they meet somewhere in the middle.

In the 1800s, large department stores in cities like Paris, London and New York made fixed price tags common. They had too many customers and staff to haggle over every sale. Fixed prices made shopping faster, though the store still had to pick a price that people would pay.

Auctions

An auction lets buyers compete openly. At big wholesale markets, called mandis in India, traders often bid for farmers' produce, and the highest bidder wins the load. Delhi's Azadpur Mandi is one of the largest fruit and vegetable markets in Asia.

Auctions can produce surprising prices. At the first auction of 2019 at Toyosu fish market in Tokyo, a sushi restaurant owner paid about 333 million yen for a single bluefin tuna. Part of that was for the fame of buying the year's first big fish.

Prices that never sit still

Some prices now change by the minute. Airline tickets usually get pricier as seats fill up. Cab apps raise fares when many people want a ride at once, such as in heavy rain. The aim is to persuade more drivers to come out and some riders to wait. Shares of companies on stock markets change price every second during trading.

When the market needs help

Prices also send signals. When a price rises, it tells farmers and factories to make more, and tells shoppers to buy less or switch to something else. When it falls, the opposite happens. Nobody plans this centrally, yet across a whole country it helps match what is made with what people want. Adam Smith called this the "invisible hand".

Markets are good at matching buyers and sellers, but not perfect. If a medicine or basic food becomes too expensive for poor families, governments may step in. India sets minimum support prices for some crops so farmers are not paid too little, and sells subsidised grain through ration shops. Economists argue about how much governments should interfere. Next to any street food stall, you can watch the same forces at work: a vendor raising prices when the queue is long and offering deals when it is quiet.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Shopkeepers simply charge whatever they like.

Truth: Sellers can set any price, but if it is too high, customers buy from someone else or not at all. Competition, costs and how much people want something keep pushing prices towards a level buyers will pay.

Try this

Hold a pretend auction at home with something nobody needs, like a drawing. Give everyone the same number of buttons as "money" and let them bid. Then run it again with two identical drawings. Watch whether the winning bids drop when there is more to go around.
