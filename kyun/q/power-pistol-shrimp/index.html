How does a shrimp make a bubble nearly as hot as the Sun?

A shrimp shorter than your finger snaps its claw so fast that it makes a bubble that flashes at over 4,000 °C.

The short answer

The pistol shrimp has one giant claw, sometimes half the size of its body. When it snaps the claw shut, a plunger on one half rams into a socket on the other and squirts out a jet of water at around 100 km/h. That is faster than a car on a highway, from a creature smaller than a crayon.

The jet moves so fast that the water pressure behind it drops and a bubble opens up, a process called cavitation. A split second later, the bubble collapses. For a tiny instant, the gas inside is squeezed so hard that it heats to an estimated 4,000 °C or more and gives off a flash of light. The Sun's surface is about 5,500 °C.

Surprising but true

Colonies of snapping shrimp are so loud that during the Second World War their crackle helped hide submarines from enemy listeners.

The bubble does the work

For years, people thought the snap was the sound of the claw clicking shut. In 2000, a team in the Netherlands filmed the snap with high-speed cameras and found the truth. The bang comes from the bubble collapsing, not from the claw.

The collapse sends out a shock wave strong enough to stun or kill small fish, worms and other shrimp nearby. The shrimp then drags its dinner into its burrow. It is a hunter with a gun made of water.

Close up, the snap is one of the loudest sounds made by any animal in the sea, about as loud as a gunshot at close range. Scientists who measure animal noises put it up there with whales.

A flash of light

In 2001, scientists found that the collapsing bubble also makes a flash of light, far too faint and short for human eyes. They nicknamed it "shrimpoluminescence". The colour of the light told them how hot the bubble got for that instant, which is how the estimate of over 4,000 °C was made.

The heat lasts less than a millionth of a second, and the bubble is smaller than a grain of sand. You couldn't cook anything with it. But for a blink, a patch of seawater gets almost as hot as the surface of the Sun.

The crackling sea

There are hundreds of kinds of snapping shrimp living in warm, shallow seas, including around India's coasts and reefs. A healthy reef can have thousands of them, all snapping. Divers say it sounds like bacon frying or twigs crackling on a fire.

During the Second World War, American and British navies discovered this noise the hard way. Their underwater microphones picked up so much crackle that it hid other sounds. Submarines sometimes used shrimp beds as cover, because enemy listeners couldn't pick out their engines over the shrimp. Navies still study the noise for modern submarines.

Shrimp with a bodyguard

Some pistol shrimp share their burrows with small fish called gobies. The shrimp is nearly blind, so it keeps one feeler touching the goby. When danger comes, the goby flicks its tail and both dive inside. The shrimp digs and cleans the burrow, and the goby keeps watch.

If a pistol shrimp loses its big claw, its small claw slowly grows into a new snapper, and a new small claw grows where the big one was. In other words, the shrimp switches hands. Its cousin, the mantis shrimp, uses an even stranger weapon: a punch so fast it also boils the water.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The pistol shrimp's snap comes from its claws clacking together.

Truth: High-speed cameras in 2000 showed the noise comes from a collapsing bubble, not from the claw itself. The claw just makes the jet that creates the bubble.

Try this

Fill a sink with water and clap two flat plastic lids together hard under the surface, then above it. Listen to the difference. Sound travels through water about four times faster than through air, which is why reefs full of snapping shrimp can be heard from far away.
