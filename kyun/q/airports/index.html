How does an airport work?

A big airport is a small city that never sleeps, with its own fire brigade, police, power supply and post office for suitcases.

The short answer

An airport is a giant sorting machine. It sorts people, bags and planes, and sends each to the right place at the right time. Passengers go one way, through check-in, security and boarding gates. Their bags go another, along hidden conveyor belts. Planes go a third way, along taxiways to the runway.

Behind all this are thousands of workers you rarely see: fuel crews, cleaners, caterers, engineers, firefighters and air traffic controllers. At big airports such as Atlanta in the United States or Delhi in India, the whole system runs day and night, and a single delayed flight can ripple through dozens of others.

Surprising but true

Runway numbers are compass directions. Runway 09 points east, towards 90 degrees, and its other end is runway 27, pointing west.

Two sides of the glass

Airports are split into two zones. The "landside" is open to anyone: car parks, drop-off lanes and check-in halls. The "airside" is everything past security, including the departure gates, the aprons where planes park and the runways themselves.

To move from one side to the other you must show a ticket and pass through security screening. On international flights you also visit immigration, where officers check your passport, and on arrival you may go through customs, where goods you carry can be checked. Every rule is about one question: who and what is allowed near the planes.

The secret life of a suitcase

When you check in a bag, it gets a paper tag with a barcode and a ten-digit number. The bag then drops onto a conveyor belt and vanishes. Behind the walls is a maze of belts, sometimes kilometres long, where scanners read each tag and push bags left or right towards the right flight.

Every bag is also X-rayed for safety. At the end, handlers load the bags into containers or straight into the plane's belly. At your destination the process runs backwards, ending on the carousel where you wait and hope. Bags that need to change planes are pulled out and sent on again, sometimes with less than an hour to make the connection. A missing bag can usually be traced, because its tag has been scanned at every step.

Runways and wind

A runway is not just a long road. It is built with thick layers of concrete or asphalt to take the thump of a landing jet weighing hundreds of tonnes. Big airports often have runways 3–4 km long, about as long as 30 to 40 football fields laid end to end.

Each runway is painted with a number from its compass direction. Pilots prefer to take off and land facing into the wind, which gives extra lift at a lower speed. That is why airports in windy places often have runways pointing in different directions, and why the runway in use can change during the day.

A city with its own rules

Big airports have their own fire stations, with crews trained to reach any point on a runway within a few minutes. They have power plants, water supplies, hotels and shops. Some are working on cleaner energy. In 2015, Kochi's Cochin International Airport in Kerala began running entirely on solar power, from fields of panels next to the runway. It is often described as the first airport in the world to do so.

Airports must keep animals away too. Birds sucked into engines are a real danger, so many airports use trained falcons, loud noises or careful grass cutting to keep flocks from settling near the runways. Airports also keep an eye on nearby ponds and rubbish dumps, since both attract birds.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Planes take off with the wind behind them, to get a push.

Truth: Pilots take off and land into the wind whenever they can. Air rushing over the wings creates lift, so a headwind lets the plane get airborne at a lower speed and on a shorter stretch of runway.

Try this

Use a compass app on a phone, with a grown-up's help, to find which way your street runs. Work out what runway number it would have from each end, by dividing the compass reading by ten and rounding. The two numbers should always differ by 18.
