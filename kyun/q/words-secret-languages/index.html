What are secret languages?

For centuries, women in one corner of China wrote to each other in a secret script that most men could not read.

The short answer

A secret language is a way of speaking or writing that only insiders can understand. Some are simple games that twist ordinary words, like Pig Latin in English. Others are full scripts or codes, created to keep messages private from outsiders.

People make secret languages for fun, to bond with friends, to hide plans from rivals or to protect themselves. Children invent them in playgrounds everywhere. But adults have used them too, from street traders to soldiers to women in a corner of China who built an entire secret script.

Surprising but true

The English slang word "ixnay", meaning "no" or "stop", comes straight from Pig Latin. It is "nix" with the first sound moved to the end and "ay" added.

Twisting ordinary words

The easiest secret languages take a normal language and scramble it with a rule. In Pig Latin, you move the first consonant sound to the end of each word and add "ay". So "secret" becomes "ecretsay". It sounds like nonsense until you crack the rule.

In the USA, a 1970s children's TV show called Zoom made Ubbi Dubbi famous. You add "ub" before each vowel sound, so "talk" becomes "tubalk". In France, young people speak verlan, flipping the syllables of words. The name itself comes from l'envers, "the reverse". Some verlan words are now so common they appear in French dictionaries. Meuf, verlan for femme, meaning woman, is one of them.

A script just for women

In Jiangyong County in Hunan province, China, women once wrote in a script called Nüshu, which means "women's writing". For centuries, most girls there were not taught to read Chinese characters. So women developed their own slender, slanted script to write songs, letters and diaries.

Mothers taught it to daughters, and friends sewed it into fans and handkerchiefs. When a girl married and moved away, her friends sometimes gave her a book of Nüshu songs. Many writings were burnt or buried with their owners, so fewer survive than were written. The last woman who learnt it the traditional way died in 2004. Today, it is taught again, and China has listed it as part of its national heritage.

A code no one could break

During the Second World War, the US Marines used a living language as a code. Navajo speakers, known as code talkers, sent messages by radio in Navajo, with special words for military things. A tank was called a "turtle", and a submarine an "iron fish".

The language was complex and almost unknown outside the Navajo Nation. Enemy codebreakers never cracked it. The code talkers' work stayed secret for decades. They were only honoured publicly long after the war.

Twins, traders and performers

Some twins develop a private way of talking as toddlers, using words only they understand. In the 1970s, twin sisters in San Diego, USA, spoke in a language so strange that experts first thought it was invented. It turned out to be a blend of English and German they had heard at home, spoken very fast and changed in their own way.

Traders in markets around the world have used private codes for prices so customers could not follow along. Travelling entertainers, actors and other groups who faced unfair treatment have used secret slangs to protect themselves. In Britain, a slang called Polari was used by performers and others in the 1900s, and some of its words slipped into everyday English.

Some secret languages are not spoken at all. In parts of Turkey, the Canary Islands and elsewhere, people talk across valleys by whistling. Others are hidden in writing, using codes and ciphers that scramble letters by a secret rule. Today, the messages on your phone are protected by a secret language of maths, called encryption, that no human could ever read by eye.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Secret languages are just for kids playing games.

Truth: Adults have used them for centuries, from market traders and travelling performers to soldiers and women in rural China. Some, like the Navajo code, helped win battles.

Try this

Invent a secret language with a friend. Pick a rule, such as adding "ub" before every vowel sound ("hello" becomes "hubellubo"), or saying every word backwards. Practise until you can hold a short conversation that others can't follow.
