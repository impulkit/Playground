How did India's numerals spread to the world?

The digits on your phone, a cricket scoreboard and a dollar bill all began in India, and Europe called them "Arabic" because Arab scholars passed them on.

The short answer

The ten digits used almost everywhere today, 0 to 9, grew out of number signs first written in India over 2,000 years ago. Indian mathematicians then added two big ideas: place value, where a digit's position changes its worth, and a zero to fill empty places.

From India the system travelled west. Scholars in Baghdad learned it and wrote books about it in Arabic. Traders and mathematicians carried it across North Africa and into Europe, where people called the digits "Arabic numerals". Their shapes changed along the way, but the idea stayed the same, and it slowly pushed aside clumsy systems like Roman numerals.

Surprising but true

India's Constitution says the official numerals of the Indian government are "the international form of Indian numerals", the same 0 to 9 used around the world.

Marks on stone

The oldest ancestors of our digits are Brahmi numerals. Some appear in inscriptions from the time of Emperor Ashoka in the 3rd century BCE, and more in a cave inscription at Naneghat in Maharashtra from about 2,000 years ago.

Brahmi numbers weren't yet a place-value system. They had separate signs for 1 to 9, and also for 10, 20, 30, 100 and so on, a bit like Roman numerals. But the signs for 1 to 9 are the ones that, over many centuries and many scripts, slowly turned into today's digits.

The big idea: position and zero

By about 1,500 years ago, Indian mathematicians were writing numbers using only nine digits, where position showed value. They also needed a symbol for "nothing here", first a dot and later a small circle.

The astronomer Aryabhata worked with place value in 499 CE. In 628, Brahmagupta wrote rules for adding, subtracting and multiplying with zero. A stone inscription in the Chaturbhuj temple at Gwalior, dated 876 CE, writes the number 270 with a clear round zero. It is one of the oldest securely dated zeros in India.

An older candidate is the Bakhshali manuscript, found buried in a field near Peshawar in 1881. In 2017, carbon dating suggested some of its birch-bark pages might be from the 3rd or 4th century. Other pages came out centuries younger, and historians still argue about what that means.

Through Baghdad to Europe

In the 8th and 9th centuries, Baghdad was home to scholars who gathered books from Greece, Persia and India and translated them into Arabic. Around 825 CE, a Persian mathematician named al-Khwarizmi wrote a book on calculating with the Indian numerals. When it was translated into Latin, his name became "Algoritmi", which later gave English the word algorithm.

In 1202, the Italian Leonardo of Pisa, better known as Fibonacci, published Liber Abaci, "The Book of Calculation". He had learned the numerals as a boy in North Africa, where his father worked as a trader. He showed Italian merchants how much faster accounts could be kept with them.

Change was slow. In 1299, the bankers' guild of Florence told members to write sums in words or Roman numerals, partly because a 0 could easily be turned into a 6 or 9. Only after printing spread in the 1400s and 1500s did the new digits win everywhere in Europe.

Same idea, different shapes

The digits look different across the world. Many Arabic-speaking countries use a set called Eastern Arabic numerals, which look quite unlike the "Arabic" digits used in Europe and the Americas. India has its own forms too, in Devanagari, Bengali, Tamil and other scripts.

When India wrote its Constitution, the drafters chose for the Union government "the international form of Indian numerals". So the digits printed on every Indian banknote, train ticket and exam paper use an idea that started in Brahmi stone inscriptions and passed through Baghdad and Pisa on its way to the whole world.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The numerals 0 to 9 were invented by Arabs, which is why they're called Arabic numerals.

Truth: They were developed in India. Arab and Persian scholars learned them, wrote about them and passed them to Europe, and Arabic writers often called them "Hindi numerals".
