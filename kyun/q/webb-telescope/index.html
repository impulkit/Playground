How does the Webb telescope see into the past?

Its golden mirror catches light that left galaxies over 13 billion years ago.

The short answer

Light takes time to travel. Sunlight takes about 8 minutes to reach us. Light from very distant galaxies has been travelling for billions of years. So when the James Webb Space Telescope catches that light, it sees those galaxies as they were long ago, not as they are now.

Webb is the largest telescope ever sent into space. Its huge golden mirror can collect very faint light from galaxies that formed only a few hundred million years after the universe began. That makes it a kind of time machine, looking back across more than 13 billion years.

Surprising but true

Webb's mirror is made of 18 hexagons, the same shape bees use for honeycomb.

Seeing heat

Webb mostly sees infrared light, which lies just beyond red in the rainbow. Our eyes can't see it, though we feel some of it as warmth.

There are two big reasons to look in infrared. First, as the universe expands, light from the most distant galaxies gets stretched on its long journey, from visible colours into infrared. Second, infrared passes through clouds of dust that block ordinary light. That lets Webb peer inside dusty nebulas to watch stars being born.

A golden honeycomb

Webb's main mirror is 6.5 m across, wider than a giraffe is tall. It is made of 18 hexagons of a light metal called beryllium, each coated with a very thin layer of gold, which reflects infrared light well. All the gold on the mirror together weighs about as much as a golf ball.

The mirror was too big to fit inside any rocket. So engineers designed Webb to fold up, like origami, and unfold in space. After launch, it took about two weeks to unfold its sunshield and mirror. Hundreds of parts had to work perfectly, with no one there to fix them.

Why are honeycomb cells hexagons?Bees don't know geometry. But they build the best shape anyway.

Kept very cold

Because Webb sees heat, its own warmth could drown out the faint glow of distant galaxies. So it must be very cold, below about −220 °C.

A sunshield the size of a tennis court, made of five thin layers, blocks heat from the Sun, Earth and Moon. One side faces the Sun and heats up to more than 80 °C. The other side stays colder than anywhere naturally found on Earth. Webb orbits a point about 1.5 million km from Earth, roughly four times farther than the Moon, where it can keep the Sun, Earth and Moon behind its shield.

What it has found

Webb launched on 25 December 2021, on a European Ariane 5 rocket from French Guiana in South America. Its first deep picture, released in July 2022, showed thousands of galaxies in a patch of sky about the size of a grain of sand held at arm's length.

Since then, it has spotted some of the most distant galaxies ever seen. It has found carbon dioxide in the air of a planet around another star, one of many exoplanets it studies. It has photographed star nurseries, dying stars and the planets of our own solar system in new detail.

Hubble's partner

Webb is often called the successor to the Hubble Space Telescope, launched in 1990. Its mirror collects about six times as much light as Hubble's. But Hubble still works, and sees mostly visible and ultraviolet light. Together, the two give astronomers a fuller picture of the universe, from nearby planets to the first galaxies.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Webb replaced the Hubble Space Telescope.

Truth: Hubble is still working. The two telescopes see different kinds of light, Hubble mainly visible and ultraviolet, Webb mainly infrared, so astronomers often use them together to study the same objects.

Try this

Point a TV remote at the camera of a phone (the front, selfie camera often works best) and press a button. On the screen you may see a light flashing on the tip of the remote. Your eyes can't see that infrared light, but the camera can, a little like Webb.
