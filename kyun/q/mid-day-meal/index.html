What is the mid-day meal scheme?

Every school day, India serves a hot lunch to more than 10 crore children, the largest school meal programme in the world.

The short answer

The mid-day meal scheme gives a free cooked lunch to children in government and government-aided schools across India. Since 2021 it has been called PM POSHAN. It feeds more than 10 crore children on school days, in more than 10 lakh schools.

A typical meal might be rice or roti with dal and vegetables. Some states add eggs, milk, fruit or millets. The idea is simple: children who are not hungry can learn better, and families are more likely to send children to school when a meal is waiting there.

Surprising but true

Some of the kitchens that cook these meals are so big that they make tens of thousands of lunches every morning before school begins.

Where it began

The idea started small. In 1925 the Madras city corporation began giving meals to poor children at some of its schools. In the 1950s and 1960s, K. Kamaraj, Chief Minister of Madras State, spread free school lunches widely, hoping more children would come to class.

In 1982 Chief Minister M. G. Ramachandran expanded it into a very large noon meal scheme in Tamil Nadu. Gujarat and Kerala ran their own programmes too. The national scheme started on 15 August 1995, though at first many states handed out dry grain instead of hot food.

A court steps in

In 2001 a group called the People's Union for Civil Liberties went to the Supreme Court. They argued that India had plenty of grain in its warehouses while children went hungry. The court agreed and ordered every state to serve cooked meals in government primary schools.

It was an unusual case: a court deciding what goes into a lunch plate. But it treated food as part of a child's right to life and education. Over the next few years hot meals spread across the country.

Does it work?

Researchers have studied the scheme for decades. Many studies found that school meals helped raise attendance, especially among girls and children from poorer families. Some found children grew taller and healthier, and that eating together helped break down old barriers of caste.

The scheme also has problems. Some schools have poor kitchens, money arrives late, or meals lack enough nutrients. In 2013 contaminated food at a school in Bihar killed 23 children, which led to stricter safety checks. Whether to serve eggs has been debated in several states, because some families are vegetarian.

Giant kitchens

In many villages, local cooks prepare meals in each school. In cities, big central kitchens often take over. The Akshaya Patra Foundation, which began in Bengaluru in 2000, runs kitchens with huge steam cauldrons and conveyor belts. Each kitchen cooks many thousands of meals every morning, and trucks deliver them to schools in time for lunch.

What is on the plate

Menus vary from state to state, and that is deliberate, because local food is cheaper and children are more likely to eat what they know. Karnataka serves dishes like bisi bele bath, and some districts have added ragi. Odisha and Tamil Nadu serve eggs on several days a week. Rajasthan has given children milk.

The government sets rules for how much grain, pulses, vegetables and oil each child should get. That way, the meal gives a fair share of the energy and protein a growing child needs in a day.

The United States has its own programme. In 1946 President Harry Truman signed the National School Lunch Act. Today the programme serves lunch to about 30 million children on a typical school day. Children from families with lower incomes eat for free or at a reduced price, and some schools serve free meals to everyone.

The menus are very different, with milk cartons and sandwiches instead of sambar and rice. But the idea is the same one Madras tried a century ago. A hungry child cannot learn much.

Myth or fact?

Myth: School meals in India are only for very poor children.

Truth: Every child in the covered classes at government and government-aided schools can eat the mid-day meal, whatever their family earns. Sharing the same food also helps children from different backgrounds eat together.

Try this

Plan a school lunch for one week on paper. Each day, include a grain like rice, roti or millet, something with protein like dal or eggs, and at least one vegetable. Compare your menu with a friend's and see whose is more varied.
