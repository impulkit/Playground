Who writes Wikipedia, and how does it stay accurate?

The biggest encyclopedia ever made is written by volunteers who are never paid, and anyone, including you, can press "Edit".

The short answer

Wikipedia is written by volunteers. There is no paid team of writers. Anyone with an internet connection can edit most pages, and hundreds of thousands of people do so every month, in more than 300 languages, from Hindi and Tamil to English and Spanish.

That sounds like a recipe for chaos. What keeps it in order is a set of rules, a huge crowd of people checking each other's work, and a complete history of every change. If someone gets it wrong, others can see exactly what changed and put it right.

Surprising but true

Every change ever made to a Wikipedia article is saved, so you can go back and read what a page said years ago.

A rule book everyone shares

Wikipedia has three main content rules. Articles must have a neutral point of view, which means describing all the important views fairly without taking sides. Every claim should be verifiable, so readers can check it against a reliable published source. And there is no original research: you can't add your own new discovery, only what trusted sources have already reported.

That last rule surprises people. A scientist who has made a new finding still has to wait until it is published elsewhere before it belongs on Wikipedia. The encyclopedia is meant to collect knowledge, not create it. When editors disagree, they argue it out on a "talk page" behind each article, and the debate stays there for anyone to read.

Watchers, bots and the history button

Each article keeps a list of every edit ever made. Editors can add pages to a watchlist and get told when something changes. Small computer programs called bots scan new edits around the clock, looking for signs of vandalism such as rude words or whole sections deleted at once.

So a joke edit on a famous page often vanishes within minutes. Quiet pages get fewer visitors, though, and a mistake there can sit unnoticed for longer. Like a cricket umpire with a TV replay, the system relies on being able to look back at exactly what happened. Pages that keep getting attacked can be locked so only experienced editors can change them.

From a failed start to a giant

Wikipedia grew out of an earlier project called Nupedia, which used experts and a slow review process. In nearly a year it finished only a handful of articles. In January 2001 its founders, Jimmy Wales and Larry Sanger, tried a side project using a "wiki", a kind of website anyone can edit. It grew so quickly that it soon swallowed the original plan.

Today the English edition alone has about 7 million articles. The site is run by the Wikimedia Foundation, a non-profit based in San Francisco that pays for the servers mostly through small donations from readers. It carries no adverts. Its text is free for anyone to copy and reuse, much like open-source software.

How to use it well

Teachers often say "don't cite Wikipedia", and they have a point. An encyclopedia is a summary, so the real evidence lives in the references at the bottom of each page. Good researchers read the article to get the big picture, then follow those links to the original books, news reports and studies.

That habit works on any website. Asking "where did this come from?" is the best defence against fake news, and it is exactly how historians judge their sources. Some topics have very good articles, written and checked by many careful people. Others are thin, out of date or have a warning box at the top saying the sources need work.

Gaps in the record

Wikipedia reflects the people who write it. Studies by the foundation found most editors are men, and many live in North America and Europe. That leaves gaps: fewer articles about women, and fewer about places in Africa and Asia, including many Indian villages and languages. Volunteer groups now run "edit-a-thons" to fill them, writing articles in languages such as Odia, Kannada and Santali. The encyclopedia that anyone can edit still depends on who actually turns up to do it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Wikipedia can't be trusted at all because anyone can write anything.

Truth: Anyone can edit, but edits must be backed by published sources, and thousands of volunteers check changes. A 2005 study in the journal Nature found its science articles had errors at a rate close to Britannica's. It is a good place to start, and its sources are a good place to check.

Try this

Pick a Wikipedia article about your town or a favourite animal. Scroll to the bottom and look at the numbered references, then click "View history" at the top to see who changed it and when. Try to find the oldest version and compare it with today's.
