What are weddings like around the world?

At a Jewish wedding the groom stamps on a glass, in Germany friends smash plates the night before, and in India a groom may ride to his wedding on a horse.

The short answer

Almost every culture celebrates marriage, but in wildly different ways. In India, a Hindu couple walks around a sacred fire seven times. At a Sikh wedding, the couple circles the holy book four times. In a Muslim nikah, both say they accept the marriage in front of witnesses. In China, red is everywhere, from the bride's dress to the envelopes of money. In Japan, a Shinto couple shares sips of sake. Guests in many countries throw rice, petals or confetti.

Weddings mix religion, family, food and fun. Some are tiny and quiet. Others last for days and feed hundreds of guests.

Surprising but true

White wedding dresses became popular only after Britain's Queen Victoria wore one in 1840. Before that, many brides simply wore their best dress, in any colour.

India's long weddings

Weddings in India can stretch over several days. Before a Hindu or Muslim wedding, the bride's hands and feet are often decorated with mehendi, patterns drawn with henna paste that dries dark orange. Haldi, a turmeric paste, is rubbed on the couple for glowing skin and good luck.

In many north Indian weddings, the groom arrives in a noisy procession called a baraat, sometimes on a white horse, with a brass band and dancing relatives. The main ceremony may happen late at night, at a time chosen as lucky. Sikh couples marry in the morning at a gurdwara, walking around the Guru Granth Sahib as hymns are sung. Guest lists can run into hundreds or even thousands, and teams of cooks stir food in giant pots for days.

Smashing and stamping

Some weddings are loud on purpose. At the end of a Jewish wedding, under a canopy called a chuppah, the groom often stamps on a glass wrapped in cloth. Everyone shouts "Mazel tov", meaning good luck. One explanation is that it reminds people of sad times in history, even on a happy day.

In Germany, friends of the couple may gather the night before for Polterabend. They smash old plates and pots, and the couple sweeps up together. It is meant to show they can face a mess as a team.

Red for luck, white for brides

Colours mean different things in different places. In China and much of India, red is the colour of luck and joy, so brides wear red saris, lehengas or silk jackets. In parts of East Asia, white was long worn for mourning.

In Britain, Queen Victoria married Prince Albert in a white satin dress in 1840. Pictures of it spread, and rich brides copied her. Over the next century, white became the classic Western wedding colour. Before that, many brides wore their best dress in blue, grey or any colour.

Cups, ropes and coins

At a Shinto wedding in Japan, the bride and groom take turns sipping sake from three cups of different sizes, three sips from each. The ritual is called san-san-kudo, meaning "three, three, nine".

In Mexico and the Philippines, a large looped cord or string of beads called a lazo is often draped around the couple's shoulders, showing they are joined. Many couples there are also given 13 coins, a symbol of sharing everything they own.

Tying the knot

In parts of Scotland and Ireland, an old custom called handfasting ties the couple's hands together with ribbon or cloth. Some people link it to the English phrase "tying the knot", though nobody knows for sure where the phrase began.

Some African American couples "jump the broom" at the end of the ceremony, a tradition remembered from the days of slavery, when many enslaved people were not allowed to marry legally. And the strangest part? In many places a wedding is seen as joining two whole families rather than two people, and family life around the world shows how different those families can be.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The ring goes on the fourth finger because a vein runs straight from it to the heart.

Truth: That idea comes from an old Roman belief in a "vein of love". It isn't true. All fingers have similar veins. The custom stuck anyway, and in some countries rings are worn on the right hand instead.
