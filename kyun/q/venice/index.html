How was Venice built on water?

Under Venice's palaces lies a hidden forest. Millions of wooden poles were hammered into the mud, and some have held the city up for over a thousand years.

The short answer

Venice sits in a shallow lagoon on the north-east coast of Italy, spread across about 118 small islands. The ground was soft mud, which could not hold up heavy stone buildings. So the Venetians drove millions of wooden poles straight down through the mud, packed them tightly together, and laid platforms of wood and stone on top. Then they built palaces, churches and houses on those platforms.

Instead of streets, much of Venice has canals, about 150 of them, crossed by around 400 bridges. There are no cars in the old city. People walk, or travel by boat.

Surprising but true

Venice's wooden foundation poles have not rotted because they are buried in mud with almost no oxygen, where rot-causing microbes cannot live.

Escape to the lagoon

About 1,500 years ago, the Roman Empire in the west was falling apart and armies swept through northern Italy. People living on the mainland fled to the marshy islands of the lagoon, where the shallow water and mudflats made it hard for attackers to follow. At first they lived as fishers and salt makers.

Over time the scattered villages joined into a city. Venetians learned to manage the lagoon, digging channels and building up the islands.

A forest upside down

To build, workers hammered wooden poles, often of oak, alder and larch, deep into the mud. The poles were packed so closely that together they acted like solid ground. The church of Santa Maria della Salute, finished in 1687, rests on more than a million of them.

Wood usually rots, but under Venice it lies in wet mud and clay with almost no oxygen. Without oxygen, the fungi and bacteria that cause rot cannot do their work. On top of the poles, builders laid stone from Istria that water can't easily soak into, to protect the brick walls above.

A trading superpower

For about 1,100 years Venice was an independent republic, led by an elected ruler called the Doge. Its ships carried silk, spices, glass and salt between Europe, the Middle East and Asia. The traveller Marco Polo was a Venetian merchant who journeyed to China in the 1270s.

Venice's trade routes made it one of the richest cities in Europe. In 1291, its glassmakers were moved to the island of Murano, partly to protect the city from fires. Murano glass is still famous today.

The republic ended in 1797, when Napoleon's army took the city.

Gondolas and canals

The Grand Canal winds through the city like a backwards S, lined with palaces. The Rialto Bridge, built of stone in 1591, crosses it. Gondolas, long thin black boats, are rowed by a gondolier standing at the back with one oar. A gondola is built slightly lopsided, curved more on one side, so it travels straight even though it is rowed from one side only.

High water

Venice floods often, especially in autumn and winter, when high tides and winds push water into the lagoon. Venetians call it acqua alta, "high water". In November 2019, water rose about 1.9 m above normal, flooding most of the city. The city is also slowly sinking, while the sea level is rising.

To fight back, engineers built MOSE, a barrier of 78 giant gates on the seabed at the lagoon's entrances. When a dangerous tide comes, the gates fill with air and rise to hold back the sea. They were first raised in October 2020, and St Mark's Square stayed dry.

A city of visitors

Only about 50,000 people now live in the old city, far fewer than in the past, while millions of tourists come each year. Since 2024, day visitors on the busiest days have had to pay a small entry fee to help the city cope with the crowds.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Venice floats on rafts or platforms on the water.

Truth: Venice does not float. Its buildings stand on wooden poles driven down through soft mud into firmer clay below. Stone platforms on top of the poles hold up the walls.

Try this

Test buried wood. Take two ice-lolly sticks. Push one deep into a jar of wet mud or wet soil and seal it. Leave the other in a jar of water open to the air. Check both after a few weeks with an adult. Which one smells or looks more rotten?
