Why do we fart?

Most people fart somewhere between 5 and 15 times a day, and the gas that smells the worst makes up less than one part in a hundred.

The short answer

You fart because gas builds up in your gut and has to go somewhere. Some of it is air you swallow while eating, drinking or talking. Most of the rest is made by bacteria living in your large intestine.

These microbes feast on the bits of food your body can't break down itself, like certain fibres and sugars in beans, onions and whole grains. As they eat, they give off gas. The gas collects, moves along the gut and finally escapes. Everyone does it, from newborn babies to kings and queens.

Surprising but true

Herring fish seem to talk to each other at night by letting out tiny streams of bubbles from their bottoms, making a high-pitched ticking sound.

Swallowed air

Every time you gulp water, chew gum or eat fast, a little air slips down with your food. Fizzy drinks add extra bubbles of carbon dioxide. Much of this air comes back up as a burp. The part that keeps going down travels about 7 to 9 m through your intestines.

That is why the main gas in many farts is nitrogen, the same gas that makes up most of the air around you. It has no smell at all.

A gas factory in your gut

Your large intestine is home to trillions of friendly microbes. There are roughly as many bacterial cells in you as human cells. They help you digest food, make some vitamins and fight off harmful germs.

But they are messy eaters. Beans and many dals contain sugars called oligosaccharides. Your body has no tool to split them, so they reach the bacteria untouched. The bacteria break them down and release hydrogen and carbon dioxide. In about a third to half of people, other microbes turn some of that hydrogen into methane.

This is one reason Indian cooks add a pinch of hing, or asafoetida, to dal. It is a traditional way to reduce gas, and some studies suggest it helps a little.

What do the bacteria in your gut do?Tens of trillions of bacteria live in your gut. They eat what you can't digest, make vitamins and help train your body to fight germs.

Where the smell comes from

Only a tiny part of a fart, usually less than 1%, makes it stink. These are sulfur gases such as hydrogen sulfide, the "rotten egg" smell. Foods rich in sulfur, like eggs, meat, cabbage, cauliflower and garlic, can make farts smellier.

Your nose is extremely sensitive to hydrogen sulfide. It can pick up less than one part in a million in the air. So a fart can be almost all harmless gas and still clear a room.

Why farts make noise

The sound has nothing to do with your bottom cheeks clapping. It comes from the muscle ring at the end of your gut vibrating as gas squeezes through. The faster the gas and the tighter the opening, the higher the note.

In the 1890s, a French performer named Joseph Pujol became famous as Le Pétomane. He could control those muscles so well that he played tunes on stage in Paris.

Farts on Earth and beyond

Animals fart too. Termites release methane as their gut microbes digest wood. Cows produce a lot of methane, though most of it actually comes out as burps from their front end.

Herring are even stranger. Scientists recorded them releasing streams of bubbles from their bottoms at night, making a high-pitched ticking sound. The researchers think it may help shoals stay together in the dark.

In the 1960s, NASA scientists studied fart gases while planning space missions, because hydrogen and methane can burn and a spacecraft cabin is a small, closed room. Their tests helped shape early astronaut menus, and planning space food has been a strange puzzle of its own ever since.

When to ask a doctor

Farting is normal and healthy. It shows your gut microbes are busy. If someone has belly pain that won't go away, a swollen tummy, or gas along with diarrhoea for days, they should tell a parent or see a doctor.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Holding in a fart is dangerous and can poison you.

Truth: It can feel uncomfortable and give you a bloated tummy, but it won't harm you. Some of the gas soaks back into your blood and leaves through your lungs when you breathe out, and the rest comes out later.

Try this

Watch microbes make gas. With a grown-up, put a spoon of dry yeast, a spoon of sugar and half a cup of warm water into a small bottle. Stretch a balloon over the top and wait 20 minutes. The yeast eats the sugar and blows up the balloon, a bit like the microbes in your gut.
