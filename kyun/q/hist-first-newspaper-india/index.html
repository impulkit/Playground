What was India's first newspaper?

India's first newspaper, printed in Calcutta in 1780, made fun of the most powerful man in British India, and its editor ended up in jail.

The short answer

India's first newspaper was Hicky's Bengal Gazette, first printed in Calcutta on 29 January 1780. Its founder, James Augustus Hicky, was an Irishman who had come to India and fallen into debt. While in prison for that debt, he learned about printing, and when he got out he started a weekly paper.

It was only two sheets, printed on both sides, and full of gossip, adverts, poems and news from ships. But it also mocked the most powerful people in British India, including the Governor-General, Warren Hastings. Within two years, the authorities shut it down.

Surprising but true

Bombay Samachar, a Gujarati paper started in 1822, is still being printed, making it one of the oldest newspapers in Asia that has never stopped.

A paper that bit back

Calcutta in 1780 was the capital of the East India Company's territory. Company officials made fortunes, sometimes dishonestly, and nobody dared to write about it. Hicky did. He gave them nicknames, hinted at bribes and complained about how ordinary soldiers and poor Calcuttans were treated.

The paper was hugely popular. People passed copies around and read them aloud in taverns and coffee houses. Hicky called his paper "open to all parties, but influenced by none".

Rich officials were not the only targets. Hicky also wrote about leaking roofs, rubbish in the streets, dangerous horse carriages and the hardships of poor European soldiers. His paper carried adverts for runaway servants, ships leaving for England, wine, horses and lost property, which today give historians a rare look at daily life in 18th-century Calcutta.

Silenced

Warren Hastings was furious. First, his government stopped the paper from being sent through the post. Then Hicky was sued for libel, which means printing false things that damage someone's name. He was sent back to prison but kept printing from his cell for a while.

In March 1782, officials seized his press and type. The Bengal Gazette was finished. Hicky died poor, years later. Today historians see him as an early, rough-edged fighter for free speech in India.

Indian voices in print

Other English papers followed in Calcutta, Madras and Bombay. Indian-language papers came next. In 1818, missionaries at Serampore, near Calcutta, began Samachar Darpan in Bengali. In 1822, Fardunji Marzban started Bombay Samachar in Gujarati, and it is still published today.

Reformer Raja Ram Mohan Roy started papers in Bengali and Persian in the early 1820s to argue for social change and against practices he thought were cruel. On 30 May 1826, Udant Martand, the first Hindi newspaper, appeared in Calcutta. It lasted only about a year and a half, but 30 May is still marked as Hindi Journalism Day. It closed partly because sending copies by post to Hindi readers far away cost too much.

Papers that fought for freedom

As the freedom movement grew, newspapers became one of its sharpest tools. Bal Gangadhar Tilak ran Kesari in Marathi and was jailed for what he wrote. Mahatma Gandhi edited Young India and Harijan. The British passed laws to control the press, and editors were often fined or imprisoned.

The Times of India began in 1838 as The Bombay Times and Journal of Commerce. The Hindu started in Madras in 1878, set up by a group of young men who were angry about how the British-run papers treated Indians.

Still reading the news

India today has one of the biggest newspaper markets on Earth. Crores of copies roll off presses every night in dozens of languages, and Hindi papers such as Dainik Jagran and Dainik Bhaskar are among the most-read in the world. Much of their news is now read on phones too. Yet every story still starts the same way it did in Hicky's day, with someone asking questions, which is the heart of journalism.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The first newspapers in India were started by Indians fighting for freedom.

Truth: The very first was started by an Irishman, James Augustus Hicky, in 1780. Indian-owned papers in Indian languages came a few decades later, and many of them did go on to fight for freedom.
