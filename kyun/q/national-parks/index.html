Why do countries make national parks?

Yellowstone became the world's first national park in 1872. India's first followed in 1936.

The short answer

A national park is land a country protects so that wild nature isn't cut down, built over or hunted out. People can still visit, learn and enjoy it, but the plants and animals come first.

In 1872, the USA made Yellowstone the world's first national park, to protect its geysers, forests and animals. Since then, almost every country has created parks of its own.

Parks protect more than pretty views. They keep forests that store water, rivers that supply towns, and animals that might otherwise vanish.

Surprising but true

India's first national park, started in 1936, was later renamed after Jim Corbett, a tiger hunter who became a famous protector of tigers.

The first park

In the 1860s and 1870s, explorers came back from the Yellowstone region, in today's Wyoming, Montana and Idaho, with stories of boiling springs and water shooting into the sky. Some people didn't believe them.

Expeditions brought back paintings and photographs. Congress was convinced, and on 1 March 1872, President Ulysses S. Grant signed a law making Yellowstone a "public park or pleasuring-ground for the benefit and enjoyment of the people". It was a new idea: land owned by the nation and kept wild for everyone.

India's parks

India's first national park opened in 1936 in the hills of today's Uttarakhand. It was called Hailey National Park, and is now Jim Corbett National Park. Corbett had once hunted tigers that attacked people, then spent his later life writing and campaigning to protect them.

Today India has more than 100 national parks, including Kaziranga in Assam for one-horned rhinos and Gir in Gujarat, the only home of wild Asiatic lions. Hemis in Ladakh shelters snow leopards high in the mountains.

Saving species

Parks helped save animals that were disappearing. In the USA, Yellowstone kept the last wild herd of bison. In 1995, wolves were brought back to the park after about 70 years away, and they changed how elk, trees and rivers behaved.

In India, Project Tiger began in 1973 with nine tiger reserves. There are now more than 50. India's 2022 tiger count estimated about 3,700 wild tigers, roughly three-quarters of all the wild tigers in the world.

How did wolves change the rivers of Yellowstone?In 1995, a few dozen wolves arrived in Yellowstone. Some scientists say the riverbanks started to change. Others say the story is too neat.

Parks and sanctuaries

India also has hundreds of wildlife sanctuaries. The difference is about rules. In a national park, activities like grazing cattle or collecting firewood are usually not allowed. In a sanctuary, some local uses may be permitted.

Some special areas are tiger reserves, biosphere reserves or marine parks. The Gulf of Mannar, between India and Sri Lanka, protects coral reefs, sea grass and dugongs under the water.

Visiting with care

Millions of people visit national parks every year. In the USA, Great Smoky Mountains is the most visited. In India, jeep safaris in parks like Ranthambore and Kanha are popular.

Visitors bring money and jobs to nearby villages, but too many vehicles and too much noise can disturb animals. Many parks limit how many vehicles can enter each day, and close core areas during the monsoon.

Not always simple

Some parks were made on land where people already lived, and those families had to move. Native American tribes lost land when some US parks were created. In India, forest-dwelling communities have sometimes been moved out of reserves.

Today, many parks try to work with local communities, not against them. India's Forest Rights Act of 2006 recognises the rights of forest dwellers. Former hunters and village youth often work as guides and guards, and nobody knows the forest better.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A national park is a kind of big zoo.

Truth: In a zoo, animals live in enclosures and are fed by people. In a national park, wild animals live freely in their natural home and find their own food. Visitors are the ones who stay on roads and paths.

Try this

Make a mini nature reserve survey. With string, mark out a square about 1 m on each side in a garden, park or school ground. Spend ten minutes listing every living thing you can find inside it, from grass and ants to snails and birds overhead. Come back in another season and see what has changed.
