What was the first website?

The world's first website ran on one computer in Switzerland with a handwritten label on it: "This machine is a server. DO NOT POWER IT DOWN!!"

The short answer

The first website was made by Tim Berners-Lee, a British scientist working at CERN, a physics laboratory near Geneva, Switzerland. Its address was info.cern.ch. It went live on his computer at the end of 1990, and opened to anyone on the internet in August 1991.

The site was a few plain pages of text. It explained what the World Wide Web was, how to get a browser, and how to set up your own web server. There were no pictures, no videos and no adverts. Yet those pages started something that now reaches billions of people.

Surprising but true

The first website was a page explaining what the World Wide Web was and how to make your own website, and you can still visit a restored copy today.

A problem at a physics lab

CERN is where scientists from many countries smash tiny particles together in giant machines called particle accelerators. In the 1980s, thousands of researchers came and went, each using different computers. Sharing information was a mess.

In 1989, Berners-Lee wrote a proposal for a system of linked documents. You would click a word in one document and jump to another, even if it sat on a computer in a different country. His boss wrote "vague but exciting" on the cover. That was enough to let him carry on.

Three big ideas

By the end of 1990, Berners-Lee had built three things that the Web still runs on. HTML is the language used to write web pages. URLs are the addresses, like info.cern.ch. HTTP is the set of rules computers use to send pages to each other.

He also wrote the first web browser and the first web server program. A server is a computer that stores pages and sends them out when someone asks. His ran on a black cube-shaped NeXT computer, which is why he stuck that warning label on it. If someone had switched it off, the whole Web would have vanished.

That computer still exists. It is kept in a museum at CERN, and an identical NeXT machine used by Berners-Lee is on show at the Science Museum in London. NeXT was the company Steve Jobs founded after he was pushed out of Apple, so the Web was born on a computer made by one of the founders of Apple.

Giving it away

Berners-Lee could have tried to charge money for the Web. Instead, on 30 April 1993, CERN announced that anyone could use the Web's technology for free, forever. That decision helped it spread at enormous speed.

In 1993 there were a few hundred websites. By 2000 there were millions. Today there are more than a billion registered sites (2025), though most sit empty or unused and only a small share are active. It is often called one of the most generous decisions in the history of technology.

The Web reaches India

India got public internet access on 15 August 1995, when the government company VSNL launched a dial-up service. It was slow and expensive, and you heard a screech of noises as your computer connected through the phone line. Only a few thousand people signed up in the first months. Early users paid thousands of rupees a year for a connection, and loading a single page with pictures could take minutes.

Today India has hundreds of millions of internet users, many using phones rather than computers, and the story of how that happened is told in the internet in India.

The first site, restored

The original pages changed often, and no copy of the very first version survives. In 2013, CERN rebuilt the earliest copy it could find, from 1992, and put it back at info.cern.ch. You can visit it and click the old blue links today. Finding a single page among the billions out there now needs a whole new tool, a search engine, which the first Web didn't have at all.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The internet and the World Wide Web are the same thing.

Truth: The internet is the huge network of connected computers and cables, and it began in the 1960s and 70s. The Web is one service that runs on it, made of linked pages, invented in 1989–1991. Email also runs on the internet but isn't part of the Web.
