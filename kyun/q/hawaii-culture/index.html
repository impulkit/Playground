What is Hawaiian culture like?

The first Hawaiians crossed thousands of kilometres of open Pacific in canoes, steering by stars, waves and birds, and found some of the most remote islands on Earth.

The short answer

Hawaiian culture grew on a chain of volcanic islands in the middle of the Pacific Ocean, about 3,800 km from the nearest continent. Its people descend from Polynesian voyagers who arrived by canoe, perhaps about a thousand years ago. Their culture is built around the sea, the land, family and respect for nature.

Today Hawaii is a US state, and its people come from many backgrounds. But Hawaiian traditions, such as hula, the Hawaiian language, surfing and the spirit of aloha, are alive and growing stronger.

Surprising but true

The Hawaiian alphabet has only 13 letters, including a mark called the ʻokina that stands for a short pause.

Finding the islands

The ancestors of Hawaiians were among the greatest sailors in history. In large double-hulled canoes, they crossed the Pacific with no maps or compasses. Navigators memorised where dozens of stars rose and set. They read the shape of ocean swells, the colour of the water and the flight of birds heading home to land at dusk.

For a long time, some people doubted that such voyages were done on purpose. So in 1976, a canoe called Hōkūleʻa sailed from Hawaii to Tahiti using only traditional methods. Its navigator, Mau Piailug, came from a small Micronesian island where these skills had survived. The voyage succeeded and inspired a revival of wayfinding across the Pacific. From 2014 to 2017, Hōkūleʻa sailed around the world.

Land from mountain to sea

Hawaiians divided their islands into slices called ahupuaʻa, running from the mountaintops down to the sea. Each slice had forest, farmland and fishing grounds, so a community had everything it needed. Taro, called kalo, was the main crop. Its root was pounded into a paste called poi.

Hawaiians teach that people should care for the land, the ʻāina, because it cares for them. Many of the islands' plants and birds live nowhere else, and some are now endangered.

Hula and aloha

Hula is far more than a dance. It tells stories through chant, music and movement, keeping alive history, family lines and legends. There are two main styles: kahiko, the older style performed to chant and drums, and ʻauana, a newer style with guitars and ukuleles. The Merrie Monarch Festival, held every year in Hilo, is one of the world's biggest hula competitions.

The word aloha means hello and goodbye, but also love, kindness and compassion. Many people in Hawaii describe the aloha spirit as a way of treating others.

Riding the waves

Surfing was a Hawaiian sport long before anywhere else. Chiefs and ordinary people rode waves on wooden boards. In the early 1900s, Duke Kahanamoku, a Hawaiian swimmer who won Olympic gold, gave surfing shows in Australia and California that helped spread it worldwide.

A kingdom and a language

In 1810, King Kamehameha I united the islands into one kingdom. In 1893, Queen Liliʻuokalani was overthrown by a group of American businessmen supported by US Marines. Hawaii was annexed by the USA in 1898 and became the 50th state in 1959. In 1993, the US Congress formally apologised for the overthrow.

After 1896, schools had to teach in English, and the Hawaiian language almost disappeared. Since the 1980s, Hawaiian-language preschools and schools have brought it back, a model for other endangered languages. Workers from Japan, China, the Philippines, Portugal and Korea also came to work on sugar farms, and their foods and words are part of island life today.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Hula is mainly a dance put on to entertain tourists.

Truth: Hula is an old Hawaiian way of keeping history. Its chants and movements tell stories of gods, chiefs, places and families. It is taught in schools called hālau, and serious competitions are held every year.

Try this

Read the waves. Fill a tray with water and gently tap one side with a pencil to make ripples. Put a small block in the middle like an island, and tap again. Watch how the ripples bend and bounce around the island. Polynesian navigators could feel similar patterns in the sea and sense land beyond the horizon.
