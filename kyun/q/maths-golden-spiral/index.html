Why does the same spiral appear in shells and galaxies?

A falcon diving at a pigeon, a snail shell and a hurricane all trace the same kind of curve, and it has nothing to do with the famous golden ratio.

The short answer

The spiral you see in a snail shell, a ram's horn, a hurricane and a spiral galaxy is usually a logarithmic spiral. It has a special property: each turn is a bigger copy of the one before, with exactly the same shape. Zoom in or out and it looks the same.

That is why it turns up everywhere. Anything that grows by adding a bit more on the outside, while keeping its shape, ends up drawing this curve. A shell can't rebuild its older inner rooms, so it adds bigger ones to the open end, and the spiral follows. The causes are different in different places, but the shape is the simplest answer to the same question.

Surprising but true

The mathematician Jacob Bernoulli asked for a logarithmic spiral on his gravestone in Basel, but the stone carver cut the wrong kind of spiral.

The wonderful spiral

The Swiss mathematician Jacob Bernoulli studied this curve in the late 1600s and fell in love with it. He called it spira mirabilis, the wonderful spiral, because it stayed the same however you stretched, turned or flipped it.

He asked for one to be carved on his gravestone with the Latin words "Eadem mutata resurgo", meaning "Though changed, I rise again the same". The carver, perhaps not a mathematician, cut an Archimedean spiral instead, where the gaps stay equal rather than growing. You can still see the mistake on his memorial in Basel Minster.

The golden myth

You have probably seen posters of a nautilus shell with a golden rectangle drawn over it. The golden ratio, about 1.618, is a real and lovely number. A golden spiral is one particular logarithmic spiral that grows by that ratio every quarter turn.

The problem is that real nautilus shells don't match it. When scientists measure them, the shells widen more slowly, by roughly 1.33 for the same kind of step, and different shells vary. Galaxies vary even more, with some arms wound tightly and others loosely. Mathematician Mario Livio and others have spent years politely debunking the golden-shell story.

Where golden numbers really hide

There is a true golden story, just in a different place. Look at the head of a sunflower and you will see two sets of spirals, one going clockwise and one anticlockwise. Count them and you usually get neighbouring Fibonacci numbers, such as 34 and 55.

That happens because each new seed grows about 137.5 degrees round from the last one. This "golden angle" packs seeds as tightly as possible with no gaps. Pine cones, pineapples and cauliflowers show the same trick. It is one of the best examples of the golden ratio in nature.

The falcon's curve

In 2000, the American biologist Vance Tucker showed that falcons diving at prey fly in a logarithmic spiral. A falcon's sharpest vision looks out to the side, at an angle of about 40 degrees. If it turned its head to look straight ahead, the air resistance would slow it down. So it keeps its head straight and its eye fixed on the prey, which bends its path into a spiral.

That is the same rule that gives the spiral its other name, equiangular. The curve crosses every line from the centre at the same angle, just as the falcon keeps the same angle to its target. The peregrine falcon, fastest animal on Earth, is drawing maths as it dives.

Storms and stars

Hurricanes and spiral galaxies form their arms in very different ways from shells. A hurricane's cloud bands curve as air spins inward toward low pressure. Galaxy arms are waves of crowding, where stars and gas pile up as they orbit, like a traffic jam on a ring road. Yet both end up close to logarithmic spirals, because that shape is what many spinning, growing systems settle into.

From a snail on a garden wall to a galaxy 100,000 light-years across, the same curve keeps appearing. And patterns that repeat at every size have their own name and their own strange beauty, called fractals.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Nautilus shells and spiral galaxies are perfect golden spirals.

Truth: Both are close to logarithmic spirals, but measurements show their growth rates vary and rarely match the golden ratio. The golden ratio does appear in the way many plants arrange seeds and leaves.

Try this

Draw a dot in the middle of a page. Draw a line out from it in any direction, then turn the page a little and draw the next line slightly longer, always increasing by the same proportion, say 10 percent. Join the line ends and you will get a logarithmic spiral. Change the percentage to make it tighter or looser.
