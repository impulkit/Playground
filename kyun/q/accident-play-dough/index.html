Was Play-Doh first made to clean wallpaper?

Before it was a toy, Play-Doh was a grey putty that people rubbed on their walls to wipe away soot from coal fires.

The short answer

Yes. Play-Doh began as a soft putty for cleaning wallpaper. In the 1930s, many American homes were heated by coal fires that coated walls with black soot. A company in Cincinnati called Kutol Products sold a dough that you rolled across the wallpaper to lift the dirt off.

In the 1950s, homes switched to cleaner heating and washable wallpaper, and sales crashed. Then a nursery school teacher named Kay Zufall noticed that children loved modelling with the putty. She suggested selling it as a toy and gave it the name Play-Doh. It went on sale in 1956 and has since sold billions of tubs.

Surprising but true

Play-Doh's smell is officially protected. In 2018 the USA registered it as a trademark, describing it as sweet and slightly musky, with a hint of cherry and salted dough.

A cleaner nobody needed

Kutol was a small family firm run by the McVicker family. Its wallpaper cleaner was a mixture of flour, water, salt and a few other ingredients, soft enough to knead and slightly sticky. It worked well, so well that it was one of the company's best sellers.

But after the Second World War, American homes changed. Coal furnaces gave way to oil and gas, which made far less soot. New vinyl wallpaper could be wiped with a damp cloth. By the mid-1950s, Kutol was in trouble.

The teacher with the idea

Kay Zufall ran a nursery school in New Jersey and was the sister-in-law of Joe McVicker, who worked at Kutol. She read that wallpaper cleaner could be used for modelling, bought some and tried it with her class. The children loved it.

She told McVicker to remove the cleaning chemicals, add colour and sell it as a toy. The company wanted to call it "Rainbow Modeling Compound". Zufall thought of something shorter: Play-Doh.

Colours, cartoons and a smell

The first tubs, in 1956, came in one colour: off-white. A year later red, yellow and blue arrived. A children's TV host in the USA, Captain Kangaroo, began showing Play-Doh on air, and sales took off across America.

Play-Doh's smell became famous on its own. For many adults, one sniff brings back childhood, because smell is closely linked to memory in the brain. In 2018, Hasbro, which owns Play-Doh, registered the scent as a trademark, so no other company can copy it.

Squished across the world

Play-Doh soon spread far beyond America. It's now sold in dozens of countries, and children squeeze it through plastic machines that turn out spaghetti strands, burgers and wild hairdos. In 1991 the toy company Hasbro bought the brand, and it still makes Play-Doh today.

Part of its success is that you can't get it wrong. A lump can become a snake, a cup or a dinosaur, then be squashed flat and started again. Teachers use modelling dough to help young children strengthen the small muscles in their fingers, which they later need for holding a pencil. Animators use similar clays too: every second of a clay film can need a dozen or more photos, with the models moved a tiny bit between each one. Fans in the USA even celebrate 16 September as National Play-Doh Day.

What's in it?

The exact recipe is secret, but Play-Doh is mostly water, flour and salt. The salt helps stop mould growing, which is why the dough can sit on a shelf for months. It also makes Play-Doh taste horrible, which is useful with small children.

Play-Doh was added to America's National Toy Hall of Fame in 1998. It belongs to a long line of accidental inventions. And the strangest part? Another product chemists threw away for being too sticky turned out to be one of the strongest glues ever made, as the story of superglue shows.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Play-Doh is safe to eat because it's made of flour.

Truth: Play-Doh isn't poisonous in a tiny taste, but it isn't food. It's very salty, and too much salt can make people sick and is dangerous for dogs. Keep it away from pets.

Try this

With an adult, make your own play dough. Mix 1 cup of flour, half a cup of salt and 2 teaspoons of cooking oil, then slowly add about half a cup of water while kneading. Add a few drops of food colouring. Store it in a closed box. It isn't for eating, because of all the salt.
