What famous treasures are still lost?

A whole room made of amber, a throne covered in jewels and the cargo of a sunken Portuguese ship have all vanished, and people are still looking.

The short answer

Some of the world's most valuable objects are missing, and no one knows exactly where they are. The Amber Room, a room in a Russian palace lined with golden amber panels, disappeared during the Second World War. The Peacock Throne, made for the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan, was carried off from Delhi in 1739 and later broken up. A Portuguese ship called the Flor de la Mar sank in 1511 with a hold full of treasure, and its wreck has never been confirmed.

Others are lost in a different way. Some royal eggs made by the jeweller Fabergé are still unaccounted for. And in 2025, thieves walked out of the Louvre in Paris with eight pieces of French crown jewellery that police have still not found.

Surprising but true

In 2012, a scrap-metal dealer in the USA found out that a golden egg he had bought to melt down was a lost Fabergé egg made for the Russian royal family, worth millions of dollars.

The room that vanished

The Amber Room was made in Prussia, in what is now Germany, in the early 1700s. In 1716, the Prussian king gave it to Peter the Great of Russia. It ended up in the Catherine Palace near St Petersburg, its walls glowing with about six tonnes of amber, gold leaf and mirrors.

In 1941, invading German soldiers took the room apart in about 36 hours, packed it into crates and shipped it to the city of Königsberg. In 1945, as the war ended, the trail goes cold. It may have burned in a fire. It may be hidden in a mine or a bunker. Treasure hunters are still searching. Russian craftsmen spent more than 20 years building a copy, which opened in 2003.

A throne of jewels

In Delhi, Shah Jahan, the emperor who built the Taj Mahal, sat on the Peacock Throne. It was covered in gold, rubies, emeralds and pearls, and took about seven years to make. Some accounts say it cost twice as much as the Taj Mahal itself.

In 1739, the Persian ruler Nader Shah invaded Delhi and carried the throne, and the Kohinoor diamond, back to Persia. After he was killed in 1747, the throne disappeared, probably broken up for its jewels. Bits of it might be sitting in museums or royal collections today without anyone knowing.

The ship with an emperor's fortune

The Flor de la Mar was a Portuguese ship. In 1511, it sailed from Malacca, in today's Malaysia, carrying riches taken from the city's sultan. A storm wrecked it off the coast of Sumatra. Divers and companies have searched the shallow seas there for decades. Several have claimed to find it. None has proved it.

Eggs, crowns and a scrap dealer

Between 1885 and 1916, the jeweller Peter Carl Fabergé made 50 jewelled eggs for the Russian royal family, each with a surprise hidden inside. After the Russian Revolution in 1917, many were sold or lost. In 2012, an American scrap dealer who had bought a little gold egg at a market searched the web and realised it was the missing Third Imperial Egg. He had nearly melted it down.

Royal treasure is still going missing today. On 19 October 2025, thieves broke into the Louvre, the world's most visited museum, and took eight pieces of jewellery that once belonged to French queens and empresses. It took them less than eight minutes. They also dropped a crown, which was found outside, broken. By mid-2026, the eight stolen pieces were still missing.

Why treasure stays lost

Wars, revolutions and storms are the great hiders of treasure. Objects get broken up, melted or quietly sold. Records burn. And a hunter needs luck as much as maps and metal detectors. Some of the best finds came from people who weren't looking at all. The most famous lost diamond in the story, though, isn't lost: it sits in a crown in the Tower of London, and four countries say it belongs to them.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pirates usually buried their treasure and drew maps with an X to mark the spot.

Truth: Almost no real pirates did this. They spent or shared their loot quickly. The buried-map idea comes mostly from the 1883 novel Treasure Island by Robert Louis Stevenson.
