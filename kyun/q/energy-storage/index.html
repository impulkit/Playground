How do you save sunshine for night-time?

Solar panels make loads of electricity at lunchtime, when many people are out. But in the evening, when everyone switches on lights and fans, the Sun has set. So how do we keep the afternoon's sunshine for later?

The short answer

You cannot put sunshine in a box, but you can turn the electricity it makes into a form that waits. Big batteries store it as chemical energy. Pumped-hydro plants store it by lifting water uphill.

Later, when the Sun sets or the wind drops, the stored energy is turned back into electricity and sent to homes, shops and factories.

Surprising but true

One gigawatt-hour of stored electricity could keep a 10-watt LED bulb glowing for more than 11,000 years.

The problem with sunshine and wind

Countries like India are building lots of solar panels and wind turbines. They make clean, renewable electricity that never runs out. But the Sun shines only by day, and wind comes and goes.

People's needs do not follow the weather. In many Indian homes, demand peaks in the evening, when lights, fans, TVs and kitchens all come on together. Without storage, extra afternoon power can go to waste, and coal plants must run hard at night. Storage helps renewable energy work round the clock.

How it works

Think of a water tank on your roof. The pump fills it when water is available, and you open the tap whenever you need it. Energy storage does the same thing with electricity.

Batteries. Grid batteries are giant versions of the one in your phone, packed into rows of shipping containers. When there is spare power, it pushes tiny charged bits called electrons from one side of each battery cell to the other, storing energy in chemicals. When power is needed, the electrons flow back and out into the wires. Batteries respond in a split second, which helps keep the grid steady when a power plant suddenly trips.

Pumped hydro. Two lakes, one high on a hill and one low. When there is spare electricity, pumps push water up into the top reservoir. That water now holds potential energy, like a ball held above the ground. When power is needed, the water flows down through a turbine, which spins a generator, just like at a dam. Then the water waits in the bottom lake, ready to be pumped up again.

Every time energy is stored and released, a little is lost as heat. So you always get back a bit less than you put in. Storage is still worth it, because the power is moved from a time when it is spare to a time when it is precious.

How do dams make electricity?Behind a big dam, a whole river waits. Let it fall through a pipe, and it can light up millions of homes.

Where it came from

Storing energy by pumping water uphill is an old idea. It was first used in the 1890s, in Italy and Switzerland, where mountain lakes made it easy. Over the next century, pumped hydro became the main way the world stored electricity. Only in the 2020s did big batteries begin to overtake it in some countries, such as the USA.

Big batteries are much newer. The lithium-ion battery reached shops in the early 1990s, first in cameras and phones, then in laptops and electric cars. As factories made more of them, prices fell year after year, and power companies began using them to store electricity for the grid.

India's story

India has had pumped-hydro plants for decades. By May 2026 it had 10 working plants, together able to give out about 7.4 gigawatts. The biggest is Pinnapuram in Andhra Pradesh, at 1,680 megawatts. Another 11 projects, adding about 15.9 gigawatts, were being built in six states. Surveys have found sites in India's hills and valleys that could hold far more.

Batteries came later. At the end of 2025, India's grid batteries could store only about 758 megawatt-hours, tiny next to the need. But many large battery projects have since been ordered, often placed right next to solar parks in states like Rajasthan and Gujarat.

India's planners think the country will need about 411 GWh of storage by 2031-32. In 2026 the government approved ₹14,500 crore to help build 92 GWh by 2030-31, most of it pumped hydro and batteries.

The numbers that matter

10 lakh units. One gigawatt-hour (GWh) is 10 lakh units, the same units shown on your home's electricity bill. That is enough to run about one lakh homes for a day.

93%. That is how much the price of lithium-ion battery packs has fallen since 2010, according to BloombergNEF. Packs for grid storage cost about $70 for each kilowatt-hour in 2025, 45% less than a year before.

7.4 gigawatts. India's working pumped-hydro plants, all together, can supply about this much power at once, roughly what several big coal power stations give out.

411 GWh. India's estimated storage need by 2031-32. That is more than 500 times what India's grid batteries could hold at the end of 2025.

What people argue about

Engineers agree India needs much more storage. They argue about the best mix.

Fans of pumped hydro say a plant can keep working for many decades, can give power for many hours at a stretch, and uses no rare minerals. Critics say it needs the right hills and lots of water, can flood forests and farmland, and takes many years to build.

Fans of batteries say they are quick to build, can sit almost anywhere, and keep getting cheaper. Critics say most battery parts and minerals like lithium are made abroad, so India depends on imports. Batteries also wear out after some years and must be recycled carefully. Many experts expect India to use both, with batteries for short bursts and pumped hydro for longer gaps.

Myths and mix-ups

"Storage makes electricity." It does not. It only moves electricity from one time to another, and loses a little on the way. The power must still be made by solar, wind, hydro or other plants first.

"A big battery is just a big phone battery." The chemistry can be similar, but grid batteries come with cooling systems, fire protection and computers that decide when to charge and when to release power.

"Pumped hydro needs a river." Many new plants use two closed lakes on a hillside and fill them once. The same water then goes up and down again and again.

How does a battery store energy?A battery doesn't hold electricity like a jug holds water. It holds chemicals that are waiting to react.

What it means for you

When storage works well, the fan keeps turning on a hot evening even after the Sun has set. Fewer power cuts means fewer spoilt groceries, steadier pumps and quieter nights without the inverter beeping.

Storage also helps India burn less coal for its electricity, which means cleaner air over time. And at home, you already use storage every day: your phone, a torch, a power bank or an inverter battery all keep electricity for later. Grid storage is the same idea at the size of a whole country. Scientists are also testing new batteries made from cheaper materials like sodium, so storing sunshine keeps getting easier.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The electricity grid can simply save extra power inside its wires for later.

Truth: Wires only carry electricity; they cannot hold it. The grid must make exactly as much power as people use, every second. That is why we need batteries and other kinds of storage.

Try this

Fill a bottle with water and hold it high over a bucket. Tip it so the water pours onto a small paper pinwheel. The higher you hold it, the faster the pinwheel spins. That is stored energy turning back into movement.
