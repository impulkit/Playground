How do you save sunshine for night-time?

Solar panels make loads of electricity at lunchtime, when many people are out. But in the evening, when everyone switches on lights and fans, the Sun has set. So how do we keep the afternoon's sunshine for later?

The short answer

You cannot put sunshine in a box, but you can turn the electricity it makes into a form that waits. Big batteries store it as chemical energy. Pumped-hydro plants store it by lifting water uphill.

Later, when the Sun sets or the wind drops, the stored energy is turned back into electricity.

Surprising but true

One gigawatt-hour of stored electricity could keep a 10-watt LED bulb glowing for more than 11,000 years.

The problem with sunshine and wind

Countries like India are building lots of solar panels and wind turbines. They make clean, renewable electricity. But the Sun shines only by day, and wind comes and goes.

People's needs do not follow the weather. Demand often peaks in the evening. Without storage, extra afternoon power can go to waste, and coal plants must run at night. Storage helps renewable energy work round the clock.

Two ways to store electricity

Batteries. Giant versions of the battery in your phone, packed into rows of shipping containers. They respond in a split second, which helps keep the grid steady.

Pumped hydro. Two lakes, one high and one low. When there is spare electricity, pumps push water up. That water now holds potential energy. When power is needed, it flows down through a turbine, just like at a dam. Pumped hydro has been used for over a hundred years and can store huge amounts.

How do dams make electricity?Behind a big dam, a whole river waits. Let it fall through a pipe, and it can light up millions of homes.

Measuring stored energy

Stored electricity is measured in watt-hours. One gigawatt-hour (GWh) is 10 lakh units, the units on your home's electricity bill. It could run about one lakh homes for a day.

In 2026, India's government approved ₹14,500 crore to help build 92 GWh of storage by 2030-31. Scientists are also working on new kinds of batteries, made from cheaper materials like sodium, so storing sunshine keeps getting easier.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The electricity grid can simply save extra power inside its wires for later.

Truth: Wires only carry electricity; they cannot hold it. The grid must make exactly as much power as people use, every second. That is why we need batteries and other kinds of storage.

Try this

Fill a bottle with water and hold it high over a bucket. Tip it so the water pours onto a small paper pinwheel. The higher you hold it, the faster the pinwheel spins. That is stored energy turning back into movement.
