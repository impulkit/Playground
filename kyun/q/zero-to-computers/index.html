How did zero help build computers?

Every photo, song and game on a computer is stored using just two digits, and one of them is a number that took humans thousands of years to accept.

The short answer

Computers store everything as long strings of 0s and 1s, a system called binary. Each tiny switch in a computer chip is either off, meaning 0, or on, meaning 1. To make this work, you need two things that took humans a very long time to invent: a symbol for nothing, and the idea that a digit's value depends on its place.

Both of these came together in India about 1,500 years ago. Indian mathematicians used a place-value system with zero as a real number. That system travelled to the Arab world and then to Europe. Centuries later, it made binary, and so modern computers, possible.

Surprising but true

Without zero, binary could not work. There would be no way to tell the binary numbers 1, 10 and 100 apart, and they mean one, two and four.

Holding an empty place

Look at the numbers 31, 301 and 3,001. The digits 3 and 1 are the same in each, but the values are completely different. Zero tells you which places are empty. Without it, you would struggle to tell them apart.

Many ancient peoples counted without a proper zero. The Romans wrote 301 as CCCI, with no need to mark empty places, but their numerals made sums slow and clumsy. The Babylonians used a gap or a mark as a placeholder, and the Maya had a zero for their calendars.

India makes zero a number

Indian mathematicians went further. They treated zero as a number in its own right. In 628 CE, Brahmagupta wrote rules for calculating with it: a number plus zero stays the same, and a number times zero is zero. An inscription from 876 CE at the Chaturbhuj temple in Gwalior shows a small round zero in the number 270, carved in stone.

The Bakhshali manuscript, found in 1881 near Peshawar and now in Oxford, uses a dot for zero. Carbon dating in 2017 suggested some of its pages are much older than others, and scholars still debate exactly how old the text is.

From India, the system passed to scholars in Baghdad, such as al-Khwarizmi, in the 800s. In 1202, the Italian mathematician Fibonacci explained these "Hindu-Arabic numerals" in a book that helped spread them across Europe.

Counting with only two digits

Our everyday system uses ten digits, 0 to 9, perhaps because we have ten fingers. But place value works with any number of digits. In 1703, the German thinker Gottfried Leibniz published an explanation of binary, which uses only two.

In binary, each place is worth double the one to its right: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16. So 101 means one 4, no 2s and one 1, making 5. The zero is essential: it is what makes 101 different from 11. Leibniz admired how simple it was, but in his time it had little practical use.

Long before Leibniz, the Sanskrit scholar Pingala, writing about poetry, described patterns of short and long syllables. Some historians point out that his method of listing them works much like binary counting.

Switches that count

Binary became useful when people built machines with switches. A switch has two states, off and on, a perfect match for 0 and 1. In the 1930s and 1940s, engineers began building electronic computers that used binary, together with George Boole's rules of logic.

Today a single phone chip contains billions of tiny switches called transistors, each flipping between 0 and 1 billions of times a second. A photo is stored as millions of numbers for the colours of its dots. A song is a long list of numbers describing a sound wave. All of it is written in 0s and 1s, a modern use of the symbol Brahmagupta treated as a number nearly 1,400 years ago.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Computers think in numbers like we do, in tens.

Truth: Inside, computers use only 0 and 1, because a switch can be off or on. They turn our decimal numbers into binary to work on them, then back into decimal to show us the answer.

Try this

Make a binary counter with five paper cards. Write 16, 8, 4, 2 and 1 on them and lay them in a row. Turn a card face up for 1 and face down for 0. Can you show your age? Can you count from 0 to 31 by flipping cards?
