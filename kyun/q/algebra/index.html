What is algebra?

The word "algebra" once also meant bone-setting, and Indian mathematicians used colours, such as black and blue, as names for numbers they didn't know.

The short answer

Algebra is the part of maths where you use a letter or symbol to stand for a number you don't know yet. Instead of saying "some number plus 7 makes 12", you write x + 7 = 12. Then you use rules to find x, which here is 5.

That might sound like a small trick, but it changed maths. With letters, you can describe patterns that work for every number at once, and solve problems that would be very hard in words alone. Algebra grew over thousands of years, with important steps in Babylon, Greece, India, Baghdad and Europe.

Surprising but true

In Spain, a person who set broken bones was once called an algebrista, because "al-jabr", the Arabic root of algebra, means putting broken parts back together.

A book from Baghdad

Around 820 CE, a scholar named Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi worked in Baghdad, at a famous centre of learning called the House of Wisdom, one of the great libraries of its time. He wrote a book on solving problems, with a title that included the words al-jabr and al-muqabala.

Al-jabr means restoring or reuniting broken parts. In maths, it meant moving a subtracted amount to the other side of an equation to make it whole again. Europeans who translated the book turned al-jabr into "algebra". His own name, al-Khwarizmi, gave us another word: algorithm.

His book had no x or y. Everything was written in words, even the numbers. He called the unknown shay, meaning "thing", and showed step by step how to balance and simplify problems so the thing could be found. Problems were about real things, such as sharing out land or money among family members.

Colours as numbers

Indian mathematicians were working on similar ideas, and in some ways went further. In 628 CE, Brahmagupta wrote rules for calculating with zero and with negative numbers, which he called debts. He also solved equations with more than one unknown.

To name unknowns, Indian mathematicians used the first syllables of words. The first unknown was often yavat-tavat, meaning "as much as". For more unknowns, writers such as Brahmagupta and, later, Bhaskara II used colour names: kalaka (black), nilaka (blue), pitaka (yellow) and so on. A problem might have a black number and a blue number to find, which is not so different from x and y.

What happened to the Library of Alexandria?The ancient world's most famous library did not vanish in a single dramatic fire. It faded away over hundreds of years.

How x took over

For centuries, most algebra was written in long sentences. Symbols arrived slowly. In the late 1500s, the French mathematician François Viète used letters for both unknown and known numbers. Then, in 1637, René Descartes used letters from the end of the alphabet, especially x, y and z, for unknowns, and letters from the start for known amounts.

His habit stuck. Nobody is quite sure why x became the favourite. One popular story says printers had plenty of spare x's, but there is no good evidence for it.

Algebra all around you

Algebra is everywhere once you look. A shopkeeper working out how many packets fit in a box is doing it. A game designer uses it to make a character jump in a smooth curve. A programmer writes it into almost every line of code, using names like "score" or "speed" for numbers that change.

Scientists use algebra to write the laws of nature in short form. Einstein's E = mc² is algebra: three letters that describe how much energy is locked inside matter. It took centuries of mathematicians writing in long sentences to make a line that short possible.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Algebra is just letters and has nothing to do with real life.

Truth: Every time you work out how many more rupees or dollars you need to buy something, you are solving an algebra problem in your head. Algebra is used in cooking, building, shopping, video games and every computer program.

Try this

Play "think of a number" with your family. Ask them to think of a number, add 3, double it, take away 6, then halve it. They will always end up back at their starting number. Try writing the steps with x to see why.
