Are naps good for you?

In a 1990s NASA study, airline pilots who took a short nap in the cockpit during long flights, while a co-pilot flew, were far more alert afterwards than pilots who didn't.

The short answer

For most people, a short nap is good for you. A rest of about 10 to 20 minutes in the early afternoon can make you more alert, improve your mood and help your brain hold on to things you learned that morning.

Longer naps are trickier. Sleep for an hour or more and you may wake up groggy, and a late or long nap can make it harder to fall asleep at night. For children, the most important sleep is still a full night's rest. Naps are a bonus, not a replacement.

Surprising but true

The afternoon dip

Many people feel sleepy in the early afternoon, often after lunch. Food plays a small part, but the main reason is your body clock. Your alertness naturally rises and falls through the day, and for most people there is a small dip between about 1 and 3 p.m.

In many warm countries, people have long built a rest into the day. Spain is famous for the siesta. In parts of India, shops in small towns may close for a couple of hours after lunch while people rest through the hottest part of the day. In Japan, a short doze at a desk, on a train or even in a meeting is called inemuri. It has often been seen as a sign that someone is working very hard, not that they are lazy.

Pilots asleep in the cockpit

In the early 1990s, NASA scientists studied pilots on long night flights across the Pacific. One group was allowed a 40-minute rest in their seats, taking turns while the other pilot flew. The pilots who rested slept for about 26 minutes on average.

Later in the flight, those pilots reacted faster and had far fewer moments of drowsiness than pilots who stayed awake the whole time. The study, published in 1994, helped some airlines introduce planned rest for pilots, always with another pilot awake at the controls.

Why short is best

Sleep comes in stages. In the first few minutes, you drift into light sleep. After about 20 to 30 minutes, you may slip into deep sleep, when the brain slows right down. If you wake up from deep sleep, you can feel heavy and confused for a while. Scientists call this sleep inertia.

A nap of 10 to 20 minutes usually ends before deep sleep begins, so you wake up feeling fresher. A much longer nap of about 90 minutes can take you through a full sleep cycle, including dreaming, but it is more likely to disturb your sleep that night.

Naps and memory

Researchers have found that a nap after learning something new, such as a list of words or a new skill, can help people remember it better later. During sleep, the brain seems to sort and store the day's experiences, moving them into longer-term memory.

This doesn't mean you can skip studying and just sleep. But a short rest between learning sessions may help.

Children and naps

Babies sleep for much of the day. Toddlers usually nap once or twice a day, and most children stop napping regularly somewhere between about 3 and 5 years old. Children aged 6 to 12 need about 9 to 12 hours of sleep each night, and teenagers about 8 to 10.

If a school-age child feels very sleepy every afternoon, the cause is often too little sleep at night, perhaps from late screen time or early school starts. Going to bed at a regular time usually helps more than daytime napping. If tiredness goes on for a long time, it is worth talking to a parent or a doctor.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Napping is just for babies and lazy people.

Truth: Short naps can sharpen attention and memory at any age. Pilots, doctors on long shifts, athletes and astronauts use planned naps to stay alert and safe.

Try this

On a weekend afternoon, with a parent's permission, try a 15-minute rest with your eyes closed, even if you don't fall asleep. Set a timer. Before and after, test yourself by catching a ruler dropped between your fingers and noting where you grab it. Did your catch get quicker?
