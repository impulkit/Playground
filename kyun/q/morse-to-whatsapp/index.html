How did messages go from Morse code to WhatsApp?

India's last telegram was sent in July 2013, 162 years after the first telegraph line opened near Kolkata, and people queued to send one just for the memory.

The short answer

For thousands of years, a message could only travel as fast as a runner, a horse, a ship or a pigeon. Then, in the 1840s, the electric telegraph sent words along wires in seconds, using dots and dashes called Morse code.

After that, each new invention made messages faster, cheaper and more personal. The telephone carried voices. Radio went without wires. Computers brought email. Mobile phones brought text messages. Finally, apps like WhatsApp let anyone send words, photos, videos and voice notes to the other side of the world for almost nothing. The journey took about 170 years.

Surprising but true

The world's first text message, sent in 1992, was typed on a computer and said simply "Merry Christmas".

Dots and dashes

In 1844 the American inventor Samuel Morse sent a message on a wire from Washington to Baltimore, about 60 km away. It read "What hath God wrought". An operator tapped a key, sending short and long pulses of electricity. At the other end, a clicking machine marked them on paper.

Each letter had its own pattern of short and long signals. E, the most common letter in English, got a single dot. Q, a rare one, got dash-dash-dot-dash. Morse code let one wire carry any words at all, in any language written in the right letters.

Later, Morse went wireless, sent by radio from ships. Its distress call, SOS, is three dots, three dashes and three dots. It was chosen because it is easy to send and recognise, not because the letters stand for words. When the Titanic sank in 1912, its radio operators sent SOS along with an older call, CQD.

Telegrams in India

India's first telegraph line opened in 1851, from Calcutta (now Kolkata) to Diamond Harbour. Within a few years, wires linked Calcutta, Bombay, Madras and Agra. During the revolt of 1857, telegraph clerks in Delhi sent warnings up the line to British stations in the Punjab. A British official later claimed the telegraph had saved British rule.

For more than a century, a telegram was how urgent news arrived: a new job, a birth, a death. Families often dreaded the knock of the telegram messenger. People paid by the word, so messages were short: "ARRIVING FRIDAY STOP". Telegrams travelled partly through the post office network. India's service finally closed on 15 July 2013.

Voices and screens

The telephone, patented in 1876, let people talk instead of tap. By the 1900s, cities had switchboards where operators connected calls by hand. In 1971 an engineer named Ray Tomlinson sent the first email between computers, choosing the @ sign to separate the person from the machine.

Mobile phones came next. In 1992, the first SMS was sent in Britain. SMS messages were limited to 160 characters, which is why people invented short forms like "gr8" and "c u". In India, by the 2000s, texting had become a cheap way for families to keep in touch. Twitter, now called X, launched in 2006 with a 140-character limit on posts, an idea borrowed from text messages.

The app age

WhatsApp was launched in 2009 by two former Yahoo engineers. It used the internet instead of the phone company's text system, so messages cost almost nothing, even abroad. By 2025 it had more than 3 billion monthly users, and India had more than any other country.

Since 2016, WhatsApp messages have been protected by end-to-end encryption, so only the sender and receiver can read them. Messaging is now so fast that false rumours can spread too, and some countries limit how often a message can be forwarded. A telegram once took hours and cost money for every word. Now a family can share a birthday video between Bengaluru and Boston before the candles finish burning.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Nobody uses Morse code any more.

Truth: Ships and armies have mostly switched to other systems, but amateur radio operators around the world still send Morse every day. It can get through when voice signals are too weak.

Try this

Tap out messages with a friend using Morse code. Find the Morse alphabet, then use short and long taps on a table for dots and dashes. Start with SOS (three short, three long, three short) and then try your names. Time how long one short sentence takes, and compare it with a WhatsApp message.
