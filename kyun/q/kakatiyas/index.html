Who were the Kakatiyas of Warangal?

Marco Polo wrote about a kingdom in south India ruled by a wise queen. She was very likely Rudrama Devi of the Kakatiyas.

The short answer

The Kakatiyas were a dynasty that ruled much of today's Telangana and Andhra Pradesh from about 1163 to 1323. Their capital was Orugallu, meaning "one rock", now Warangal, about 150 kilometres from Hyderabad.

They are remembered for a strong queen, Rudrama Devi, and for richly carved temples such as the Ramappa temple. They also built hundreds of water tanks that let farmers grow crops through the dry months. The kingdom ended in 1323, when armies of the Delhi Sultanate captured Warangal.

Surprising but true

The Ramappa temple is named after its chief sculptor, not after a god or a king.

A queen on the throne

King Ganapatideva, who ruled for about sixty years, had no son. He chose his daughter Rudrama Devi to succeed him, and she took the throne around 1262.

Some nobles refused to accept a woman as ruler and rebelled, and neighbouring kingdoms attacked. She defeated them and ruled for nearly thirty years. Inscriptions often call her by a male title, Rudradeva Maharaja. The traveller Marco Polo, who visited the east coast of India around the 1290s, described a kingdom ruled by a queen who governed with justice and was loved by her people. Most historians think he meant Rudrama Devi, or the kingdom she had just left to her grandson.

The temple of the sculptor

The Rudreshwara temple at Palampet was built in 1213 by Recharla Rudra, a general of Ganapatideva. Everyone calls it the Ramappa temple, after its chief sculptor. It is rare in India for a temple to carry an artist's name.

The temple sits on a star-shaped platform. Its pillars are carved from hard black stone and polished so smoothly that they shine. Bracket figures of dancers and musicians lean out from the tops of the pillars. Its tower is made of light bricks, and tests suggest they are porous enough to float in water. UNESCO named the temple a World Heritage Site in 2021.

Lakes made by hand

The Deccan plateau gets most of its rain in a few monsoon months, then dries out. The Kakatiyas built a huge network of tanks, artificial lakes held back by earth walls, to catch that rain.

Large ones like Ramappa lake and Pakhal lake were built by kings and generals. Thousands of smaller tanks were built by villages and temples. Water flowed from one tank to the next down gentle slopes, a little like steps. Many still exist, and in 2015 the Telangana government launched a programme named Mission Kakatiya to clean and repair them.

Gates of stone

At Warangal, the Kakatiyas built a fort with rings of walls, one inside another: an outer earth wall, then a stone wall with a moat. At its centre stand four carved stone gateways called Kakatiya Kala Thoranam. Each is made of tall pillars topped by a beam carved with swans and flowers.

The gateways are now the emblem of the state of Telangana. A popular story says the Koh-i-Noor diamond was found in mines in Kakatiya lands, near the Krishna river. The mines were real and famous for diamonds, but no record proves the Koh-i-Noor came from them.

The fall of Warangal

In the early 1300s, armies from the Delhi Sultanate attacked Warangal several times. King Prataparudra paid them tribute, but in 1323 an army led by Ulugh Khan, the future Sultan Muhammad bin Tughlaq, captured the fort. The king was taken prisoner and died soon after.

Some Kakatiya commanders later helped found new kingdoms in the south, and their temples and tanks remained. Each monsoon, rain still fills lakes dug by Kakatiya farmers eight hundred years ago.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Women never ruled kingdoms in medieval India.

Truth: Rudrama Devi ruled the Kakatiya kingdom for nearly thirty years in the 13th century. Other women, such as Razia Sultan of Delhi, also held the throne.
