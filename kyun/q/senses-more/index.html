Do we really have only five senses?

Close your eyes and touch your nose. You just used a sense that isn't on the famous list of five.

The short answer

No. The idea of five senses (sight, hearing, smell, taste and touch) comes from the ancient Greek thinker Aristotle. It is easy to remember, but it leaves a lot out.

Scientists define a sense as a way of detecting something using special sensors called receptors. By that measure, you also have a sense of balance, a sense of where your arms and legs are, a sense of hot and cold, and a sense of pain. You can feel hunger, thirst and a full bladder. Some lists reach more than 20.

The exact number depends on how you count. What is clear is that five is too few.

Surprising but true

In 1971, a British man named Ian Waterman lost his sense of where his body was after an illness. He learned to walk again by watching his own legs every step of the way.

Knowing where you are

Close your eyes and touch your nose with your finger. You can do it because of proprioception, the sense of where your body parts are and how they're moving. Sensors in your muscles, tendons and joints keep reporting to your brain, all day, without you noticing.

Ian Waterman, from England, lost this sense at the age of 19 after a rare illness damaged his nerves. He could still feel pain and temperature, but he could no longer tell where his limbs were without looking. Doctors expected him never to walk again. By watching his body and thinking about every movement, he taught himself to walk, write and drive. The scientist Jonathan Cole wrote a book about him.

Staying upright

Your sense of balance comes from the inner ear, right next to the part that handles hearing. It contains three tiny curved tubes filled with fluid, set at different angles, plus small sacs with crystals that sense gravity and speed.

This system works with your eyes and your body-position sense to keep you steady. When they disagree, for instance reading in a moving car, you can feel sick.

Hot, cold and hurt

Your skin holds separate receptors for different jobs. Some respond to light touch, some to pressure, some to vibration. Others respond to warmth or to cold. Pain receptors respond to damage or the threat of it.

Chilli peppers trick your heat receptors. The chemical capsaicin switches on the same sensors that react to heat, which is why a hot curry feels hot. Mint does the opposite, triggering cold sensors.

Inside your body, more sensors track your blood pressure, oxygen levels and stomach stretch. This inner sensing helps explain feelings like hunger or a racing heart.

Senses that animals have and we don't

Many animals sense things we can't. Sharks and rays have tiny jelly-filled pores on their snouts that detect the faint electric signals from other animals' muscles, letting sharks find fish buried in sand. The platypus does something similar.

Some snakes, including pit vipers, sense heat from warm-blooded prey in the dark. Many birds, turtles and other animals can sense Earth's magnetic field and use it to find their way on long migrations. Bats and dolphins use echoes to "see" with sound.

A brain that blends it all

Your brain doesn't treat senses separately. It mixes them. Much of what you call taste is really smell, which is why food tastes dull when you have a cold. What your eyes see can change what you think you hear.

Some people experience synaesthesia, where one sense triggers another. They might see colours when they hear music, or feel that each number has its own colour. For them, it is a normal part of how the world feels.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Humans have exactly five senses.

Truth: The list of five goes back to Aristotle, over 2,300 years ago. Scientists now count many more, including balance, temperature, pain and the sense of where your body parts are.

Try this

Bend a paper clip into a U shape with the two ends about 1 centimetre apart. With a friend's eyes closed, gently touch either one end or both ends to their fingertip, then to their arm, and ask "one or two?" Fingertips can usually tell, but on the arm two points often feel like one.
