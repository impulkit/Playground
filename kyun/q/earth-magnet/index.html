Why does a compass point north?

A tiny needle that knows the way, without batteries.

The short answer

Swirling liquid iron in Earth's core creates a magnetic field all around the planet.

A compass needle is a small magnet. It turns to line up with that field.

The magnetic field also shields us from a stream of particles blasted out by the Sun.

Surprising but true

Birds, turtles and even some bees can sense Earth's magnetism and use it to find their way.

A planet-sized dynamo

About 2,900 km below your feet, Earth's outer core is a churning ocean of liquid iron and nickel, hotter than 4,000 °C. Heat escaping from deeper inside makes the metal rise and sink, a bit like soup bubbling in a pot. Earth's spin twists those currents into spirals.

Moving metal that carries electric currents makes magnetism. So the outer core works like a giant dynamo, the same idea as the generator on some bicycle lights. The result is a magnetic field shaped a lot like the one around a bar magnet, with one end near each pole.

North isn't quite north

A compass doesn't point exactly at the North Pole, the spot Earth spins around. It points to the magnetic north pole, which is somewhere else, and it moves. For most of the last century it sat in the islands of northern Canada. Since the 1990s it has been racing towards Siberia, at times by about 50 km a year.

That shift matters in surprising places. Airport runways are numbered by their compass direction. As magnetic north drifts, some airports have had to repaint their runway numbers. In 2011, the airport in Tampa, Florida, closed runways so workers could paint new numbers on them. Navigation systems in phones and planes use a model of the field that scientists update every five years.

The flip-flopping field

Every so often, over hundreds of thousands of years, Earth's magnetic field flips. North becomes south, and a compass would point the other way. The last full flip happened about 780,000 years ago.

We know because rocks remember. When lava cools, tiny magnetic minerals inside it line up with the field like frozen compass needles. Lava on the ocean floor shows stripes of normal and flipped magnetism on either side of undersea volcanic ridges. In the 1960s, those stripes helped prove that the sea floor spreads and continents move, as India did on its long journey north.

A shield against the Sun

The Sun constantly blows out a stream of charged particles called the solar wind. Earth's magnetic field steers most of them around the planet. Near the poles, some slip down into the air and make it glow green and red. That glow is the aurora. The same particles can damage satellites and disturb radio signals, so space agencies keep a close watch on the Sun.

Mars has almost no magnetic field today. Many scientists think that over billions of years the solar wind stripped away much of its air, which is one reason Mars is so cold and dry.

Living compasses

Many animals sense Earth's magnetism. Baby loggerhead sea turtles hatch on a beach, swim thousands of kilometres around an ocean, and years later return to nest on the coast where they were born. Scientists think they remember the magnetic "signature" of their home beach. Migrating birds, salmon and some insects seem to use the field too.

People caught on much later. Chinese inventors made the first magnetic compasses for finding the way around 1,000 years ago, and the idea spread to sailors across the Indian Ocean and Europe.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A compass points exactly to the North Pole.

Truth: It points to the magnetic north pole, which is hundreds of kilometres from the geographic North Pole and wanders every year. Map makers publish the difference for each place so travellers can correct for it.

Try this

Make a floating compass. Stroke a steel paperclip, straightened out, about 30 times in one direction with a fridge magnet. Lay it on a small leaf or bottle cap floating in a bowl of water. Watch it slowly turn and settle. Check its direction with a phone compass or the setting Sun.
