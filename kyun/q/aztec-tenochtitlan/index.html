What was Tenochtitlan like?

Five hundred years ago, one of the biggest cities on Earth stood on an island in a lake, and Mexico City is built right on top of it.

The short answer

Tenochtitlan was the capital of the Aztecs, or Mexica, as they called themselves. It stood on an island in Lake Texcoco, high in the Valley of Mexico. In 1519, when Spanish soldiers first saw it, it was probably bigger than any city in Spain.

Three long stone causeways linked it to the shore. Canals ran between the streets, crowded with canoes. At the centre rose a great twin temple painted in bright colours. Gardens floated in the shallows, and fresh water arrived by aqueduct from a hill called Chapultepec. Today the lake has been drained, and Mexico City sits on top of the ruins.

Surprising but true

In 1978, electricity workers digging under a Mexico City street hit a giant carved stone, and it led archaeologists to the ruins of the Aztecs' great temple.

An eagle on a cactus

The Mexica told a story about their beginnings. Their god Huitzilopochtli told them to wander until they saw an eagle perched on a cactus. That would be the place for their city. According to tradition, they found it in 1325, on a swampy island nobody else wanted.

It was a poor spot for a city, but a good spot for defence. Over the next two centuries the Mexica turned it into the heart of an empire. The eagle on the cactus is on Mexico's flag today.

Gardens in the water

There was not enough dry land to feed the city, so the Mexica made more. They built chinampas: long, narrow plots raised from layers of lake mud and rotting plants, held together with posts and willow roots. Maize, beans, squash, chillies and flowers grew on them.

Canals between the plots worked like streets. Farmers paddled canoes to market, a little like the boatmen of Venice. At Xochimilco, in the south of Mexico City, some chinampas are still farmed, and families ride colourful flat boats along the old canals on weekends.

The great market

North of the main island lay Tlatelolco, a twin city with an enormous market. Hernán Cortés, the Spanish leader, wrote that tens of thousands of people came there every day. Traders sold turkeys, rabbits, cloth, feathers, pottery, gold and medicine. Cacao beans worked as small change, the same beans used to make a frothy, bitter chocolate drink for nobles.

Officials walked through the market checking that measures were fair. Cortés's soldiers, many from Spanish towns of a few thousand people, had never seen anything like it.

The temple at the centre

The Templo Mayor, or Great Temple, was a stepped pyramid probably more than 40 metres tall, with two shrines on top. One honoured Huitzilopochtli, god of sun and war. The other honoured Tlaloc, god of rain. Ceremonies there included human sacrifice, which the Mexica believed kept the sun moving and the world alive. Historians still debate how many people were killed.

After the Spanish took the city in 1521, they pulled the temple down and built a cathedral nearby. For centuries its exact place was forgotten. Then, in 1978, workers laying electrical cables found a huge round stone carved with the goddess Coyolxauhqui. Archaeologists dug, and the temple's base came to light. You can now walk through the ruins in the middle of the city, a short stroll from the cathedral.

A city that sank, slowly

The siege of 1521 destroyed much of Tenochtitlan, and disease had already killed many of its people. The story of how a few hundred Spaniards and many thousands of Indigenous allies brought down the Aztecs is told in the Spanish conquest.

The Spanish drained the lake over the following centuries. Mexico City now sits on soft, old lake mud, and parts of it sink by up to 30 centimetres or more a year as water is pumped from below. Buildings tilt. The ground remembers it was once a lake.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Aztecs were a small, simple tribe when the Spanish arrived.

Truth: The Aztec Empire ruled millions of people across central Mexico. Tenochtitlan had paved causeways, an aqueduct, schools, huge markets and a written system of picture signs.
