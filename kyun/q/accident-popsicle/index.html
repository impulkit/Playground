Did a boy invent the ice lolly?

In 1905, an 11-year-old in California left a cup of fizzy drink outside with a stick in it. By morning, he had invented the ice lolly, or so the story goes.

The short answer

The most famous story says yes. In 1905, an 11-year-old named Frank Epperson, living in the San Francisco Bay Area, mixed soda powder and water in a cup with a wooden stirring stick. He left it outside on the porch overnight. It was an unusually cold night, and by morning the drink had frozen solid around the stick.

He pulled it out by the stick and licked it. Many years later, in 1924, he patented the idea. Today we call it an ice lolly in Britain and India, a popsicle in America, and sometimes an ice candy or chuski at the school gate.

Surprising but true

During the Great Depression, Popsicle sold a "twin" lolly with two sticks, so two children could share one for the price of one.

The cold night

Frank told this story himself, when he was an adult. Nobody wrote it down at the time, so we can't check it. San Francisco rarely gets cold enough to freeze water outdoors, so some people doubt it. Still, frosty nights do happen there now and then, so the tale is possible.

Whether or not every detail is true, the idea stayed with him. For years he made the frozen treats for friends and, later, his own children.

From Epsicle to Popsicle

In 1922, Epperson sold his frozen lollies at Neptune Beach, an amusement park across the bay from San Francisco. They were a hit. In 1924 he got a patent for "frozen confectionery" on a stick.

He first called them "Epsicles", joining his name and "icicle". His children called them "Pop's 'sicles", because their dad made them. That name stuck. In 1925, he sold the rights to a company in New York, and Popsicle became a famous brand.

The twin lolly

During the Great Depression of the 1930s, many American families had very little money. Popsicle came up with an idea: a lolly with two sticks that could be snapped in half. Two children could share one treat that cost just a few cents.

That twin design lasted for decades. And the flat wooden sticks became famous on their own, as millions of children used them to build model houses, bridges and puppets.

Why sugar fights the freezer

Pure water freezes at 0°C. But when you dissolve sugar in it, the sugar gets in the way of water molecules lining up into ice crystals. The sweeter the drink, the colder it must get before it freezes.

That's why a very sugary lolly stays slightly soft, while a watery one turns rock hard. Home freezers run at about minus 18°C, cold enough for both. The science is close to what happens when ice cream freezes, where salt and stirring play a part too.

India's frozen treats are older

Long before 1905, India had kulfi. It is a dense frozen milk sweet, flavoured with cardamom, saffron and pistachio. It was being made in north India by the time of the Mughal emperors in the 1500s and 1600s, frozen in metal cones packed into pots of ice and salt.

Kulfi sellers still push their carts through Indian streets, slicing kulfi onto leaves or serving it on a stick. There's also gola, crushed ice pressed onto a stick and soaked with bright syrup. These are cousins of the popsicle, not copies of it.

People in ancient Persia and China also made sweet chilled foods using ice stored from winter. So Epperson didn't invent frozen treats. He made one simple version cheap, easy to hold and easy to sell.

Kid inventors

Frank Epperson is often listed among famous child inventors. Louis Braille was 15 when he worked out his raised-dot alphabet for blind readers. Children notice odd things, like a drink frozen around a stick, and ask what could come of it.

One warning comes with every lolly, though. Bite it too fast and a sharp pain stabs your forehead, a strange alarm known as brain freeze.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Before 1905, nobody had ever eaten a frozen treat on a stick or from a mould.

Truth: People were enjoying frozen sweets long before. In India, kulfi was set in metal cones and sold on sticks for generations. Epperson's version made frozen fruit drinks on sticks popular in America and turned them into a business.

Try this

With an adult, pour some fruit juice into a small cup or ice-cube tray, push in a clean wooden spoon or stick, and freeze it overnight. Try one cup of plain juice and one with extra water added. Which freezes harder, and which tastes sweeter?
