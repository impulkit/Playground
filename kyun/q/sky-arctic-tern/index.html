Which bird flies the farthest every year?

A bird lighter than a cricket ball flies from the Arctic to Antarctica and back every year, a trip of more than 70,000 kilometres.

The short answer

The bird with the longest yearly migration known is the Arctic tern. It nests in the far north, in places like Greenland, Iceland, northern Canada and the British Isles. Then, when the northern summer ends, it flies all the way to the seas around Antarctica, and back again the next spring.

Scientists who fixed tiny trackers to terns in 2010 found that the average round trip was about 70,900 kilometres. One tern from the Farne Islands in England, tracked in 2016, flew about 96,000 kilometres in a single year. That's more than twice around the Earth, for a bird that weighs about 100 grams, less than a cricket ball.

Surprising but true

Over a lifetime of 30 years, an Arctic tern may fly about 2.4 million kilometres, the same as going to the Moon and back three times.

Chasing endless summer

Why go so far? Because the tern lives in two summers. When it's summer in the Arctic, the Sun barely sets, and the cold seas swarm with small fish. When winter comes in the north, the tern flies south to Antarctica, where it's now summer, with long days and plenty of food.

This happens because Earth is tilted, which is why the seasons in the two halves of the planet are opposite. The tern's trick means it probably sees more daylight in a year than any other animal.

Tracking a tiny traveller

For a long time, nobody could follow terns across the ocean. Then scientists made trackers weighing just over a gram, about the weight of a paper clip, and strapped them to the birds' legs. The trackers recorded light levels, which revealed when the Sun rose and set, and so roughly where each bird was.

The results were a surprise. The terns didn't fly straight. They zigzagged down the Atlantic in a giant S shape, riding winds that blow in great loops over the ocean. Many stopped for about a month in the middle of the North Atlantic to fatten up on fish before going on.

Why do we have seasons?Earth is closest to the Sun in early January, during winter in India and the USA. So distance can't be the reason for summer.

Life on the wing

Arctic terns are built for flying. They have long, narrow wings and forked tails, like swallows of the sea. They rarely land on water, because their feathers aren't very waterproof. Instead, they hover over the waves, then dive head first to snatch small fish.

They can live a long time. The oldest known Arctic tern was about 34 years old. Add up all those journeys and a tern may fly about 2.4 million kilometres in its lifetime, the distance of three trips to the Moon and back.

Don't get too close

On the Farne Islands, visitors walking past tern nests often wear hats. Terns defend their eggs fiercely and dive-bomb the heads of anyone who comes too near, sometimes pecking hard enough to draw blood.

Arctic terns usually lay two eggs in a simple scrape on the ground, with hardly any nest at all. The eggs and chicks are speckled to blend with pebbles and grass. Parents take turns flying out to sea to bring back fish, and the chicks can fly within about a month of hatching. Young terns often stay in the far south for their first couple of years before flying north to breed.

Other record-breakers

Other birds hold different records. In 2022 a young bar-tailed godwit flew about 13,560 kilometres from Alaska to Tasmania without stopping, in 11 days, the longest non-stop bird flight ever recorded.

India has its own migration marvels. Each autumn, huge flocks of Amur falcons stop in Nagaland on their way from East Asia to southern Africa, crossing the Arabian Sea. Villages that once hunted them now protect them. And the strangest part? Some birds go up instead of across: bar-headed geese fly over the Himalayas, where the air is too thin for most people to breathe.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Birds migrate in a straight line by the shortest route.

Truth: Many migrating birds take long, curving routes that follow good winds and food stops. For Arctic terns, the curvy path is much longer, but easier.
