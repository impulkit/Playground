How do parrots copy our words?

Parrots have no lips and no vocal cords like ours, yet some can say hundreds of words.

The short answer

Parrots copy our words by listening carefully and then reshaping their own sounds until they match. They don't have vocal cords like ours. Instead, they make sounds with an organ called the syrinx, deep in the chest where the windpipe splits in two.

A parrot then shapes those sounds with its thick, muscly tongue, its throat and its beak, in a similar way to how we use our mouth. Their brains are especially good at learning new sounds, so a pet parrot that hears a word again and again may start to say it back.

Surprising but true

Parrots have an extra layer in the singing part of their brains, which other song-learning birds lack. It may help explain why they copy so well.

Built for copying

Only a few groups of animals can learn brand-new sounds by copying them. Humans can, and so can parrots, songbirds and hummingbirds. Some whales, dolphins, bats and elephants can too.

Parrots are among the best of all. In 2015, scientists comparing bird brains found that parrots have the usual song-learning areas found in songbirds, plus an extra outer "shell" around them. This shell may be one reason parrots are such good copycats, able to mimic human speech, door bells and even ringing phones.

The tongue matters too. A parrot's tongue is thick and moves a lot, like ours. Scientists using X-ray videos have watched parrots move their tongues to change their sounds, much as we shape vowels.

Why parrots copy

In the wild, parrots live in noisy flocks. Each flock has its own calls, a bit like a local accent. A young parrot learns its flock's calls by copying the birds around it. Fitting in helps it find food, friends and safety.

In some species, parents even give each chick its own call, a sort of name, which the chick keeps. A pet parrot has no flock, so it treats its human family as one, and copies us instead.

Do they understand?

This is where scientists disagree. Most talking parrots are copying sounds without knowing what the words mean.

But some seem to understand more. In 1977, the American scientist Irene Pepperberg began working with an African grey parrot named Alex. Over 30 years, Alex learned to name around 50 objects, seven colours and several shapes. He could answer questions like "What colour is the key?" or "How many blocks?", even about objects he had never seen before. Some scientists think Alex really understood. Others say he may have learned clever tricks without understanding in the way we do.

Record talkers

Guinness World Records once listed a budgie named Puck as knowing more than 1,700 words. Budgies are small parrots, and some of them become chatterboxes.

The African grey is often called the best talker, but many kinds of parrots can learn words. Some songbirds copy sounds too. The Australian lyrebird can imitate camera shutters and chainsaws.

Parrots in India and the USA

The green rose-ringed parakeet, the tota, is one of India's most familiar birds. Many people once kept them in cages, but catching and selling India's native parrots is now against the law. They belong in the wild, where they can talk to their flocks.

In San Francisco, a wild flock of parrots, mostly descended from escaped pets, has lived on Telegraph Hill for decades. The birds became famous after a film was made about them, and they are now part of the story of San Francisco. For another brainy bird, read about crows. Myth or fact?

Myth: Parrots talk by using a voice box with vocal cords, just like ours.

Truth: Parrots have no vocal cords. They make sounds with a syrinx deep in the chest, and shape them with their tongue, throat and beak.

Try this

Try saying "hello parrot" without moving your lips at all, the way a ventriloquist does. Some sounds, like "p" and "b", are very hard. Parrots have no lips, so they make those sounds in other clever ways, using their tongue and throat.
