Which animals have gone to space?

The first animals in space weren't dogs or monkeys. They were fruit flies, launched from New Mexico in 1947, and they came back alive.

The short answer

Before any person went to space, scientists sent animals. Nobody knew if a living body could survive the shaking of a launch, the radiation above the air, or floating without weight. Animals went first to find out.

The earliest were fruit flies, in 1947. Then came monkeys, mice and dogs. The Soviet Union mostly sent dogs. The USA mostly sent monkeys and chimpanzees. Later flights carried cats, tortoises, frogs, fish, spiders, jellyfish, worms and tiny tough creatures called tardigrades.

Their flights showed that living things could survive space. They also raised hard questions. Some animals died, and people still debate how far it is right to use animals in research.

Surprising but true

In 1968, two Soviet tortoises flew around the Moon on Zond 5 and returned safely, months before any human did.

Flies, monkeys and mice

On 20 February 1947, American scientists put fruit flies on a captured German V-2 rocket at White Sands, New Mexico. The rocket climbed above 100 km, the usual border of space, and the flies parachuted back down alive.

In 1949, a rhesus monkey named Albert II reached space on another V-2. He survived the flight but died when his parachute failed. Over the next years, mice and more monkeys flew. Each flight taught engineers how to protect passengers better.

Laika and the dogs

The Soviet space programme chose small, tough stray dogs from the streets of Moscow, because they were used to cold and hunger. Females were often preferred, partly because the gear for collecting their waste was simpler to design.

On 3 November 1957, a month after Sputnik, a dog named Laika rode Sputnik 2 into orbit. She was the first animal to circle Earth. There was no way to bring her back, and she died within hours as the capsule overheated. The full story was only made public decades later.

In August 1960, two dogs, Belka and Strelka, orbited Earth for a day and landed safely. Strelka later had puppies, and one of them, Pushinka, was given to the daughter of US President John F. Kennedy.

What happens to the body in space?Astronauts come home a few centimetres taller, with weaker bones and puffy faces, and some have to relearn how to walk.

Training for humans

On 31 January 1961, a chimpanzee named Ham flew in an American Mercury capsule. He had been trained to pull levers when lights flashed, and he did so in space, proving that a pilot could work up there. Ham splashed down in the Atlantic and was picked up safely. He lived for many more years, at zoos in Washington, D.C., and North Carolina. Three months later, Yuri Gagarin became the first human in space.

In 1963 France sent a cat named Félicette on a short rocket flight. She came back alive.

Around the Moon

In September 1968, the Soviet spacecraft Zond 5 carried two Russian tortoises, along with worms, flies and seeds, around the Moon and back. The tortoises lost some weight but were otherwise healthy. They were the first living things to travel around the Moon, three months before the astronauts of Apollo 8.

Webs, worms and water bears

Later flights were less about survival and more about science. In 1973, two garden spiders named Anita and Arabella spun webs aboard the US space station Skylab. Fish, newts and jellyfish have flown to study balance and growth.

In 2007, European scientists exposed tardigrades, animals smaller than a grain of salt, to open space outside a satellite. Some survived the vacuum and radiation and even had babies afterwards.

India's turn

India hasn't flown animals, but it has sent living things. In early 2025, ISRO germinated cowpea seeds in orbit on a small experiment platform. Its human spaceflight programme, Gaganyaan, plans to send a humanoid robot called Vyommitra on test flights before any astronaut goes up.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Laika was the first animal in space, and she came back safely.

Truth: Fruit flies, mice and monkeys flew before her. Laika was the first animal to orbit Earth, in 1957, but there was no plan to bring her back, and she died within hours, probably from overheating.

Try this

Fold a paper "space capsule" from a small box and plan a mission for a pretend animal crew. List what they would need for a three-day trip, such as air, water, food, a way to deal with waste and a seat that keeps them still at launch. You'll end up with roughly the same checklist space engineers use.
