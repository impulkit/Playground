What makes a new species new?

Howler monkeys are some of the loudest animals on land, yet two kinds of them went unnoticed by science until 2026.

The short answer

Some new species are not hiding in unexplored jungles. They have been seen many times, but everyone thought they were the same as a known animal.

Scientists only spotted the difference by reading the monkeys' DNA and carefully comparing old skulls and skins in museums. That is how two "new" howler monkeys came to light.

Surprising but true

Before this study, the last new species of howler monkey was described in 1941, more than 80 years ago.

Why it's in the news

On 4 November 2026, researchers led by the University of Salford in the UK announced two new species of howler monkey from South America. They are called Alouatta iroi, from the Guianas, and Alouatta matogrossensis, the Mato Grosso red howler, from southern Amazonia in Brazil. Live Science reported the discovery on 13 November.

The team studied the genomes, the complete DNA instructions, of 11 kinds of howler monkey, and matched them with measurements of museum specimens. Animals that had been lumped together as one species turned out to belong to separate family lines.

Rivers that make new species

The Amazon is crossed by giant rivers such as the Amazon itself, the Rio Branco and the Xingu. Howler monkeys spend almost their whole lives high in the forest canopy. To them, a wide river is like a wall.

When a group of animals is cut off for a very long time, it slowly changes in its own way. The study found that these rivers have kept howler populations apart for millions of years, helping new species form. This is one reason rainforests are bursting with so many kinds of life.

Who are the howlers?

Howler monkeys live in Central and South America. They eat mostly leaves and use their strong tail like a fifth hand to hold on to branches. At dawn, males roar to tell other groups where they are. A special hollow bone in the throat makes the sound boom, and it can carry several kilometres through the trees, which puts howlers among the loudest animals on land.

What happens next

Southern Amazonia is one of the fastest-changing landscapes on Earth, as forest is cleared for farms. The scientists warn that the new species may already be at risk because their habitat is shrinking. Naming them is the first step: an animal must be known before it can be counted and protected.

Myth or fact?

Myth: By now, scientists have found and named every kind of monkey and mammal on Earth.

Truth: New mammal species are still described every year, from bats and rodents to primates. Many look almost the same as known animals, so only careful study of DNA and skeletons reveals they are different.

Try this

Collect leaves from two trees that look almost the same, perhaps two kinds of mango or ficus. Lay them side by side and list every difference you can find in shape, edges, veins and smell. This is what taxonomists do when they compare museum specimens.
