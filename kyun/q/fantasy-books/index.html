What makes fantasy books so popular?

J. R. R. Tolkien invented whole languages first, then wrote The Lord of the Rings partly to give them a world to live in.

The short answer

Fantasy books take you to a world with rules different from ours. There might be magic, dragons, talking animals or a hidden school for wizards. That sense of stepping somewhere new is a big part of the fun.

But the best fantasy also feels real. Its worlds have maps, histories, languages and laws, so you can believe in them. And beneath the magic, the stories are about things everyone knows: being afraid, being brave, losing friends and finding them. Readers can think about hard problems from a safe distance. Fantasy also makes long series possible, and a world you love can keep you company for seven books.

Surprising but true

Tolkien and C. S. Lewis, the authors of The Lord of the Rings and Narnia, were friends and fellow teachers at Oxford University. They belonged to a small club that met to read unfinished stories aloud to each other.

Old stories, new books

People have always told stories with magic in them. The Greek myths have monsters and gods. The Ramayana and Mahabharata have flying chariots and enchanted weapons. The tales of Vikram and Betal, where a ghost on a king's back asks riddles, are more than a thousand years old.

What changed in the 1900s was the idea of a whole invented world, written as a novel. Earlier writers like Lewis Carroll and L. Frank Baum, author of The Wonderful Wizard of Oz (1900), sent children into magic lands. But the modern shape of fantasy came mostly from two friends at Oxford.

Tolkien's languages

J. R. R. Tolkien was a professor who studied Old English and other ancient languages. As a hobby, he invented his own, including two Elvish languages. He wrote The Hobbit in 1937 and The Lord of the Rings in the 1950s. He said the stories grew partly to give his languages a world.

His Middle-earth had thousands of years of history, family trees, songs and detailed maps. Readers loved the feeling that the world went on past the edges of the page. His friend C. S. Lewis wrote the Narnia books, starting in 1950. The two belonged to a group called the Inklings, who read their writing aloud to each other.

The Harry Potter explosion

In 1997, J. K. Rowling's first Harry Potter book came out with a small first print run. Within a few years, children around the world were queuing at midnight for new books. By 2023, the seven books had sold more than 600 million copies in more than 80 languages.

The books mixed magic with something ordinary: school. Children knew about lessons, homework, unfair teachers and best friends. That made the magic easy to step into.

Fantasy from everywhere

Fantasy today comes from many cultures. Rick Riordan's Percy Jackson series puts Greek gods in modern America. Roshani Chokshi, an American writer with Indian roots, wrote the Aru Shah series, in which a 12-year-old girl finds herself mixed up with the gods and demons of Hindu stories.

In India, generations grew up on fantasy through comics and magazines such as Chandamama, full of kings, magic and clever tricksters. Many Indian writers now draw on Indian myths and folk tales for new fantasy novels.

Why it pulls us in

Tolkien wrote that fantasy offers "escape", but he meant it in a good way, like a prisoner escaping a cell. Fantasy lets us picture a world where things could be different.

Some writers say fantasy helps young readers practise courage. A hobbit is small and scared, yet still carries the ring. A child reading that might feel a little braver at school the next day. Researchers who study reading and empathy have found mixed results, so nobody can say for sure that fantasy changes people. Readers, though, keep coming back. Fantasy has also spread to films and video games, where players can explore invented worlds for themselves.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Fantasy is only for little children.

Truth: Some of the best-known fantasy was written by university professors, and adults buy a large share of fantasy books. Many fantasy stories deal with serious ideas like war, loss, power and friendship.
