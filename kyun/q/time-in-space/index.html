Does time pass differently in space?

Astronauts who spend a year on the space station come home a tiny bit younger than if they had stayed on Earth.

The short answer

Yes, but only by a tiny amount. Time doesn't tick at the same rate everywhere. It runs slightly slower for things that move very fast, and slightly faster where gravity is weaker. Both ideas come from Albert Einstein.

On the International Space Station, the speed effect wins. The station races around Earth at about 28,000 km/h, so its clocks run a little slow compared with clocks on the ground. An astronaut who spends a year up there ages less than a hundredth of a second less than someone at home. It is far too small to feel, but extremely precise clocks can measure it.

Surprising but true

Clocks are now so precise that scientists have measured time passing faster on a clock raised just 33 cm higher than another.

Fast clocks run slow

In 1905, Einstein worked out something very strange. The speed of light is the same for everyone, however fast they are moving. For that to be true, time itself has to stretch for moving objects. The faster something moves, the slower its clocks tick, compared with someone standing still.

At everyday speeds, the effect is almost zero. Even on a jet plane it amounts to billionths of a second. But for particles racing near the speed of light, time stretches hugely. Scientists see this every day in particle accelerators, where fast-moving particles survive far longer than slow ones before breaking down.

Gravity slows time too

In 1915, Einstein added gravity to the picture. Close to a heavy object, time runs slower. Higher up, where gravity is a little weaker, time runs faster.

This means your head is ageing very slightly faster than your feet. In 2010, scientists in the United States used two extremely accurate clocks to show that raising one by just 33 cm made it run measurably faster. Near a black hole, where gravity is extreme, the effect would be enormous.

Clocks on aeroplanes

In 1971, two American scientists, Joseph Hafele and Richard Keating, carried four atomic clocks around the world on ordinary passenger flights, first heading east and then west. Then they compared them with clocks left behind at the US Naval Observatory in Washington. The travelling clocks had gained or lost tiny amounts of time, from tens to hundreds of billionths of a second, roughly as Einstein's theories predicted.

That might sound like a strange use of airline seats, but it was one of the first direct tests of these ideas using real clocks.

GPS depends on it

GPS satellites orbit about 20,200 km up. They move fast, which slows their clocks. But they are also far from Earth, where gravity is weaker, which speeds them up more. Overall, their clocks gain about 38 millionths of a second every day.

If engineers did not correct for this, GPS positions would drift by about 10 km a day, and your map app would soon be useless. So every time you see your blue dot in the right place, Einstein's ideas are being put to work.

Twins in space

From 2015 to 2016, NASA astronaut Scott Kelly spent almost a year on the space station while his identical twin, Mark, a retired astronaut, stayed on Earth. Doctors studied both brothers to see how space changes the body.

The time difference was only a few thousandths of a second. The study found more interesting changes in Scott's body, such as in his eyes and immune system, most of which went back to normal after he came home.

The Russian cosmonaut Oleg Kononenko has spent more than 1,000 days in orbit, more than anyone else. Even so, by this calculation he is only a few hundredths of a second younger than he would otherwise be.

Could you travel into the future?

In a way, yes. A spaceship travelling at nearly the speed of light could return after what felt like a few years to its crew, while decades had passed on Earth. Nobody can build such a ship. But the physics says it would work, and every second on the space station proves the idea in a small way.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Astronauts come back from long missions years younger than their friends.

Truth: The difference is real but tiny, a few hundredths of a second at most, even for people who have spent more than three years in orbit. Big time differences only happen at speeds close to the speed of light or near something like a black hole.

Try this

Walk steadily across a room while bouncing a ball straight up and down in front of you. To you, the ball goes straight up and down. Ask a friend to watch from the side. To them, the ball traces a row of arches, a longer path. Einstein used a similar idea, with light instead of a ball, to show why moving clocks run slow.
