What inventions came from space programmes?

The camera in almost every phone grew out of work at a NASA laboratory that builds robots for other planets.

The short answer

Space is a harsh place. Rockets shake violently, temperatures swing wildly and every gram costs money to launch. Solving those problems forces engineers to invent new materials, sensors and machines. Many of those inventions later find jobs on Earth.

The best known examples are memory foam, the tiny camera sensor in your phone, better ways to clean water and lighter, tougher materials. Just as important are the satellites themselves. They help forecast storms, guide ships, plan crops and tell your phone where you are.

Surprising but true

Memory foam, the squishy stuff in many pillows and mattresses, was first made in 1966 for NASA aircraft seats.

A camera on a chip

In the early 1990s, a team at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory in California wanted smaller, lighter cameras for spacecraft. Engineer Eric Fossum and his colleagues developed a new kind of image sensor that put the light-catching parts and the electronics on one cheap chip. It also used far less power than older sensors.

The idea spread into webcams, then into phones. Today almost every phone camera uses this kind of sensor, which works in much the same way as the ones in cameras that fly on spacecraft. A selfie in Kolkata or Kansas City owes something to a design meant for Mars.

How do satellites help us every day?Weather forecasts, maps on your phone, card payments and cricket on TV all depend on machines circling far above your head.

Foam, drills and coatings

In 1966, an engineer named Charles Yost, working with NASA's Ames Research Center, made a foam that slowly moulds to a body and then springs back. It was meant to make aircraft seats safer in a crash. It is now found in mattresses, pillows, shoes and sports helmets.

For the Apollo Moon landings, the tool company Black & Decker designed a battery-powered drill that astronauts could use to pull up samples of Moon rock. What the company learned helped shape cordless tools and small handheld vacuum cleaners. NASA research also helped create scratch-resistant coatings now used on glasses.

Help from the sky

Some of the biggest benefits came from what satellites can see. Weather satellites watch clouds and storms grow over the oceans, so weather forecasts can warn coastal towns days before a cyclone arrives. The GPS system, built by the United States, now guides everything from delivery riders to farm tractors.

India's space agency, ISRO, built its programme around this idea from the start. Satellites help map groundwater, track crops and monitor forests. Indian ocean scientists use satellite readings of sea temperature and colour to send fishermen daily advice on where fish are likely to gather, which can save fuel and time at sea.

The same satellites watch for disasters. When Cyclone Phailin approached the coast of Odisha in 2013, satellite images and forecasts helped officials move close to a million people to shelters before it struck. Far fewer lives were lost than in a similar cyclone in 1999. Tools built for space ended up guarding fishing villages.

Myths that stuck

Space programmes get credit for some things they never made. Velcro came from a Swiss engineer who noticed burrs sticking to his dog's fur, and it was patented in the 1950s. Velcro became famous partly because astronauts used it to stop things floating away. Non-stick Teflon and the orange drink powder Tang have the same story.

NASA publishes a yearly report called Spinoff that lists technologies traced back to its work. It has been printed every year since 1976 and now includes more than 2,000 examples. Many are humble: better insulation, cleaner water filters, safer firefighters' masks.

Myth or fact?

Myth: NASA invented Velcro, Teflon and Tang.

Truth: Velcro was patented by George de Mestral of Switzerland in the 1950s, Teflon was discovered at DuPont in 1938 and Tang was made by a food company in 1957. Astronauts used all three, so people assumed space programmes created them.

Try this

Press your hand firmly into a memory-foam pillow for ten seconds, then lift it and count how long the handprint lasts. Do the same with a kitchen sponge. The foam slowly creeps back, which is why it spreads the force of a bump or a fall.
