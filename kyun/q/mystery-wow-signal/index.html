What was the Wow! signal?

In 1977, an astronomer saw six strange characters on a computer printout, circled them in red and wrote one word beside them, "Wow!"

The short answer

On 15 August 1977, a radio telescope in Ohio called Big Ear picked up a strong, narrow burst of radio waves from the direction of the constellation Sagittarius. It lasted about 72 seconds. A few days later, a volunteer astronomer named Jerry Ehman was checking the printouts and spotted it. He circled the code "6EQUJ5" and wrote "Wow!" in the margin.

The burst sat almost exactly on the frequency that hydrogen, the most common element in space, gives off naturally. Some scientists had guessed aliens might use that frequency to get noticed. The signal has never been heard again, and nobody knows for sure what caused it.

Surprising but true

The telescope that caught the Wow signal was later knocked down, and the land became part of a golf course.

A telescope as big as three football fields

Big Ear did not look like a dish. It was a flat, tilted metal field, roughly the size of three American football fields, with a reflector at each end. It could not move. Instead, it waited for the Earth's spin to sweep the sky past it.

That is why the signal lasted 72 seconds. A star or other source would drift through the telescope's view in about that time, getting stronger, peaking and fading. The Wow signal did exactly that, which is a strong clue it came from space, not from a nearby radio or aeroplane.

Reading the code

The computer printed one character every 12 seconds for each radio channel. A blank or a 1 meant almost nothing, just the usual hiss. Numbers went up to 9, and then letters took over for stronger signals. "6EQUJ5" rose to "U", about 30 times louder than the background, and fell again.

It was a perfect rise and fall, shaped like a hill. Big Ear had two receiving horns, side by side, and each should have seen the source a few minutes apart. The signal showed up in only one of them, a puzzle that remains unsolved. Either the source switched off within minutes, or something stranger happened.

Why hydrogen matters

Hydrogen atoms in clouds across the galaxy give off radio waves at about 1,420 megahertz. In 1959, two scientists suggested that any civilisation hoping to be found might broadcast close to this frequency, because every astronomer in the galaxy would know it. It is a bit like agreeing to meet friends at the main gate of a cricket ground, because everyone knows where it is.

So when the strongest signal of Big Ear's survey landed right there, people got excited about alien life.

Searching again and again

Ehman and his colleagues pointed Big Ear back at the same spot many times. Other astronomers used bigger telescopes, in the USA and Australia, over the decades since. They found nothing. A real alien beacon would probably repeat, so the silence counts against the alien idea.

Earth-made radio was checked too. No satellites or aircraft were known to be there, and that frequency is protected for astronomers, so transmitters are not allowed to use it.

A natural explanation

In 2024, a team led by Abel Méndez in Puerto Rico suggested something new. Perhaps a powerful flash from a dead star, such as a magnetar, hit a cold cloud of hydrogen. The cloud would then glow briefly at its natural frequency, like a bell struck in the dark. They found cold hydrogen clouds in that part of the sky, but not the flash itself. The idea is still being tested.

Big Ear was knocked down in 1998, and the land became part of a golf course. Meanwhile humans have sent out signals of our own. In 1977, the same year as the Wow signal, two Voyager spacecraft left Earth carrying a golden record of music and greetings, a message in a bottle for whoever finds it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Wow signal contained a message, with words or pictures.

Truth: It carried no message at all. It was a single strong burst of radio noise at one frequency. What made it exciting was where it sat on the radio dial, not what it said.

Try this

Make your own Wow printout. Ask a friend to clap at different loudness, once every five seconds. Write 1 for the softest clap up to 9, then A, B, C and on for louder ones. Can someone else guess, just from your list, when the loudest clap came?
