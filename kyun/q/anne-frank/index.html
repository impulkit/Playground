Why is Anne Frank's diary important?

A 13-year-old girl hid in a secret set of rooms for two years and wrote one of the most-read books in the world.

The short answer

Anne Frank was a Jewish girl who hid from the Nazis in Amsterdam during the Second World War. For just over two years she kept a diary in a secret set of rooms behind her father's office. She was found, arrested and sent to concentration camps, where she died at 15.

Her diary survived. It was published in 1947 and has been read by tens of millions of people. It matters because it lets readers meet one real, funny, moody, clever teenager among the millions of people killed in the Holocaust.

Surprising but true

Anne rewrote large parts of her diary herself, because she hoped to publish it as a book after the war.

A birthday present

For her 13th birthday on 12 June 1942, Anne was given an autograph book with a red-and-white checked cover. She decided to use it as a diary and wrote her entries as letters to an imaginary friend, Kitty.

Less than a month later, her older sister Margot was ordered to report to a Nazi work camp. The family went into hiding the next day. Anne, Margot and their parents Otto and Edith were joined by four more people, packed into a few rooms at Prinsengracht 263.

Life in the Secret Annex

A bookcase hid the door to the rooms. During working hours the eight people had to keep almost silent, so the office workers below would not hear them. Toilets could not be flushed. Anne could not go outside, and she watched one chestnut tree through the attic window.

A few of Otto's employees, including Miep Gies, risked their lives to bring food, books and news. Anne wrote about all of it. She described arguments over meals and her crush on a boy in the annex. She wrote about her anger at her mother, her fear of bombs and her hopes of becoming a writer.

What happened to Anne

On 4 August 1944 the police raided the annex. Nobody knows for certain who told them. All eight were sent to camps. Anne and Margot died of disease at Bergen-Belsen in Germany in early 1945, only weeks before the camp was freed. Otto was the only one of the eight to survive.

When he read the diary, he found a daughter he felt he had never fully known. He decided to fulfil her wish to be published.

Why so many people read it

History books give numbers: about six million Jewish people were murdered. A number that large is hard to feel. Anne's diary gives one name, one voice and one ordinary bedroom wall covered in film-star pictures.

It has been put into more than 70 languages through translation and is read in schools across India, the United States and Europe. It has been made into plays and films. The first edition left out some passages, such as Anne's sharpest words about her mother. Later editions put them back, so readers now meet a fuller and more honest Anne. People have come to see the parallels with children forced from their homes today, including refugees fleeing war.

The house today

The annex opened as the Anne Frank House museum in 1960. The rooms are kept empty, as Otto asked, and the original diary is on display. In 2009 UNESCO added Anne's diaries to its Memory of the World register, a list of documents that belong to everyone. Like Malala decades later, Anne showed that a young person's words, written down, can reach the whole world.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Anne wrote her diary only for herself and never meant anyone to read it.

Truth: In 1944 she heard a radio broadcast asking Dutch people to keep diaries and letters for after the war. She began rewriting her diary as a book, with a planned title, The Secret Annex.

Try this

Keep a diary for one week. Each day, write about one small thing that happened and one thing you thought about it. At the end of the week, read it back and notice which entries show what you were really like.
