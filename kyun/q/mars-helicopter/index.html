How did a helicopter fly on Mars?

NASA planned five short test flights for its little Mars helicopter. It ended up flying 72 times over nearly three years.

The short answer

In April 2021, a small NASA helicopter named Ingenuity lifted off from the floor of Jezero Crater on Mars. It rose three metres, hovered for about 30 seconds and landed. It was the first powered, controlled flight on another planet.

Flying on Mars is tough because the atmosphere is about 1% as thick as Earth's. To get lift from such thin air, Ingenuity used two sets of carbon-fibre blades, each about 1.2 metres from tip to tip, spinning in opposite directions roughly 2,400 times a minute. It weighed only about 1.8 kg, and it flew itself.

Surprising but true

Ingenuity carried a tiny scrap of fabric from the wing of the Wright brothers' 1903 plane, the first powered aircraft on Earth.

A stowaway on a rover

Ingenuity travelled to Mars tucked under the belly of the Perseverance rover. They landed together on 18 February 2021. A few weeks later the rover dropped it gently onto the ground and drove away so the helicopter could charge its batteries in the sunshine using a small solar panel.

The first night was a test in itself. Temperatures in Jezero can drop to around minus 90°C. Ingenuity had to use some of its battery power just to keep its electronics warm enough to survive.

Why the blades spin so fast

A helicopter's blades are wings that go round in circles. As they spin, they push air downwards, and the air pushes the helicopter up. In thin air there is less to push against, so the blades must be bigger and spin faster.

On Earth, a helicopter's blades might spin a few hundred times a minute. Ingenuity's spun about five times faster. Engineers at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory in California tested it in a big chamber with most of the air pumped out and the rest replaced with carbon dioxide, to copy Mars. The low gravity on Mars, about a third of Earth's, helped a little.

Flying on its own

Mars is so far away that a radio message takes between 5 and 20 minutes to arrive, depending on where the two planets are in their orbits. No pilot could steer it live. Instead, the team sent up a flight plan, and Ingenuity's onboard computer did the rest.

It used a downward-looking camera to track the ground below, plus sensors that measure tilt and speed, many of them similar to the parts in a smartphone. If something went wrong mid-air, it had to sort it out by itself. On its sixth flight, a glitch confused its camera timing and it wobbled wildly, but it still landed safely.

The Wright brothers' scrap

Tucked under Ingenuity's solar panel was a piece of fabric about the size of a postage stamp. It came from the Wright Flyer, the plane in which Orville and Wilbur Wright made the first powered flight at Kitty Hawk, North Carolina, in 1903. NASA named Ingenuity's first airfield "Wright Brothers Field". Almost 118 years separated the two first flights.

From test to scout

The plan was for up to five test flights in about a month. Ingenuity kept going. It began scouting ahead for Perseverance, taking photos to help the rover's drivers choose safe routes. Over 72 flights it covered about 17 km, climbed as high as 24 metres and spent more than two hours in the air in total.

In January 2024, after its 72nd flight, photos showed that at least one rotor blade had broken. It probably struck the ground while landing on smooth sand, which gave its camera few features to track. It could no longer fly. Ingenuity stayed where it landed and, for a while, kept sending back data as a tiny weather station. NASA is building a much bigger flying machine, the Dragonfly drone, to explore Saturn's moon Titan, where thick air will make flying far easier.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ingenuity was flown live by a pilot on Earth using a joystick.

Truth: Radio signals take between about 5 and 20 minutes to travel between Earth and Mars. Engineers sent Ingenuity a flight plan in advance, and it flew itself, using its own camera and sensors to stay on course.
