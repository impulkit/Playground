What makes New Orleans unique?

Much of this American city sits below the level of the river beside it, and it gave the world jazz.

The short answer

New Orleans is a port city in Louisiana, near where the Mississippi River meets the Gulf of Mexico. It was founded by the French in 1718, ruled by Spain for about forty years, then sold to the United States with the Louisiana Purchase in 1803.

Those owners, along with enslaved Africans, free people of colour and later immigrants from Italy, Ireland and the Caribbean, mixed their languages, food and music. The result is a city that sounds, tastes and looks different from anywhere else in America. It is the birthplace of jazz, the home of the biggest Mardi Gras party in the country, and a place that lives with water on every side.

Surprising but true

The famous French Quarter of New Orleans is mostly Spanish in style, because two great fires in the 1780s and 1790s burned the old French buildings while Spain ruled the city.

A city in a bowl

New Orleans was built on the only firm ground French settlers could find in a swampy bend of the river. As the city grew, swamps were drained and houses spread onto land that slowly sank. Today about half the city lies below sea level, some of it by 2 metres or more.

Tall earth banks called levees hold back the river on one side and a huge lake, Lake Pontchartrain, on the other. Giant pumps push rainwater out. In 2005, when Hurricane Katrina struck, several levees failed and about 80 percent of the city flooded. More than a thousand people died in Louisiana, and much of the city had to be rebuilt.

The marshes south of the city used to soften storms before they arrived. They are disappearing as the land sinks and the sea creeps in. The US Geological Survey has estimated that Louisiana loses coastal wetland the size of a football field roughly every 100 minutes.

Where jazz was born

On Sunday afternoons in the early 1800s, enslaved Africans in New Orleans were allowed to gather in an open space now called Congo Square. They drummed, sang and danced in styles carried from West Africa. Few other American cities allowed this at the time.

Those rhythms mixed with church hymns, military brass bands, the blues and the dance music of the Caribbean. By about 1900, musicians were playing something new and loose and full of surprises: jazz. Louis Armstrong was born here in 1901 and grew up playing the cornet. He took the New Orleans sound to Chicago, New York and the whole world.

Mardi Gras and the parades

Mardi Gras means "Fat Tuesday" in French. It is the last day of feasting before Lent, a time when many Christians traditionally give things up. In New Orleans it grows into weeks of parades. Clubs called krewes build giant floats, and riders throw strings of plastic beads to the crowds below.

The official colours are purple, green and gold. Bakeries sell king cake, a ring of sweet dough with a tiny plastic baby hidden inside. Whoever finds the baby in their slice is supposed to bring the cake next time.

Gumbo, beignets and two cooking styles

Two cooking traditions meet here. Creole food grew in the city itself, mixing French, Spanish, African and Caribbean cooking. Cajun food came from French-speaking settlers expelled from Canada in the 1700s, who made a new home in the Louisiana swamps.

Both love gumbo, a thick stew of seafood or sausage, often thickened with okra. The word "gumbo" is thought to come from a West African word for okra, the same vegetable Indian kitchens call bhindi. For breakfast, people queue for beignets: square fried doughnuts buried under so much powdered sugar that it snows onto your shirt.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The French Quarter's old buildings were built by the French.

Truth: Fires in 1788 and 1794 destroyed most of the French-era town. Spain ruled New Orleans at the time, so much of what stands today, with its courtyards and iron balconies, was built in Spanish times or later.
