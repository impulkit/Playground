What happens when you donate blood?

Giving blood takes about ten minutes in the chair, and the blood from one donation can be split to help up to three different patients.

The short answer

When an adult donates blood, a nurse puts a thin needle into a vein in the arm. Blood flows down a tube into a sterile bag, which is gently rocked so it doesn't clot. In about ten minutes, the bag holds around 350 to 450 millilitres, roughly two cups. That is only about a tenth of the blood in an adult's body.

The donor then rests for a few minutes with a drink and a snack. Meanwhile, the bag goes to a laboratory, where it is tested, sorted into parts and stored. Within days it could be inside someone who has had an accident, an operation or a serious illness.

Surprising but true

Your body replaces the liquid part of donated blood within a day or two, but it takes several weeks to rebuild all the red blood cells.

Before the needle

Not everyone can donate. In India, donors must be between 18 and 65, weigh at least 45 kg and be in good health. In the United States, the Red Cross accepts some 16- and 17-year-olds with a parent's permission, depending on the state.

First, a health worker asks questions about recent illnesses, medicines and travel. Then they prick a finger to check haemoglobin, the iron-rich protein that carries oxygen. If it is too low, the person is asked to come back another day. These checks protect the donor as much as the patient.

Splitting one bag into three

A full bag of blood is spun very fast in a machine called a centrifuge. The heavy red cells sink to the bottom. The yellow plasma floats on top, and a thin layer of platelets sits in between.

Each part helps a different patient. Red cells help someone who has lost a lot of blood. Platelets help blood clot, and are often needed by people being treated for cancer. Plasma helps patients with burns or bleeding problems. So one donation can help up to three people.

Red cells keep for about five to six weeks in a fridge. Platelets last only about five days. Plasma can be frozen for around a year.

Testing and matching

Every donated bag is tested for infections that can spread through blood, such as HIV, hepatitis B and C, and malaria. Any bag that fails is thrown away.

Blood also has to match. In 1901, the Austrian doctor Karl Landsteiner discovered that people have different blood groups, now called A, B, AB and O, each either positive or negative. Giving the wrong type can make a patient very ill. People with O negative blood are especially valued, because their red cells can usually be given to anyone in an emergency.

How the body refills itself

Right after giving blood, your heart and blood vessels adjust and keep blood pressure steady. Drinking water helps. Within a day or two, the body has replaced the lost plasma by pulling in fluid.

Red cells take longer. Bone marrow speeds up its work, and after about four to eight weeks the count is back to normal. That is why there are rules about waiting. In India, men may donate every three months and women every four. In the United States, the usual gap is 56 days.

Why donors are always needed

Blood can't be made in a factory, and it doesn't keep forever. Hospitals need a steady stream of donors every week. Shortages often come during holidays, exam seasons and heatwaves, when fewer people turn up.

India marks National Voluntary Blood Donation Day on 1 October, and the world celebrates World Blood Donor Day on 14 June, Landsteiner's birthday. Children can't give blood, but they can help at school blood drives, make posters, or remind the adults around them that ten minutes in a chair can save a stranger's life.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Donating blood makes you weak for a long time.

Truth: A healthy adult gives only about a tenth of their blood. Most people feel normal within a few hours, after a rest, a drink and a snack. The body refills the lost liquid within a day or two.

Try this

Ask an adult whether they know their blood group, and make a family chart. You might find that some people share a group and some don't. If an adult in your family donates blood, ask what the donation centre was like and what snack they got afterwards.
