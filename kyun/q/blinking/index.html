Why do we blink?

You blink thousands of times a day, and each blink plunges you into darkness. So why doesn't the world keep switching off?

The short answer

You blink to keep your eyes wet, clean and safe. Each blink sweeps a thin layer of tears across the front of the eye, a bit like a windscreen wiper. This film keeps the surface smooth so you can see clearly, washes away dust, and stops the eye drying out.

Blinking also protects you. If something flies at your face, or a bright flash goes off, your eyelids snap shut in a fraction of a second, before you even know what happened.

Most of the time you don't notice blinking at all. Your brain even hides the tiny moments of darkness from you.

Surprising but true

Newborn babies blink only a couple of times a minute, far less than adults, and scientists are still not completely sure why.

A coat of tears

The front of your eye, the cornea, has no blood vessels. It gets oxygen straight from the air, and it needs to stay wet to work. The tear film is very thin, but it has three layers. A sticky mucus layer helps it cling to the eye. A watery middle layer, made by glands above each eye, carries salts and germ-fighting substances. A thin oily layer on top slows evaporation.

Between blinks, this film slowly dries and breaks up. Blinking spreads a fresh coat. Used tears drain through tiny holes at the inner corners of your eyelids and down into your nose. That's why your nose runs when you cry.

A reflex that guards your eyes

Some blinks happen on purpose, like a wink. Most are automatic. And some are reflexes, which travel through a quick nerve loop that doesn't wait for the thinking part of your brain.

Touch your eyelashes or the surface of your eye, and both eyes close. A loud bang or a sudden movement near your face can also trigger a blink. Your eyes shut when you sneeze, too, though no one is completely sure why.

How the brain hides your blinks

An adult blinks around 15 to 20 times a minute. Add it up and your eyes are closed for several seconds every minute. Yet the world doesn't flicker.

That is because your brain does some clever editing. Just before and during a blink, it turns down activity in the parts that handle vision, and it fills the gap using what you saw a moment before. You don't see the darkness, and the world seems steady. Scientists have measured this dip in brain activity using scanners, showing how your eyes and brain work as a team.

Screens, books and dry eyes

How often you blink depends on what you're doing. In conversation, people blink often. When they concentrate on reading, driving or a video game, their blink rate can drop to less than half.

This is one reason eyes can feel dry, itchy or tired after a long time staring at a phone, tablet or computer. Eye doctors often suggest a simple habit: every 20 minutes, look at something about 6 metres (20 feet) away for 20 seconds.

Eyelids across the animal kingdom

Not every animal blinks like us. Snakes have no eyelids. Instead, each eye is covered by a clear, fixed scale, which they shed along with their skin. Most fish have no eyelids either, because water keeps their eyes wet.

Many birds, reptiles and some mammals, including cats, have a third eyelid, a see-through sheet that slides sideways across the eye to clean and protect it. Humans have only a tiny pink fold in the inner corner of each eye, which scientists think is a leftover of this third eyelid from our distant ancestors.

Babies are a puzzle. Newborns blink only a couple of times a minute. Their rate rises slowly through childhood until it reaches adult levels in the teenage years.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If you keep your eyes open when you sneeze, your eyeballs could pop out.

Truth: Eyes usually close during a sneeze as part of a reflex, but nothing would push them out if they didn't. Some people can sneeze with their eyes open, and their eyes stay exactly where they are.

Try this

Sit with a friend and secretly count their blinks for one minute while you chat. Then ask them to read a page of a book or watch a short video, and count again for one minute. Swap roles. Compare your numbers and see whether concentrating changes how often you blink.
