What was the Oregon Trail?

Between the 1840s and 1860s, hundreds of thousands of Americans walked about 3,200 km beside ox-drawn wagons, and their wheel ruts can still be seen in the rock today.

The short answer

The Oregon Trail was a long wagon route across the middle of North America. From the 1840s to the 1860s, families set off from towns on the Missouri River, such as Independence, and headed west to the green farmland of Oregon's Willamette Valley. The trip covered about 3,200 km (2,000 miles) of prairie, desert and mountains and took four to six months.

Roughly 300,000 to 400,000 people travelled the western trails in those years, including routes that branched off to California and Utah. They wanted free or cheap land, gold or a fresh start. For the Native nations already living along the way, the flood of wagons brought disease and the loss of land.

Surprising but true

Most travellers didn't ride in their covered wagons. The wagons were packed with food and tools, so people walked beside them almost the whole way.

Walking across a continent

Most families used wagons pulled by oxen, which were slower than horses but tougher and cheaper. With canvas stretched over wooden hoops, the wagons looked like ships on a sea of grass, so people called them prairie schooners.

The wagons were so full that hardly anyone rode in them, and they had no springs, so riding was bumpy anyway. People walked, often 20 km a day. Children herded cattle, collected dried buffalo dung for fuel and kept an eye on smaller siblings. Some walked barefoot when their shoes wore out.

Who were Lewis and Clark?In 1804 a small team set off up the Missouri River to find a water route to the Pacific. There wasn't one, but what they did find changed the map of North America.

Landmarks on the way

Travellers measured progress by famous rocks. Chimney Rock, a tall spire in Nebraska, could be seen for days. At Independence Rock in Wyoming, they hoped to arrive by 4 July, to be sure of crossing the mountains before snow fell. Thousands carved their names into it.

At Guernsey, Wyoming, wagon wheels wore ruts up to about 1.5 m deep into soft sandstone. You can still walk in them today. In other places, faint wagon tracks across grassland are visible from the air more than 150 years later.

Dangers on the trail

The trail's worst danger was invisible. Cholera, a disease spread by dirty water, swept through crowded campsites. A person could feel fine at breakfast and be dead by evening. Historians estimate that somewhere between one in twenty and one in ten travellers died on the journey, though numbers are uncertain.

River crossings were scary too, because wagons could tip and people drown. Then came the mountains. Families had to haul wagons over the Rocky Mountains and the Blue Mountains, sometimes lowering them down cliffs with ropes. Precious furniture was dumped along the way to lighten the load.

The other side of the story

The trail cut through land that had belonged to Native nations for thousands of years, including the Lakota, Cheyenne, Shoshone and many others. At first, many tribes traded horses, salmon and help with river crossings. As tens of thousands of wagons passed each year, though, settlers' animals ate the grass and hunters killed bison. New diseases spread.

The US government used the trail to strengthen its claim to Oregon, which Britain also wanted. Settlement led to treaties, wars and the forced removal of Native peoples onto reservations.

Game over for the wagons

The trail's busy years ended when the transcontinental railroad opened in 1869. A trip of months shrank to about a week. People still used parts of the route afterwards, but the great wagon trains faded into history.

They came back to life in an unexpected way. In 1971, three student teachers in Minnesota made a computer game called The Oregon Trail. Players chose supplies, hunted for food and tried not to "die of dysentery". It became one of the most played educational games ever, and a chapter in the history of video games.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pioneers on the Oregon Trail were constantly fighting Native Americans.

Truth: Fights were rare, and many Native people traded with travellers, guided them or helped them cross rivers. The bigger harm went the other way, as settlers took land, game and grass from Native nations.
