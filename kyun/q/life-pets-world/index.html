What pets do people keep around the world?

In Japan there are more pet cats and dogs than children under 15, and in parts of China people keep singing crickets in carved gourds.

The short answer

Dogs and cats are the most popular pets almost everywhere, but the rest of the list changes a lot from place to place. Americans keep fish, birds, rabbits and lizards. In Japan, tiny dog breeds and cats rule small city flats. In China, some people keep crickets for their songs. In India, many families keep dogs, fish or rabbits, and lots of children help feed street dogs and cats they don't own at all.

About 94 million American homes had a pet in 2025. In Japan, pet cats and dogs now outnumber children under 15. Pets fit the way people live: how big their homes are, how hot or cold the weather is, and what their grandparents kept.

Surprising but true

Goldfish come from dull grey-green wild carp. Breeders in China began keeping the rare golden ones more than a thousand years ago.

Fourteen thousand years of friends

People have kept animals for company for a very long time. At Bonn-Oberkassel in Germany, archaeologists found a grave about 14,000 years old with two people and a young dog. The puppy had been very sick, and someone had nursed it for weeks before it died. That looks like care, not just usefulness.

Ancient Egypt loved cats so much that killing one could be punished severely. Families shaved their eyebrows in mourning when a pet cat died, according to the Greek writer Herodotus.

Crickets in gourds and fish in bags

In China, keeping crickets goes back more than a thousand years. Owners kept them in small boxes or hollow gourds, sometimes carved with pictures, and carried them close to the body in winter to keep them warm. Some prized the insects' chirping. Others trained males to fight.

Goldfish come from China too. Wild carp sometimes have a gold-coloured fish among them, and Chinese breeders began keeping those as pets over a thousand years ago. Today, Hong Kong has a street nicknamed Goldfish Market, where fish hang in plastic bags along shop walls like bunches of coloured balloons.

Cities full of shared cats

In Istanbul, street cats belong to everybody. Shopkeepers put out food bowls and little cat houses, and some cats wander into mosques and offices as if they own them. Many Indian neighbourhoods have their own famous street dogs, fed by several families at once.

Japan has gone a step further. Some of its islands, such as Aoshima, are so full of cats that they are nicknamed cat islands, and tourists take ferries just to see them. Cat cafés, where you pay to sit with resident cats, spread from Taiwan to Japan in the 2000s and then around the world. People living in tiny flats where pets aren't allowed can still stroke a cat over a cup of tea.

Not every animal should be a pet

In India, it is against the law to keep native wild birds such as parakeets and munias in cages. They are protected under the Wildlife (Protection) Act of 1972. Many people still don't know this, and birds are still sold illegally in some markets.

In the US, owning exotic animals like big cats is now heavily restricted. A federal law passed in 2022 stops most private owners from keeping lions, tigers and similar animals.

What a pet tells you

Pets change with the times. Fewer children in Japan has meant more small dogs dressed in coats and pushed in prams. In crowded cities, people choose fish, hamsters or turtles that fit on a shelf.

Every pet you see, from a lap dog to a goldfish, has a wild ancestor. The cat on your neighbour's wall is a close cousin of the wildcats of the Middle East and North Africa. Exactly how cats moved in with farmers thousands of years ago is still being worked out from ancient bones and DNA, and the answer may surprise you.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Goldfish only remember things for three seconds.

Truth: Experiments show goldfish can remember things for weeks or months. They can be trained to push levers, follow colours and swim to a spot at feeding time.
