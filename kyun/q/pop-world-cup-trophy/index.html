What is the football World Cup trophy made of?

The FIFA World Cup trophy contains nearly 5 kg of 18-carat gold, yet the winners only get to keep a gold-plated copy.

The short answer

The FIFA World Cup trophy is made of 18-carat gold, with two green bands of a mineral called malachite around its base. It stands 36.8 cm tall, about the height of a school ruler and a bit, and weighs about 6.2 kg, as heavy as a big watermelon. Around 4.9 kg of that is gold.

It shows two human figures spiralling upwards and holding up the Earth. The Italian sculptor Silvio Gazzaniga designed it, and it has been used since 1974. Winners only borrow it for the celebration, then FIFA takes it back and gives them a gold-plated copy.

Surprising but true

The first World Cup trophy was stolen in England in 1966 and found a week later by a dog called Pickles, sniffing under a hedge in a London garden.

What 18-carat means

Pure gold is called 24-carat. It is beautiful but soft enough to scratch with a fingernail. Eighteen-carat gold is 18 parts gold to 6 parts other metals, such as copper and silver, mixed into an alloy. That makes it much harder and better at surviving a dressing room full of excited players.

Some scientists have calculated that if the trophy were solid gold, it would weigh far more than 6.2 kg. So it is believed to be hollow, though FIFA hasn't said. The gold in it may have come from mines anywhere in the world.

The first trophy and the dog

Before 1974, teams played for a different trophy, made of gold-plated silver and named after Jules Rimet, the Frenchman who started the World Cup. It was smaller, showing a winged figure of Victory.

In March 1966, a few months before England hosted the World Cup, the Jules Rimet Trophy was stolen from an exhibition in London. A week later, a black-and-white dog called Pickles was out for a walk with his owner in south London. He sniffed at a parcel wrapped in newspaper under a hedge. Inside was the trophy. Pickles became a national hero and even appeared in a film.

Stolen again, never found

Brazil won the Jules Rimet Trophy for a third time in 1970 and was allowed to keep it for ever. In 1983, thieves broke into the Brazilian football federation's offices in Rio de Janeiro and stole it. It has never been found. Many people believe it was melted down for its gold.

That is one reason FIFA guards today's trophy so carefully. When it tours the world before each tournament, it travels with security staff and is kept under glass.

Room for more winners

Underneath the base, the names of the winning countries are engraved on a plate, starting with West Germany in 1974. There is room for winners up to 2038. After that, FIFA will have to change the plate or find another solution.

Argentina won in 2022, with Lionel Messi lifting the trophy in Qatar. The 2026 World Cup, played across the USA, Canada and Mexico, was the first with 48 teams. Spain beat Argentina 1–0 in the final in New Jersey, on 19 July 2026.

Why malachite?

Malachite is a copper mineral with swirling bands of green, the colour of a football pitch. It has been polished into jewellery and decoration for thousands of years, and Russian palaces once used it for whole columns and tables. On the trophy, it is the only part that isn't gold.

India and the trophy

India has never played at a men's World Cup, though it qualified in 1950 and did not go. Even so, millions of fans in Kolkata, Kerala and Goa, the country's football heartlands, stay up past midnight to watch the final. Few sporting prizes are guarded so closely. Its only real rivals for security are royal treasures such as the Crown Jewels in London, kept behind bombproof glass.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The country that wins the World Cup takes home the original gold trophy.

Truth: FIFA keeps the original. At the ceremony, the winning team holds the real one, but takes home a gold-plated bronze replica called the FIFA World Cup Winner's Trophy.
