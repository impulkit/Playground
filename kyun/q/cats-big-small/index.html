How are big cats and house cats alike?

A tiger and a house cat both bury their droppings, sharpen their claws on trees and rub their scent on things they claim as theirs.

The short answer

Big cats and house cats belong to the same family, the Felidae. They share an ancestor that lived millions of years ago, so they are built in much the same way. All cats walk on their toes, have sharp curved claws, eyes that see well in dim light and teeth made for slicing meat.

They also behave alike. A tiger in Ranthambore and a cat on a sofa in Chennai both stalk, pounce, groom their fur with a rough tongue and nap for long parts of the day. The biggest differences are size, how they make sounds and how they live with people. A big male tiger can weigh about fifty times as much as a house cat.

Surprising but true

Lions and tigers can roar but cannot purr steadily, while house cats can purr but cannot roar, because their throats are built differently.

Same body plan

Every cat is a meat eater. Their tongues are covered in tiny backward-pointing hooks that scrape meat off bones and comb their fur. Their back teeth work like scissors, not grinders, so they slice food rather than chew it.

Most cats can pull their claws in to keep them sharp. That is why a house cat scratches a sofa, and a tiger scrapes a tree trunk. Both are sharpening claws and leaving scent from glands in their paws. The cheetah is the odd one out. Its claws stay partly out, like a sprinter's spiked shoes.

Roar or purr

Here the cat family splits. Lions, tigers, leopards and jaguars can roar. A lion's roar can be heard about 8 kilometres away. Their throats have a more flexible set of small bones and large, thick vocal folds that make deep, loud sounds.

Small cats, including house cats, have stiffer throat bones and smaller vocal folds. They cannot roar, but they can purr steadily, breathing in and out. Big cats can't purr in the same way. The snow leopard belongs with the big cats but can't roar at all, and the cheetah chirps like a bird.

How does your voice work?Every word you speak starts as two small folds of tissue in your throat, slapping together hundreds of times a second.

Hunting the same way

Most cats hunt alone, by stalking and ambush. They creep up low, freeze, then burst forward. A house cat watching a sparrow and a leopard watching a deer go through almost the same routine. Even the tail twitch and the little wiggle of the back legs before the pounce are the same. Both cats are checking their grip and balance before they spring.

Lions are the exception. They live in groups called prides, and the females often hunt together. India's Asiatic lions in Gir live in smaller groups than African lions, but they still share food and raise cubs together.

India, a country of cats

India has about 15 kinds of wild cat, more than any other country. The big ones are the tiger, Asiatic lion, leopard, snow leopard and clouded leopard. The small ones include the jungle cat, fishing cat, leopard cat, caracal and desert cat.

The rusty-spotted cat, found in India and Sri Lanka, is among the smallest cats in the world, weighing about a kilogram. In 2022 India counted about 3,700 tigers, around three-quarters of all wild tigers. Cheetahs died out in India by the 1950s, and in 2022 the country began bringing them in from Africa to Kuno National Park.

How house cats came to live with us

House cats come from the African wildcat. About 10,000 years ago, when people in the Middle East began storing grain, mice moved in, and wildcats followed the mice. People seem to have welcomed them.

A grave in Cyprus, about 9,500 years old, holds a person buried with a cat. Later, the ancient Egyptians kept cats in homes and temples. Today there are hundreds of millions of house cats. Under the fur, each one is still a small hunter.

Myth or fact?

Myth: All big cats roar.

Truth: Only four cats roar properly, the lion, tiger, leopard and jaguar. The snow leopard is a big cat but cannot roar. Cheetahs and pumas chirp, hiss and purr instead.

Try this

If you have a cat or can safely watch one, keep a five-minute "cat diary". Note every time it stretches, licks its paws, rubs its face on something or stalks a toy. Then watch a short wildlife video of a tiger or leopard and tick off which of the same moves you see.
