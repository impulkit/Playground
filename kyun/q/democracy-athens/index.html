How did democracy begin in Athens?

In ancient Athens, many officials were chosen by a machine that worked like a lottery, because the Athenians thought elections favoured the rich.

The short answer

Around 508 BCE, in the ancient Greek city-state of Athens, and after years of rule by powerful families and tyrants, a leader named Cleisthenes reorganised Athens. He gave power to an assembly of ordinary male citizens, who met on a hillside to debate and vote on laws, war, peace and taxes. The Athenians called this demokratia, from demos, the people, and kratos, power.

Unlike most democracies today, Athens was direct. Citizens did not elect representatives to decide for them. They voted on decisions themselves. And many officials were picked by lot, a random draw, so that any citizen might end up in charge for a while.

Surprising but true

Athenians could vote to send a citizen away from the city for ten years by scratching his name on a piece of broken pottery.

From tyrants to the people

Before democracy, Athens was ruled by rich landowners, and at times by a single strongman called a tyrant. Poor farmers fell into debt and some were sold into slavery. In 594 BCE a lawmaker named Solon cancelled debts and banned enslaving Athenians for debt.

After the last tyrant was thrown out, Cleisthenes went further. He split citizens into ten new tribes mixed from different parts of the region, so that old family loyalties mattered less. Each tribe sent 50 men, chosen by lot, to a Council of 500 that prepared business for the assembly.

A meeting on a hill

The assembly met about 40 times a year on the Pnyx, a rocky hill facing the Acropolis. Any citizen could come, and some votes needed at least 6,000 to be valid. Anyone could stand up and speak. Votes were usually by a show of hands, and officials judged which side won by eye.

Athens may have had around 30,000 adult male citizens in the 400s BCE. That is fewer than the number of people who can sit in a large cricket stadium today. In India's general election in 2024, by comparison, more than 60 crore people cast a vote.

Choosing by lottery

Athenians believed elections favoured the rich and the famous. Picking officials by chance felt fairer, because it gave every citizen the same odds. In the language of probability, each name had an equal chance.

They even built a machine for it: the kleroterion, a stone slab with rows of slots. Citizens slotted in bronze name tickets. Coloured balls dropped through a tube decided which rows were chosen. Jurors for trials were picked this way too, and juries could have hundreds of members. In 399 BCE, a jury of about 500 found the philosopher Socrates guilty, and sentenced him to death.

Generals, though, were elected, because the city needed skill, not luck, to win wars.

Sending someone away

Once a year, citizens could vote to ostracise someone they thought was becoming too powerful. Each voter scratched a name on a pottery shard. If at least 6,000 votes were cast, the man with the most had to leave Athens for ten years. He kept his property and could return afterwards. It was a way to remove a threat without a trial or bloodshed.

Who was left out

Athenian democracy had big limits. Women could not vote. Neither could the many enslaved people whose work kept the city running, or foreigners who lived there for generations. Critics at the time, including Plato, thought crowds could be swayed by clever speakers into bad decisions.

What came after

Athens lost its independence to Macedonia in 322 BCE, and its democracy ended. Other ancient peoples also had councils and assemblies. In India, around 2,500 years ago, some states such as the Licchavis of Vaishali were ruled by assemblies of leading families, which some historians call republics.

Modern democracies, from India's Parliament to the US Congress, mostly use elected representatives instead of mass meetings. But traces of Athens remain. Jury service, and the gram sabha, where villagers meet to discuss local matters, both carry the old idea that ordinary people can decide.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In ancient Athens, everyone could vote.

Truth: Only free adult men born to Athenian parents could take part. Women, enslaved people and foreigners living in Athens were left out, so perhaps only one person in five or fewer had a vote.

Run a class decision two ways. First, hold a vote on a question, such as what game to play. Then choose a small "council" of five by drawing names from a jar, and let them decide the same question after discussing it. Talk about which way felt fairer.
