Why did the Beatles go to Rishikesh?

In 1968, the most famous band in the world turned up at an ashram by the Ganga.

The short answer

The Beatles were a band from Liverpool, England, who became the most famous group of the 1960s. By 1968 they were exhausted by fame, screaming crowds and the sudden death of their manager. They had become interested in meditation, and they travelled to Rishikesh, in today's Uttarakhand, to study it with a teacher called Maharishi Mahesh Yogi.

George Harrison had already fallen in love with Indian music. Through him, millions of young people around the world heard the sitar for the first time. Their trip to Rishikesh made many people curious about India, yoga and meditation.

Surprising but true

The Beatles wrote around 40 songs in just a few weeks in Rishikesh.

A sitar on a pop record

In 1965, during a film shoot, Harrison picked up a sitar that was lying around the set. He taught himself enough to play a tune on the Beatles' song "Norwegian Wood". It was one of the first times millions of pop fans heard one.

He wanted to learn properly. In 1966 he travelled to India to study with the great sitar player Ravi Shankar, who told him that mastering the instrument takes many years. Harrison never became a concert sitarist, but he kept bringing Indian instruments and ideas into Beatles songs, such as "Within You Without You" in 1967.

Life at the ashram

The Beatles arrived in Rishikesh in February 1968 with their wives and partners. The ashram sat on a hillside above the Ganga, with stone cottages among the trees. Other famous visitors joined them, including the folk singer Donovan, a member of the Beach Boys, and the actor Mia Farrow and her sister Prudence.

They meditated for hours, attended lectures, and ate simple vegetarian food. Prudence Farrow meditated so much, hardly leaving her room, that John Lennon wrote a song asking her to come out and play: "Dear Prudence".

A burst of songs

With no TV, no concerts and no fans outside, the Beatles had time to write. They sat on rooftops and verandas with acoustic guitars, and Donovan taught Lennon and McCartney a new way of picking the strings. In a few weeks they wrote around 40 songs.

Many ended up on their next album, simply called The Beatles but known as the "White Album" for its plain white cover, released in November 1968. Others appeared later on other records.

The group left at different times. Ringo Starr went home first. Lennon and Harrison stayed until April, leaving after a falling-out with the Maharishi, whose details are still disputed.

East meets West

In the 1960s, many young people in Europe and America became curious about Indian music, yoga and philosophy. The Beatles' interest made that curiosity even bigger. Ravi Shankar played sitar at huge Western music festivals, including Woodstock in 1969, and taught many musicians.

In 1971, Harrison and Shankar organised the Concert for Bangladesh in New York, to raise money for refugees from war in what is now Bangladesh. It was one of the first big charity concerts of its kind. Shankar helped make Indian classical music and its ragas famous around the world.

The Beatles Ashram today

After the Maharishi left, the ashram was abandoned and the forest grew back over it. It now lies inside Rajaji Tiger Reserve, and was opened to visitors in 2015. Artists have covered its crumbling walls with murals of the band and of Indian gods. Below it, the Ganga still flows out of the Himalaya, cold and fast, as it did in 1968.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The sitar is just an Indian guitar.

Truth: A sitar has a long neck with curved, movable frets, a gourd for a body, and two sets of strings. Besides the strings that are played, it has around 11 to 13 "sympathetic" strings underneath that ring by themselves, giving it a shimmering sound no guitar makes.

Try this

Stretch two rubber bands of the same size around an open shoebox. Pluck one hard and watch the other closely. If they are tuned alike, the second may start to shiver on its own. A sitar uses the same idea, with extra strings that ring without being plucked.
