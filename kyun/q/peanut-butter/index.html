Who invented peanut butter?

Many people credit the scientist George Washington Carver with inventing peanut butter. He didn't, and the real story starts with the Aztecs.

The short answer

Nobody invented peanut butter all at once. People in South and Central America, including the Aztecs, were grinding roasted peanuts into paste centuries ago. In 1884, a Canadian named Marcellus Gilmore Edson took out a patent on a peanut paste made by grinding roasted peanuts between heated surfaces.

In the 1890s, the American doctor John Harvey Kellogg made nut butters for patients at his health resort. Soon after, inventors built machines to grind peanuts in large amounts. Peanut butter was sold to crowds at the 1904 World's Fair in St. Louis, and it never looked back. The scientist George Washington Carver is often named as its inventor, but he was not.

Surprising but true

A peanut flower blooms above the ground, then its stalk bends down and pushes into the soil, where the peanut grows in the dark.

A strange way to grow

Peanuts are not really nuts. They are legumes, relatives of peas, beans and the many kinds of dal eaten in India. The plant is a low, leafy bush with small yellow flowers.

After a flower is pollinated, something odd happens. Its stalk grows longer, bends downwards and pushes the young pod into the soil. The peanuts grow underground, which is why they are also called groundnuts. Farmers dig the whole plant up to harvest them.

From the Andes to the world

Peanuts were first farmed in South America, probably around today's Bolivia and northern Argentina, thousands of years ago. Spanish and Portuguese sailors carried them to Africa, Asia and Europe in the 1500s.

India took to them quickly. It is now the world's second-biggest peanut grower after China, with Gujarat growing the most. Peanuts go into poha, chikki, chutney, sabudana khichdi and groundnut oil, and roasted peanuts in paper cones are sold at beaches and railway stations everywhere.

What is a patent?Abraham Lincoln is the only US president to hold a patent, for a way of lifting riverboats over sandbanks. It was never built.

The real story of Carver

George Washington Carver was born into slavery in Missouri in the 1860s and became a scientist and teacher at the Tuskegee Institute in Alabama. Cotton farming had worn out the soil of the American South. Carver urged farmers to also grow peanuts and other legumes, which put nitrogen back into the soil.

To give farmers a market for their crop, he searched for new uses. He developed or popularised hundreds of peanut products and recipes, from dyes to milk substitutes. He became so famous for peanuts that people assumed he must have invented peanut butter too. In 1921 he even spoke to a committee of the US Congress about the many uses of the peanut.

Spreading smoothly

Early peanut butter had a problem: the oil separated and floated to the top, and it could go rancid. In the 1920s, a businessman named Joseph Rosefield found a way to keep the oil mixed in by adding a small amount of hardened oil. This made the smooth, creamy jars sold in American supermarkets. Today's "natural" peanut butters skip the hardened oil, which is why you have to stir them before spreading.

Peanut butter and jelly sandwiches became an American school-lunch classic. During the Second World War, American soldiers carried peanut butter in their rations. In India, peanut butter has become far more common in recent years, and many jars on the shelves are made from Indian groundnuts.

A word of caution

For most people peanuts are a cheap, healthy source of protein. But for some, even a tiny amount can cause a serious allergic reaction. That is why many schools in the United States, and some in India, have strict rules about peanuts. If a friend has a food allergy, never share food without checking with an adult first.

Myth or fact?

Myth: George Washington Carver invented peanut butter.

Truth: Peanut pastes are much older, and a peanut paste was patented in 1884, when Carver was a young man. Carver became famous for finding hundreds of other uses for peanuts and for helping poor Southern farmers grow them.

Try this

If nobody at home has a peanut allergy, ask an adult for a handful of peanuts in their shells. Crack a few open and look at the papery pod, the thin red skin and the two halves of each seed. Find the tiny bump at one end of a seed. It is the baby plant, ready to sprout.
