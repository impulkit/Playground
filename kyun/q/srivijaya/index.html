What was the Srivijaya empire?

For about 600 years, a Buddhist kingdom on the island of Sumatra controlled one of the busiest sea lanes on Earth, and the world almost forgot it existed.

The short answer

Srivijaya was a sea-trading kingdom based on the island of Sumatra, in today's Indonesia. From about the 7th century to the 13th century, it controlled the narrow waters between Sumatra and the Malay Peninsula. Almost every ship sailing between India and China had to pass that way.

Srivijaya didn't need huge armies on land. It used a strong fleet to keep pirates away, made ships stop at its ports and charged them for the privilege. In return, traders got safe harbours, fresh water, and a market where Chinese silk, Indian cloth and island spices changed hands.

It was also a great centre of Buddhism, where monks from China stopped to study on their way to India.

Surprising but true

Srivijaya was largely forgotten until 1918, when the French scholar George Cœdès matched old inscriptions with Chinese records and showed it had been a great power.

A kingdom of water

Look at a map and you'll see why it worked. Ships from the Bay of Bengal heading for China had to squeeze through the Strait of Malacca, or the Sunda Strait further south. Both were within Srivijaya's reach.

Sailors also had to wait for the monsoon winds to change before going on. That meant weeks or months in port, buying food, repairing ships and trading. Srivijaya's harbours, probably near today's city of Palembang, filled with boats from Arabia, India and China. Srivijaya also sold goods from Sumatra's own forests and hills, such as camphor, sweet-smelling resins and gold. Rivers carried them down to the coast.

The earliest stone inscription that names Srivijaya, found near Palembang, dates to 682 CE. It is written in Old Malay using an Indian-style script, and it tells of a king leading thousands of soldiers on a holy journey.

A monk's long stay

In 671, a Chinese monk named Yijing stopped in Srivijaya on his way to India. He found more than a thousand Buddhist monks there, and he advised other Chinese pilgrims to study Sanskrit in Srivijaya before going on to India.

Yijing later spent years at Nalanda, the great Buddhist university in Bihar. Then he returned to Srivijaya and stayed for years more, copying and translating texts.

The link with Nalanda was close. In the 9th century, a king named Balaputradeva, from the Sailendra family that ruled Srivijaya, paid for a monastery at Nalanda. A copper plate issued by Devapala, a king of Bengal, records the gift.

Rivals at sea

Wealth brought enemies. Around 1025, the Tamil king Rajendra I sent the Chola navy across the Bay of Bengal. His inscriptions list a string of Srivijayan ports that his forces attacked, and say he captured the king.

Historians still argue about why. Some think the Cholas wanted to protect their own merchants' trade with China. The raid didn't destroy Srivijaya, but it left the kingdom weaker.

Fading from memory

Over the next centuries, rival ports grew, Javanese kingdoms became stronger, and Chinese merchants began sailing direct to more ports. By the late 13th century, Srivijaya had faded.

Because its buildings were mostly made of wood, little was left to see. The name nearly vanished. In 1918, the French scholar George Cœdès matched Chinese records of a kingdom called "Shilifoshi" with old Sumatran inscriptions and showed they were the same place.

What it left behind

Srivijaya's story lives on in the monuments of its neighbours and relatives. The Sailendra rulers in Java built Borobudur, the largest Buddhist monument in the world, around the 8th and 9th centuries. Divers have found wrecks of trading ships in Indonesian waters full of Chinese pottery, the same kind of cargo that once passed through Srivijaya's ports. The Malay language, now spoken by millions, grew in part from the Old Malay of its inscriptions.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Indian kings ruled Southeast Asia as colonies in ancient times.

Truth: Apart from raids like the Chola one, Southeast Asian kingdoms such as Srivijaya were ruled by local kings. They chose to adopt ideas from India, including Buddhism, Sanskrit words and styles of art, through trade and learning.

Try this

Find the Strait of Malacca on a map or globe. Use a piece of string to trace a sea route from Kolkata or Chennai to Guangzhou in China. Notice how the string has to squeeze through the narrow gap between Sumatra and Malaysia, and you'll see why whoever held that gap held the trade.
