How did a mouldy dish lead to antibiotics?

In 1928 a scientist came back from holiday to a messy lab, spotted a blob of mould, and changed medicine for ever.

The short answer

In September 1928, Alexander Fleming returned to his lab at St Mary's Hospital in London after a summer holiday. He had left dishes of bacteria on his bench. On one of them, a fuzzy green mould had grown, and around it the bacteria had died, leaving a clear ring.

Fleming worked out that the mould, called Penicillium, was making a substance that killed bacteria. He named it penicillin. It was the first antibiotic: a medicine that kills bacteria or stops them growing, without harming the person taking it.

But Fleming could not make enough of it to use as a medicine. That took another twelve years and a team of other scientists.

Surprising but true

Before penicillin, a scratch from a rose thorn could become a deadly infection, and doctors had almost nothing to stop it.

From a blob of mould to a medicine

At Oxford University in the late 1930s, Howard Florey and Ernst Chain picked up Fleming's forgotten paper. With the chemist Norman Heatley, they found ways to grow the mould and squeeze out tiny amounts of pure penicillin. Their lab used bathtubs, milk churns and bedpans as growing trays.

In 1941 they treated their first patient, a policeman named Albert Alexander with a badly infected face. He began to recover within days. Then the penicillin ran out, even though they were collecting it from his urine to reuse, and he died. The drug clearly worked. The problem was making enough.

A war and a mouldy melon

During the Second World War, wounded soldiers were dying from infected injuries, so the USA and Britain raced to mass-produce penicillin. At a government lab in Peoria, Illinois, researchers hunted for better moulds. The winner came from a mouldy cantaloupe bought at a local market. It made far more penicillin than Fleming's mould.

By the end of the war, factories were producing billions of doses. Fleming, Florey and Chain shared the Nobel Prize in 1945.

India set up its own factory, Hindustan Antibiotics at Pimpri near Pune, in 1954, so that the country could make penicillin instead of importing it.

How antibiotics work

Bacteria are living cells, much smaller than your own, and you can only see them through a microscope. Many antibiotics attack something bacteria have and human cells don't. Penicillin, for example, stops bacteria building their tough outer wall, so they burst.

A virus is different. It is much smaller, has no cell wall, and hides inside your own cells. That is why antibiotics do nothing for a cold or the flu.

Most bacteria are harmless, and many help you. Your gut is home to trillions of friendly microbes, and antibiotics can knock them out too, which is why they sometimes upset your stomach.

The germs fight back

In his Nobel lecture, Fleming warned that bacteria could become resistant if people took too little penicillin, or took it when they didn't need it. He was right.

Every time an antibiotic is used, a few bacteria may survive because of small differences in their genes. Those survivors multiply. Over years, some infections have become very hard to treat. The World Health Organization calls antibiotic resistance one of the biggest health threats today.

This is why doctors ask you to take antibiotics exactly as prescribed, and why they won't give them for a virus. Scientists are now searching soil, oceans and even insects for new antibiotics, the same way Fleming's mould was found: by noticing something odd and asking why.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Antibiotics cure colds and flu.

Truth: Colds and flu are caused by viruses, and antibiotics don't work on viruses. Rest, fluids and time are what help most, and a doctor can tell you when something else is needed.

Try this

Mix 18 white beans and 2 dark beans in a bowl. White beans are bacteria an antibiotic can kill, dark beans are resistant ones. Give a "dose" by taking out all the white beans, then let the survivors multiply by adding one matching bean for each bean left. Repeat a few times and watch what takes over the bowl.
