Why does the RBI try to keep inflation near 4%?

India's central bank has a promise written into law. It must try to keep price rises close to 4% a year, and if it misses for too long, it has to write to the government and explain why.

The short answer

The Reserve Bank of India, or RBI, tries to keep inflation near 4% because that is low enough to protect people's money, but not so low that the economy stalls. Inflation means the general rise in prices over a year, as the story of why prices go up explains.

The law gives the RBI a target of 4%, with a safe band from 2% to 6%. When inflation goes above 6%, as it did in October 2026, the RBI usually raises interest rates to cool things down.

Surprising but true

If prices rise by 6% every year, they double in about 12 years. At 4%, they take about 18 years to double. That small-looking gap makes a big difference to a family's savings.

How it works

First, India has to measure inflation. Every month, government workers note the prices of hundreds of things, from rice, milk and onions to bus fares, school fees, medicines and haircuts, in cities and villages across the country. Using statistics, these are combined into one number called the Consumer Price Index. Things families spend more on, like food, count for more in the average. The yearly change in this index is the inflation rate, written as a percentage.

Then the RBI acts, mainly through the repo rate. This is the interest rate at which the RBI lends money to banks for short periods.

  • 1. Prices rise too fast. The RBI's Monetary Policy Committee, a group of six people, raises the repo rate.
  • 2. Banks follow. Borrowing from the RBI costs banks more, so they charge more on loans for homes, cars and businesses, and often pay more on savings.
  • 3. Spending slows. People borrow and buy a little less, and companies expand more carefully.
  • 4. Prices cool. With less rush to buy, shops and makers raise prices more slowly.

When inflation is too low and the economy is weak, the RBI does the opposite and cuts the repo rate.

It works a bit like the regulator on a gas stove. Turn it up and things heat faster. Turn it down and they cool. But the change is slow, so the RBI has to guess where prices will be months ahead.

Why 4% and not zero?

Zero might sound perfect, but a little inflation is healthy. If prices were falling, people might wait to buy, hoping things get cheaper, and businesses would struggle. Workers' wages are also hard to cut, so a gentle rise in prices gives the economy room to adjust.

On the other hand, high inflation hurts. It eats away the value of savings and hits poor families hardest, because so much of their money goes on food and fuel. Experts chose 4% for India as a middle path for a fast-growing country, while many rich countries aim for 2%.

Where it came from

The RBI was founded on 1 April 1935, and became fully owned by the government in 1949. For decades, it tried to balance many goals at once, such as growth, jobs, the rupee and prices. In some years, inflation was very high. In the 1970s, oil shocks pushed price rises above 20% for a while.

New Zealand became the first country to set a clear inflation target, in 1990, and many others followed. In India, an RBI committee recommended a 4% target in 2014. In 2016, the law was changed. It set up the Monetary Policy Committee and made the 4% target, with the 2% to 6% band, official. If inflation stays outside the band for three quarters in a row, the RBI must write to the government explaining why and what it will do.

India's story

For several years after 2016, inflation mostly stayed inside the band, though it jumped during the pandemic and when food or fuel prices spiked. In late 2025, inflation fell very low, then began climbing again in 2026.

By October 2026, a weak monsoon had hurt crops, and a war in West Asia earlier in the year had made oil much dearer. Food prices rose 8.94% in a year. Onions cost more than double, and garlic, ginger and lemons jumped too. Overall inflation reached 6.09%, above the top of the band for the first time in two years.

The RBI had already raised the repo rate to 5.50% in October 2026, its first increase in nearly four years, and signalled it might go further.

The numbers that matter

  • - 4%: India's inflation target, with a safe band of 2% to 6%.
  • - 6.09%: inflation in October 2026, just over the top of that band.
  • - 8.94%: food inflation in October 2026. Food matters most for poorer families.
  • - 6 members: the Monetary Policy Committee, three from the RBI and three experts named by the government.
  • - 12 years versus 18 years: roughly how long prices take to double at 6% and at 4% inflation.

What people argue about

Raising interest rates can slow inflation, but it also makes home loans and business loans costlier, which can slow growth and jobs. Some economists say the RBI must act firmly to protect savers and the poor. Others say much of today's inflation comes from weather and world events, like a poor harvest or costly oil, which higher interest rates cannot fix. They argue that better storage, transport and supply would help more.

There is also debate about the target itself. Some think 4% is right for India. Others suggest a slightly different target or band.

Myths and mix-ups

"The RBI decides the price of onions." It does not set any shop price. It can only influence how much people borrow and spend.

"Inflation of 6% means prices rose 6% this month." It compares prices with the same month a year earlier.

"Low inflation means prices are falling." Low inflation means prices are rising slowly. Falling prices are called deflation.

What it means for you

Inflation decides how much your family's money can buy. If your pocket money stays the same but prices rise 6%, you can buy a little less each year. When the RBI raises rates, a family with a home loan may pay a bigger monthly instalment, while savers may earn more on fixed deposits.

Learning how money grows with interest and what banks do with your money helps you see why one number, announced each month, is watched by everyone from shopkeepers to the government.

Myth or fact?

Myth: When inflation is 6%, every price has gone up by 6%.

Truth: Inflation is an average over hundreds of goods and services. Some prices, like onions in a bad season, may double, while others, like phones, may even fall. The 6% is the overall change compared with a year earlier.

Try this

Ask an adult what a packet of milk, a kilo of onions and a cinema ticket cost a year ago, and what they cost now. Work out the percentage rise for each. Are they all the same? Inflation is an average of many such changes.
