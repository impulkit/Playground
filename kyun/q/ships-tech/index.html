How do giant container ships work?

The biggest container ships are about 400 metres long, longer than four football fields end to end, and can carry over 24,000 steel boxes.

The short answer

A container ship is a floating warehouse built around one idea: put everything in identical steel boxes. Shoes, phones, bananas and car parts all travel in containers of the same standard sizes. Cranes can lift any of them, trucks and trains can carry any of them, and ships can stack them like building blocks.

The biggest ships today are about 400 metres long and 61 metres wide. They can carry more than 24,000 TEU, the unit for one twenty-foot container. Stacked below and above deck, the boxes tower higher than a ten-storey building.

A single giant diesel engine turns one enormous propeller. The ship crosses oceans at around 30 to 40 km an hour, with a crew small enough to fit in a school classroom.

Surprising but true

A ship that carries more than 24,000 containers often sails with a crew of only about 20 to 30 people.

The box that changed everything

Before containers, cargo was loaded piece by piece: sacks, crates and barrels carried by dock workers. Loading a ship could take a week, and goods were often lost or stolen.

In 1956, an American trucking businessman named Malcom McLean had a better idea. He fitted an old tanker, the Ideal X, to carry 58 truck trailer boxes from New Jersey to Texas. Loading took hours, not days.

Containers soon became standard sizes. Today, cranes at a big port can move a container every minute or two. Every box has its own ID code, so computers can track it from factory to shop. Containers made moving goods across oceans so cheap that they changed where things are made. A shirt can be sewn in Bangladesh and sold in Texas a few weeks later.

Floating, steering, stopping

A steel ship floats because its hull pushes aside a huge weight of water, much more than the steel itself. Ballast tanks inside fill with seawater or empty out to keep the ship level as boxes are loaded and unloaded.

At the front, just under the water, many big ships have a rounded bulb. This bulbous bow makes its own small wave that cancels part of the ship's main bow wave, so the ship slides through the water using less fuel.

Stopping is the slow part. A fully loaded giant can take several kilometres to come to a halt, so captains plan turns and stops long in advance. Near ports, tugboats help push and pull. A local pilot, who knows every sandbank, usually comes aboard to guide the ship in.

When one ship blocked the world

In March 2021, the 400-metre Ever Given turned sideways in a sandstorm and got stuck across the Suez Canal. For six days, hundreds of ships queued at both ends. Diggers, dredgers and tugs finally worked it free at a high tide.

The jam showed how much the world depends on these ships. Experts at the United Nations estimate that around 80% of world trade by volume travels by sea.

Giants come to India

India's big container ports include JNPA near Mumbai and Mundra in Gujarat. In June 2025, the MSC Irina, one of the largest container ships ever built, docked at the new Vizhinjam port near Thiruvananthapuram in Kerala. Vizhinjam has deep water close to shore, so giant ships can berth there without long channels being dug.

Cleaner seas ahead

Container ships burn a lot of fuel, mostly heavy oil, and shipping produces about 3% of the world's greenhouse gases. To cut that, some new ships run on liquefied gas or methanol. Engineers are testing giant sails and kites, and many ships already practise "slow steaming", sailing slower to save fuel. Even a few knots make a big difference when your ship weighs as much as a small mountain of steel.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A ship as heavy as a container ship should sink, because steel is heavier than water.

Truth: A ship floats because its hull is a hollow shape full of air. It pushes aside a mass of water heavier than itself, and the water pushes back up. A solid steel block would sink, but the same steel shaped into a hull floats.

Try this

Fill a tray or a bathtub with water and float an empty plastic box, like a lunch box. Add coins one at a time and watch it sink lower. Count how many it holds before water comes over the edge. Then try spreading the coins evenly versus piling them on one side, and see what happens to the balance.
