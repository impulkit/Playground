What is the ozone layer?

If you squeezed all the ozone in the sky down to ground level, it would make a layer only about 3 mm thick. That thin shield protects all life on land.

The short answer

The ozone layer is a part of the atmosphere, mostly about 15 to 35 km above the ground, that contains more ozone than anywhere else. Ozone is a kind of oxygen. Ordinary oxygen has two oxygen atoms stuck together, but ozone has three.

The ozone layer absorbs most of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet light, especially the kind that causes sunburn and damages living cells. Without it, life on land would be much harder. In the 1980s, scientists discovered that chemicals made by people were thinning it. The world agreed to stop using them, and the layer is now slowly healing.

Surprising but true

The Montreal Protocol, the treaty that protects the ozone layer, was the first treaty in UN history to be ratified, or formally accepted, by every country in the world (in 2009).

A thin shield

Ozone is spread thinly through the air, mixed with much more ordinary oxygen and nitrogen. If you could gather all the ozone above your head and squeeze it to the pressure at ground level, it would make a layer only about 3 mm thick.

Yet that small amount does a big job. When ultraviolet light from the Sun hits an ozone molecule, the molecule absorbs the energy and breaks apart. The pieces later join up again into new ozone. So the layer keeps absorbing ultraviolet light, over and over.

Too much ultraviolet light burns skin, damages eyes and harms plants and tiny sea creatures. That's why the ozone layer matters so much.

The chemicals that thinned it

In the late 1920s, chemists invented chlorofluorocarbons, or CFCs. They were safe to handle, didn't burn and didn't react with much, so they were used in fridges, air conditioners and spray cans.

In 1974, two chemists in California, Mario Molina and Sherwood Rowland, warned that CFCs would drift up into the high atmosphere. There, strong sunlight would break them apart and release chlorine. A single chlorine atom can destroy thousands of ozone molecules. Many people doubted them at first.

The hole over Antarctica

In 1985, three scientists from the British Antarctic Survey, Joseph Farman, Brian Gardiner and Jonathan Shanklin, reported something startling. Every southern spring, the ozone over Antarctica was dropping sharply, far more than expected.

Extremely cold clouds high over Antarctica speed up the chemical reactions that let chlorine destroy ozone. So each September and October, a large area of thin ozone forms. It became known as the ozone hole. In some years, it has been bigger than the whole of North America.

The world agrees

The discovery led to quick action. In 1987, countries signed the Montreal Protocol, agreeing to cut and then stop making CFCs and similar chemicals. India joined in 1992 and, like other developing countries, had stopped making CFCs by 2010.

In 2009, the Montreal Protocol became the first treaty in the history of the United Nations to be ratified by every country. In 1995, Molina, Rowland and the Dutch scientist Paul Crutzen won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for their work on ozone.

Healing slowly

CFCs last a long time in the air, often 50 to 100 years, so the damage takes decades to repair. Scientists from the UN and the World Meteorological Organization say the ozone layer is recovering. Over Antarctica, it should return to 1980 levels around 2066, and over most of the world, around 2040.

Many of the chemicals that replaced CFCs trap heat strongly, adding to climate change. In 2016, countries agreed to phase those down too.

Good up high, bad down low

Ozone in the high sky protects us. Ozone near the ground is a different story. It forms when sunlight cooks gases from car exhausts and factories, and it is part of smog. Breathing it can make coughing and asthma worse. So scientists sometimes say ozone is "good up high, bad nearby". The same gas can help or harm, depending on where it is. Ground-level ozone is one part of the wider problem of air pollution.

World Ozone Day is marked every year on 16 September, the day the Montreal Protocol was signed.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The ozone hole is a real hole in the sky that you could fly through.

Truth: It is an area where the ozone layer becomes much thinner, mostly over Antarctica in the southern spring. There is still some ozone there, just far less than normal.

Try this

Test the Sun's invisible light. On a sunny day, lay a sheet of dark coloured paper on a windowsill and put a few objects on it, such as a coin, a key and a leaf. Leave it in the sun for several days, then lift the objects off. The paper fades where the sunlight hit it, partly because of ultraviolet light, but stays darker under the objects.
