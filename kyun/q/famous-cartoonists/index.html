Who was R.K. Laxman and his Common Man?

For more than 50 years, a bald little man in a checked coat watched India's leaders on the front page, and never said a word.

The short answer

R.K. Laxman was one of India's best-loved cartoonists. Every day for more than half a century, he drew a small cartoon called You Said It on the front page of The Times of India. In a few lines of ink and one sentence of text, he made fun of politicians, potholes, power cuts and endless promises.

His most famous character was the Common Man: a short, balding man with glasses, a checked coat and a dhoti. He stood quietly at the side of the scene while important people talked. He stood for ordinary Indians who put up with whatever their leaders did next.

Surprising but true

R.K. Laxman was turned down by the J.J. School of Art in Bombay, which told him he did not have enough talent.

A boy who drew on the walls

Laxman grew up in Mysore, the youngest child in a large family that loved books. As a small boy he drew on walls, doors and floors: crows on a tree, his teachers, the neighbours. He later said that a British cartoonist in an old magazine, David Low, made him want to draw cartoons for a living.

When he applied to the J.J. School of Art in Bombay, he was rejected. He kept drawing anyway. He sent cartoons to newspapers, and drew pictures for the stories of his older brother, R.K. Narayan, including the tales of the town of Malgudi.

The Common Man

Laxman joined The Times of India in Bombay in 1947. In 1951 he began You Said It. According to Laxman, the Common Man arrived slowly. At first he drew crowds, then fewer people, until one figure was left watching.

He never gave the Common Man a name, a job or a home. That was the point. Anyone could see themselves in him: the clerk waiting for a bus, the shopkeeper hit by a new tax, the family whose street had not been repaired in years. Because the cartoon was small and sat on the front page, it was often the first thing a reader saw each morning. In 1988, India Post put him on a stamp, and a bronze statue of him stands in Pune.

Laughing at the powerful

A political cartoon works like a joke with a sting. It shows what a leader said next to what actually happened, and lets the reader notice the gap. In a democracy, that kind of teasing helps people question those in charge.

Laxman's cartoons were sharp but rarely cruel. He drew prime ministers for decades, starting with Jawaharlal Nehru. During the Emergency of 1975 to 1977, when newspapers were censored, cartoonists had to be careful about what they printed.

He also created a cheerful mascot. In 1954 he drew Gattu, the paintbrush-holding boy used for years in advertisements for Asian Paints.

Cartoonists around the world

India has other famous cartoonists. K. Shankar Pillai, known as Shankar, started Shankar's Weekly in 1948 and later founded a dolls museum in Delhi. Mario Miranda filled his drawings of Goa with crowds of tiny, funny people. Pran created Chacha Chaudhary, whose brain is said to work faster than a computer, a favourite of Indian comics readers.

In the USA, Charles Schulz drew the Peanuts comic strip, with Charlie Brown and his dog Snoopy, for about 50 years, from 1950 to 2000. Like Laxman, he drew nearly every strip himself. Both men showed that a few lines on paper, repeated every day, can become part of a country's daily life.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Cartoons are only for children.

Truth: Newspaper cartoons are mostly made for adults. They poke fun at leaders and point out problems in a few lines. Laxman's cartoons were read every morning by millions of grown-ups, including the politicians he teased.
