How was the Atal Tunnel dug?

Under the Pir Panjal mountains in Himachal Pradesh, a road tunnel 9 km long lets cars skip a pass that stays snowed in for about half the year.

The short answer

The Atal Tunnel runs for about 9 km under the Pir Panjal range of the Himalaya, near Manali in Himachal Pradesh. Before it opened, the road north crossed the Rohtang Pass, nearly 4,000 metres high. Snow and avalanches shut that road for about six months every year, cutting off the valleys of Lahaul.

To build the tunnel, crews drilled holes into the rock, packed them with explosives and blasted their way through, a few metres at a time. After each blast they sprayed concrete and fixed long steel bolts into the rock to stop the roof falling in.

It took about ten years. The tunnel opened on 3 October 2020 and is named after the former prime minister Atal Bihari Vajpayee.

Surprising but true

Engineers hid a whole emergency escape tunnel inside the main tunnel, running underneath the road.

Blast, clear, spray, repeat

The engineers used a method sometimes called the New Austrian Tunnelling Method. The idea is to let the rock help hold itself up. After each blast, workers cleared the broken stone. Then they sprayed a layer of concrete onto the fresh rock, and drove rock bolts deep into it, like giant nails pinning it together.

Instruments measured how the rock squeezed and moved. If it shifted too much, the crew added more support before going further. Later, a smooth concrete lining was added inside. You can see how other tunnels are built, from boring machines to underwater tubes.

Crews dug from the Manali side in the south and the Lahaul side in the north. Snow closed the northern end for months each winter, so most digging came from the south. On 15 October 2017, the two sides finally broke through to meet.

The trouble with Seri Nalla

The Himalaya is a young mountain range, still being pushed up as India presses into Asia. Its rock is often cracked and crushed. About halfway through, the diggers hit a fault zone under a stream called Seri Nalla.

Here the rock was broken into rubble, and water gushed in. Mud and loose stone poured into the tunnel. A stretch of only about 600 metres took years to get through. Engineers had to drain the water, support the loose ground with steel and concrete, and creep forward slowly.

That one zone is a big reason the tunnel opened later than first planned.

A safe road under a mountain

The Atal Tunnel is horseshoe-shaped, with one tube carrying two lanes of traffic. Beneath the road runs a separate escape tunnel. If there is a fire or crash, people can go down to this hidden passage and walk out safely.

Inside there are CCTV cameras every 250 metres, emergency phones every 150 metres and fire hydrants every 60 metres. Big fans keep fresh air moving, so car fumes don't build up. Lights brighten gradually near the exits, so drivers' eyes can adjust to the glare of snow outside.

At about 3,000 metres up, the air holds less oxygen than at sea level, and altitude affects people. Building here meant workers had to cope with cold, thin air and long winters.

Why it matters

The tunnel cuts the drive from Manali to Lahaul by about 46 km and saves up to four or five hours. Villages that were once snowed in for half the year can now reach hospitals, markets and schools in winter. The road continues on towards Ladakh.

In 2022, the tunnel was certified by the World Book of Records as the longest highway tunnel above 10,000 feet. It is a favourite stop for tourists, who pause at each end for photos of the mountains.

And the strangest part? Even longer tunnels run under the Alps and the sea, including one in Switzerland that stretches for about 57 km, as you can read in the world's longest tunnels.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Tunnels through mountains are simply drilled out by one giant machine.

Truth: Some tunnels use huge boring machines, but the Atal Tunnel was mostly dug by drilling holes, blasting rock with explosives and spraying concrete to support the walls, metre by metre.
