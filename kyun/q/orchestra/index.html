How does an orchestra play together?

Up to a hundred musicians, each reading a different part, start and stop together because of one person waving a stick.

The short answer

An orchestra plays together thanks to three things: written music, careful rehearsal and a conductor. Each musician reads a part showing exactly which notes to play and when. Over many rehearsals, the players learn how their parts fit together.

During the concert, the conductor stands at the front, facing the orchestra. With a thin stick called a baton, or just the hands, the conductor shows the beat, cues players to come in, and signals when to get louder or softer. Every musician can see the conductor, so up to a hundred people can breathe, start and stop as one.

Surprising but true

Musicians at the back of a big orchestra can hear the front a tiny moment late, so they follow the conductor's hands instead of their ears.

Four families of instruments

A symphony orchestra is arranged in sections. The biggest is the strings: violins, violas, cellos and double basses. They usually sit at the front, curved around the conductor.

Behind them sit the woodwinds, such as flutes, oboes, clarinets and bassoons. Further back are the brass: trumpets, horns, trombones and the tuba. At the very back is percussion, including the big timpani drums, cymbals and xylophone. Louder instruments sit further away so they don't drown out the quieter strings.

Tuning up

Before a concert, you hear a strange, swirling noise as the orchestra tunes. The oboe plays a single note, an A, and everyone adjusts their instruments to match it. String players turn pegs. Wind players slide parts of their instruments in or out.

Why the oboe? Its sound is bright and cuts through, and its pitch is steady. Many orchestras tune to an A that vibrates 440 times a second, though some prefer a little higher.

Why the conductor matters

Sound travels at about 340 metres a second. On a wide stage, the sound from a violin at the front takes a tiny fraction of a second to reach a drummer at the back. If the drummer played exactly with what he heard, he would always be slightly late. So musicians watch the conductor's hands, which reach everyone's eyes at once.

Until the 1800s, orchestras were small, and a keyboard player or the leading violinist kept time. As orchestras grew bigger and music more complicated, especially after Beethoven, a separate conductor became normal.

Reading the whole picture

Each player reads only a part. The conductor reads the full score, which shows every instrument's part stacked on the same page, like a giant timetable. A single page of score can have more than twenty lines of music. You can learn how it is written in music notation.

In rehearsal, the conductor might ask the trumpets to play more softly, or the cellos to stretch a phrase. Two conductors can lead the same piece and make it sound quite different. Some conductors are famous for tiny, precise movements. Others leap, crouch and sweep their arms, and both approaches can work.

Orchestras in India and America

The New York Philharmonic, founded in 1842, is the oldest orchestra in the USA. New York's Carnegie Hall opened in 1891, with the Russian composer Tchaikovsky conducting on its opening night.

India has its own story. The conductor Zubin Mehta, born in Mumbai, led the New York Philharmonic and the Israel Philharmonic for decades. In 2006, the Symphony Orchestra of India was founded in Mumbai. Orchestras also play behind many Indian film songs, and composers like A.R. Rahman have recorded with large string sections for their soundtracks.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The conductor just waves the beat, and the orchestra would play the same without them.

Truth: Much of a conductor's work happens in rehearsal, deciding how fast, loud or gentle each part should be and keeping the sections balanced. Small groups can play without a conductor, but large orchestras find one very helpful.

Try this

Gather a few friends and choose one to be the conductor. Everyone claps a steady beat while the conductor moves a hand up and down, speeding up and slowing down. Then try again with your eyes closed, listening only. Which way stays together better?
