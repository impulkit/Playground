Why are snail shells spirals?

A snail never moves house. It builds its shell one thin ring at a time, and a simple rule turns those rings into a spiral.

The short answer

A snail's shell grows with the snail. It doesn't swap it for a bigger one the way a hermit crab does. Instead, a soft layer of its body called the mantle lays down new shell along the rim of the opening. The new material is mostly calcium carbonate, the same mineral that makes chalk.

The trick is that the mantle adds a little more on one side than the other. Each new ring is slightly wider than the last and slightly twisted. Keep doing that for months and the tube curls around itself. The result is a spiral, with the oldest, tiniest part at the tip and the newest part at the mouth.

Surprising but true

About 9 in 10 snail species coil to the right. A left-coiling garden snail is so rare that one found in London in 2016 made the news around the world.

A spiral that keeps its shape

Mathematicians call this shape a logarithmic spiral. Its special property is that it grows without changing shape. A small piece near the tip looks just like a big piece near the opening, only scaled up.

That suits a growing animal well. A young snail and an old snail of the same kind have the same proportions, so the soft body inside always fits. Compare a coiled rope, where every turn sits the same distance from the last. Its outer turns would be far too tight for an animal that keeps getting bigger.

The tiny shell the snail hatched with, called the protoconch, stays at the very tip for life. In 1917 the Scottish biologist D'Arcy Thompson showed in his book On Growth and Form that horns, tusks and shells follow the same rule. Add at one end, never remodel the old part, and a spiral appears.

Left or right?

Hold most snail shells with the point up and the opening facing you, and the opening sits on the right. About 9 out of 10 snail species coil this way. Biologists call them dextral, or right-handed.

Left-handed snails do exist, but in a right-handed species they are very rare. In 2016 a left-coiling garden snail turned up in London. Scientists named him Jeremy and asked the public to find him a mate, because a left-coiler's body doesn't line up easily with a right-coiler's. Two more left-coilers were found. Their babies, and Jeremy's, all coiled to the right.

In India, the conch blown in temples, the shankha, is the shell of a sea snail from the Gulf of Mannar. A rare chank that coils the opposite way, called valampuri, is treasured.

Spirals all over the sea

Ammonites, relatives of squid that lived alongside the dinosaurs, built coiled shells too. So does the living nautilus, a cephalopod of the Indian and Pacific Oceans. Its shell is split into chambers, and the animal lives only in the newest, biggest one. It can float or sink by changing the gas and liquid in the older rooms.

What were ammonites, the spiral shells of the ancient seas?Ammonites floated through the seas for more than 300 million years, and in Nepal their fossils are honoured as sacred stones.

The same idea of growing at the edge shows up in sunflower heads and pine cones, which line up with the Fibonacci numbers. Nothing magical is going on. Anything that builds by adding new parts beside old ones tends to end up as a spiral.

The golden ratio question

You may read that shells follow the golden ratio, a number close to 1.618 that people like to find in art and buildings. It's a lovely idea, but the measurements disagree. A golden spiral widens by 1.618 every quarter turn. Real nautilus shells widen much more slowly than that.

Different snails simply use different growth rates. A tall, pointed tower snail stretches its spiral downwards like a drill bit. A flat ramshorn snail from a pond coils almost like a jalebi. They all follow the same rule. Only the numbers change.

You can see the rule in action on a shell you find. The lines running across it, from the rim back towards the tip, are growth lines. Each one marks an old edge of the opening, a bit like rings in a tree trunk. Count them and you are reading the snail's diary.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The nautilus shell is a perfect golden-ratio spiral.

Truth: Measurements of real nautilus shells show their spirals widen much more slowly than a golden spiral, and they vary from shell to shell. They are logarithmic spirals, but not golden ones.

Try this

Draw two spirals on paper. In the first, make each turn the same distance from the last, like a coiled rope. In the second, make each gap twice as wide as the one before. Hold them next to a real shell or a photo of one and decide which the snail is using.
