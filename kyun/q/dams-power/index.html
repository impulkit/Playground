How do dams make electricity?

Behind a big dam, a whole river waits. Let it fall through a pipe, and it can light up millions of homes.

The short answer

A dam is a giant wall across a river. Water piles up behind it into a deep lake called a reservoir. Near the bottom of the dam, big pipes called penstocks let water rush downhill. At the end of each pipe the water slams into a turbine, a wheel with curved blades, and spins it round very fast.

The turbine is joined by a shaft to a generator, where magnets spin past coils of wire and make electricity. The water then flows out at the bottom and carries on down the river. The higher the water falls and the more of it there is, the more power comes out.

Where does the energy really come from? The Sun. Its heat lifts water from the sea as vapour, which falls as rain and snow on high mountains. A hydropower station collects that water on its way back down, as part of the water cycle.

Surprising but true

In 1897, a small hydroelectric station opened at Sidrapong near Darjeeling, one of the first in Asia. It still runs today.

Water wheels to power stations

People have used falling water for over 2,000 years. Water wheels, a cousin of the wheel on a cart, ground grain, sawed wood and hammered iron. In 1882, the first hydroelectric power station to light homes opened on the Fox River in Appleton, Wisconsin, USA.

India was quick to follow. A small plant at Sidrapong near Darjeeling began supplying electricity in 1897, and it is still working. In 1902, a hydroelectric station at Shivanasamudra, on the Kaveri River in Karnataka, began sending power to the Kolar Gold Fields almost 150 km away.

Giant dams

The Hoover Dam on the Colorado River, on the border between Nevada and Arizona, was finished in 1936. It is 221 metres tall, taller than a 60-storey building, and the lake behind it, Lake Mead, became one of the largest reservoirs in the USA. Thousands of workers built it during the Great Depression, when jobs were scarce.

In India, the Bhakra Dam on the Sutlej River, finished in 1963, stands about 226 metres tall. Its water and electricity helped turn Punjab and Haryana into major wheat-growing states. Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru famously called projects like this "the temples of modern India". Today, India's tallest dam is the Tehri Dam in Uttarakhand, at about 260 metres.

Storing power in a lake

Some hydropower stations work like giant batteries. They have two reservoirs, one high and one low. When there is spare electricity, often from sunny or windy afternoons, pumps push water up to the top lake. When people need extra power, the water is let back down through the turbines.

This is called pumped storage. India and the USA are both building more of it, to back up solar and wind power when the Sun sets or the wind drops.

Who pays the price?

Big dams bring electricity, drinking water, irrigation and protection from floods. They can also flood villages, farmland and forests, forcing many families to move. They block fish from swimming upstream and trap the silt that rivers carry to their deltas.

In India, the Sardar Sarovar Dam on the Narmada River was opposed for decades by the Narmada Bachao Andolan, a movement led by people who would lose their homes. Supporters said the dam would bring water to drought-hit regions of Gujarat and Rajasthan. It was completed in 2017. The debate over how to balance these gains and losses continues wherever new dams are planned.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A hydroelectric dam uses up the river's water to make electricity.

Truth: The water flows through the turbines and carries on down the river. What a dam does change is when and how the water flows, and some is lost by evaporation from the lake behind it.

Try this

Make a water wheel. Push six plastic spoons into the sides of a cork or a small foam ball, all facing the same way, and slide a pencil through the middle as an axle. Hold the pencil ends loosely and pour water from a jug onto the spoons over a sink. Try pouring from higher up and see if it spins faster.
