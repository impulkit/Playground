How was non-stick coating discovered?

In 1938, a young chemist opened a gas cylinder and nothing came out. Inside was a slippery white powder that would end up on millions of frying pans.

The short answer

Non-stick coating was discovered by accident. On 6 April 1938, Roy Plunkett, a 27-year-old chemist at the DuPont company in New Jersey, USA, was working on new gases for refrigerators. He opened the valve on a cylinder of one gas, and nothing came out.

The cylinder still weighed as much as a full one. So he and his assistant cut it open. Inside was a waxy white powder. The gas had joined itself into a brand new solid that almost nothing would stick to. It was later named Teflon.

Surprising but true

Teflon is so slippery that even a gecko, which can climb glass, can't grip it.

What happened in the cylinder

The gas Plunkett used was made of small molecules of carbon and fluorine. Under the pressure and cold inside the cylinder, the molecules linked together into very long chains, like beads clipped into a necklace. Chemists call this kind of material a polymer.

The new chains were wrapped tightly in fluorine atoms. Fluorine holds onto carbon extremely firmly and barely reacts with anything else. That makes PTFE resistant to almost every chemical, and so slippery that water, oil and food slide straight off.

Too slippery to use

At first, nobody knew what to do with it. It was expensive, hard to shape and wouldn't stick to anything, which made it difficult to coat onto other surfaces.

Its first big job was secret. During the Second World War, scientists building the first atomic bombs needed seals and linings that could survive a fiercely corrosive gas made from uranium. PTFE was one of the few materials that could.

DuPont registered the name Teflon in 1945. After the war it found uses in wires, pipes and machine parts.

From fishing line to frying pan

The leap to the kitchen came from France. In the early 1950s, an engineer named Marc Grégoire coated his fishing line with PTFE so it wouldn't tangle. According to the family story, his wife Colette suggested he try it on her cooking pans.

He worked out how to make it grip aluminium, and in 1956 he started the company Tefal. Its name joins "Teflon" and "aluminium". Non-stick pans arrived in American shops in the early 1960s and soon spread worldwide, including to India, where a non-stick tawa now makes dosas slide off with very little oil.

The slipperiest solid

PTFE has one of the lowest friction levels of any solid. A block of it sliding on more PTFE feels almost as slick as wet ice on wet ice.

Geckos climb walls using millions of tiny hairs on their toes, which cling to surfaces using weak forces between molecules. On Teflon, those forces are too weak to hold them. Scientists studying geckos have even used Teflon to test the limits of their grip.

Space, bridges and bodies

NASA used PTFE coatings in the outer fabric of Apollo spacesuits, which helped start the myth that Teflon came from space research. It also coats the giant fabric roofs of some stadiums, because rain and dirt slide right off.

Doctors use PTFE in some artificial blood vessels and stitches, since the body tends to leave it alone.

Using it safely

Non-stick pans are safe for everyday cooking. But if an empty pan is overheated, above about 260°C, the coating can start to break down and give off fumes. These can make people feel ill and can kill pet birds, whose lungs are very sensitive. So cooks are advised not to heat non-stick pans empty on high flame, and to throw away badly scratched ones.

There was another concern. A chemical called PFOA was once used to make Teflon, and it lingers in the environment. In the USA, major makers agreed to phase it out, and they stopped using it by 2015.

Plunkett's "failed" experiment changed kitchens around the world. Thirty years later, at another American company, a chemist made a glue that wasn't sticky enough, and that mistake turned out just as useful.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Teflon was invented by NASA for the space programme.

Truth: Teflon was discovered by accident in 1938, two decades before NASA existed. NASA did use it later, including in the fabric of Apollo spacesuits, which may be how the myth started.

Try this

With an adult, place a small drop of honey on a steel plate and another on a non-stick pan, both cold. Tilt each one slowly and watch which drop slides first. The non-stick surface gives the honey almost nothing to hold on to.
