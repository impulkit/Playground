What happened to the Tasmanian tiger?

The last known Tasmanian tiger died in a zoo in 1936, just 59 days after the government finally made it a protected animal.

The short answer

The Tasmanian tiger, properly called the thylacine, was a striped hunter that once lived across Australia and New Guinea. It disappeared from the mainland about 3,000 years ago and survived only on the island of Tasmania. There, after Europeans arrived, it was hunted, trapped and poisoned. The last known thylacine died in Hobart Zoo on 7 September 1936.

Since then, people have reported thousands of sightings, but none has been proven with a clear photo, body or DNA. Scientists consider the thylacine extinct. Some are now trying to bring it back.

Surprising but true

The Tasmanian tiger wasn't a tiger or a dog. It was a marsupial with a pouch, more closely related to kangaroos than to wolves.

Not a tiger, not a dog

A thylacine was about the size of a medium dog, with a stiff tail and between 13 and 21 dark stripes across its back. Those stripes gave it the nickname "tiger". Its head looked so much like a wolf's that even experts can mix up the skulls.

But it was a marsupial, related to kangaroos and koalas. Females raised their babies in a pouch. It could open its jaws extremely wide, and it sometimes stood up on its back legs and tail, like a kangaroo. Thylacines and wolves ended up looking alike because they did the same job: chasing and catching prey.

Lost from the mainland

Aboriginal rock paintings in northern Australia show thylacines, some painted thousands of years ago. Then, around 3,000 years ago, they vanished from mainland Australia. Scientists think a mix of causes was to blame: a drier climate, growing human populations, and the arrival of the dingo, a wild dog that competed for the same food. Dingoes never reached Tasmania, which may be why thylacines lasted there.

A price on their heads

When European settlers brought sheep to Tasmania in the 1800s, farmers blamed thylacines for killing them, though wild dogs and poor farming were often the real cause. From 1888 to 1909, the Tasmanian government paid a reward, called a bounty, for every thylacine killed. It paid out 2,184 times, and private companies paid more.

Disease, dogs and the clearing of forests added to the pressure. By the 1920s thylacines were rare. Tasmania made them a protected species in July 1936, and the last known animal died in the zoo two months later. Short black-and-white films of it pacing in its cage are the only moving pictures we have.

The last one

The last known thylacine lived at Beaumaris Zoo in Hobart. It's often called Benjamin, but historians say that name appeared decades later, and there's no record of keepers using it. On some cold nights in 1936 it was reportedly locked out of its shelter, and it died on 7 September.

In 1933 a naturalist named David Fleay filmed it for about a minute. The clip shows it pacing, sniffing and opening its jaws in a huge yawn. Since 1996, Australia has marked 7 September as National Threatened Species Day, in its memory.

Still out there?

Every year people report seeing a thylacine in the Tasmanian bush. Searches with cameras have found nothing certain. A 2023 study of sighting records suggested a few might have survived into the late 1900s, though other scientists doubt it.

Meanwhile, in 2022 a company working with the University of Melbourne announced a plan to recreate the thylacine. It wants to use DNA from museum specimens and the cells of a living relative, the fat-tailed dunnart. Whether that would really bring back a thylacine is a question at the heart of de-extinction. And the thylacine wasn't the only animal hunted away within living memory. In America, the continent's most common bird went the same way, as the story of the passenger pigeon shows.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The thylacine was a kind of wild dog or striped tiger.

Truth: It was a marsupial, a mammal whose babies grow in a pouch. It only looked like a dog because it hunted like one. Scientists call this kind of lookalike convergent evolution.
