Why do corals turn white?

A bleached coral looks like a ghost of itself, but it is not dead yet. It is starving.

The short answer

Corals turn white when the sea gets too warm for too long. A coral is an animal, but it lives in partnership with tiny algae inside its body. The algae use sunlight to make food by photosynthesis and share most of it with the coral. In return, the coral gives them shelter. The algae also give corals their browns, greens and golds.

When the water stays about 1 °C or more above its usual summer maximum for several weeks, this partnership breaks down. The coral pushes the algae out. Without them, its tissue becomes see-through, and you can see the white limestone skeleton underneath. That is bleaching.

Surprising but true

Most of a coral's colour, and up to about 90 percent of its food, comes from tiny algae living inside its body.

A partnership under pressure

Each coral is a colony of thousands of tiny animals called polyps, each smaller than a pea. Their algae partners are so small that millions can fit in a patch of coral the size of a fingernail.

Scientists think heat makes the algae's food-making machinery go wrong. Instead of sugar, they start releasing harmful chemicals. The coral reacts by getting rid of them, much as you might drop a hot pan. It solves one problem but creates another, because the coral has lost its main supply of food.

A bleached coral can survive for a while on stored energy and by catching plankton with its tentacles. If temperatures fall within a few weeks, the algae return and the colour comes back. If the heat lasts, the coral slowly starves.

Bleaching around the world

Small patches of bleaching have always happened. What changed is the scale. In 1998, a strong El Niño warmed oceans across the planet, and reefs bleached from the Caribbean to the Indian Ocean. More global events followed in 2010 and from 2014 to 2017. In April 2024, scientists at NOAA in the United States declared a fourth global bleaching event.

Australia's Great Barrier Reef has had several mass bleachings since 2016. In the summer of 2023, a marine heatwave hit the reefs of the Florida Keys, and some shallow water near the coast felt as warm as a hot bath. Most scientists link the rising number of events to climate change, as the oceans soak up extra heat.

India's reefs

India has coral reefs in four main places: the Gulf of Kachchh in Gujarat, the Gulf of Mannar between India and Sri Lanka, the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, and Lakshadweep. India's reefs have bleached many times during marine heatwaves, with major events in 1998 and 2010.

For people on these islands, reefs are more than pretty scenery. They protect beaches from waves, feed fish that families eat and sell, and draw visitors. Researchers from Indian institutes dive regularly to count living and bleached corals, so they can see which reefs bounce back and which do not.

Watching from space

Satellites cannot see a single coral, but they can measure the temperature of the sea surface every day. NOAA's Coral Reef Watch adds up how many weeks a patch of ocean has stayed unusually warm. When the total climbs, reef managers get a warning that bleaching is likely, sometimes before divers see any white at all.

Some reefs seem tougher than others, and scientists are studying corals that survive heatwaves. They hope those tough corals hold clues for helping reefs through warmer decades ahead.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A white coral is a dead coral.

Truth: Bleached corals are still alive. They have lost the algae that colour and feed them. Many recover if the water cools in time, though they are weaker and more likely to get diseases.

Try this

Put two small leaves from the same plant in two clear glasses of water. Keep one glass in a cool shaded spot and the other in a warm sunny window for a few days, and compare how they look. Living things often cope fine with small changes, but steady heat can damage them, which is what happens to corals during a marine heatwave.
