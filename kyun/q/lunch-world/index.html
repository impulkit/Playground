What do school lunches look like around the world?

In Japan, children serve lunch to their own classmates, wearing white coats and caps, and then help clean up afterwards.

The short answer

Around the world, hundreds of millions of children eat lunch at school every day. What's on the plate depends on where they live. In India, it might be rice, dal and vegetables. In Japan, it could be fish, miso soup and rice. In France, children may get a starter, a main course, cheese and fruit.

Most countries see school meals as more than food. A good lunch helps children concentrate, grow and stay healthy. In poorer places, a free meal can be the reason a child comes to school at all. The way lunch is served also teaches something about each country: how people share, what they value and what grows nearby.

Surprising but true

India's school meal scheme feeds about 11 crore children, which is more people than live in most countries.

India's giant kitchen

India runs the biggest school meal programme on Earth. Under a scheme called PM POSHAN, about 11 crore children in government schools got a free hot lunch in 2025. Most meals are cooked fresh in school kitchens or big central kitchens, then served on steel plates.

The idea grew over a century. Madras (now Chennai) served meals in some schools in the 1920s, and Tamil Nadu spread them widely from the 1950s. In 2001, India's Supreme Court ordered every state to give cooked meals in government primary schools. The mid-day meal is now the main meal of the day for many children. Menus vary: eggs in some states, millets in others, and rice or rotis depending on the region.

Japan: lunch as a lesson

In Japanese schools, lunch, called kyushoku, is part of the timetable. There's usually no canteen. Children take turns putting on white coats, caps and masks, then wheel trolleys of food to their classroom and serve everyone. After eating together, they clear up and sort the rubbish.

Meals are planned by trained nutritionists and are often cooked from fresh local food. A typical tray might hold rice, a bowl of soup, grilled fish and a small salad, with a carton of milk. The custom dates to 1889, when a school in the town of Tsuruoka gave rice balls and salted fish to poor pupils.

What is daily life like in Japan?In most Japanese schools there are no cleaners. After lunch, the students sweep, mop and scrub their own classrooms, corridors and even toilets.

France, Finland and Brazil

France treats lunch as a proper sit-down meal that can take an hour. Many school canteens serve several small courses, often including a piece of cheese, and children are expected to taste a bit of everything.

Finland has served free school meals to every pupil since 1948. Children help themselves from a buffet, and teachers often eat with them. In Brazil, a law passed in 2009 says at least 30% of the money spent on school food must buy from local family farms. That gives children fresh fruit and gives small farmers a steady customer.

The American lunch tray

In the USA, the National School Lunch Program began in 1946. Schools served more than 4.8 billion lunches in 2024. Families on low incomes get them free or cheap. A typical tray has sections for a main dish, fruit, vegetables, grain and a carton of milk.

American school lunches have long sparked arguments over what counts as healthy. In 2011, Congress let the tomato paste on pizza count as a vegetable serving, and people joked that pizza had become a vegetable. New rules since then have added more whole grains and cut salt.

Same goal, different plates

Nutrition experts agree on the basics. A good school lunch has a mix of grains, protein, vegetables and fruit, with not too much sugar or salt. These give the nutrients children need for growing bones and busy brains.

Beyond that, every country does it its own way. Korean pupils eat kimchi with almost every lunch. Children in Kenya might get a stew of maize and beans called githeri. A tray in Ohio and a steel plate in Odisha look nothing alike. Both are trying to make sure a hungry child can learn after lunch.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In the USA, pizza is officially counted as a vegetable in school lunches.

Truth: Only the tomato paste on it can count towards the vegetable part of the meal, under a 2011 rule. The cheese and crust count as other food groups.
