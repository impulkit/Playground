Could a kid lift an elephant?

Archimedes said: give me a place to stand and a long enough lever, and I'll move the Earth.

The short answer

A lever is a bar that turns on a point called a fulcrum, like a seesaw.

Push far from the fulcrum and a small push can lift a heavy load close to it. You trade distance for force: your end moves a long way, while the heavy end moves only a little.

Scissors, bottle openers, hand pumps and your own arm are all levers.

Surprising but true

To lift a 4-tonne elephant 1 m from the fulcrum, a 30 kg kid would need to sit about 133 m away.

The seesaw rule

Two children on a seesaw show the rule. If one child is twice as heavy, the lighter child must sit twice as far from the middle to balance. In maths, the push multiplied by its distance from the fulcrum must equal the load multiplied by its distance.

That's how a kid could, in theory, lift an elephant. A 4-tonne elephant sitting 1 m from the fulcrum would need a 30 kg child about 133 m away on the other side, roughly the width of a big cricket ground. The bar would have to be impossibly strong, but the maths works.

Archimedes and the Earth

The Greek scientist Archimedes, who lived in Syracuse in Sicily about 2,250 years ago, was the first known person to prove the rule of the lever with mathematics. He was so confident that, according to later writers, he said that with a place to stand and a long enough lever, he could move the Earth.

He was right in principle. The lever would have to be unimaginably long, and he would need to push it a vast distance to shift the planet even a tiny bit.

Three kinds of lever

Levers come in three types, depending on where the fulcrum, the load and your push sit.

In the first kind, the fulcrum is in the middle, like a seesaw, scissors or a crowbar. In the second, the load is in the middle, like a wheelbarrow or a nutcracker. The wheel is the fulcrum, the load sits in the tray, and you lift the handles.

In the third kind, your push is in the middle. Tweezers, a cricket bat and a fishing rod work this way. These levers don't make lifting easier. Instead, they turn a small movement of your hand into a big, fast movement at the other end.

Levers inside you

Your forearm is a third-kind lever. The elbow is the fulcrum, and your biceps muscle pulls on the bone just below it. Because the muscle is attached so close to the joint, it must pull very hard, but a small shortening swings your hand a long way, quickly. That's useful for throwing.

Your jaw, your foot when you stand on tiptoe, and even your head nodding on your neck are levers too.

Lifting water and stone

People have used levers for thousands of years. In ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia, farmers lifted water with a shaduf: a long pole on a post, with a bucket at one end and a heavy weight at the other. Very similar well-sweeps are still seen in some villages in India and other countries.

Builders of the pyramids and of the brick cities of the Indus Valley almost certainly used levers to shift heavy blocks, though we don't know all their methods. Levers often worked alongside ramps, rollers and later pulleys.

Machines all around you

Look around a kitchen and levers are everywhere: a tap handle, a bottle opener, tongs, the lid of a dustbin with a foot pedal. A car jack, a pair of pliers and the handle of a hand pump at a village well all trade a long, easy push for a short, strong one. Archimedes would recognise every one of them.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A lever gives you extra energy, letting you get something for nothing.

Truth: A lever only swaps force for distance. You push with less force, but you have to move your end much further. The total work you do stays the same, or a little more because of friction.

Try this

Lay a ruler across a pencil to make a seesaw. Put two coins on one end. Then find how many coins you need on the other end to balance them as you slide the pencil closer to the two coins. Write down the distances and look for the pattern.
