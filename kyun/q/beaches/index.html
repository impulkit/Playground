Why do some beaches have black sand?

Some of the white sand on tropical beaches has passed through the gut of a parrotfish, a reef fish that grinds up coral and poops out sand.

The short answer

A beach's colour comes from whatever its sand is made of, and that depends on the rocks and living things nearby. Most black sand comes from dark volcanic rock called basalt. When lava pours into the sea, or old lava cliffs are pounded by waves, the rock breaks into tiny black grains.

Other black sands are made of heavy dark minerals that waves and currents sort out from lighter grains and pile up on the shore. Pale and golden beaches are usually made of quartz, a tough, light-coloured mineral. Dazzling white tropical beaches are often ground-up coral and shells.

Surprising but true

In Kerala's Kollam district, the black sand on some beaches is mined, because it is full of minerals used to make paint, rocket parts and nuclear fuel.

Born from lava

Volcanic islands are where black beaches are most common. A volcano in the sea keeps adding new rock, and the waves never stop breaking it down. In Hawaii, Punaluʻu Beach on the Big Island is famous for its jet-black sand, where green sea turtles often come ashore to rest. In 2018, lava from the Kilauea volcano poured into the sea and a brand-new black sand beach formed at Pohoiki within months.

In Iceland, Reynisfjara has black sand, thundering waves and tall columns of basalt along the cliffs. Black sand soaks up sunlight, so on a sunny day it can get hot enough to burn bare feet, much hotter than pale sand next to it.

India's mineral sands

India has black sand too, though not mostly from volcanoes. Along parts of the Kerala coast near Kollam, such as Chavara and Neendakara, rivers and waves have gathered heavy minerals washed out of the Western Ghats. Because these grains are heavier than quartz, the waves wash away the lighter sand and leave dark streaks behind.

These sands contain ilmenite, used to make titanium for paint, aircraft and rocket parts, and monazite, which holds thorium, a possible nuclear fuel. A government company, Indian Rare Earths Limited, has mined them for decades. Mining has also worn away parts of the coast, and some villages nearby have protested that the sea now reaches closer to their homes. Further north, near Karwar in Karnataka, Tilmati Beach has small coves of dark sand that come from local volcanic rock.

White beaches and parrotfish

The bright white sand of places like the Maldives and Lakshadweep is mostly made of the skeletons of sea creatures: broken coral, shells and the hard bits of tiny animals. Much of it is made by parrotfish. These colourful reef fish bite off chunks of coral to eat the algae on it. They grind the hard coral with teeth in their throats and pass it out as fine sand.

A single large parrotfish can turn hundreds of kilograms of coral into sand in a year. So a healthy coral reef helps keep its nearby beaches supplied with fresh sand.

Green, pink and golden

On Papakolea Beach in Hawaii, the sand is olive green. It comes from olivine, a green mineral in the local volcanic rock that is heavier than the other grains, so it collects on the beach. On Harbour Island in the Bahamas and on Bermuda, beaches look pink because tiny red-shelled creatures called foraminifera mix with white sand.

Most beaches, though, are some shade of gold or tan. Quartz is hard and doesn't dissolve easily, so after other minerals wear away, it is what's left. Over thousands of years, rivers carry grains from mountains to the sea, and waves sort them by size and weight. Every handful of beach sand is a record of where it came from. Look closely with a magnifying glass, and you may see clear quartz, black specks, pink shell bits and the odd sparkle of mica, all from different places.

Myth or fact?

Myth: All sand is made of the same stuff, just in different colours.

Truth: Sand is any loose grains between about the size of a grain of salt and a small peppercorn. It can be made of quartz, volcanic rock, coral, shells, heavy minerals or even glass, and each gives a beach its own colour.

Try this

Put a little dry sand from a beach, a riverbank or a sandpit on a sheet of white paper. Wrap a strong magnet in a thin plastic bag and move it slowly just above the sand. Any black grains that jump up are probably magnetite, an iron mineral. Look at the rest with a magnifying glass and sort the grains by colour.
