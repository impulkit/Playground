What does it mean to be fair?

In a famous experiment, a monkey happily ate cucumber until it saw its neighbour get a grape for the same job. Then it threw the cucumber back.

The short answer

Being fair means treating people in a way that they, and others watching, can accept as right. It often means following the same rules for everyone, sharing in a way that makes sense, and not cheating or playing favourites.

But fairness is tricky, because there is more than one kind. Sometimes fair means equal, like each player getting the same number of turns. Sometimes it means giving according to need, like more water for the thirsty runner. Sometimes it means giving according to effort, like more marks for the better answer. People argue about which kind fits which situation, and those arguments are one of the oldest conversations in human history.

Surprising but true

There is an ancient way to split a cake fairly between two people. One cuts, and the other chooses first.

Even monkeys care

In 2003, scientists Sarah Brosnan and Frans de Waal ran a famous experiment with capuchin monkeys. Each monkey handed a small stone to a researcher and got a piece of cucumber in return. They were perfectly happy with the deal.

Then one monkey started getting grapes, which capuchins like much more. Its neighbour, still getting cucumber, often refused the food or even threw it back. The video went around the world. Scientists still debate exactly what the monkeys were thinking, but many see it as a sign that a sense of fairness goes deep, perhaps older than humans.

Children are fairness experts

Psychologists have found that children as young as three or four notice when sweets or stickers are shared unequally, especially if they get less. By around seven or eight, many children will even turn down a bigger share for themselves if it seems unfair to a friend.

That is why "It's not fair" is one of the first arguments children learn to make. It isn't just a complaint. It shows a child is checking whether rules apply to everyone, including adults. Being able to put yourself in someone else's place, called empathy, is part of the same skill.

What is game theory?The best way to win at rock-paper-scissors, according to mathematics, is to stop trying to be clever and choose at random.

Fair rules and fair umpires

Games are a good place to see fairness at work. Both teams follow the same rules, the toss decides who goes first, and an umpire makes the calls. When fans feel an umpire has been unfair, they get very angry, which shows how much fairness matters to people.

Sport has also worked hard to be fairer. Technology like the third umpire in cricket and video review in football helps catch mistakes. Anti-doping tests try to stop athletes from cheating with drugs. Each of these exists because people wanted results they could trust.

Equal, or what each person needs?

Laws in many countries promise equal treatment. Article 14 of India's Constitution says everyone is equal before the law, and the US Constitution's 14th Amendment makes a similar promise.

But equality can mean different things. If two children are watching a cricket match over a wall and one is much shorter, giving them the same-sized box to stand on may still leave one unable to see. Giving the shorter child a taller box treats them differently, so both can see. Governments in India and the United States have tried policies to help groups who were treated unfairly in the past. People disagree, sometimes strongly, about how well these policies work and how long they should last.

The veil of ignorance

The American thinker John Rawls, in a book published in 1971, suggested a clever test. Picture yourself designing the rules for a society without knowing who you will be in it: rich or poor, healthy or ill, from a big city or a small village. He called this the veil of ignorance.

If you didn't know where you'd end up, you'd probably want rules that are decent to everyone. You can try the same test at home. When you make a rule for your siblings, ask whether you'd still like it if you were the one on the other side.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Being fair always means giving everyone exactly the same.

Truth: Sometimes equal is fair, like everyone getting one vote. But a ramp for a wheelchair user or glasses for someone who can't see the board give different people different things so they can take part equally. Many people call this kind of fairness equity.

Try this

When you next share a snack, try divide-and-choose. One person splits it into two parts, and the other person picks first. Notice how carefully the cutter works. Then try a harder case, with three people and a slice of pizza, and see if you can invent your own fair method.
