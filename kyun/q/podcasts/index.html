What is a podcast?

The word "podcast" was first suggested in a British newspaper in 2004, by a writer trying to name a new kind of radio that lived on the internet.

The short answer

A podcast is a series of audio shows, a bit like radio programmes, that you can play whenever you want. Each show is called an episode. You can listen on a phone, a computer or a smart speaker, and you can pause, skip back or listen at double speed.

Podcasts cover almost everything: science, history, cricket, mysteries, cooking, stories for bedtime and interviews with interesting people. Anyone can make one. That freedom is the big difference from traditional radio, where a station decides what goes on air and when.

Surprising but true

Most podcasts are not stored inside podcast apps at all. The apps read a simple list, called a feed, that points to audio files kept elsewhere on the internet.

Where the name came from

In February 2004, the British journalist Ben Hammersley wrote an article in The Guardian about people putting radio-style shows online. Looking for a name, he suggested a few, including "podcasting", from iPod and broadcasting. The iPod was Apple's portable music player, the must-have gadget of that time.

The word caught on fast. In 2005, Apple added podcasts to its iTunes store, and the New Oxford American Dictionary named "podcast" its word of the year.

How a podcast reaches you

A podcast maker records an episode and saves it as an audio file on a website or hosting service. They also keep a feed, a simple list that says: here are my episodes, here are their titles and here is where each file lives.

Podcast apps check these feeds regularly. When a new episode appears, your app notices and can download it automatically. This is why one podcast can appear in many different apps at once.

Radio's grandchildren

Long before podcasts, families gathered around radios for serials, comedies and mystery plays. Many podcasts bring back those old forms: a story told in parts, ending each time on a cliffhanger, a moment that makes you desperate to hear what happens next.

In 2014, an American podcast called Serial, which re-examined a real crime case over many episodes, became a huge hit and made millions of new people try podcasts. Since then, newspapers, radio stations and schools have all started their own. India now has thousands of podcasts in Hindi, Tamil, Bengali, Marathi and other languages, from mythology retold for children to long interviews about cricket.

Why people love listening

Listening leaves your eyes and hands free. People hear podcasts while travelling on a crowded local train, walking to school, cooking or doing chores. A voice in your ears can feel personal, as if the host is talking just to you.

Stories told aloud are also very old. Long before printing, India had storytellers who travelled from village to village, performing epics and folk tales. A good podcast host uses some of the same tricks: a pause before a surprise, a change of voice, a question left hanging.

Making your own

Many podcasts are recorded with nothing more than a phone. The most important things are a quiet room, a clear plan and speaking close to the microphone. Soft things such as cushions, curtains and blankets help stop echoes.

Afterwards, free editing apps let you cut out mistakes and add music. Children's podcasts made by children themselves are now played in classrooms in India, the USA and many other countries. Always ask a parent or teacher before sharing anything online.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You need a professional studio to make a podcast.

Truth: Many popular podcasts started with a cheap microphone or just a phone in a quiet room. Clear planning and a good topic matter more than expensive equipment.

Try this

Record a five-minute podcast with a friend or family member on a phone. Pick one topic, such as your favourite animal, and plan three questions to ask each other. Record in a small room with cushions or curtains, which soak up echoes and make voices sound clearer.
