What is the Magna Carta?

In 1215, a group of angry barons forced King John of England to agree that even a king must obey the law. He broke his promise within weeks.

The short answer

Magna Carta, Latin for "Great Charter", is an agreement between King John of England and a group of rebel barons. It was made on 15 June 1215, at a meadow called Runnymede beside the River Thames. Its main idea was that the king, too, had to follow the law.

Most of its 63 clauses dealt with problems of the time, such as taxes, forests and the rights of barons. The agreement quickly fell apart. Yet a few clauses, especially the promise of fair justice, became famous. Over the centuries, people in England, the United States, India and elsewhere pointed to Magna Carta when they argued for rights and limits on power.

Surprising but true

One of the few surviving copies of Magna Carta from the 1200s is on display at the National Archives in Washington, D.C., the same building that holds the US Constitution.

A king in trouble

King John was unpopular. He had lost most of England's lands in France, and he raised huge taxes from his barons to pay for wars to win them back. He also punished people without fair trials and quarrelled with the Pope.

In 1215 many barons rebelled and captured London. John agreed to meet them at Runnymede, between his castle at Windsor and the rebel camp. There he set his seal on a document listing their demands. Copies were written out by hand in Latin and sent around the kingdom.

Broken in weeks

Neither side trusted the other. John asked Pope Innocent III to cancel the charter, and in August 1215 the Pope declared it invalid. Civil war broke out, and some barons even invited a French prince to take the English throne.

Then, in October 1216, John died. Advisers for his nine-year-old son, Henry III, reissued a revised Magna Carta to win the barons back. It was reissued again in 1217 and 1225. The 1225 version became part of English law and was confirmed many times by later kings.

The words that lasted

Most clauses are long out of date. One deals with fish weirs in rivers, another with debts owed to moneylenders. Only three clauses are still part of English law today.

The most famous lines were later joined into a single clause. In modern English, they say that no free man shall be arrested, imprisoned or punished "except by the lawful judgement of his equals or by the law of the land". They go on: "To no one will we sell, to no one deny or delay right or justice."

These lines express ideas still at the heart of many legal systems: a fair trial, and a government that must follow the law.

Across the Atlantic

English settlers carried the idea of Magna Carta to America. When the colonists rebelled against Britain in the 1770s, they argued that the king was denying them rights that went back to 1215.

The US Constitution and its Bill of Rights promise that no person shall be "deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law", words that trace back to Magna Carta. In 1957, the American Bar Association built a memorial at Runnymede. A copy of Magna Carta from 1297 is on display in the National Archives in Washington, D.C.

Where the copies are

Four original copies from 1215 survive. Two are in the British Library in London, one in Lincoln Castle and one in Salisbury Cathedral. They are written in tiny, neat Latin on a single sheet of parchment, made from animal skin.

In India, the idea that no one can be deprived of life or liberty except according to law is part of Article 21 of the Constitution. The meadow at Runnymede is now looked after by the National Trust, and visitors can walk where the barons and the king once faced each other.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Magna Carta gave rights to everyone in England.

Truth: Most of its promises were for "free men", mainly barons, knights and churchmen. Most ordinary people at the time were serfs, called villeins, who were not free and gained little from it.
