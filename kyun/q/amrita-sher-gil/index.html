Who was Amrita Sher-Gil?

She won a top prize in Paris while still a student, then came home to paint India in a way nobody had before, and died at just 28.

The short answer

Amrita Sher-Gil (1913–1941) was a painter who is often called one of the founders of modern Indian art. Her father was a Sikh scholar from Punjab and her mother was a Hungarian singer. She was born in Budapest, studied art in Paris, and then chose to make her life and her art in India.

Her paintings show ordinary Indian people, especially women, with quiet, serious faces and warm, earthy colours. She worked for only about ten years as a grown-up artist, and died at 28. Even so, her pictures changed what Indian painters thought was possible.

Surprising but true

The Government of India has declared Amrita Sher-Gil's paintings National Art Treasures, so most of them may not be taken out of the country.

A childhood in two worlds

Amrita grew up between Hungary and India. Her family moved to Shimla when she was eight, and she began taking painting lessons. Her mother saw how talented she was and took her to Europe to study.

As a teenager she joined the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris, one of the most famous art schools in the world. She learned from European masters and from artists such as Paul Gauguin, whose bold colours she admired. In 1933 her painting Young Girls won a gold medal at the Paris Salon, a major exhibition. She was barely 20.

Coming home to paint India

Paris could have been her home for good. But Amrita later wrote of a strong longing to return to India, and she came back in 1934. She said she wanted to paint Indian life and Indian people, not copy European pictures.

Her first paintings in India, like Three Girls, show young women sitting close together, quiet and thoughtful. Their faces do not smile for the viewer. Many people found that unusual, because most paintings of Indian women at the time, like those of Raja Ravi Varma, showed them as beautiful heroines from stories.

Why is Paris called the City of Light?Paris was one of the first big cities to light its streets at night, in the 1600s. It was also a centre of bold new ideas, and both may have earned it the name City of Light.

A journey south

In 1937 she travelled through south India. She saw the ancient Ajanta cave paintings, the temples of Mahabalipuram and the murals of Kerala. They moved her deeply, because here was an Indian tradition as rich as anything in Europe.

Afterwards she painted three famous pictures sometimes called her South Indian trilogy: Bride's Toilet, Brahmacharis and South Indian Villagers Going to Market. The colours became warmer, the shapes flatter and simpler. She was blending what she learned in Paris with what she saw on Indian walls.

Her last years

Later she was inspired by Mughal and Pahari miniature paintings and began painting village scenes with small figures and strong patterns. She married a Hungarian doctor and lived for a time at Saraya, a sugar estate in Uttar Pradesh. In 1941 the couple moved to Lahore, then still part of British India.

Only a few months later she became suddenly ill and died. She was 28. Her early death means there are only about 170 paintings by her that we know of.

Why she still matters

After her death, her reputation kept growing. In 1976 the Government of India declared her work National Art Treasures, a protection given to very few artists. Most of her paintings are now in the National Gallery of Modern Art in New Delhi, where visitors can see them in the museum.

Her paintings sell for very high prices at auctions today. More importantly, she showed later artists that they did not have to choose between India and the wider world. She was a woman painting women at a time when that was rare, and she painted them as real people with inner lives.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Amrita Sher-Gil learned to paint in India and only later went abroad.

Truth: It was the other way round. She was born in Hungary, trained in Paris, and only settled in India in her twenties, when she decided that India was where her art belonged.
