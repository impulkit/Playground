What is a supernova?

When a giant star dies, it can blaze brighter than a whole galaxy of billions of stars, and scatter the stuff you are made of.

The short answer

A supernova is the gigantic explosion of a star. For a few weeks, a single supernova can shine as brightly as all the billions of stars in its galaxy put together. It blasts most of the star out into space at thousands of kilometres per second.

There are two main kinds. In one, a very heavy star runs out of fuel, its core collapses in less than a second, and the rest of the star blows apart. In the other, a small dead star called a white dwarf steals gas from a neighbour until it becomes unstable and explodes completely. Either way, the explosion spreads newly made elements across space.

Surprising but true

Much of the iron in your blood and the calcium in your bones was made inside stars and blasted into space by supernovas, long before the Sun was born.

How a giant star dies

Stars shine by fusing light elements into heavier ones in their cores. Our Sun fuses hydrogen into helium. Much heavier stars, at least about eight times the Sun's mass, go further. They make carbon, oxygen, silicon and finally iron, in layers like an onion.

Iron is a dead end. Fusing it uses up energy instead of releasing it. Once the core is iron, nothing holds it up against its own crushing gravity. In a fraction of a second, a core bigger than the Earth collapses into a ball perhaps 20 km across, about the size of a city. The collapse rebounds and, together with a flood of tiny particles called neutrinos, blows the outer layers of the star apart.

What is left behind is a neutron star, so dense that a teaspoon of it would weigh billions of tonnes, or, for the heaviest stars, a black hole.

A teenager's calculation

In 1930, a 19-year-old Indian student named Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar sailed from Madras to England to study. During the voyage, he worked out that a white dwarf heavier than about 1.4 times the Sun cannot support itself and must collapse. This is now called the Chandrasekhar limit.

Some senior scientists dismissed his idea at first. He kept working, moved to the University of Chicago, and in 1983 shared the Nobel Prize in Physics. NASA later named its Chandra X-ray Observatory after him. It has photographed the glowing wreckage of many supernovas.

Guest stars in history

In 1054, astronomers in China recorded a "guest star" that appeared suddenly and was bright enough to see in daytime for about three weeks. Today, at that spot in the sky, we see the Crab Nebula, a cloud of glowing gas still expanding from the explosion, with a spinning neutron star at its centre. Records like these, kept carefully in ancient China, help astronomers date such events.

What did ancient China invent?The oldest printed book with a date on it was made in China in 868 CE, almost 600 years before Gutenberg's printing press.

In 1572 the Danish astronomer Tycho Brahe watched a new star appear, and in 1604 Johannes Kepler saw another. That 1604 supernova is the last one definitely seen with bare eyes in our own galaxy.

In February 1987, a supernova lit up in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a small galaxy near ours, about 168,000 light-years away. Detectors in Japan and the United States caught a burst of neutrinos a few hours before the light arrived, proving that a core really had collapsed.

We are made of star stuff

The Big Bang made mainly hydrogen and helium. Almost every heavier element, including the carbon in your cells, the oxygen you breathe, the calcium in your bones and the iron in your blood, was made inside stars. Supernovas threw it into space, where it mixed into new clouds of gas.

About 4.6 billion years ago, one of those enriched clouds collapsed to form the Sun and the planets. Some scientists think a nearby supernova may even have helped trigger that collapse. As the astronomer Carl Sagan liked to say, we are made of star stuff.

Could one happen nearby?

Betelgeuse, the reddish star at the shoulder of Orion in the constellations, is a giant expected to explode some time in the next 100,000 years or so. In 2019 and 2020 it dimmed oddly, and some people wondered if it was about to blow. It turned out to be a cloud of dust it had puffed out.

When Betelgeuse does explode, it will be bright enough to see in daylight for weeks, but it is far enough away to do Earth no harm.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Our Sun will one day explode as a supernova.

Truth: The Sun is too small. In about 5 billion years it will swell into a red giant, then shrink into a small, dim white dwarf. Only stars about eight times heavier than the Sun or more end with that kind of explosion.

Try this

Outdoors, on hard ground, hold a tennis ball on top of a basketball or football and drop them together from waist height. The small ball bounces far higher than it would alone. Astronomers use this to show how energy from a collapsing star's core is passed outward. Keep faces and windows well out of the way.
