How do shepherds move their flocks across the Himalaya?

Every spring, Gaddi shepherds walk hundreds of sheep and goats up into the Himalaya, crossing snowy passes over 4,000 m high.

The short answer

In the Himalaya, many shepherd families move their flocks with the seasons. In summer they climb to high meadows above the forests, where melting snow leaves rich grass. In winter they walk back down to warmer foothills and plains. This back-and-forth journey is called transhumance.

The best-known groups include the Gaddis of Himachal Pradesh, the Bakarwals of Jammu and Kashmir, and the Changpas of Ladakh. A flock may hold hundreds of sheep and goats. The shepherds walk with them on foot, with dogs for protection and ponies or mules to carry tents, food and salt.

Surprising but true

A Gaddi flock moving uphill covers only about 7–8 km a day, so the whole climb to the high summer meadows can take around three months.

A three-month climb

A Gaddi shepherd's year might start in the scrubby foothills of the Shivaliks. From around February or March, the flock starts moving uphill. The animals graze as they go, covering about 7–8 km a day, roughly the length of a long city walk with plenty of snacking. Shepherds avoid roads where they can and follow river valleys and old bridle paths.

The full climb to alpine meadows can take around three months. Lambs are often born during the journey, and shepherds may carry newborns over rough ground in their arms or tucked in their coats.

Over the passes

To reach the best summer grass, flocks must cross high passes. In the Dhauladhar range above Dharamshala, routes like the Indrahar Pass rise above 4,000 m. Snow can linger there into summer. A late storm can trap a flock for days.

Gaddi herders traditionally wear a thick woollen coat called a chola, tied with a long rope of black wool called a dora. They sleep in the open or in small tents and rock shelters, with dogs curled around the flock.

Dogs with spiked collars

Leopards, bears, wolves and, high up, snow leopards all hunt sheep and goats. The Gaddi dog, a big, shaggy guard dog, protects the flock at night. Many wear iron collars with spikes, so a big cat can't grab them by the throat.

Losses still happen. Predators, falls and sudden snow can take a share of a flock in a bad year. Shepherds count their animals often and know many of them individually. Before crossing a dangerous pass, some families still make offerings to local gods, asking for a safe journey for people and animals alike.

The Changpas of the cold desert

In Ladakh's Changthang plateau, above 4,000 m, Changpa herders raise goats in one of the coldest inhabited places in India. Winter nights can drop far below -30°C. To survive, their goats grow a super-fine undercoat of wool. That fibre is pashmina, combed out by hand each spring and woven into famous shawls, as told in the story of pashmina.

Changpa families move between fixed pastures several times a year, living in tents made of yak hair.

Shepherds around the world

Moving flocks with the seasons is an ancient practice found across mountain lands, from the Alps to the Andes. In the American West, shepherds from the Basque region of Spain and France arrived in the late 1800s and herded sheep across Nevada, Idaho and California. The Navajo of the southwestern USA have kept sheep for centuries too.

A changing way of life

Life on the move is getting harder. Roads, dams, new farms and forest rules have blocked some old routes. Weather is less predictable, and fewer young people want such a tough life. In some years flocks travel by truck for part of the way.

Still, Himalayan shepherds supply wool, meat and skills that the mountains need. Their journey follows the same rhythm as many wild animals, which also move up and down the mountains with the seasons, as you can see in animal migrations on land.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Nomadic shepherds wander wherever they like with no plan.

Truth: Most follow the same routes their families have used for generations, with known stopping places, grazing rights and dates. Forest departments issue grazing permits, and shepherds time their moves to the melting snow.
