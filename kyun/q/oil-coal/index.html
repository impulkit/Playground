How were coal and oil made?

When you switch on a light in India, there's a good chance the power comes from plants that died 250 million years ago.

The short answer

Coal, oil and natural gas are called fossil fuels, because they are made from things that lived long ago. Coal is the squashed remains of ancient plants. Oil and gas come mostly from tiny sea creatures.

In both cases, dead living things were buried before they could rot away. Layer after layer of mud and sand piled on top. Deep underground, heat and pressure slowly changed them over millions of years. When we burn them, we release energy that those plants and plankton first captured from sunlight.

Surprising but true

In the Jharia coalfield in Jharkhand, underground coal fires have been burning for more than a hundred years. The first was recorded in 1916.

Forests that became stone

About 300 to 360 million years ago, during a time called the Carboniferous, much of the land was covered in warm swamps. Giant ferns and tree-sized relatives of today's little horsetail plants grew there. When they died, they fell into still water with little oxygen, so they didn't rot completely. They piled up as a soggy brown mass called peat.

As more mud buried the peat, it was squeezed and heated. Water and gases were pressed out. Peat became soft brown coal, called lignite, then harder black coal. It takes many metres of plants to make one metre of coal.

India's coal is a bit younger. Most formed around 250 to 300 million years ago, when India was part of Gondwana, joined to Antarctica and Africa. The same kinds of fossil leaves turn up in coal on all those continents, one of the clues that India drifted across the planet.

What was the Industrial Revolution?About 250 years ago, machines began doing work that hands had done for thousands of years, and the way people lived changed faster than ever before.

Oil from the sea

Oil has a different recipe. Oceans are full of plankton, living things so small you need a microscope to see them. When they die, many sink and settle in mud on the sea floor.

If that mud gets buried deep, it heats up. Between about 60 and 120 °C, the remains slowly break down into oil. Hotter still, they turn into natural gas. Oil and gas are lighter than water, so they creep upwards through rock until something stops them, such as a layer of tight rock like a lid. That's where drillers look.

Digging and drilling

In 1859, Edwin Drake drilled a well at Titusville, Pennsylvania, and struck oil about 21 m down. It started the modern oil industry. In India, oil was found at Digboi in Assam in 1889, and its refinery, opened in 1901, is one of the oldest still working anywhere. In 1974, drillers working from a rig called Sagar Samrat struck a huge oilfield under the Arabian Sea, about 170 km off Mumbai. Named Bombay High, it is still India's largest oilfield, pumped from platforms standing in the open sea.

Coal powered the steam engines of the Industrial Revolution, and it still makes most of India's electricity. Oil gives us petrol, diesel and jet fuel. It is also the starting point for most plastics, and even some paints, medicines and clothing.

Burning old sunlight

Fossil fuels took millions of years to form, and we are using them up in a few centuries. Burning them releases carbon dioxide that was locked away underground, which is the main cause of climate change. It also makes smoke that dirties the air.

That's why countries are building more solar and wind power. Coal mining has left its own marks too. In Jharia, fires that started in underground coal seams more than a century ago still smoulder beneath towns and roads.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Oil comes from dead dinosaurs.

Truth: Almost none of it does. Most oil formed from tiny ocean plankton and algae, and much of it is older or younger than the dinosaurs. Coal comes from plants. The dinosaur idea was helped along by old advertising.

Try this

Lay a few fresh leaves between sheets of newspaper and pile heavy books on top. After two weeks, look at how flat and dark they have become, and how they've stained the paper. That squashing is the first step in turning plants into coal. Nature just adds far more weight and millions of years.
