What happens along the Ganga from its source to the sea?

The Ganga starts as meltwater from an ice cave high in the Himalaya and ends about 2,500 km later in the world's biggest mangrove forest.

The short answer

The Ganga begins at about 4,000 metres above sea level, where meltwater pours out of an ice cave at the end of the Gangotri Glacier. People call that cave Gaumukh, "the cow's mouth". From there the river runs about 2,525 km across northern India and Bangladesh to the Bay of Bengal.

Along the way it drops out of the mountains and crosses one of the flattest, most crowded plains on Earth. It picks up dozens of other rivers and finally splits into hundreds of channels in the Sundarbans. More than 40 crore people live in its basin, the land that drains into it.

Surprising but true

The river does not take the name Ganga until Devprayag, where two mountain rivers, the Bhagirathi and the Alaknanda, meet.

Fast and cold in the mountains

Before it reaches Haridwar, about 290 km from the glacier, the river falls more than 3,500 metres. The water is icy, grey with ground-up rock, and loud. This young stream is called the Bhagirathi, after a king in the Puranas who is said to have brought the river down from the heavens.

The Gangotri Glacier itself is about 30 km long, a river of ice that creeps downhill a few metres a year. Like most Himalayan glaciers, it is shrinking. The US Geological Survey reported that its snout moved back more than a kilometre between 1936 and 1996, and it has kept retreating since. Pilgrims who walk up to Gaumukh today stop farther upstream than their grandparents did.

At Devprayag the green Bhagirathi meets the muddier Alaknanda, and you can see the two colours side by side for a short stretch. From here on, the joined river is the Ganga. It runs through Rishikesh and leaves the hills at Haridwar, where much of its water is sent into canals to irrigate fields.

Slow and wide across the plains

Below Haridwar, the river slows down. It now flows through the Indo-Gangetic plain, a vast stretch of soft soil laid down by Himalayan rivers over millions of years. This soil is why so much wheat, rice and sugarcane grows here.

At Prayagraj the Yamuna joins it, and every twelve years crowds gather there for the Kumbh Mela. Further east is Varanasi, where steps called ghats run down to the water for about 6 km. In Bihar, rivers such as the Gandak and the Kosi pour in from Nepal. The Kosi changes its course so often that it has earned the name "Sorrow of Bihar".

Who lives in the river

The Ganga river dolphin is India's national aquatic animal. It is almost blind and finds fish by sending out clicks and listening to the echoes. A 2025 survey counted about 6,300 river dolphins in the Ganga and nearby rivers. The river also holds gharials, a long-snouted crocodile that eats only fish, and freshwater turtles.

Pollution is a real problem. Sewage from cities and waste from factories flow in, and dams take water out. The government's Namami Gange programme, started in 2014, builds sewage plants along the banks. Scientists say some stretches are cleaner, while others still need a lot of work.

Into the delta and the sea

At Farakka, in West Bengal, a barrage splits the flow. One branch, the Hooghly, runs south past Kolkata. The main river flows into Bangladesh, where it is called the Padma. There it meets the Brahmaputra and then the Meghna.

Together they build the Ganga Delta, the largest river delta in the world. Its seaward edge is the Sundarbans, a maze of mangrove islands where tigers swim between the trees and the tide rises and falls twice a day. Every year these rivers carry about a billion tonnes of mud to the sea, so the coast keeps slowly changing shape. The ice that melted at Gaumukh months ago has finally become salt water.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Ganga flows from one single spring at Gangotri town.

Truth: The water comes from the Gangotri Glacier, about 18 km beyond the town, and from many other glaciers, streams and monsoon rains. Big tributaries such as the Yamuna, Ghaghara, Gandak and Kosi add much more water along the way.
