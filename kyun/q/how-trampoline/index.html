Why do trampolines make you bounce?

Olympic trampolinists fly as high as a three-storey building, and every bit of that height comes from their own legs, stored in springs for a split second.

The short answer

When you land on a trampoline, the mat sinks and stretches dozens of springs around the edge. Those springs store the energy of your fall, a bit like a bow being drawn back. A split second later they snap back to their normal length and fling the mat, and you, upward.

On hard ground, most of the energy of landing is lost in your knees, the ground and the sound of your feet. A trampoline wastes much less. And if you push down with your legs just as the mat reaches its lowest point, you add new energy, so you go higher than before.

Surprising but true

During the Second World War, the US Navy used trampolines to train pilots, helping them get used to twisting and tumbling in the air.

Energy on a round trip

At the top of a bounce, you are moving slowly and are full of stored "height energy". As you fall, gravity turns that into speed. When you hit the mat, the speed turns into stretch energy in the springs. Then the springs turn it back into speed, and the speed back into height.

If nothing were lost, you would bounce to the same height forever. In real life, every bounce loses a little energy as heat and noise. So jumpers bend their knees and push hard at the bottom of each bounce, topping up the energy each time, like pushing a swing at the right moment.

Why a trampoline beats a floor

Jump on a concrete floor and you stop in a few hundredths of a second. That sudden stop jolts your bones and wastes energy. On a trampoline, you slow down over a much longer time, perhaps a fifth of a second, as the mat sinks.

That longer, softer stop means your legs have more time to push. It's the same reason a high jumper lands on a thick mat and a cricketer pulls the hands back when catching a fast ball. Spreading out a stop makes it gentler.

Invented by a teenage gymnast

In the 1930s, George Nissen was a teenage gymnast in Cedar Rapids, Iowa. He watched trapeze artists at the circus drop into safety nets and bounce back up, and thought the bounce looked more fun than the act. With his coach, Larry Griswold, he built a frame of scrap iron with canvas stretched across it.

Nissen named it after "trampolín", the Spanish word for a diving board, which he heard while touring Mexico. He sold trampolines for decades. To advertise them, he once bounced alongside a kangaroo in New York.

During the Second World War, the US Navy used trampolines to train pilots and navigators, teaching them to stay aware of which way was up while flipping in the air.

Olympic flyers

Trampoline became an Olympic sport at the Sydney Games in 2000. Elite athletes bounce on very springy beds, reaching about 8 to 10 metres, roughly the height of a three-storey building. At the top, they have a little over two seconds in the air to fit in somersaults and twists.

Judges score the difficulty of the moves, how neatly they are done, how long the jumper spends in the air and how close they stay to the centre of the bed. Drift too far and you lose points, or worse, land on the frame.

Bouncing safely

Most trampoline injuries happen when more than one person jumps at once. A lighter child can be launched sideways when a heavier person lands at the same moment. Safety nets, padding over the springs and one jumper at a time cut the risk a lot.

Nature had the idea long before Nissen. A kangaroo's leg tendons stretch like springs every time it lands, storing energy and giving it back, so a hopping kangaroo uses surprisingly little effort as it speeds up.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A trampoline adds extra energy to your jump, which is why you go so high.

Truth: A trampoline cannot create energy. It stores the energy of your fall and your leg push in its springs and gives most of it back. You go higher on a trampoline mainly because it wastes less energy than the ground and gives you more time to push.

Try this

Drop a tennis ball and a ball of clay from the same height onto a hard floor and watch which bounces higher. Then drop the tennis ball onto a pillow. Where did the "bounce energy" go in each case? A trampoline is built to give back as much energy as possible.
