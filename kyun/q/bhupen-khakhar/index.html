Who was Bhupen Khakhar?

One of India's most celebrated modern painters spent his mornings as an accountant, adding up other people's bills, and painted in the afternoons.

The short answer

Bhupen Khakhar was an Indian painter, born in Mumbai in 1934, who became famous for colourful, witty pictures of ordinary life. He painted barbers, tailors, watch repairers and men eating sweets, the kind of people most artists of his time ignored.

Khakhar was largely self-taught and worked as an accountant for much of his life. His style mixed Indian bazaar prints and calendar pictures with ideas from Western Pop Art and old Italian paintings. Later, he painted his own life with great honesty, including his love for other men, at a time when few Indian artists dared. He died in Baroda in 2003, and his work is now in major museums around the world.

Surprising but true

Bhupen Khakhar kept working part-time as a chartered accountant in Baroda for years, even after museums around the world were showing his paintings.

The accountant who painted

Khakhar grew up in a Gujarati family in Bombay. He studied commerce and qualified as a chartered accountant, a job that involves checking companies' accounts and taxes.

Art pulled at him, though. In the late 1950s he met the painter and poet Gulammohammed Sheikh, who encouraged him to come to Baroda. There, in the early 1960s, Khakhar studied art criticism at the city's famous Faculty of Fine Arts. He taught himself to paint, often working late, and kept a part-time accounting job for years. The two worlds fed each other. Through his work he knew the offices, shops and streets where ordinary people spent their days, and those became his subjects.

Painting the everyday

In the 1970s Khakhar found his own style. His paintings were bright and flat, with sugary pinks, greens and yellows, and they often looked deliberately simple. One famous work, Janata Watch Repairing (1972), shows a watch repairer at his little shop counter, surrounded by clocks. Another, Man Eating Jalebee (1975), is just what it says.

He borrowed from many places. The cheap printed pictures of gods sold in Indian markets gave him strong colours and outlines. Western Pop artists such as Andy Warhol showed him that everyday life could be art. Early Italian paintings, with small figures scattered across a landscape, taught him to tell many stories in one picture.

Who was Andy Warhol?In 1962, Andy Warhol showed 32 paintings of soup cans, one for each flavour, and helped turn supermarket shelves into gallery art.

A braver kind of honesty

In the 1980s, Khakhar's paintings became more personal. He began to show his own feelings openly, including love between men. In India at the time, such relationships were against the law. A painting from 1981, You Can't Please All, shows a man standing on a balcony looking down at an old folk tale being acted out below. Many critics see it as Khakhar telling the world he would live and paint as himself.

Some people were shocked. Others admired his courage. India's Supreme Court ended the law against same-sex relationships in 2018, fifteen years after his death, and many writers now point to Khakhar as an early, quiet voice for equality.

Words, friends and late works

Khakhar also wrote short stories and plays in Gujarati, often funny and gently satirical. He was a sociable man, with friends among artists and writers in India and Britain, and he exhibited regularly in Europe from the 1970s.

In his last years he became ill with cancer and painted his own body and fears in watercolour, with the same frankness as before. India gave him the Padma Shri in 1984, and the Netherlands gave him the Prince Claus Award in 2000.

In 2016, Tate Modern in London held a large show of his work, named after his famous painting. Visitors who knew little about Indian art found themselves smiling at barbers, tailors and jalebi-eaters painted half a century earlier in Baroda. Alongside painters like S.H. Raza and M.F. Husain, he had helped give Indian modern art its many different voices.

Myth or fact?

Myth: To be a real artist, you have to go to art school from a young age.

Truth: Khakhar had no full-time art training as a child. He was largely self-taught, started painting seriously in his late twenties and still became one of India's best-known artists.

Try this

Pick a shop or stall you pass often, like a tailor, a vegetable seller or a cycle repair shop. Draw it from memory, with the shopkeeper at work, the signboard and the things on the shelves. Use bright, flat colours and don't worry about perfect perspective, just like Khakhar.
