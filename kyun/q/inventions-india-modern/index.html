What has India invented recently?

In December 2025 alone, Indians made about 21.6 billion payments with UPI, a system built in India that lets you pay by scanning a code.

The short answer

Ancient India gave the world the zero, chess and the decimal number system. Modern India is still inventing, often things that are cheap, tough and useful to millions of people at once.

In the past few decades, India has built UPI, a way of paying by phone that now handles billions of payments a month. Indian doctors and craftsmen made the Jaipur Foot, a low-cost artificial leg. ISRO landed a spacecraft near the Moon's south pole. And village inventors have made clay fridges, cheap sanitary pads and machines that help farmers. A common thread is doing more with less, sometimes called jugaad.

Surprising but true

The Jaipur Foot, an artificial leg made in India in 1969, costs a tiny fraction of many Western artificial limbs and lets people squat, sit cross-legged and walk barefoot.

Paying with a scan

UPI, the Unified Payments Interface, was launched in 2016 by the National Payments Corporation of India. It lets you send money from one bank to another in seconds, using just a phone number or by scanning a QR code. Street vendors, autorickshaw drivers and big shops all use it.

By December 2025, Indians were making about 21.6 billion UPI payments in a single month, more than two and a half payments for every person on Earth. Other countries, from Singapore to France, have linked up with UPI. The step-by-step workings are in the story of UPI payments.

A foot for walking barefoot

In 1969, in Jaipur, the surgeon P. K. Sethi teamed up with a craftsman named Ram Chandra Sharma to make a better artificial foot. Western limbs were expensive and built for people wearing shoes and sitting on chairs. Many Indians walk barefoot, squat and sit cross-legged.

The Jaipur Foot was made of rubber and wood, cheap and flexible, and fitted in a single day. A charity in Jaipur has given it free to well over a million people in many countries, including landmine survivors. It changed what artificial limbs could look like.

Cheap and clever space missions

In 2014, India's Mars Orbiter Mission, called Mangalyaan, reached Mars on its first attempt. It cost about 450 crore rupees, less than the Hollywood space film Gravity. In 2023, Chandrayaan-3 landed near the Moon's south pole, the first mission to land in that region. Its little rover, Pragyan, drove across the surface and tested the soil.

ISRO is also known for launching many satellites at once. In 2017, a single PSLV rocket put 104 satellites into orbit. The full list sits with ISRO's missions.

Inventors from villages

Not all inventions come from labs. In Gujarat, the potter Mansukhbhai Prajapati made Mitticool, a fridge made of clay that keeps vegetables and water cool with no electricity, using the cooling effect of water evaporating from the clay.

In Tamil Nadu, Arunachalam Muruganantham built a simple machine to make low-cost sanitary pads, so women in villages could make and sell them. His story, told in the Padman film, made him famous.

India's National Innovation Foundation hunts for inventors like these in villages, and has collected many thousands of grassroots ideas.

What comes next

Indian scientists and companies also produced COVID-19 vaccines, including Covaxin, developed in India in 2020 and 2021. India's satellite navigation system NavIC gives location signals over India. Start-ups work on electric scooters, cheap medical tests and farm drones.

Many of these began with a simple question: how can this work for a family that has very little money? That question also produced Aadhaar, which now holds identity records for over a billion people, and which raised its own big debates about privacy.

Myth or fact?

Myth: India's inventions all happened long ago, like zero and chess.

Truth: India is still inventing today. Digital payments, low-cost artificial limbs, cheap space missions, vaccines and simple tools for farmers have all come out of India in the last few decades.

Try this

Make a mini clay-pot cooler. Put a small cup of water inside a bigger unglazed clay pot or flowerpot, fill the gap with damp sand and cover it with a wet cloth. After a few hours, compare the water's coolness with a cup left beside it. That is the same idea behind the Mitticool fridge.
