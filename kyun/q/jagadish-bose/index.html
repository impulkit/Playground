How did Jagadish Chandra Bose study plants and radio?

In 1890s Calcutta, a physics teacher sent invisible waves through walls, then turned his instruments on plants to watch them twitch.

The short answer

Jagadish Chandra Bose was a physicist and plant scientist who worked in Calcutta, now Kolkata, about 130 years ago. In the 1890s he built some of the earliest equipment for sending and detecting very short radio waves, the kind now used in radar, Wi-Fi and 5G phones. He did this in a small room at his college, often with tools made by a local tinsmith.

Later he turned to plants. Using machines he designed himself, he showed that plants respond to touch, heat and light with tiny electrical signals and movements. That work helped open up the study of how plants sense the world.

Surprising but true

Bose's crescograph could magnify the growth of a plant about 10,000 times, making a shoot's tiny movements visible within minutes.

A teacher who would not be paid less

Bose was born in 1858 in Mymensingh, in what is now Bangladesh. He studied in Calcutta, then at Cambridge and London. In 1885 he became a professor of physics at Presidency College, Calcutta.

At that time, Indian professors were paid less than British ones for the same job. Bose refused to accept the lower salary, and for three years he taught without taking any pay at all. In the end the college gave in and paid him in full, including the missing years.

Waves through walls

In 1894, Bose began experimenting with electric waves after reading about the German scientist Heinrich Hertz. He made waves only a few millimetres to a few centimetres long, far shorter than those used in early radio. He built lenses, prisms and detectors to bend and catch them.

In a public demonstration in Calcutta, he is reported to have sent waves through walls and a human body to set off a bell some distance away. He also invented sensitive detectors, including one made with a crystal of galena, a lead mineral. His 1904 American patent for it is one of the earliest for a semiconductor device, the family of materials that now fills every computer chip.

Bose chose not to make money from most of his inventions. He believed science should be shared. In 2012 the IEEE, the world's largest engineering society, honoured his millimetre-wave experiments as a landmark in the history of electronics.

Listening to plants

Around 1900, Bose noticed that his metal detectors seemed to "tire" and recover, much like a muscle. He wondered whether living things, including plants, behaved in a similar way. He built delicate instruments to find out.

His crescograph magnified a plant's growth about 10,000 times. He showed that plants move and respond in measurable ways. A touch-me-not leaf folds when a signal passes along it. Shoots slow down when chilled and react quickly to some chemicals. Some of his claims went too far, but modern scientists studying electrical signals in plants often cite his work.

A laboratory and a legacy

In 1917, Bose opened the Bose Institute in Kolkata, one of India's first modern research centres. It still runs today. He was a close friend of the poet Rabindranath Tagore, and the two wrote to each other about science, art and India's future.

Bose also wrote one of the earliest science fiction stories in Bengali, about a man who stops a cyclone with a bottle of hair oil. A crater on the Moon is named after him. The tiny radio waves he studied in a Calcutta classroom now carry phone calls and video in cities across the world.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Bose proved that plants feel pain the way animals do.

Truth: Bose showed that plants send electrical signals when they are touched, cut or heated, which was a real discovery. But plants have no brain or nerves, and biologists do not think they feel pain as we do. Bose sometimes described his results in ways that went further than his measurements.

Try this

If you can find a touch-me-not plant (chhui-mui, or Mimosa pudica, sold in many garden shops), gently touch one leaf with a finger and time how long it takes to open again. Try touching a leaf at the tip and at the base. Bose measured the tiny electrical signals that travel through this plant when it folds.
