What does science say about yoga?

Scientists have run hundreds of studies on yoga. Some of the clearest results are for something very ordinary, an aching lower back.

The short answer

Science says yoga is a good form of exercise that can help with some specific problems, but it is not a cure-all. Careful studies show it can improve flexibility, balance and strength. It can ease long-lasting lower back pain about as well as other exercise. Many people say it lowers stress and helps them sleep, and some studies agree, though the effects are usually modest.

Bigger claims, like yoga curing diabetes, heart disease or cancer, are not backed by strong evidence. That doesn't make yoga useless. It means its benefits are real but more ordinary than some advertisements say.

Surprising but true

The idea of yoga as a sequence of physical poses is fairly modern. Older yoga texts focus far more on breathing, stillness and meditation than on poses.

Very old, quite new

Yoga began in ancient India as a spiritual practice. The Yoga Sutras, a collection of short teachings linked to the sage Patanjali, are roughly 1,600 to 2,000 years old. Scholars still argue about the exact date. They describe yoga mostly as training the mind through ethics, breathing, concentration and meditation. Poses, called asanas, get only a few lines.

Long sequences of poses developed much later, especially in the last few hundred years. In the early 1900s, teachers in places like Mysore mixed old practices with gymnastics and exercise ideas. Swami Vivekananda had already introduced yoga's spiritual ideas to American audiences in the 1890s. Later Indian teachers took posture-based yoga to Europe and America, where it became hugely popular.

Who was Swami Vivekananda?In 1893 a young Indian monk walked onto a stage in Chicago, began with "Sisters and brothers of America", and the crowd of thousands rose to applaud.

What studies find

Testing yoga is tricky. You can't give someone fake yoga the way you can give a fake pill, so people know if they're doing it. Many studies are small and short. Researchers try to compare yoga with other exercise, to see if it does anything special.

The best results are for chronic lower back pain, balance and flexibility. Yoga also seems to help some people with mild anxiety and low mood, alongside other care. Slow breathing may calm the heart rate for a while, which may explain why people feel relaxed after a session. Some benefits may come from simply moving, resting and expecting to feel better, which is partly a placebo effect.

Scientists in India and elsewhere also study pranayama, the breathing exercises of yoga. Slow, deep breathing can lower heart rate and blood pressure for a short time. Whether years of practice give lasting benefits is still being tested in larger trials. India's Ministry of AYUSH, set up in 2014, funds some of this research.

Mind and body

Many yoga classes end with quiet breathing or lying still. That part is close to mindfulness, which trains attention to stay with the present moment. Studies suggest mindfulness can reduce stress a little for many people.

Yoga also teaches body awareness. Holding a pose makes you notice how you stand, sit and breathe. That can help with posture, though slouching is not as dangerous as people sometimes claim.

A world practice

In 2014 the United Nations declared 21 June the International Day of Yoga, after India proposed it. The date is the longest day of the year in the northern hemisphere. In 2016 UNESCO added yoga to its list of the world's living cultural heritage. On the first International Day of Yoga, in 2015, nearly 36,000 people did yoga together on Rajpath in New Delhi, which set a Guinness world record for the largest yoga lesson.

Yoga is safe for most people, but it can cause injuries if poses are pushed too hard, especially twists and headstands. A trained teacher helps, especially for beginners, who often try to copy poses they see online before their bodies are ready. Anyone with a health condition should check with a doctor first. Done gently, it is one more way to keep a body moving, whether in a park in Pune or a gym in Portland.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Yoga alone can cure serious illnesses like diabetes or cancer.

Truth: There's no good evidence for that. Yoga may help some people feel better, sleep better or move more easily, but serious illness needs a doctor's treatment. Yoga can sit alongside it.

Try this

Try a simple breathing test. Sit still and count your breaths for one minute. Then breathe slowly for two minutes, in through your nose for a count of four and out for a count of six. Count your breaths for another minute and notice whether you feel any different. Write down both numbers.
