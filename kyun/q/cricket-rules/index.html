How does cricket actually work?

A cricket pitch is 22 yards long, about the length of two yellow school buses parked end to end, and nearly every rule starts there.

The short answer

Cricket is a bat-and-ball game between two teams of 11. One team bats and tries to score runs. The other bowls and fields, and tries to get the batters out. Then the teams swap.

In the middle of a big oval field is a strip called the pitch, 22 yards long. At each end stand three wooden stumps with two small bails on top. This is the wicket. A bowler runs up and bowls the ball at one wicket, and a batter guards it with a flat wooden bat. Whichever team scores more runs, by the rules of that format, wins.

Surprising but true

A Test match can last five full days and still end in a draw, with neither team winning.

Scoring runs

There are always two batters on the field, one at each end. When the striker hits the ball, both can run to swap ends. Each swap is one run. If the ball reaches the boundary rope after bouncing, it is four runs. If it flies over the rope without touching the ground, it is six.

Bowlers can also give away extra runs. A wide is a ball too far from the batter to hit. A no-ball is usually when the bowler's front foot lands over the line. Both add a run, and the ball has to be bowled again.

Getting out

A batter is out if the bowler knocks the bails off the stumps (bowled), or if a fielder catches the ball before it touches the ground (caught). They are run out if a fielder breaks the wicket while they are running and not yet safe in their crease.

There is also LBW, or leg before wicket. If the ball would have hit the stumps but strikes the batter's pad first, the umpire can give them out. LBW is the rule fans argue about most. In big matches, the Decision Review System uses ball tracking to show where the ball would have gone. When ten batters are out, the innings is over, because the last batter has no partner left to run with.

Overs and formats

The bowling comes in overs of six balls. After each over, a different bowler takes over from the other end. The length of a match depends on the format.

  • - Test cricket: each team bats twice, over up to five days. If time runs out before a result, it is a draw.
  • - One Day Internationals (ODIs): each team bats once for up to 50 overs.
  • - T20: each team bats for 20 overs, and a match takes about three and a half hours. Leagues like the IPL use this format.

Shorter formats reward big hitting. Test cricket rewards patience, because the side batting second can't just chase a target. It has to bowl the other team out twice.

The fielding side

The captain places nine fielders around the ground, along with the bowler and the wicketkeeper, who crouches behind the stumps in big gloves. Fielding positions have odd names like slip, gully, silly point and fine leg. A captain moves them to suit each bowler and each batter.

Bowlers come in two main kinds. Fast bowlers rely on speed, bounce and swing, and the best top 150 km/h. Spin bowlers bowl slower but twist the ball with their fingers or wrist so it turns sharply after bouncing.

Cricket and the United States

For Americans, cricket can look a little like baseball. Both have a bowler or pitcher and a batter, but in cricket the batter doesn't have to run after every hit, and can bat for hours. The USA co-hosted the 2024 men's T20 World Cup, and cricket will be part of the 2028 Olympics in Los Angeles.

Myth or fact?

Myth: LBW means the ball hit the batter's leg, so they are out.

Truth: Leg before wicket has several conditions. The ball must be pitched in line or outside off stump, hit the batter in line with the stumps (in most cases), and be going on to hit the stumps. A ball that would have missed is not out, even if it hits the pad.

Try this

Measure out 20 metres in a corridor, park or playground with a measuring tape or by counting big steps, and mark both ends. Stand at one end and roll a ball to a friend at the other. That is how far a bowler sends the ball, and how little time a batter has to react to a fast one.
