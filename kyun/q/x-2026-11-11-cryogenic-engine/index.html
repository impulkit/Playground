What is a cryogenic rocket engine?

The top stage of India's GSLV rocket carries hydrogen at minus 253 °C, more than 160 degrees colder than the coldest spot ever measured in Antarctica.

The short answer

Hydrogen and oxygen make one of the most powerful rocket fuels there is. But as gases they are bulky and light, so a rocket could never carry enough.

Cool them until they turn into liquids, and they shrink enormously. Hydrogen only becomes liquid at about minus 253 °C, and oxygen at about minus 183 °C. A rocket stage that uses fuels this cold is called cryogenic, from Greek words meaning "producing cold".

Surprising but true

When liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen burn together in a rocket engine, the main thing that comes out of the nozzle is hot water vapour.

Why it's in the news

On 10 November 2026, ISRO announced that it had tested the cryogenic engine for its next GSLV rocket, called GSLV-F19. The test took place on 5 November at the ISRO Propulsion Complex in Mahendragiri, Tamil Nadu. The engine fired for 200 seconds, and it performed just as engineers had predicted.

The engine will now be fitted into the rocket's top stage. GSLV-F19 will carry NVS-04, a new satellite for NavIC, India's own satellite navigation system, which helps phones, ships and planes work out where they are.

Making gases into liquids

Every gas turns into a liquid if you make it cold enough. Water vapour becomes water below 100 °C. Oxygen needs minus 183 °C. Hydrogen, the lightest of all, needs minus 253 °C, only 20 degrees above the coldest temperature possible.

Keeping fuel that cold is hard. Tanks need thick insulation, metals can turn brittle, and the liquids slowly boil away, which is why rockets are filled only in the final hours before launch. Your fridge uses a gentler version of the same science.

Why go to all this trouble?

When hydrogen burns with oxygen, it releases a huge amount of energy for its weight. A cryogenic engine gives more push, or thrust, from each kilogram of fuel than most other rocket fuels. That extra power lets a rocket lift heavy satellites high above Earth.

Very few countries have mastered this. ISRO worked for many years to build its own cryogenic stage, and it first flew successfully on GSLV-D5 in January 2014. Now GSLV and India's bigger LVM3 rocket both use cryogenic stages.

What happens next

The engine will be joined to the rest of GSLV-F19 at Sriharikota. Once NVS-04 reaches space, NavIC will have another satellite to make India's own navigation signals stronger.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The white clouds pouring off a rocket before launch are smoke from a fire.

Truth: They are mostly water vapour from the air, turning into mist as it touches the super-cold fuel tanks. It is the same thing you see around an ice cube on a hot day, only much bigger.

Try this

Put a few ice cubes in a dry steel glass and wait a few minutes. Watch drops form on the outside. Water vapour in the air turns to liquid when it touches something cold. Rocket engineers do the same trick on a much colder scale to turn gases into liquids.
