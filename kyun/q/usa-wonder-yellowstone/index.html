What is under Yellowstone?

The world's first national park sits on top of a supervolcano, and its hot springs gave us a tool used in COVID-19 tests.

The short answer

Under Yellowstone National Park lies one of the largest volcanic systems on Earth. Several kilometres down sits a huge reservoir of hot, partly molten rock. Its heat powers the park's geysers, hot springs, bubbling mud pots and steaming vents.

Three times in the past 2.1 million years, Yellowstone erupted with enormous force. The last giant eruption, about 631,000 years ago, left a caldera, a collapsed bowl about 72 km long and 48 km wide (45 by 30 miles). Much of the park sits inside it. Today the volcano is quiet but alive. The ground rises and falls a few centimetres, and small earthquakes happen almost every day.

Surprising but true

Yellowstone holds roughly half of all the geysers on Earth, more than 500 of them.

A sponge of hot rock

People often picture a lake of lava under the park. Scientists using earthquake waves, rather like an ultrasound scan, have found something different. The magma reservoir starts about 5 km down and is mostly solid rock. Only around 5–15% of it is molten, trapped between hot crystals like water in a sponge.

There's a deeper source too. Hot rock rises from far below, perhaps hundreds of kilometres down, as a plume. It is a bit like a blowtorch held under North America as the continent slowly slides over it.

The three giant blasts

Yellowstone's biggest eruption, about 2.1 million years ago, threw out roughly 2,450 cubic kilometres of rock and ash. That is enough to bury the whole of Delhi more than a kilometre deep. It was thousands of times larger than the 1980 eruption of Mount St. Helens.

Ash from these eruptions blew across much of what is now the USA. India has its own record of such giants. About 74,000 years ago, Toba in Indonesia erupted, and a layer of its ash can still be found in parts of India. Further back, the Deccan Traps poured lava over western India for hundreds of thousands of years.

Half the world's geysers

Yellowstone has more than 10,000 hot springs, geysers, mud pots and steam vents. Over 500 of them are geysers, roughly half the world's total. Rain and snowmelt seep underground, get heated by the hot rock, and burst back up. You can see how pressure builds in geysers and hot springs.

Old Faithful erupts roughly every 60 to 110 minutes, shooting water up to about 55 m high. Steamboat Geyser, also in the park, is the world's tallest active geyser and can blast water over 90 m into the air, higher than a 25-storey building.

Rainbow pools and tiny life

Grand Prismatic Spring is about 110 m across, longer than a football field. Its centre is deep blue, ringed by bands of yellow, orange and red. Those colours come from mats of microbes that love scalding water.

In 1966 scientist Thomas Brock found a bacterium in one of these hot springs. It is called Thermus aquaticus. It carries a protein that keeps working at temperatures that would destroy most living things. Today that protein is used to copy DNA millions of times in labs, including in many COVID-19 tests.

The first national park

In 1872, President Ulysses S. Grant signed a law making Yellowstone the world's first national park. The land had long been home or hunting ground to many Native American nations, including the Shoshone, Crow and Blackfeet, a history often left out of early stories.

The idea spread. Countries everywhere, including India, now protect wild land in national parks. Yellowstone's bison, bears and elk still roam above the sleeping volcano. Its bison herd is one of the few in the USA that has lived wild in the same place since prehistoric times. Wolves were wiped out here in the 1920s, then brought back in 1995, and the story of what happened next surprised scientists.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Yellowstone is overdue for a super-eruption.

Truth: Volcanoes don't run on a timetable, and the gaps between Yellowstone's big eruptions were not equal. The US Geological Survey puts the chance of a super-eruption in any one year at about 1 in 730,000, and sees no signs that one is coming.

Make a model geyser with an adult. Fill a plastic bottle with water, add a drop of food colouring and squeeze it hard in a sink or garden. Then think about how Yellowstone's underground heat builds pressure in a similar way, only much more slowly.
