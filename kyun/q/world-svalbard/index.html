What is the seed vault in Svalbard?

Inside a frozen Arctic mountain, more than a million packets of seeds wait at minus 18 °C, a backup copy of the world's crops.

The short answer

The Svalbard Global Seed Vault is a giant freezer dug into a mountain on Spitsbergen, an island in Norway's Svalbard islands, far north in the Arctic Ocean. Inside are seeds of rice, wheat, beans, millets and thousands of other crops from almost every country.

It isn't meant to be used every day. It is a backup. Countries keep their own seed banks, but those can be hit by floods, fires, wars or broken freezers. If that happens, a spare copy is waiting in Svalbard. By the end of 2025, it held about 13.7 lakh seed samples from 131 depositors.

Surprising but true

The first seeds ever taken back out of the vault, in 2015, were sent to replace a seed bank damaged by war in Syria.

Why put it here?

Svalbard is about 1,300 km from the North Pole. The ground stays frozen all year, a layer called permafrost. Even if the electricity failed, the rock around the vault would stay well below freezing for a long time.

The vault sits about 130 m above sea level, high enough to stay dry even if all the ice on Earth melted. It is also far from wars and big cities. Norway runs it for free, together with the Crop Trust and a Nordic gene bank called NordGen.

Inside the mountain

From outside, all you see is a narrow concrete wedge sticking out of the snow. At night its top glows with an artwork of mirrors and lights. Behind the door, a tunnel runs more than 100 m into the rock.

At the end are three halls, kept at minus 18 °C. Seeds are sealed in foil packets, packed in plain boxes and stacked on shelves like books in a library. Each sample usually holds about 500 seeds. At this cold, many seeds can stay alive for decades or centuries.

Scientists have even started an experiment to find out exactly how long. In 2008 they sealed packets of 13 crops in the vault, planning to test a few every five years, for a hundred years. The last packets will be opened in 2108. Some seeds, like sorghum and pea, may last for thousands of years at this cold.

The day seeds came out

For years, a research centre in Aleppo, Syria, held a huge collection of wheat, barley and lentil seeds adapted to dry lands. When war broke out there, scientists couldn't keep it running. In 2015, they asked for their backup copies from Svalbard.

The seeds were sent to new gene banks in Lebanon and Morocco, planted, and grown into fresh seed. Some were later sent back to the vault. It was the first time anyone had needed it, and it worked as planned.

India's seeds in the Arctic

India's gene banks and research centres also send seeds. ICRISAT, a crop research institute near Hyderabad, has deposited samples of chickpea, sorghum, pigeon pea and millets, crops that feed millions of people in dry regions. India also runs a research station called Himadri in Svalbard, where its scientists study Arctic weather and glaciers.

Many of these seeds come from old local varieties that farmers grew for generations. Some may hold the secret to surviving heat, drought or new diseases.

A town with polar bears

The nearest town, Longyearbyen, has around 2,500 people, a school, a university centre and a supermarket. Polar bears live on the islands, so anyone leaving town is advised to carry protection and travel with a guide. From late October to mid-February the sun never rises.

Svalbard is also unusual politically. Under a treaty from 1920, it belongs to Norway, but citizens of the countries that signed it can live and work there without a visa. That is why Russian miners, Thai cooks and Indian scientists can all be found on the same Arctic islands.

In 2017, unusually warm weather sent meltwater into the entrance tunnel. No seeds were harmed, but the tunnel was rebuilt. It was a small sign of how climate change is warming the Arctic faster than almost anywhere else.

Myth or fact?

Myth: It is against the law to die in Svalbard.

Truth: There is no such law. But bodies buried in the frozen ground do not decay, so burials in Longyearbyen stopped long ago, and people who are very ill or old are usually flown to mainland Norway.

Try this

Make a mini seed bank. Collect seeds from fruits and vegetables you eat, such as tomato, lemon or pumpkin. Dry them on paper for a week, then store them in labelled paper envelopes in a cool, dry jar. Plant a few after a month and see which ones still sprout.
