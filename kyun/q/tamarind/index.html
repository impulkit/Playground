What is tamarind?

Its name means "date of India", but the tamarind tree probably came from Africa, and its sour pulp can polish a dull brass lamp until it shines.

The short answer

Tamarind is the fruit of a large, shady tree. Its curved brown pods hold a sticky, dark pulp around shiny seeds. The pulp is sour, a little sweet and very tangy, and cooks across India use it to give food a sharp, fruity sourness.

It goes into sambar and rasam in the south, into the sweet-sour chutney served with samosas and chaat in the north, and into the spicy water of pani puri. Thai, Mexican, Caribbean and many African kitchens use it too. Tamarind trees probably came from Africa long ago, but India now grows more tamarind than any other country.

Surprising but true

Tamarind is a legume, in the same plant family as peas, beans and peanuts. Its brown pods are just giant, sticky bean pods.

A bean pod in disguise

The tamarind tree belongs to the legume family, the same family as peas, beans and peanuts. Its pods are a kind of bean pod, but instead of soft green beans, they're filled with brown pulp held together by tough threads.

Like many plants in the pea family, the tree has feathery leaves made of tiny leaflets that fold up at night. It can grow taller than a three-storey building and live for well over a century. In parts of South India, old tamarind trees line village roads, and families have harvested the same trees for generations.

Tamarind wood is very hard. The seeds, once roasted, can be eaten, and a powder made from them is used in factories to stiffen cotton and jute threads.

From Africa to the Indian kitchen

Botanists think tamarind first grew wild in tropical Africa, perhaps in the drier grasslands of the east. It reached India so long ago that it now seems completely at home. Old Sanskrit texts mention it, and it has been part of Indian cooking for centuries.

Arab traders bought it in Indian ports and called it tamar hindi, "the Indian date", because the pulp looked like dried dates. Europeans heard that name and turned it into "tamarind". Spanish and Portuguese sailors later carried tamarind across the Atlantic. It grows widely in Mexico, where children eat tamarind sweets coated in chilli and drink chilled agua de tamarindo.

Why it tastes sour

Tamarind's sourness comes mainly from tartaric acid, the same acid found in grapes. The pulp also holds a lot of natural sugar, so ripe tamarind tastes sweet and sour at once. Cooks balance it with jaggery, salt and chilli. Unripe green pods are much sharper and are pickled or ground into chutneys.

In Tamil Nadu and Karnataka, tamarind rice, called puliyogare or puli sadam, is a favourite for journeys and temple offerings, because the sourness helps it keep for longer. Many South Indian meals rely on it. Cooks soak a lump of pulp in warm water, then squeeze it to make the thin, sour liquid that goes into rasam. Worcestershire sauce, the dark sauce from England, has tamarind in it too.

A cleaner as well as a food

The same acid that makes tamarind sour can clean metal. Copper and brass slowly darken as they react with air, forming a dull layer on top, a cousin of rust. Rubbing them with tamarind pulp dissolves that layer and brings back the shine. In many Indian homes and temples, brass lamps and pooja vessels are still cleaned this way.

Tamarind trees also feature in folk tales. In some parts of South India, people say ghosts live in them and warn children not to sleep beneath one at night. Scientists would say that's only a story. For many children, though, the best part of a tamarind tree is simpler: a fresh, sour pod, a pinch of salt and chilli, and a face screwed up in delight.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Tamarind must be an Indian plant, since its scientific name is "indica".

Truth: The name records where Europeans and Arabs met it, not where it began. Most botanists think the tree is originally from tropical Africa and reached India in ancient times.

Try this

Ask an adult for a small piece of tamarind and a dull copper or brass coin or object. Rub a little tamarind pulp onto half of it with a cloth, wait a few minutes, then rinse and dry it. Compare the two halves. The acid in tamarind dissolves the dark layer on the metal.
