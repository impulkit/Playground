How do satellites help us every day?

Weather forecasts, maps on your phone, card payments and cricket on TV all depend on machines circling far above your head.

The short answer

Satellites are machines that circle Earth, high above the air. More than 10,000 of them are working right now. They do four big jobs. Weather satellites watch clouds, storms and oceans. Communication satellites carry TV, radio and some phone and internet signals. Navigation satellites, like GPS, help phones, ships and planes work out where they are. And Earth-watching satellites photograph forests, farms, cities and ice.

You may not notice them, but you probably used a satellite today. If you checked the weather, followed a map, watched live sport or paid by card, a satellite was likely somewhere in the chain.

Surprising but true

In 1975 and 1976, India used an American satellite to beam science and farming lessons to TV sets in about 2,400 villages, many of which had never seen television before.

Watching the weather

Before satellites, forecasters could only see weather where there were people to report it. Huge storms could form over the ocean unseen. In 1960 the US launched TIROS-1, the first weather satellite, and its first fuzzy picture of clouds changed forecasting for good.

Today India's INSAT satellites and America's GOES satellites send fresh images every 15 minutes or less. They help forecasters track the monsoon and spot cyclones days before they reach land.

How do computers forecast the weather?In 1922, a scientist dreamed of a giant hall where 64,000 people with pencils would calculate tomorrow's weather. Today one supercomputer does far more, in minutes.

In October 2013, Cyclone Phailin, one of the strongest storms to hit India in years, struck Odisha. Thanks to satellite tracking and good forecasts, authorities moved close to a million people to safety, and far fewer lives were lost than in a similar cyclone in 1999.

Hanging still in the sky

In 1945, the science fiction writer Arthur C. Clarke pointed out something useful. A satellite about 36,000 km above the equator takes exactly one day to circle Earth. Because Earth turns at the same rate, the satellite seems to stay in one spot.

This is called a geostationary orbit. It is why the TV dish on a roof can point at one place and never move. Communication and weather satellites often sit there, each able to see a huge part of the globe at once.

Most other satellites fly much lower, a few hundred to a few thousand kilometres up, and race around Earth in about 90 minutes to two hours. Lower satellites can take sharper pictures. Signals to and from them also arrive with much less delay, which is why new internet satellites fly low.

Television in the villages

In 1975, India borrowed time on an American satellite for a year-long experiment called SITE, the Satellite Instructional Television Experiment. Community TV sets were placed in about 2,400 villages across six states. Every day they showed programmes on farming, health, hygiene and science, many in local languages.

For many villagers it was the first television they had ever seen. The experiment showed that satellites could reach places that cables never would, and it helped persuade India to build its own INSAT satellites. India's satellites now support everything from TV to fishing.

Eyes on Earth

Earth-watching satellites have photographed the planet since the US launched Landsat 1 in 1972. They show farmers how healthy crops are, help track forest fires and floods, measure melting glaciers and rising seas, and help draw accurate maps. ISRO's satellites help India estimate harvests and plan where to dig wells.

Fishing boats along India's coast get messages, based on satellite readings of sea temperature and plankton, about where fish are likely to gather. That saves fuel and time.

Keeping time

Many satellites carry extremely precise clocks. Phone networks, power grids and banks use satellite time signals to keep their computers in step. When you tap a card to pay, a satellite clock may help stamp the exact time of the payment.

Not everything goes through space, though. Most of the world's internet traffic travels through cables, many of them on the sea floor, as you can read in how the internet works.

Myth or fact?

Myth: When you use the internet or make a phone call, the signal usually goes through a satellite.

Truth: Most calls and internet traffic travel through mobile towers and cables, including huge cables on the sea floor that carry the vast majority of data between continents. Satellites help in remote places, on ships and planes, and in disasters.

Try this

With an adult, look up today's satellite cloud picture on the India Meteorological Department website or NOAA's website. Then go outside and look at the sky. Do it every day for a week and see whether the clouds you notice match what the satellite shows over your area.
