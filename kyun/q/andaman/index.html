What is special about the Andaman Islands?

The Andaman and Nicobar Islands are closer to Myanmar and Indonesia than to mainland India, and one of them holds India's only active volcano.

The short answer

The Andaman and Nicobar Islands are a chain of about 836 islands and islets in the Bay of Bengal. Only a few dozen have people living on them. The Andamans lie to the north and the Nicobars to the south. Together they form a union territory of India, though they are far closer to Myanmar, Thailand and Indonesia than to Chennai.

The islands are special for their coral reefs and rainforests, for some of the oldest human communities in the world, and for a painful place in India's freedom struggle. Visitors come for white beaches such as Radhanagar, on Swaraj Dweep, and for clear water full of fish.

Surprising but true

In the 2004 tsunami, several of the islands' oldest communities are reported to have escaped to high ground after reading signs in the sea and the birds.

Reefs and rainforests

Most of the islands are covered in thick tropical forest, with giant trees, orchids and mangroves along the coasts. Under the water, coral reefs ring many islands. Divers see parrotfish, manta rays, turtles and, rarely, the dugong, a gentle sea mammal that grazes on seagrass. The dugong is the territory's official animal.

Saltwater crocodiles live in the creeks and mangroves. Many birds, snakes and plants on the islands are found nowhere else, because they evolved separately from the mainland.

The first islanders

People have lived on the islands for many thousands of years. Several of their oldest communities are still there. The Great Andamanese, Jarawa, Onge and Sentinelese live in the Andamans, and the Shompen and Nicobarese in the Nicobars.

The Sentinelese live on North Sentinel Island and make it clear that they do not want visitors. Indian law forbids anyone from going near the island. This protects them from diseases they have no defence against and lets them live their own way. Some of the other groups are now very small in number, and India tries to protect their forests and way of life.

Kala Pani

The British built a large prison at Port Blair, the old capital, now called Sri Vijaya Puram. The Cellular Jail, completed in 1906, had 693 small cells, so each prisoner was kept alone. Many Indians who fought for freedom were sent there. People called the islands Kala Pani, "black water", because being sent across the sea meant being cut off from home and family.

The jail is now a national memorial. During the Second World War, Japan occupied the islands, and in December 1943 Subhas Chandra Bose raised the Indian flag in Port Blair. Today the islands' story is an important part of India's freedom struggle.

The volcano and the wave

The islands sit near the edge of two plates of Earth's crust. Barren Island, about 140 km from the capital, is India's only confirmed active volcano. It has erupted many times since 1991.

On 26 December 2004, a huge earthquake off Sumatra caused a tsunami. The Nicobar Islands were hit very hard, and many people died. Reports said some indigenous communities, such as the Onge, survived by moving to high ground after noticing changes in the sea, using knowledge passed down through generations.

New names

In 2018, three islands were renamed: Ross Island became Netaji Subhas Chandra Bose Dweep, Neil Island became Shaheed Dweep, and Havelock became Swaraj Dweep. In 2024 Port Blair was renamed Sri Vijaya Puram. Some people welcomed the new names as a way to honour India's freedom fighters. Others were used to the old ones, which had been in use for well over a century. Either way, getting there still takes a two-hour flight or a ship journey of about three days from the mainland.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Andaman Islands lie just off the coast of India.

Truth: They are about 1,200 km from Chennai and Kolkata, across the Bay of Bengal. The southern tip of the Nicobar Islands is only about 150 km from Indonesia's Sumatra.
