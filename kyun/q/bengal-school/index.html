What was the Bengal School of art?

In 1905, as Bengal was split in two by the British, Abanindranath Tagore painted a gentle saffron-robed woman who became an image of India itself.

The short answer

The Bengal School was a group of artists, based in Kolkata from about 1900, who wanted Indian painting to look Indian again. At the time, art schools in British India mostly taught European oil painting. Abanindranath Tagore and his students turned instead to Indian stories, old Indian paintings and soft watercolours.

The movement grew alongside the freedom struggle. For many people, painting in an Indian way was a quiet form of protest. It shaped Indian art for decades and trained artists who went on to teach all over the country.

Surprising but true

Two Japanese painters who visited Kolkata in 1903 helped shape the Bengal School's soft, misty "wash" style.

Abanindranath and a British ally

Abanindranath Tagore was the nephew of the poet Rabindranath Tagore. As a young man he learned European-style painting, but he became more interested in Mughal miniatures he saw in books and museums. In 1902 he painted The Passing of Shah Jahan, which shows the old emperor on his deathbed, gazing across the river at the Taj Mahal he built for his wife. It is small and sad, with fine lines and pale colours, and it showed the direction his art would take.

An unexpected supporter helped him. Ernest Binfield Havell, the British principal of the Government School of Art in Calcutta, believed Indian students were wrongly being taught to copy European art. He invited Abanindranath to teach there in 1905. Together they changed what students learned, and Havell even moved some European paintings out of the school's gallery to make room for Indian ones.

A painting in a time of protest

In 1905 the British government split Bengal into two parts. Many Bengalis saw it as a way to divide and weaken them, and the Swadeshi movement began. People stopped buying British goods and wore Indian cloth instead.

That year, Abanindranath painted a young woman in a saffron robe, with four arms, standing on green ground under a pale sky. She holds a book, a sheaf of rice, a white cloth and prayer beads. First called Banga Mata (Mother Bengal), the painting became known as Bharat Mata, Mother India. It is small, quiet and almost dreamy, yet it was carried in processions and printed widely.

What was the Swadeshi movement?In 1905, people in Bengal lit bonfires of British cloth in the streets and began buying Indian-made goods instead, to protest against their province being cut in two.

Washes from Japan, ideas from Ajanta

In 1902, the Japanese thinker Okakura Kakuzo stayed in Kolkata with the Tagore family. The next year two Japanese painters, Yokoyama Taikan and Hishida Shunso, followed. They showed how to paint with thin layers of watery colour, letting each dry before the next. Abanindranath adopted and changed the method. His pictures often look as if seen through morning haze.

His students looked backward as well as east. Nandalal Bose and Asit Kumar Haldar travelled to copy the ancient murals in the Ajanta caves, where artists had made their own colours from earth and minerals. Their paintings show gods, poets, village women and scenes from the epics, usually in long, flowing lines.

Students who spread out

The school's biggest influence came through its students. Nandalal Bose went on to lead the art school at Santiniketan, called Kala Bhavana. K. Venkatappa took the style to Mysore. Abdur Rahman Chughtai, in Lahore, developed his own graceful version that is admired in Pakistan today.

Abanindranath also wrote much-loved children's books in Bengali, such as Kheerer Putul (The Sugar Doll), and illustrated many of them himself.

The critics

Not everyone loved the Bengal School. By the 1930s, some artists found it too soft, too sentimental and too fond of the past. Amrita Sher-Gil, who had trained in Paris, painted bold, strong figures instead. Jamini Roy turned to the bright, simple shapes of Bengal's village painters.

These arguments were part of a bigger question: what should modern Indian art look like? The Bengal School gave one of the first answers. Even artists who disagreed with it were answering the question it had asked.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Bengal School copied old Indian paintings exactly.

Truth: Its artists borrowed ideas from Ajanta murals, Mughal miniatures and Japanese ink painting, then mixed them into something new. Their aim was a modern Indian style, not a copy of any one past tradition.

Try this

Try a wash painting. Wet a sheet of thick paper with a clean brush, then touch it with very watery paint in one colour so it spreads and blurs. Let it dry, add a second pale layer, and see how the soft edges make the picture look misty.
