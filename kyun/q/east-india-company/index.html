How did a trading company end up ruling India?

A group of London merchants set out to buy pepper. Within 200 years, their company ran its own army and governed most of India.

The short answer

The East India Company was a business set up by London merchants in 1600 to trade in Asian spices. Queen Elizabeth I gave it a monopoly, the sole right among the English, to trade east of Africa.

In India, it built trading posts at Surat, Madras, Bombay and Calcutta, buying cotton cloth, indigo, saltpetre and later tea. As the Mughal Empire weakened in the 1700s, the Company built its own army of Indian soldiers. In 1757 it won the Battle of Plassey in Bengal, and in 1765 it gained the right to collect taxes in Bengal, Bihar and Odisha. Over the next 90 years it took over most of India by war, treaties and annexation. After the revolt of 1857, the British government took control and the Company was later shut down.

Surprising but true

The English word "loot" comes from Hindi, and it entered English during the time of the East India Company.

Asking the emperor's permission

The Company's first ships reached India in 1608. In 1613 the emperor Jahangir allowed it to open a trading post, called a factory, at Surat in Gujarat. An English ambassador, Sir Thomas Roe, spent years at Jahangir's court seeking better terms.

At first, the Company was one trader among many, competing with the Portuguese, the Dutch and later the French. It needed Indian goods far more than India needed English ones. European buyers loved Indian cotton cloth, especially light printed calicoes and chintz.

Three forts, three cities

The Company built Fort St George in Madras, now Chennai, in the 1640s. Bombay, now Mumbai, came to the English Crown in 1661 as part of a royal wedding gift from Portugal, and the Crown rented it to the Company for £10 a year.

In 1690, the Company set up a post on the Hooghly river that grew into Calcutta, now Kolkata. Each of these forts grew into a great city, and today they are among India's largest.

The battle at Plassey

In 1756, the young Nawab of Bengal, Siraj ud-Daulah, captured Calcutta. A year later, Company troops under Robert Clive met his much larger army at Plassey, now Palashi.

Clive had secretly agreed with the Nawab's commander, Mir Jafar, to make him Nawab if he held his troops back. Mir Jafar did, and the Company won easily. In 1765, after another victory at Buxar, the Mughal emperor Shah Alam II granted the Company the right to collect revenue in Bengal, Bihar and Odisha.

Company rule

The Company now had a government's income. By around 1800, its army had grown to roughly 2 lakh soldiers, most of them Indian sepoys. It fought and defeated Mysore under Tipu Sultan, the Marathas and the Sikh kingdom of Punjab.

Its rule brought heavy taxes, and many officials grew rich. In 1770, a terrible famine struck Bengal, and millions died while tax collection continued. In Britain, critics in Parliament attacked the Company's greed. The Company also introduced new courts, surveys, railways and the telegraph, mainly to help its own rule and trade.

The end of the Company

In 1857, Indian soldiers rose up in a great revolt that spread across north India. It was crushed, but it shocked Britain.

In 1858, Parliament took India away from the Company and placed it directly under the British Crown. The Company carried on as a small business for a few years and was closed in 1874. Its story left behind cities, laws and railways, and English words such as "loot", "jungle" and "shampoo", borrowed from Indian languages.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The British government conquered India from the start.

Truth: For about 100 years, it was a private company, with shareholders in London, that conquered and ruled large parts of India. The British government took over directly only in 1858.
