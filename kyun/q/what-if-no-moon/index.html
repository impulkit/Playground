What if Earth had no Moon?

Without the Moon, nights would be far darker, tides would shrink to a third, and a day on Earth might last only 8 to 12 hours.

The short answer

Earth would still be a planet with air, oceans and seasons. But a lot would feel different. Nights would be much darker. Ocean tides would be about a third as high, because the Moon causes about two-thirds of them. There would be no solar eclipses, and no month as we know it.

Over billions of years, the bigger changes would stack up. Our day might be much shorter, and Earth's tilt might swing about more wildly, making seasons far more extreme. Scientists are still arguing about how wild that swing would be.

Surprising but true

The Moon drifts about 3.8 cm further from Earth every year, roughly as fast as your fingernails grow.

A darker sky

A full moon is the brightest thing in our night sky after the Sun. Without it, nights would be dark every single night, like the darkest new-moon night you've ever seen.

Many animals rely on moonlight. Some dung beetles find their way using light from the night sky. Studies of lions in Tanzania found they make more kills on the darkest nights, when prey can't see them coming.

On the Great Barrier Reef, many corals release their eggs together on a few nights after a full moon in spring. It is one of the largest spawning events on Earth. Without the Moon, they would need another signal.

Smaller tides

The Moon's gravity pulls the oceans into two bulges, one facing the Moon and one on the far side. As Earth turns, coasts pass through them, giving high and low tide twice a day. The Sun does this too, but less than half as strongly.

With only the Sun, the sea would still rise and fall, just much less. The rock pools where crabs and starfish live would shrink. Some scientists think tides helped early sea creatures move onto land. Stranded for hours at low tide, they slowly got used to air, an idea explored in how the Moon may have shaped life.

A faster spin

The Moon acts like a gentle brake on Earth. The tides rub against the sea floor, and over time that friction slows our spin. Our day gets longer by about 2 thousandths of a second every century.

Long ago, days were shorter. Growth rings in fossil corals show that about 400 million years ago, a year had around 400 days, each about 22 hours long. Without the Moon braking it, Earth might still spin much faster today. Some estimates suggest a day of only 8 to 12 hours, though nobody can be sure.

A wobbly tilt

Earth leans over at about 23.4 degrees. That tilt gives us seasons. The Moon's pull helps keep the angle steady.

In 1993, a team led by French astronomer Jacques Laskar calculated that without the Moon, Earth's tilt could swing chaotically, from almost upright to lying nearly on its side. That would mean ice ages and scorching summers. Later studies suggested the swings would be slower and smaller than first feared. The debate is still going.

Calendars and festivals

The word "month" comes from "moon". Many calendars still follow it. The Islamic calendar is fully lunar, which is why Eid moves about 11 days earlier each year. Hindu calendars count lunar days called tithis, so Diwali falls on a new-moon night and Holi near a full moon. Chinese New Year follows the Moon too.

Without a Moon, every one of these would have needed a different clock. The Indian calendars we use would look completely unfamiliar.

Could Earth have lost it?

The Moon probably formed about 4.5 billion years ago, when a planet-sized object crashed into the young Earth and splashed rock into space. It wasn't guaranteed. Mercury and Venus have no moons at all.

And one day, very slowly, ours will appear smaller in the sky. As it drifts away, total solar eclipses will end, because the Moon will no longer cover the whole Sun. That will take hundreds of millions of years, and it is the same perfect fit that makes today's eclipses possible.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Moon only affects the ocean, not the land.

Truth: The Moon pulls on solid rock too. The ground under your feet rises and falls by about 20 to 30 cm twice a day, but because everything around you moves with it, you never notice.

Try this

Hold a ball (the Earth) and a small orange (the Moon) at arm's length. Put the orange about 30 Earth-widths away from the ball. That is the real scale gap between them, and it is much wider than most pictures show.
