Why are earthworms a farmer's friend?

Charles Darwin spent forty years studying earthworms, and even played the piano to them.

The short answer

Earthworms turn dead stuff into good soil. They pull fallen leaves and plant scraps down into their burrows and eat them, along with mouthfuls of soil. What comes out the other end, called castings, is rich in nutrients that plants can easily use.

Their tunnels help too. They let air and rainwater soak deep into the ground, so roots can grow and breathe, and the soil doesn't wash away so easily in heavy rain. As worms burrow, they mix the soil, bringing deep earth up and carrying surface leaves down.

A field with lots of earthworms is usually a healthy field. That is why farmers in India and around the world call them the farmer's friend, and why some make compost with them.

Surprising but true

An earthworm has no lungs. It breathes through its damp skin, which is why it must stay moist to survive.

A body built for burrowing

An earthworm is a long tube of rings called segments. Tiny bristles on each segment grip the soil, and muscles squeeze and stretch the body, pushing the worm forward like a wave.

It has no eyes, but its skin can sense light, and it quickly wriggles away from bright sun. It has no lungs either. It breathes through its skin, which must stay damp. Instead of one heart, it has five pairs of pumping tubes near its front end.

Each worm is both male and female. The pale band partway along the body makes a little case for the eggs, which is left in the soil to hatch.

Darwin's favourite animal

Charles Darwin is famous for his ideas about evolution, but he also loved earthworms. For about forty years he studied them in his garden and in pots in his house in England.

He tested whether worms could hear by playing a whistle, a bassoon and the piano to them. They ignored the music. But when he set their pots on top of the piano and struck a note, the worms dived for cover. They felt the vibration.

He also measured how fast worms buried stones, ancient ruins and anything left on the surface. His last book, published in 1881, was all about worms. It showed that these small animals reshape the land over time.

Worms after rain

After heavy rain, worms often crawl out onto paths and roads. People used to think they were escaping drowning. But worms can survive under water for a long time.

Scientists now have other ideas. Damp ground may make it safe for worms to travel across the surface to new places. The drumming of raindrops may also feel like a mole digging, which sends worms fleeing upwards.

In Florida, in the USA, people collect fishing worms by rubbing a wooden stake driven into the ground, a practice called worm grunting. In 2008, a scientist showed that the buzzing feels to worms like a hungry mole, so they wriggle up to escape.

Worm farms in India

Many Indian farmers and households now practise vermicomposting. They feed kitchen scraps, dung and crop waste to worms in pits or tanks. Within weeks, the worms turn it into dark, crumbly compost for fields and gardens.

It is a cheap way to make fertiliser and to deal with food waste. The red wiggler, a small worm that loves rotting food, is a favourite.

Not everywhere a friend

In the northern USA and Canada, ice sheets scraped away the native worms during the last Ice Age. For thousands of years, forests there grew without earthworms. A thick, spongy carpet of fallen leaves built up on the forest floor.

Then settlers brought worms from Europe, in soil around plants and in ships. These worms eat the leaf carpet quickly, which harms wildflowers, salamanders and birds that depend on it. In a vegetable patch they help. In those forests, scientists treat them as invaders.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If you cut an earthworm in half, you get two worms.

Truth: Usually you just get a hurt worm. Sometimes the front half, with the mouth and important organs, can grow a new tail, but the back half cannot grow a new head and soon dies.

Try this

Fill a large clear jar with layers of damp soil and sand, and lay a few dead leaves on top. Gently add two or three earthworms from the garden, wrap the jar in dark paper and keep it cool. After a week, unwrap it and look at the tunnels and the mixed-up layers. Then put the worms back where you found them.
