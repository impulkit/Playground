What was the first film ever shown?

On 28 December 1895, 33 people in a Paris café basement paid one franc each and watched ten films. The whole show lasted about 20 minutes.

The short answer

The first film show with a paying audience, the kind most people mean by "the first film", happened in Paris on 28 December 1895. Two brothers from France, Auguste and Louis Lumière, projected ten short films onto a screen in a basement room called the Salon Indien, below the Grand Café.

Each film was under a minute long. One showed workers leaving a factory, another a baby being fed, another a gardener getting sprayed by his own hose. About 33 people paid one franc to watch. Within weeks, queues stretched down the street.

Surprising but true

Just seven months after the Paris show, the same films were screened in Bombay, at Watson's Hotel, on 7 July 1896.

Before the Lumières

The Lumières were not the first to capture moving pictures. In 1888, a French inventor named Louis Le Prince filmed his family walking in a garden in Leeds, England. That clip, about 2 seconds long, is the oldest surviving moving picture.

In the USA, Thomas Edison's team built the Kinetoscope, a wooden cabinet with a peephole. From 1893, one person at a time could watch a short film inside. In Berlin, the Skladanowsky brothers projected films for an audience on 1 November 1895, weeks before Paris. Their machine was clumsy, though, and the Lumières' design spread round the world.

A camera that did everything

The Lumière brothers ran a big photography factory in Lyon. Their machine, the Cinématographe, weighed only about 5 kg. It could film, print and project all in one box, turned by a hand crank.

Films ran at about 16 pictures a second. Your eye and brain blend the still frames into smooth motion, which is the trick behind why movies seem to move. Their films had no sound and no colour, and almost none of them told a story. People came to see ordinary life on a giant screen: waves on a beach, a street, a boat leaving harbour.

How was photography invented?The oldest surviving photograph from a camera needed hours, perhaps days, of sunlight to appear. It shows a blurry view of rooftops from a window in France.

Film comes to Bombay

On 7 July 1896, a cameraman working for the Lumières showed six films at Watson's Hotel in Bombay. Newspapers in the city wrote about it with excitement. Tickets cost one rupee, a lot of money then. Soon films were shown in tents, theatres and fairgrounds across India.

Watching those early shows inspired Indians to make their own. In 1913, Dadasaheb Phalke released Raja Harishchandra, India's first full-length feature film. Because women didn't act on stage then, men played the female roles.

The famous train

The film everyone remembers is Arrival of a Train at La Ciotat, shot in 1895 and shown in 1896. The train steams towards the camera at an angle, growing bigger until it fills the screen. A famous story says people ran screaming out of the room. Historians have found no real evidence of this, but the film did show how powerful a moving image could be.

The Lumières sent camera operators all over the world to film and show films, including to India, Egypt, Japan, Mexico and the USA. Each operator carried a Cinématographe, shot local scenes, developed the film and showed it the same week. Many of the earliest moving images of cities like Cairo, Moscow and New York were taken this way, and some can still be watched online.

From 20 minutes to a giant industry

Within ten years, filmmakers were telling stories, adding special effects and building studios. Today India makes more films than any other country, often well over 1,500 a year, and Hollywood earns billions. All of it grew from ten tiny films in a café basement. The brothers themselves soon went back to photography. A famous line has the Lumière family calling cinema "an invention without a future". Nobody is sure who really said it, and the people who built Hollywood went on to prove the opposite.

Myth or fact?

Myth: When the Lumières first showed a train arriving at a station, the audience screamed and ran out of the room.

Truth: There is no reliable report of this from the time. The story appeared years later and grew with each telling. The train film was not even part of the first public programme.
