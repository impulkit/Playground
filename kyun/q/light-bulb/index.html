How does a light bulb make light?

Old bulbs glowed by getting white-hot. New LED bulbs use a much cooler trick.

The short answer

An old-style bulb has a very thin wire called a filament. Electricity pushes through it and heats it to about 2,500°C, until it glows white-hot. But most of the energy turns into heat, not light.

An LED, a light-emitting diode, works differently. Inside a tiny chip, electricity makes electrons jump down in energy, and each jump gives off a little burst of light. Very little heat is wasted.

Surprising but true

An old-style bulb turns about 90% of its electricity into heat, not light. That's why they were too hot to touch.

Glowing hot

Anything gets hot enough, and it glows. A stove coil turns dull red, and the Sun, far hotter, shines white. Old bulbs used the same idea. The filament was coiled tightly so a long wire could fit inside, and the glass held no oxygen, so the wire couldn't burn up.

Most of what a hot filament gives off is invisible infrared, which we feel as heat. Only about a tenth comes out as light your eyes can see.

Who invented the light bulb?

Many inventors made glowing bulbs. In 1879, Thomas Edison's team in the USA made one that lasted long enough to be useful, and Joseph Swan in England made one too. Edison also built the power stations and wires needed to light whole streets.

Edison's team tested thousands of materials for the filament. One of the best was carbonised bamboo, from Japan, which could glow for over a thousand hours. Later bulbs used tungsten, a metal that can survive enormous heat.

Tube lights

Between old bulbs and LEDs came fluorescent tube lights, long familiar in Indian classrooms and offices. Electricity passes through a gas containing a little mercury, which gives off invisible ultraviolet light. A white powder coating the inside of the glass turns that ultraviolet into visible light. Tube lights and the curly CFL bulbs used far less power than filament bulbs, but their mercury makes them tricky to throw away safely.

The bulb that won't quit

In a fire station in Livermore, California, a hand-blown bulb has been glowing almost without a break since 1901. It's very dim and runs on low power, which helps it last. Firefighters and visitors watch it on a webcam, and it has outlived several of the cameras pointed at it.

The missing blue

Red and green LEDs were invented first. The first practical visible-light LED, a red one, was made in the USA in 1962. A bright blue LED took decades more, and you need blue to make white light. Three Japanese scientists won the 2014 Nobel Prize for it.

Most white LED bulbs today use a blue LED covered with a yellow coating. Some blue light shines through, the coating turns the rest yellow, and together they look white.

India's switch to LEDs

In 2015, the Government of India started the UJALA scheme to sell LED bulbs cheaply. More than 36 crore bulbs were handed out across the country. Because each LED uses so much less power than an old bulb, the scheme cut electricity use and bills in millions of homes.

LEDs also last many years. A good one can run for 25,000 hours or more, compared with about 1,000 for an old bulb. They now light streets, phone screens, traffic signals and cricket stadiums.

Nature got there first

Fireflies make cold light too, with chemistry instead of electricity. Inside a firefly's tail, a chemical reaction makes light with almost no heat at all, a trick engineers took more than a century to match.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Switching a light off and on again uses more electricity than leaving it on.

Truth: The extra power used at the moment of switching on is tiny. For ordinary bulbs and LEDs, turning lights off whenever you leave a room saves energy, says the US Department of Energy.

Try this

Find an LED bulb box or label at home and look for two numbers, watts (power used) and lumens (brightness). Divide the lumens by the watts. An old 60-watt bulb gave about 800 lumens, which is about 13 lumens per watt. How many times better is your LED?
