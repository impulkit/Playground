How does social media work?

Every time you open a social media app, a computer program picks a handful of posts out of millions and decides what you see first.

The short answer

A social media app is a giant website where people post photos, videos and messages, and see what others have posted. Behind the screen, a set of rules run by a computer, called an algorithm, decides which posts to show each person and in what order.

The algorithm watches what you tap, like, share and linger on. It then guesses what will keep you watching and shows you more of that. Most apps are free because companies earn money from adverts that appear between posts. The more time people spend, the more adverts they see.

Surprising but true

Most social media apps cost nothing to download because they earn money by showing you adverts chosen using what they learn about you.

From friend lists to feeds

One of the first social networks, SixDegrees, appeared in 1997. It let people list their friends and see friends of friends. Facebook started in 2004 for students at Harvard University, then opened to everyone. YouTube followed in 2005, Twitter in 2006 and Instagram in 2010.

At first, most apps showed posts in the order they were made, newest first. Over time, companies switched to ranked feeds, where the algorithm picks what goes at the top. Facebook added its "Like" button in 2009, giving the algorithm a simple signal of what people enjoyed.

How the algorithm decides

Each time you open an app, there may be thousands of possible posts it could show you. The algorithm gives each one a score based on hundreds of clues. Did you watch a similar video to the end? Do you often reply to this friend? Is the post getting lots of comments quickly?

The highest-scoring posts go to the top. Posts that make people feel strongly, whether excited, angry or amused, often get more reactions, so they can spread faster. That is one reason rumours and arguments travel quickly online, and why it helps to learn how to tell if something is true.

How should we behave online?In 2018, false messages forwarded on phones led to real harm in parts of India. Soon after, WhatsApp limited how many chats you could forward a message to.

Designed to keep you scrolling

Many features are designed to hold attention. Infinite scroll means the feed never ends. Autoplay starts the next video before you decide. Notifications buzz to pull you back. Small rewards, like a new like or comment, arrive at unpredictable times.

Doctors and scientists are still studying what this means for children. In 2023 the US Surgeon General, America's top public health doctor, warned that heavy use could harm some young people's sleep and mood. The same report noted that social media can also help people find friends and support. Researchers disagree about how big the effects are. Learning about screen time and sleep is a good place to start.

Age limits and rules

Most big apps say users must be at least 13. That limit comes from a 1998 American law, COPPA, which makes it hard for companies to collect personal information from younger children without a parent's permission. In India, a 2023 data protection law says companies need a parent's consent before handling the personal data of anyone under 18.

Some countries are going further. Australia has a law, in force since December 2025, to keep children under 16 off major social media platforms. Others are debating similar steps, and not everyone agrees on the right approach.

Social media is also where much of modern language is born, from hashtags to emojis. Knowing the basics of online safety, such as keeping accounts private and never sharing your address or school, matters whichever app you use.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If an app is free, you are not paying anything for it.

Truth: You pay with your time and attention, and often with information about what you like, where you are and who your friends are. That information helps the company sell adverts.

Try this

Write ten made-up posts on small cards, such as a funny pet photo, a homework tip, a scary rumour and a friend's birthday. Ask three family members to rank them by which they would tap first. Compare the rankings. A social media algorithm tries to make that same guess for millions of people every second.
