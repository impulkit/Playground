How long do stars live?

The biggest stars burn out in a few million years. The smallest ones may shine for trillions, so long that not one has died of old age yet.

The short answer

How long a star lives depends mostly on how big it is at birth. Surprisingly, the bigger the star, the shorter its life. Giant stars, many times heavier than the Sun, burn their fuel so fast that they last only a few million years. Our Sun, a medium-sized star, will shine for about 10 billion years in total and is roughly halfway through. The smallest stars, red dwarfs, sip their fuel so slowly that they could last trillions of years.

Stars end in different ways too. Small and medium stars gently shed their outer layers and leave behind a white dwarf. Giant stars explode as a supernova, leaving a neutron star or a black hole.

Surprising but true

Much of the gold in your family's jewellery was probably made when two dead stars, called neutron stars, smashed into each other, long before Earth formed.

Born in a cloud

Every star begins inside a huge, cold cloud of gas and dust called a nebula. Gravity pulls clumps of it together. As a clump shrinks, its centre gets hotter and denser.

When the core reaches about 10 million °C, something new happens. Hydrogen atoms start smashing together and joining to make helium. This is nuclear fusion, and it releases enormous energy. The outward push of that energy balances gravity's squeeze, and a star switches on.

A star spends about 90% of its life in this steady stage, quietly turning hydrogen into helium.

The Sun's future

The Sun formed about 4.6 billion years ago. In roughly another 5 billion years, it will run short of hydrogen in its core. The core will shrink and heat up, while the outer layers swell enormously. The Sun will become a red giant, perhaps 100 times wider than today. It will swallow Mercury and Venus, and possibly Earth.

Later, the Sun will gently puff its outer layers into space as a glowing shell called a planetary nebula. What remains will be a white dwarf, a core about the size of Earth but so dense that a teaspoon of it would weigh several tonnes. Over a very long time it will cool and fade.

Giants go out with a bang

Stars more than about eight times heavier than the Sun live fast. They fuse hydrogen, then helium, then heavier elements like carbon, oxygen and silicon, layer upon layer like an onion. Once the core turns to iron, fusion stops giving out energy.

In less than a second, the core collapses and the star blows itself apart in a supernova that can outshine a whole galaxy for weeks. In 1054 CE, Chinese astronomers recorded a "guest star" bright enough to see in daylight. Its remains are the Crab Nebula, still spreading out today.

What is a supernova?When a giant star dies, it can blaze brighter than a whole galaxy of billions of stars, and scatter the stuff you are made of.

The leftover core becomes a neutron star, about as wide as a city, where a teaspoonful would weigh around a billion tonnes. If the core is heavy enough, it collapses completely into a black hole.

An Indian scientist's limit

In 1930, a 19-year-old Indian student named Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar was sailing to England to study. On the voyage, he worked out that a white dwarf can't be heavier than about 1.4 times the Sun. Above that, gravity wins and it collapses further.

Some famous astronomers rejected his idea at first. He later moved to the University of Chicago, and his limit turned out to be right. He shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1983, and NASA named its Chandra X-ray Observatory after him.

Made of star stuff

When stars die, they scatter the elements they made into space. Carbon in your cells, oxygen in your lungs and calcium in your bones were made inside ancient stars. Heavier elements, including much of the world's gold, probably formed when neutron stars crashed together. In 2017, astronomers watched exactly such a collision, more than 100 million light years away, and saw signs of fresh gold and platinum.

Myth or fact?

Myth: One day the Sun will explode as a supernova.

Truth: The Sun is too small to explode. In about 5 billion years it will swell into a red giant, puff its outer layers into space and shrink to a white dwarf, a dim, hot core about the size of Earth.

Try this

Use a pencil to poke a big hole in the bottom of one paper cup and a tiny hole in another. Over a sink, fill both with the same amount of water at the same moment and time how long each takes to empty. The big hole empties fast, like a giant star burning through its fuel. The tiny hole drips on and on, like a red dwarf.
