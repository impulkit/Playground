How did the Marathas build an empire?

The Marathas began as hill fighters in the Sahyadri mountains. Within a century their horsemen were riding into Delhi.

The short answer

The Maratha empire began with Shivaji, a chief from the hills near Pune who carved out a kingdom in the 1600s and was crowned Chhatrapati, or emperor, in 1674. His soldiers used speed, surprise and a chain of mountain forts to wear down much larger Mughal armies.

After Shivaji, the Marathas survived a long war with the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb. Then, in the 1700s, their chief ministers, the Peshwas, pushed north and east. By the 1750s, Maratha power stretched from Tamil Nadu to Punjab and from Gujarat to Odisha. A great defeat at Panipat in 1761, and later wars with the British, brought it to an end in 1818.

Surprising but true

Twelve Maratha-era forts, including one on an island in the sea and one in Tamil Nadu, were named a UNESCO World Heritage Site together in 2025.

Hill forts and fast horses

The Sahyadri mountains, part of the Western Ghats, are steep and cut by deep valleys. Shivaji turned this landscape into a weapon. He captured and built dozens of forts on hilltops, linked by narrow paths that only locals knew well.

Maratha soldiers rode small, hardy horses and carried light weapons. They avoided big pitched battles. Instead they raided supply lines, struck at night and vanished into the hills. Shivaji also built a navy and sea forts such as Sindhudurg, on an island off the Konkan coast, to defend against the Siddis, the Portuguese and the British.

The long war with Aurangzeb

After Shivaji died in 1680, his son Sambhaji took over. In 1689 he was captured and executed by Aurangzeb, who had moved his whole court to the Deccan to finish off the Marathas.

The Marathas did not give up. Sambhaji's brother Rajaram escaped all the way to Gingee in Tamil Nadu. When Rajaram died in 1700, his widow Tarabai led the resistance. Maratha bands raided across the Deccan, and the war dragged on for years. Aurangzeb died in 1707, still in the Deccan, without having won. The war had drained the Mughal treasury.

Why are the Western Ghats so important?A wall of old hills along India's west coast catches the monsoon, feeds rivers used by crores of people, and hides animals found nowhere else.

The Peshwas ride north

Under Shivaji's grandson Shahu, real power passed to his chief minister, the Peshwa. The Peshwa Bajirao I, who led from 1720 to 1740, was a brilliant cavalry general. In 1737 his army reached the outskirts of Delhi.

The Marathas collected taxes called chauth, a quarter of the revenue, from lands they did not rule directly, in return for leaving them alone. The Peshwas made Pune their capital and built the Shaniwar Wada palace there. Great Maratha families ruled large regions: the Scindias from Gwalior, the Holkars from Indore, the Gaekwads from Baroda and the Bhonsles from Nagpur.

A queen who rebuilt temples

One of the most admired Maratha rulers was Ahilyabai Holkar, who governed the Holkar lands from Maheshwar between 1767 and 1795. She was known for fair justice and careful government.

She paid for temples, wells, rest houses and river steps across India, from the Himalaya to the far south. In 1780 she built the present Kashi Vishwanath temple in Varanasi. The handloom weaving she supported at Maheshwar still produces the famous Maheshwari saris.

Panipat and the end

In 1761 a huge Maratha army met the Afghan ruler Ahmad Shah Durrani at Panipat, north of Delhi. The Marathas were badly defeated, with enormous losses. It took them a decade to recover.

They did recover, and for a time controlled the Mughal emperor in Delhi. But rivalries between the great families weakened them. The British East India Company fought three wars with the Marathas, and after the last, in 1818, the Peshwa's rule ended. Many Maratha princes, such as the Scindias and Gaekwads, kept their states under British protection. In 2025, twelve forts, from Raigad to Gingee, became a World Heritage Site as the Maratha Military Landscapes of India.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Marathas were a single kingdom ruled from one throne.

Truth: By the mid-1700s they were a confederacy. The Peshwa in Pune led, but great families such as the Scindias, Holkars, Gaekwads and Bhonsles ruled their own large territories.
