How can you focus better?

In one large study, people's minds wandered away from what they were doing almost half the time, and they were usually less happy when it did.

The short answer

Focus, or attention, is your brain's way of choosing what to notice. At any moment, your senses send in far more than you could ever handle: sounds, colours, the feel of your clothes, thoughts about lunch. Areas at the front of the brain help pick one thing and turn down the rest.

That system has limits. It tires, it gets pulled away by anything new or exciting, and it can only really handle one demanding task at a time. You can't force focus by sheer will. But you can make it easier, mainly by cutting distractions, working in short bursts, resting properly and getting enough sleep.

Surprising but true

The popular Pomodoro method of working in 25-minute bursts is named after a tomato-shaped kitchen timer.

One thing at a time

Many people believe they are good at multitasking. For simple things, like walking while chatting, they are. But when two tasks both need thinking, such as solving a maths problem and replying to a message, the brain mostly switches between them.

Each switch costs a little time while your brain reloads where it was. Psychologists call this a switching cost. It might be a fraction of a second, or much longer for complicated work. Do it a hundred times in an evening and homework that should take 30 minutes stretches to an hour, with more mistakes.

Arjuna and the bird's eye

In the Mahabharata, the teacher Drona tests his students with a wooden bird placed high in a tree. Before each young prince shoots, Drona asks what he sees. One says the tree, the branches and the sky. Another sees the bird and his brothers beside him.

Arjuna answers that he sees only the bird's eye. Drona tells him to shoot, and he hits it. The story has been told in India for centuries as a lesson in concentration. Modern science would add one detail: even Arjuna needed rest, and nobody can hold that kind of focus for hours without a break.

What is the Mahabharata?It has about 100,000 verses, making it one of the longest poems ever composed. Hidden inside it is the Bhagavad Gita.

A wandering mind

Your attention drifts more than you might think. In a 2010 study, Harvard researchers used a phone app to ask thousands of adults, at random moments, what they were doing and what they were thinking about. Their minds were wandering about 47% of the time.

Mind-wandering isn't always bad. Daydreams can help with planning and creative ideas. But when you need to learn or finish a task, catching your mind as it drifts and gently bringing it back is a skill that improves with practice. Some people practise it through mindfulness, where you sit quietly and notice your breathing.

Tricks that help

In the late 1980s, an Italian university student named Francesco Cirillo was struggling to study. He grabbed a kitchen timer shaped like a tomato, or pomodoro in Italian, and promised himself just a few minutes of real work. His method grew into a routine: 25 minutes of focused work, then a five-minute break, with a longer break after four rounds.

Other simple habits help too. Clear your desk of everything except what you need. Keep a "later" list for thoughts that pop up. Move your body between study sessions, since exercise seems to leave people more alert. Spending time outdoors, near trees or a park, may also help tired attention recover.

Phones and focus

A phone is designed to grab attention, with buzzes, red dots and endless feeds. Even when you aren't using it, part of your mind may be wondering whether a message has arrived. Many people find they focus better with the phone out of sight, not just face down. Good habits around screen time can make a real difference.

Some people find focusing much harder than others, through no fault of their own. A condition called ADHD affects how the brain manages attention. If focusing always feels like a struggle, it is worth talking to a parent, teacher or doctor, who can help work out what is going on.

Myth or fact?

Myth: People now have a shorter attention span than a goldfish, about 8 seconds.

Truth: The claim spread from a 2015 marketing report and has no solid science behind it. Attention span isn't a single number, and goldfish can learn tasks and remember them for months.

Try this

Try one "focus sprint". Put your phone in another room, set a timer for 20 minutes and work on one task only. Keep a paper next to you and jot down any distracting thought instead of acting on it. When the timer rings, take a five-minute break, then compare how much you got done with a normal 20 minutes.
