What is propaganda?

A poster of a man pointing straight at you, saying "I want YOU", helped recruit American soldiers in 1917. Versions of it are still copied today.

The short answer

Propaganda is information spread to push people towards a belief or action, usually for a political cause, often by playing on feelings instead of giving balanced facts. Governments, armies, parties and other groups have all used it.

Unlike honest argument, propaganda doesn't want you to weigh up both sides. It repeats simple slogans, uses powerful images, picks out enemies and makes one choice feel like the only one. It can contain lies, but often it uses half-truths: real facts, carefully selected. Learning to spot it is a big part of thinking for yourself.

Surprising but true

The word propaganda first referred to a Catholic Church office set up in 1622 to spread the faith. Only in the 20th century did it take on its darker meaning.

Where the word came from

In 1622, the Catholic Church in Rome set up an office to spread the faith to new lands. Its Latin name included the word propaganda, meaning "to be spread". For centuries the word simply meant spreading a belief.

That changed with the two world wars. Governments realised that newspapers, posters, radio and films could move whole nations. Afterwards, people grew suspicious of the way they had been persuaded, and propaganda became a word for manipulation.

Posters and pointing fingers

During the First World War, Britain printed a poster of Lord Kitchener pointing at the viewer, telling men their country needed them. In 1917, the American artist James Montgomery Flagg drew Uncle Sam pointing the same way, with the words "I want YOU for U.S. Army". Millions of copies were printed.

Both sides made posters showing the enemy as monsters and their own soldiers as heroes. Simple images, bold words and strong feelings made the messages hard to forget.

When propaganda turns dangerous

In Germany in the 1930s, the Nazi party set up a whole ministry for propaganda. It controlled newspapers, radio and films, burned books it disliked, and spread hatred of Jewish people and other groups. Years of repeated lies helped make terrible crimes, including the Holocaust, seem acceptable to many ordinary people.

During the Cold War, the USA and the Soviet Union each used radio, films and even sports to show their own system as the best. George Orwell's novel Nineteen Eighty-Four, published in 1949, imagined a state where the government rewrote history every day. Terms from the book, like "Big Brother", are still used.

Tricks to watch for

In 1937, a group of American teachers and researchers, the Institute for Propaganda Analysis, listed seven common tricks. Among them were name-calling, attaching nasty labels to opponents; glittering generalities, using shiny words like "freedom" without saying what they mean; and the bandwagon, telling you everyone else already agrees.

Many of these tricks also appear in advertising. Today, propaganda spreads on social media, through memes, edited videos and accounts that pretend to be ordinary people. Some uses fake news or AI-made images.

Thinking for yourself

People often call any message they disagree with propaganda, which can make the word less useful. A better test is to ask some questions. Who made this, and what do they want? Does it give reasons, or only feelings? What might it be leaving out? Does it treat a group of people as less than human?

During India's freedom struggle, both the British government and Indian nationalists used newspapers, songs and pamphlets to win people over. Looking at how each side told the story is a good way to practise.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Propaganda is always made of lies.

Truth: Much propaganda uses true facts, but picks only the ones that help its side and hides the rest. Choosing, repeating and framing facts can mislead as well as a lie can.
