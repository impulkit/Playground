What are some great adventure books?

Treasure Island began as a map a writer drew to amuse his stepson on a rainy holiday in Scotland in 1881.

The short answer

Great adventure books take ordinary people into danger and make them find out what they're made of. There might be a shipwreck, a hunt for treasure, a race across the world or a night alone in the woods. You keep turning pages because you need to know what happens next.

Some classics have been read for centuries, like Robinson Crusoe (1719) and Treasure Island (1883). Others are newer favourites, like Hatchet (1987). India has its own heroes too, from Satyajit Ray's detective Feluda to the boys and girls of Ruskin Bond's mountain stories. Here are some of the best, and why they still work.

Surprising but true

Gary Paulsen, who wrote Hatchet, ran the Iditarod, a sled-dog race of more than 1,500 km across Alaska, twice.

Shipwrecks and islands

Daniel Defoe's Robinson Crusoe came out in 1719. A sailor is shipwrecked on an island and survives alone for years, building a shelter, growing crops and keeping a diary. It was one of the first English novels, and it started a craze for castaway stories.

Island of the Blue Dolphins (1960), by the American writer Scott O'Dell, is based on a real Native American woman. She lived alone on San Nicolas Island, off California, for about 18 years in the 1800s. In the book, a girl named Karana builds a home, tames a wild dog and survives by her own skill.

Treasure, maps and pirates

In 1881, Robert Louis Stevenson was stuck indoors on a rainy holiday in Scotland. He painted a map of an island with his 13-year-old stepson, Lloyd Osbourne. Looking at it, he started imagining the pirates who had been there. That became Treasure Island, published as a book in 1883.

It follows young Jim Hawkins, a one-legged pirate named Long John Silver and a hunt for buried gold. Much of what people "know" about pirates, from treasure maps to parrots on shoulders, comes from this book.

Were pirates real?Pirates were very real, but most of what you know about them, from walking the plank to treasure maps, comes from one Scottish novel.

Racing the world

In Jules Verne's Around the World in Eighty Days (1872), the very precise Englishman Phileas Fogg bets that he can circle the globe in 80 days. His journey includes a train across India and a ride on an elephant when the railway line runs out. Verne made travel itself the adventure.

Mark Twain's The Adventures of Tom Sawyer (1876) brought adventure closer to home: caves, a haunted house and a raft on the Mississippi River.

Alone in the wild

In Gary Paulsen's Hatchet, 13-year-old Brian Robeson is the only survivor when a small plane crashes in the Canadian wilderness. He has nothing but a hatchet his mother gave him. He learns to make fire, catch fish and survive a moose attack.

Paulsen knew wild country first-hand. He trapped, hunted and ran the Iditarod sled-dog race across Alaska twice. Hatchet was named a Newbery Honor Book in 1988.

Adventure, Indian style

In 1965, Satyajit Ray published the first story about Feluda, a sharp-eyed private detective from Kolkata, in the children's magazine Sandesh. With his young cousin Topshe narrating, Feluda solves mysteries in places like Rajasthan, Varanasi and Sikkim. Many stories are as much travel adventures as crime puzzles.

Ruskin Bond wrote many short adventure stories set in the hills and forests of north India. In Angry River, a girl named Sita is swept away when floods rise around her island home. The Jungle Book by Rudyard Kipling, set in the forests of central India, has kept children reading since 1894.

Why they work

Adventure books share a few tricks. The hero is usually young or ordinary, so you can picture yourself in their place. There's a clear goal and a clock ticking. And the hero has to solve problems with their own wits and courage.

They also teach you things without seeming to. After Hatchet, many readers know how to make a fire with a spark. After Around the World in Eighty Days, they know how the date line works. A good adventure leaves you feeling as if you went too.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Real pirates buried their treasure and drew maps with an X to mark the spot.

Truth: Real pirates almost never buried treasure. They usually spent or shared it quickly. Buried chests, X-marked maps and parrots on shoulders were made famous by Treasure Island and later films.

Try this

Draw a treasure map of your home or school, with a "treasure" hidden somewhere real, like a sweet in a cupboard. Add clues, a compass arrow and an X. Swap maps with a family member and race to see who finds their treasure first.
