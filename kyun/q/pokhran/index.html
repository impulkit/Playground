What happened at Pokhran?

In May 1998, India tested five nuclear devices under the Rajasthan desert near Pokhran, and US spy satellites failed to spot the preparations.

The short answer

Pokhran is a small town in the Thar Desert of Rajasthan, near the army's Pokhran test range. India has carried out nuclear tests there twice. On 18 May 1974, it exploded one nuclear device underground. On 11 and 13 May 1998, it tested five more.

After the 1998 tests, India declared itself a nuclear weapons state. Two weeks later, Pakistan carried out its own tests. Many countries condemned India and Pakistan, and some placed sanctions on them. The tests remain a source of national pride for many Indians, and a subject of worry and argument for others.

Surprising but true

Scientists and soldiers at the 1998 test site worked mostly at night and wore army uniforms, so that from space they would look like ordinary soldiers at a firing range.

Why India wanted the bomb

India's nuclear programme began in the 1940s under the physicist Homi Bhabha. At first it focused on nuclear power and research. But India had fought a war with China in 1962, and China tested its first nuclear bomb in 1964. Many Indian leaders felt the country could not be left behind.

India also argued that the world's nuclear rules were unfair. The Non-Proliferation Treaty of 1968 allowed five countries, including the US, the Soviet Union and China, to keep nuclear weapons while asking everyone else to give them up. India refused to sign it, and still has not.

1974: Smiling Buddha

On 18 May 1974, under Prime Minister Indira Gandhi, scientists led by Raja Ramanna set off a device in a shaft at Pokhran. India called it a "peaceful nuclear explosion". The code name was Smiling Buddha, because the day was Buddha Purnima.

Other countries were not convinced it was peaceful. Canada and the US cut back nuclear help, and a group of countries formed new rules to control the sale of nuclear materials. For the next 24 years India did not test again, though work continued in secret.

1998: Operation Shakti

In 1998, Prime Minister Atal Bihari Vajpayee's government approved new tests. The effort was led by A. P. J. Abdul Kalam, then head of the Defence Research and Development Organisation, and R. Chidambaram of the Department of Atomic Energy.

India knew American satellites were watching. In 1995, a planned test had been called off after US satellites spotted signs of preparation. This time, the team worked mostly at night, shifted equipment back into the same position by daylight, and wore army uniforms. Some scientists used false names.

At 3:45 in the afternoon on 11 May 1998, three devices went off together. Two smaller ones followed on 13 May. India's government said the largest was a thermonuclear, or hydrogen, device. Some scientists in India and abroad have questioned how powerful it really was.

What happened next

The reaction came quickly. The US, Japan and other countries put sanctions on India, cutting aid and loans. The UN Security Council condemned the tests. On 28 and 30 May, Pakistan tested its own devices in the Chagai Hills.

India then announced a voluntary stop on further testing and a "no first use" policy, meaning it says it will not be the first to use nuclear weapons in a conflict. Over the next decade, relations with the US improved, and in 2008 the two countries signed a deal allowing India to buy fuel for its nuclear power plants.

The desert today

The test range is still a closed military area in the Thar Desert. The town of Pokhran itself is much older than the tests, with a 14th-century sandstone fort called Balagarh, built when camel caravans crossed this part of the desert. People in nearby villages remember the ground shaking and walls cracking in 1998. In 2018, a Bollywood film about the tests renewed public interest.

India marks 11 May every year as National Technology Day, to honour science and engineering, not just the bombs.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Pokhran tests were explosions in the open air, like in films.

Truth: All the tests at Pokhran were underground, in deep shafts dug into the desert. On the surface, people saw the ground jump and a cloud of sand and dust, but no mushroom cloud.
