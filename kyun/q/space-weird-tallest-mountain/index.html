What is the tallest mountain in the solar system?

Olympus Mons on Mars is about 22 km high, two and a half times taller than Everest, and nearly as wide as Maharashtra.

The short answer

The tallest mountain we know of is Olympus Mons, a giant volcano on Mars. It rises about 22 km above the planet's average surface level. That is two and a half Everests stacked on top of each other, and about twice as high as passenger jets fly.

It is also enormous across. Its base is about 600 km wide, covering an area nearly as big as Maharashtra, or the US state of Arizona. At the top sits a crater about 80 km long, big enough to swallow the whole of Mumbai with room to spare.

There is one rival. On the asteroid Vesta, a peak in the middle of a giant crater called Rheasilvia stands roughly as tall. Which is higher depends on where you decide each mountain starts.

Surprising but true

Olympus Mons slopes so gently that if you stood on it, the curve of Mars would hide the summit from view.

Too wide to see

Olympus Mons is a shield volcano. Runny lava flowed out over millions of years and spread into a broad, gentle dome. Most of its slope is only a few degrees steep, gentler than many wheelchair ramps.

That makes for a strange view. If you stood near the base, you could not see the summit at all. The mountain is so wide that the curve of Mars bends it out of sight. Climbing it would feel less like scaling a peak and more like a very long uphill walk lasting weeks.

The edges are a different story. Around much of the base runs a cliff up to 8 km high, almost as tall as Everest by itself. Giant landslides once tumbled off these cliffs, spreading rubble hundreds of kilometres across the plains.

Why Mars grows giants

Earth has shield volcanoes too. The islands of Hawaii were built by them. But Earth's crust is broken into plates that slowly drift. As a plate slides over a hot spot deep below, old volcanoes get carried away and new ones pop up. That is why Hawaii is a chain of islands rather than one giant mountain.

Mars has no moving plates like ours. Its crust sat still over the hot spot, and lava kept pouring out in the same place for a very long time. The pile just grew and grew.

Gravity helps as well. Mars pulls with only about 38% of Earth's gravity. A huge mountain weighs less there, so the rock beneath it can hold up a much taller pile before it sags.

Is it still alive?

Olympus Mons is quiet now. Some of its lava flows look young for a planet, perhaps a few million years old, though scientists are still working out exact ages. Mars has cooled a great deal inside, so a big eruption today seems unlikely. Mars still shakes now and then, though. NASA's InSight lander recorded more than 1,300 marsquakes between 2018 and 2022.

Spacecraft circling Mars have mapped it in fine detail. The European Space Agency's Mars Express and NASA orbiters have photographed its cliffs and landslides. Some of its slopes show ancient lava channels and signs that glaciers may once have crept down its sides.

Earth's champion is hiding

On Earth, the answer to "tallest mountain" depends on the question. Everest has the highest summit above sea level. But Mauna Kea in Hawaii starts deep on the ocean floor and rises about 10 km in total, so it is taller from base to top. You can compare the contenders in the world's tallest mountains.

And the strangest rival of all? Vesta's peak sits on a lumpy space rock only about 525 km wide, one of the biggest objects in the asteroid belt. Find out just how big they get in how big asteroids can be.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Mount Everest is the tallest mountain anywhere.

Truth: Everest has the highest summit above sea level on Earth, at 8,849 m. Mauna Kea in Hawaii is taller from its base on the sea floor, and Olympus Mons on Mars is more than twice Everest's height.

Try this

Pour a spoonful of honey slowly onto a plate in one spot and watch it spread into a wide, low dome. Then slide the plate a little while pouring and see how you get a row of small mounds instead. That is the difference between Mars and Hawaii.
