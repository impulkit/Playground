Why are tigers orange? Wouldn't green hide better?

A bright orange hunter in a green forest. Something doesn't add up.

The short answer

Deer and many other prey animals have eyes with only two kinds of colour detectors. Orange and green look similar to them.

Stripes break up the tiger's outline in tall grass and shadows. That's camouflage.

Every tiger's stripes are different, like fingerprints. Scientists count tigers from camera-trap photos this way.

So the tiger's coat isn't really designed for our eyes. To a chital or a sambar deer, a tiger crouching in dry grass is a dull greenish shape broken into stripy pieces. By the time the deer spots it, the tiger may already be springing.

Surprising but true

India is home to about three out of every four wild tigers on Earth.

Seeing like a deer

Our eyes have three kinds of colour cells; a deer's have two.

People with normal vision have cone cells that respond to red, green and blue light. Deer lack the red-sensitive kind, so reds and oranges blend with greens, a little like a person with red-green colour blindness. In 2019, scientists at the University of Bristol modelled how a tiger looks through a deer's eyes. To the deer, the orange tiger was almost as well hidden as a green one would be.

Why not just be green?

There's another reason tigers aren't green. Almost no mammal can make green fur. Mammal hair gets its colour from just two pigments, one making black and brown, the other making orange and yellow. Mix them in different amounts and you get the colours of tigers, lions, dogs and people. Sloths look greenish only because tiny algae grow in their fur.

For a hunter whose main prey can't see red, orange works perfectly well. It may even be the best a mammal can do.

Stripes all the way down

A tiger's stripes aren't only in its fur. Shave a patch, and the skin underneath is striped too, because the colour comes from the skin cells that grow each hair. Most tigers have over 100 stripes, and no two tigers share the same pattern. Even a tiger's left and right sides are different.

Counting tigers

Camera traps take photos when a tiger walks past. Computer programs compare stripe patterns to tell tigers apart and count them.

Every four years, India runs one of the biggest wildlife surveys in the world. In the 2022 count, forest staff set up camera traps at tens of thousands of spots and collected millions of photos. They identified over 3,000 individual tigers and estimated about 3,682 in total.

Project Tiger

India started Project Tiger in 1973 to protect tigers and their forests. Today there are over 50 tiger reserves.

It began with nine reserves, including Corbett in Uttarakhand, Kanha in Madhya Pradesh and the Sundarbans in West Bengal. In the early 1970s, only about 1,800 tigers were counted in India, though methods then were rough. Protecting tigers also protects the rivers and forests they need, along with the deer, birds and plants that share them. That's why the tiger, India's national animal, is often called an umbrella species.

White tigers

Once in a while, a Bengal tiger is born white. In 1951, a white cub named Mohan was captured in the forests of Rewa, in present-day Madhya Pradesh. He was kept by the Maharaja of Rewa, and many white tigers in zoos around the world descend from him.

Myth or fact?

Myth: White tigers are albinos, or a separate kind of tiger.

Truth: White tigers are Bengal tigers with a rare gene that stops orange colour forming. Unlike albinos, they still have dark stripes and usually blue eyes. They are very rare in the wild, and almost all live in zoos.

Try this

See like a deer. Lay an orange object, such as an orange or a toy, on green grass or leaves and take a photo with a phone. Use the phone's photo editor to turn the picture black and white, or to lower the colour to almost nothing. Notice how much harder the orange is to spot. Add some dark stripes with a marker on paper and try again.
