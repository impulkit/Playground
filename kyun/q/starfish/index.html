How does a starfish eat?

A starfish eats by pushing its stomach out of its body and digesting its dinner outside.

The short answer

A starfish eats in a way that sounds impossible. Its mouth is on its underside, in the middle. When it finds a mussel, clam or oyster, it wraps its arms around the shell and grips it with hundreds of tiny tube feet. Then it pulls, slowly and steadily, sometimes for hours. The shellfish eventually tires and its shell opens a crack.

That is all the starfish needs. It pushes part of its stomach out through its mouth and slides it into the gap. The stomach releases juices that start digesting the soft animal inside its own shell. When the meal has turned to soup, the starfish draws its stomach back in. Your own stomach works very differently, as you can read in how digestion works.

Surprising but true

Many starfish have a simple eye at the tip of each arm. They can't see detail, but they can tell light from dark and spot large shapes.

Hundreds of tiny feet

Turn a starfish over and you will see rows of small, soft tubes along each arm. These are tube feet. Each one is a little hollow tube, often with a sucker at its tip. The starfish moves them using water pumped through a network of canals inside its body, like a hydraulic machine.

By pumping water in and out, the starfish can stretch and pull back each foot. Hundreds of them working together let it crawl over rocks, cling on in crashing waves and pull open shells. Starfish have no blood. Seawater flowing through these canals does much of the work that blood does in other animals.

No brain, five arms

A starfish has no brain. Instead, a ring of nerves circles its mouth, with a nerve running down each arm. Each arm can sense and react, and somehow they work together, though scientists are still studying how.

Most starfish have five arms, arranged in a star pattern. That fivefold symmetry is shared with their relatives, the sea urchins and sand dollars. They belong to a group called echinoderms, meaning "spiny skin". Some starfish have many more arms. The sunflower sea star of the Pacific coast of North America can have more than 20.

Many starfish can regrow a lost arm. In a few kinds, a single arm with part of the central body can grow into a whole new starfish.

The starfish that changed science

In the 1960s, an American scientist named Robert Paine did a simple experiment on the rocky coast of Washington State. In one area, he kept removing a purple starfish that ate mussels. In a nearby area, he left the starfish alone.

Without the starfish, mussels took over the rocks and crowded out many other animals and seaweeds. The variety of life dropped sharply. Paine called the starfish a keystone species, after the central stone that holds up an arch. Take it away and the arch collapses. The idea is now used around the world to understand how nature fits together. You can see starfish like these in tide pools.

When starfish disappear

Since 2013, a disease called sea star wasting has killed billions of starfish along the Pacific coast of North America. Affected animals develop sores and fall apart within days. The sunflower sea star was among the worst hit, with its numbers crashing by more than 90 per cent across much of its range.

Sunflower sea stars eat sea urchins. Without them, urchins multiplied and munched through underwater forests of giant seaweed, as you can read in kelp forests. On the Great Barrier Reef, the problem is reversed: outbreaks of the crown-of-thorns starfish eat large areas of coral.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Starfish are fish.

Truth: Starfish are not fish at all. They have no backbone, gills or fins. They belong to a group called echinoderms, along with sea urchins and sea cucumbers, which is why many scientists prefer the name sea star.

Try this

Feel how suction works. Press a small rubber suction cup or a soft plastic bottle cap against a smooth tile and try to pull it off. A starfish has hundreds of tiny cups pulling at once, for hours. That is how a starfish's tube feet open a shell.
