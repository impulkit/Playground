What are latitude and longitude?

For centuries, sailors could tell how far north they were, but not how far east or west. The answer turned out to be a very good clock.

The short answer

Latitude and longitude are a grid of imaginary lines drawn over the Earth, so that every place can be given an exact address made of two numbers.

Latitude tells you how far north or south you are. The equator is 0 degrees. The North Pole is 90 degrees north and the South Pole 90 degrees south. Lines of latitude run around the Earth in rings, like the hoops on a barrel.

Longitude tells you how far east or west you are. Lines of longitude run from pole to pole, like the segments of an orange. The starting line, 0 degrees, runs through Greenwich in London. Places go up to 180 degrees east or west of it.

Bengaluru, for example, is at about 13 degrees north and 77.6 degrees east. New York is about 40.7 degrees north and 74 degrees west.

Surprising but true

Indian Standard Time is set by the line of longitude at 82.5 degrees east, which passes close to the city of Mirzapur in Uttar Pradesh.

Finding latitude from the stars

Latitude was the easy one. In the northern half of the world, the North Star sits almost directly above the North Pole. The higher it appears in your sky, the further north you are. At the equator it sits on the horizon. At the North Pole it is straight overhead.

So sailors could measure the angle of the North Star, or of the Sun at noon, and work out their latitude. Arab, Indian and Chinese navigators in the Indian Ocean used simple instruments to do this hundreds of years ago.

The longitude problem

Longitude was much harder. The stars move across the sky as the Earth turns, so there's no fixed star to measure. Without knowing their longitude, ships often had no idea how far east or west they were. Some ran onto rocks.

The answer lay in time. The Earth turns 15 degrees every hour. If a sailor knew the exact time at home and compared it with noon where the ship was, the difference told them how far east or west they had gone. But it needed a clock that kept perfect time on a rocking, damp ship.

In 1714, the British government offered a prize of up to £20,000 for a solution. A carpenter and clockmaker named John Harrison spent decades building better and better sea clocks. His fourth, a large pocket-watch called H4, was tested on a voyage to Jamaica in 1761–62 and lost only a few seconds.

How does a clock keep time?Every clock, from a sundial to an atomic clock, has two parts: something that repeats steadily, and something that counts.

Choosing a starting line

For a long time, different countries used their own starting line for longitude, often through their own capital. In 1884, delegates from 25 countries met in Washington DC and agreed to use the meridian through the Royal Observatory at Greenwich. Many of the world's sea charts already used it.

France didn't accept it straight away and kept using Paris on its own maps for several more decades.

India's time line

Time zones are based on longitude too. Indian Standard Time is set by the line at 82.5 degrees east, which passes near Mirzapur in Uttar Pradesh. It is 5 hours 30 minutes ahead of Greenwich.

India is wide, though. The Sun rises in Arunachal Pradesh nearly two hours before it rises in Gujarat, yet the whole country uses the same clock time. The United States, which is wider still, uses several time zones.

Your address on the planet

Today the GPS in a phone gives your latitude and longitude in an instant, often to within a few metres. Each degree is split into 60 minutes, and each minute into 60 seconds, just like time. Ships, planes, delivery apps and rescue teams all use these two numbers to find their way.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Lines of longitude are the same distance apart everywhere.

Truth: Lines of longitude all meet at the North and South Poles. They are furthest apart at the equator, about 111 km per degree, and get closer and closer together towards the poles.

Try this

Take an orange and draw a line around its middle for the equator. Draw lines from top to bottom, like the segments, for longitude. Then draw rings parallel to the equator for latitude. Stick a pin where you think your home is, and try to describe its position using only the two sets of lines.
