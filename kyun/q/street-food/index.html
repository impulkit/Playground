What makes Indian street food special?

The same crispy ball filled with spicy water is called pani puri in Mumbai, golgappa in Delhi and puchka in Kolkata, and each city makes it differently.

The short answer

Indian street food is cheap, quick and full of strong flavours: sour tamarind, sweet chutney, spicy green chilli, crunchy sev and cooling curd, often in a single bite. It's cooked fresh in front of you by vendors who may have made the same snack for decades.

Every region has its own specialities. Mumbai has vada pav and pav bhaji, Delhi has chole bhature and aloo tikki, Kolkata has kathi rolls and jhalmuri, and Indore is famous for its night market. Street food also tells a story about history. Many snacks were invented to feed workers, travellers and students who needed a filling meal for a few rupees.

Surprising but true

The word chaat comes from the Hindi word chaatna, meaning "to lick", as in licking your fingers clean.

Chaat, the finger-licking snack

Chaat is a family of tangy snacks. Papdi chaat piles crisp wafers with potato, chickpeas, curd and chutneys. Bhel puri mixes puffed rice, onion, tomato and sev. The name comes from the Hindi word chaatna, to lick.

A well-loved legend traces chaat to Mughal Delhi. It says the emperor Shah Jahan's physician told people to eat spicy, fried food to protect themselves from the river water. There's no clear proof for this story. But Old Delhi's lanes, near the Red Fort, are still famous for their chaat.

One snack, many names

Hollow, crisp puris filled with spiced water are called pani puri in Mumbai, golgappa in Delhi and much of north India, and puchka in Kolkata. In Kolkata, the filling is often sharper and the potato more mashed. In Mumbai, there's sweet chutney too.

Arguments about whose version is best are a national pastime. Each city's version reflects local tastes, from the tamarind of the south to the spicy mint water of the north. Most of these snacks rely on chillies, which came to India only about 500 years ago.

Food for workers

Many famous street foods were created for people on the move. Pav bhaji, a spicy mashed vegetable curry eaten with buttered bread, is said to have been invented in Mumbai for textile mill workers who needed a quick dinner late at night. Vada pav, a potato fritter in a bun, fed the same workers in the 1960s.

Kolkata's kathi roll, a paratha wrapped around kebabs, is often traced to a restaurant called Nizam's, which opened in the 1930s. Momos, the steamed dumplings, came to India from Tibet and Nepal, and spread from Darjeeling and the Northeast to cities across the country. Mumbai's workers also rely on the dabbawalas for home food.

Streets around the world

Street food isn't only Indian. In Singapore, hawker centres, open food courts full of stalls, are so loved that UNESCO added hawker culture to its heritage list in 2020. In the USA, food trucks sell tacos in Los Angeles and halal food in New York.

Vendors and the law

India has many millions of street vendors. For years, many faced fines and were moved on by officials. In 2014, Parliament passed a law to protect their right to earn a living and to set aside vending zones in cities.

India's food safety agency also runs a programme that trains vendors in hygiene, such as using clean water, gloves and covered food. Streets that pass are certified as "clean street food hubs". Many famous food lanes, from Mumbai to Bhopal, have joined.

What makes it special

Street food is often the first place to try a city's flavours. You eat standing up, shoulder to shoulder with office workers, students and families, all for a small price. It changes with the seasons, too: hot corn in the monsoon, gajak and hot jalebis in a Delhi winter, kulfi in the heat of May. A good vendor remembers how spicy you like it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Street food is always less safe than restaurant food.

Truth: Safety depends on clean water, clean hands and freshly cooked food, not on whether it is sold from a cart or a kitchen. Food fried or cooked hot in front of you is often fine. India's food safety agency trains vendors and certifies clean street food hubs.
