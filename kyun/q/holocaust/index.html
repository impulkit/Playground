What was the Holocaust?

Between 1941 and 1945, Nazi Germany and its helpers set out to murder every Jewish person they could reach. They killed about six million.

The short answer

The Holocaust was the murder of about six million Jewish men, women and children by Nazi Germany and those who helped it, during the Second World War. It is one of the worst crimes in human history. The word holocaust comes from an ancient Greek word for a sacrifice that was completely burned. Many Jewish people use the Hebrew word Shoah, meaning catastrophe.

Before the war, Europe was home to about nine and a half million Jewish people. By 1945, about two out of every three of them had been murdered. Whole communities, with their schools, songs and ways of life, disappeared.

The Nazis also killed many others they saw as enemies or "unworthy", including Roma people, disabled people, Polish and Soviet civilians, prisoners of war and gay men. Historians know a great deal about how it happened, because the Nazis kept records, and survivors told what they saw.

Surprising but true

Anne Frank's diary, written by a teenager hiding in an Amsterdam attic, has been translated into more than 70 languages.

Hatred becomes law

Adolf Hitler and his Nazi party came to power in Germany in 1933. They blamed Jewish people for Germany's problems, using lies that spread through newspapers, films and school lessons. This kind of propaganda made cruelty seem normal to many people.

Step by step, Jewish Germans lost their rights. In 1935 new laws took away their citizenship. They were pushed out of jobs and schools. In November 1938, in a night of attacks later called Kristallnacht, synagogues were burned and Jewish shops smashed across Germany and Austria. Many families tried to leave, but other countries took in only some of them.

War and mass murder

When Germany invaded Poland in 1939 and then much of Europe, millions more Jewish people came under Nazi control. Many were forced into crowded, walled-off parts of cities called ghettos, with little food.

From 1941, the Nazis moved to killing on a massive scale. Special units shot huge numbers of people in eastern Europe. The Nazis also built death camps in occupied Poland, and sent people there by train from all over Europe. Auschwitz-Birkenau was the largest. About one million Jewish people were killed there.

Why is Anne Frank's diary important?A 13-year-old girl hid in a secret set of rooms for two years and wrote one of the most-read books in the world.

People who helped

Some people risked their lives to save others. In Denmark, ordinary people helped about 7,000 Jews escape by boat to Sweden in 1943. Before the war, a rescue effort called the Kindertransport carried about 10,000 Jewish children to safety in Britain. Many never saw their parents again.

In 1940 a Japanese diplomat in Lithuania, Chiune Sugihara, went against his own government's orders. For about six weeks he wrote travel visas by hand, sometimes for hours a day, so that Jewish refugees could cross Russia and Japan to safety. His visas are thought to have saved thousands of people, and many of their descendants are alive today.

Israel's Holocaust memorial, Yad Vashem, honours rescuers as "Righteous Among the Nations". More than 28,000 people have been recognised so far.

After the war

Allied soldiers freed the camps in 1945 and were shocked by what they found. Leading Nazis were put on trial at Nuremberg in Germany, one of the first times a country's leaders faced an international court for crimes against humanity.

The horror helped push the world to write the Universal Declaration of Human Rights in 1948. Survivors spent the rest of their lives telling their stories, so that nobody could deny what happened. Today, museums such as the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum in Washington, D.C., and the Anne Frank House in Amsterdam keep those memories alive. It can be a heavy subject, and it helps to talk about it with a parent or teacher.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Jewish people did not fight back during the Holocaust.

Truth: Many resisted, in many ways. In 1943 Jews in the Warsaw Ghetto rose up and held off German troops for about a month. Others escaped to join resistance fighters in the forests, hid children, or secretly kept records so the truth would survive.
