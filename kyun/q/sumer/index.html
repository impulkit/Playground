Who were the Sumerians?

Some of the world's oldest writing isn't poetry or prayers. It's receipts for barley, sheep and beer, pressed into clay in Sumer over 5,000 years ago.

The short answer

The Sumerians lived in southern Mesopotamia, the flat land between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers in what is now Iraq. From about 3500 BCE they built some of the world's first cities, with temples, markets, schools and thousands of people living close together.

They are best known for one of the oldest writing systems on Earth, called cuneiform. They also developed counting in sixties, built huge stepped temples called ziggurats and wrote early laws, poems and epics. Their language died out more than 3,000 years ago, but some of their ideas still shape how we tell the time.

Surprising but true

Every time you read a clock, you use a Sumerian idea. Counting in sixties began in Sumer, and it's why an hour has 60 minutes and a circle has 360 degrees.

Cities from mud

Southern Mesopotamia had rich soil but very little rain, no stone and hardly any trees. The Sumerians solved this with mud. They dug canals to carry river water to fields of barley and wheat, and they shaped mud into bricks for houses, walls and temples.

Farming grew so well that not everyone had to farm. Some people became priests, potters, metalworkers, traders or soldiers. Villages grew into cities. By about 3000 BCE, Uruk may have held 40,000 people or more, making it one of the largest cities in the world at the time.

Each city had its own ruler and its own god. The cities often traded with each other and just as often fought over water and land.

Wedges in clay

The temples collected and handed out huge amounts of grain, oil, wool and animals. Officials needed to keep track. At first they used small clay tokens, then drew pictures on clay tablets. Over time the pictures became patterns of wedge-shaped marks pressed with a cut reed. "Cuneiform" means "wedge-shaped" in Latin.

Thousands of these tablets survive. Many are school exercises written by children learning to be scribes, complete with mistakes. Some are copies of the Epic of Gilgamesh, the story of a king of Uruk who searches for the secret of living forever. It's one of the oldest great stories in the world.

Sixty minutes and a game board

The Sumerians counted in groups of 60. Sixty is handy because it divides evenly by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20 and 30. Later peoples in Mesopotamia passed this system on, and it survives in our 60-second minutes, 60-minute hours and 360-degree circles, all part of how we measure time.

In the 1920s, the British archaeologist Leonard Woolley dug up the Royal Cemetery at Ur. Among gold helmets, jewellery and harps decorated with bulls' heads, his team found a beautiful game board inlaid with shell and stone. Today it's called the Royal Game of Ur. Using a clay tablet from much later, a British Museum curator worked out how it was probably played, and people play it again today.

Ships from Meluhha

Sumer had few raw materials, so it traded far and wide. Sumerian texts mention ships arriving from a land called Meluhha, carrying carnelian beads, timber, ivory and gold. Most historians think Meluhha was the Indus Valley civilisation. Indus-style seals and beads have turned up in Mesopotamian cities, evidence of the Harappan trade network that linked the two great river cultures.

Kings, queens and a poet

Around 2300 BCE, Sargon of Akkad, from just north of Sumer, conquered its cities and built one of the first empires. He made his daughter Enheduanna high priestess at Ur. She wrote hymns to the moon god and the goddess Inanna, and she is often described as the earliest author known by name.

Sumerian cities rose again under the kings of Ur, who built its great ziggurat around 2100 BCE. Its lower levels still stand near Nasiriyah in Iraq. About 2000 BCE, Ur was sacked and Sumer faded. Scribes kept copying the Sumerian language for centuries, much as Europeans later used Latin, long after anyone spoke it at home.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Writing was invented so people could record stories and history.

Truth: In Sumer, writing grew out of bookkeeping. Officials needed to track who owed what to the temples. Literature came centuries later.

Try this

Roll out a flat slab of clay or play dough. Use the end of a lolly stick or a chopstick, cut at an angle, to press wedge-shaped marks into it. Invent symbols for three things in your house, like a cup or a book, and "write" a list of how many you have. You've made your own cuneiform tablet.
