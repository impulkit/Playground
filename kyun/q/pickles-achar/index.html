How are Indian pickles made?

Many Indian grandmothers set out jars of raw mango, salt, chilli and oil to sit in the hot summer sun, sometimes for weeks.

The short answer

Indian pickles, called achar, are fruits or vegetables preserved with salt, oil, spices and often something sour, like lemon juice or vinegar. Raw mango, lemon, lime, chilli, carrot and gooseberry (amla) are common. They are cut into pieces, mixed with salt and spices like mustard, fenugreek, turmeric and chilli, and packed into jars with oil.

The salt draws water out, the oil seals out air, and the sourness makes life hard for germs. Many pickles also sit in the sun for days to help them mature. A good pickle can keep for a year or more, adding a burst of flavour to a simple meal of dal and rice.

Surprising but true

Salt pulls water out of microbes by osmosis, drying them out so they can't grow. That's one of the oldest food-preserving tricks on Earth.

Why pickles don't rot

Food spoils when bacteria, yeasts and moulds grow in it. These microbes need water. Salt pulls water out of the fruit and out of microbes too, by a process called osmosis. With little free water left, most harmful microbes can't survive.

Oil covers the pickle and keeps out air, which moulds need. Sour ingredients like lemon juice, vinegar or the natural acid in raw mango make the pickle too acidic for many germs. Spices such as turmeric and mustard add flavour and may help slow the growth of some microbes too. Together, these tricks let families preserve food long before anyone had a fridge.

Mango season

In many Indian homes, pickle-making is a summer event. When raw mangoes appear in markets, families buy them by the kilo. The mangoes are washed, dried completely and chopped, often with a special cutter for the hard stone.

In Andhra Pradesh, the famous avakaya is made with raw mango pieces, mustard powder, red chilli powder, salt and sesame oil. In Punjab, mango pickle uses mustard oil and fennel. In Tamil Nadu and Kerala, tiny whole raw mangoes are pickled in brine to make vadu manga.

The jars are often set out in the sun for several days, stirred each day. The heat helps the salt and spices soak in.

Pickles with microbes

Some Indian pickles are fermented, which means friendly microbes are allowed to grow on purpose. In Punjab, a winter pickle called kanji is a purple drink made by fermenting black carrots in water with mustard seeds.

In Northeast India, bamboo shoot pickles are fermented for a sour, tangy taste. These friendly microbes are related to the ones that turn milk into curd.

Are all germs bad?Right now, about as many bacteria live in and on you as there are cells in your body, and most of them are on your side.

American pickles

In the USA, "pickle" usually means a cucumber pickle. Many are kept in vinegar, while traditional dill pickles are fermented in salty water. Both use the same basic ideas as Indian achar: salt and acid stop spoiling microbes.

The big difference is oil and spice. American pickles are mostly sour and salty. Indian pickles are often hot, oily and strongly spiced, and a small spoonful goes a long way.

Keeping the jar safe

Families have rules for pickle jars, and most of them make scientific sense. Use a clean, dry spoon. Keep the lid tight. Make sure there's always a layer of oil on top. Store the jar in a cool, dry place.

If water gets in, mould can grow. That's why pickles are made in the dry summer heat, why fruit is dried well before pickling, and why a grandmother will not let anyone dip a wet spoon in her jar.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pickles keep forever and can never go bad.

Truth: Well-made pickles last a long time, often a year or more, but they can still spoil. Too little salt or oil, or water getting into the jar, can let mould grow. A pickle that smells odd or has fuzzy spots should be thrown away.

Try this

Do a salt test with an adult. Slice a cucumber into two equal piles. Sprinkle one with salt and leave the other plain. After 20 minutes, look at both. The salted slices will have sweated out water, the same thing that happens inside a pickle jar.
