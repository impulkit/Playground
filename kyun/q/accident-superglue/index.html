How was superglue discovered by accident?

Superglue was invented twice by accident, and the first time its inventor threw it away because it stuck to everything.

The short answer

Superglue was found by a chemist named Harry Coover, not once but twice. In 1942, during the Second World War, his team at the Eastman Kodak company in the USA was trying to make clear plastic for the sights on guns. One substance, called cyanoacrylate, stuck to everything it touched, including the equipment. It was useless for gun sights, so they set it aside.

Nine years later, in 1951, Coover and a colleague, Fred Joyner, met the same chemical while hunting for a tough coating for jet aircraft. This time Coover saw what it really was: an extraordinarily strong glue. It went on sale in 1958.

Surprising but true

Police use superglue fumes to find hidden fingerprints, a trick discovered when the fumes made prints appear by surprise.

The ruined machine

The 1951 accident was an expensive one. Joyner spread a thin film of cyanoacrylate between two glass prisms of a refractometer, an instrument that measures how light bends. He couldn't separate them again. The costly instrument was wrecked, and the team began testing the liquid on anything they could find.

Everything stuck. Metal to metal, glass to glass, rubber to wood, all in seconds and without heat or clamps. In 1958 Eastman sold it as "Eastman 910". A year later, on an American TV show called I've Got a Secret, Coover stuck two metal bars together with a single drop. Then he hung the show's host from them, lifting him off the ground.

Why it sets so fast

Most glues dry when water or another liquid evaporates out of them. Superglue does the opposite: water makes it set. Its small molecules are waiting to link up into long chains called polymers. The slightest touch of water starts a chain reaction, and the chains lock into a hard plastic web.

There's a faint film of moisture on almost every surface, and plenty on human skin. That's why superglue grips your fingers in a few seconds, and why a tube left open hardens inside.

Why is glue sticky?Glue works by doing two jobs at once. It has to cling to the things you're joining, and it has to cling to itself.

Glue that heals

Doctors soon noticed that a glue which sets on wet skin might close wounds. Early versions could irritate the body, but army medics in the Vietnam War used spray versions to slow bleeding on the battlefield. In 1998 the USA approved a special medical skin glue, and today doctors use similar glues to close some small cuts without stitches.

Coral scientists even use thick superglue gels underwater, to fix broken pieces of living coral back onto reefs.

Handle with care

Superglue's speed makes it tricky. Because it sets in seconds, there's little time to line pieces up, and a drop in the wrong place can glue your fingers to the thing you're fixing. It works best on small, tight joints, not on big gaps or on soft things like cloth.

It also gives off sharp fumes that can sting the eyes and nose, so it's best used with a window open, and with an adult. Nail polish remover can soften dried superglue, but an adult should use it and keep it well away from eyes.

Chemists have since made many kinds: gels that don't drip, slower versions that give you a few extra seconds, and bendy ones that don't crack when the object flexes.

The fingerprint trick

In the late 1970s, police scientists noticed that superglue fumes in a closed space left white crusts on fingerprints. The fumes react with the tiny traces of water and oil a finger leaves behind, building a white plastic copy of every ridge. Detectives now place objects in a box, warm a few drops of glue, and wait for invisible prints to appear.

Coover was honoured with America's National Medal of Technology and Innovation in 2010. And the strangest part? About ten years after superglue went on sale, another chemist made a glue so weak it was almost a failure, and it became the sticky note.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If superglue sticks your fingers together, pull them apart fast.

Truth: Pulling can tear the skin. The safe way is to soak the fingers in warm soapy water and gently roll them apart over a few minutes. Ask an adult to help, and get medical help if glue goes near the eyes or mouth.

Try this

With an adult, compare how three glues you have at home behave, such as a glue stick, white school glue and tape. Stick two strips of paper with each, wait ten minutes, then gently pull. Rank them and notice which ones you can peel apart and which tear the paper first.
