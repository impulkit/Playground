Why do we pay to use some roads?

Most streets are free to drive on. Then, on a big highway, a sign appears asking for money. Why do some roads charge and others don't?

The short answer

Building a big highway costs crores of rupees for every kilometre, and keeping it smooth costs more each year. A toll is a fee that drivers pay to help cover that cost.

The idea is simple: the people who use the road most pay for it most. Roads without tolls are paid for out of taxes, shared by everyone.

Surprising but true

Tolls are thousands of years old. Ancient rulers charged travellers and traders to cross bridges, enter cities or use mountain passes.

Who builds the road?

Sometimes a government builds a highway with tax money and charges a toll to win some of it back. Often, a private company builds the road and is allowed to collect tolls for a set number of years, perhaps 15 or 30, to earn its money back. After that, the road returns to the government.

In India, national highways are looked after by the National Highways Authority of India. Building a road means buying land, laying layers of stone and asphalt, and putting up bridges, lights and signs, so tolls help pay for all of it.

An old idea

Tolls go back thousands of years. Ancient rulers taxed traders at bridges, city gates and mountain passes. The Romans charged fees at some bridges, and medieval towns in Europe collected tolls to fix their roads.

In the 1700s and 1800s, England had thousands of turnpikes, roads blocked by a pole that was turned aside once you paid. That is where the barrier at a toll plaza comes from.

How does a toll booth know your car has paid?The FASTag sticker on an Indian windscreen has no battery. It wakes up only when a toll booth's radio beam hits it.

Paying without stopping

Stopping at a toll booth wastes time and fuel. So India uses FASTag, a sticker on the windscreen that pays by radio as the car passes, a bit like a UPI payment.

Newer plazas go further, with no barrier at all. A sensor on a metal frame above the road, called a gantry, reads the tag, while cameras photograph the number plate, much like traffic cameras. If the payment fails, the owner gets a notice to pay. Tolls are also set by vehicle, so heavy trucks, which wear out the road much faster, pay the most.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Once a toll road is paid for, the toll always stops.

Truth: Some tolls end when the building cost is paid back, but many continue to pay for repairs, lighting, ambulances and future widening. The rules differ from road to road and country to country.

Try this

On your next highway trip, look for the board at the toll plaza that lists charges. Compare the price for a car, a bus and a truck. Then guess how much heavier each one is.
