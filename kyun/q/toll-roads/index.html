Why do we pay to use some roads?

Most streets are free to drive on. Then, on a big highway, a sign appears asking for money. Why do some roads charge and others don't?

The short answer

Building a big highway costs crores of rupees for every kilometre, and keeping it smooth costs more each year. A toll is a fee that drivers pay to help cover that cost.

The idea is simple: the people who use the road most pay for it most. Roads without tolls, like the street outside your home, are paid for out of taxes, shared by everyone.

Surprising but true

Tolls are thousands of years old. Ancient rulers charged travellers and traders to cross bridges, enter cities or use mountain passes.

How it works

Think of a city's free footpath and its metro. Everyone can walk on the footpath for free. But the metro is faster, cost far more to build, and needs staff to run it, so riders buy a ticket. A highway is like the metro, and the toll is its ticket.

There are two main ways a toll road gets built. Sometimes the government builds it with tax money and charges a toll to win some of it back. Often, a private company builds it and is allowed to collect tolls for a set number of years, perhaps 15 or 30, to earn its money back. After that, the road returns to the government.

Building a road means buying land, laying layers of stone and asphalt, and putting up bridges, lights and signs. After that come repairs, cranes to clear broken-down vehicles, ambulances and patrol vans. Tolls help pay for all of it.

The price depends on the vehicle and the distance. In India, the rates are set by the National Highways Fee Rules of 2008. Cars pay the least among four-wheelers, buses and trucks pay more, and the biggest trucks pay the most, because heavy vehicles wear out a road much faster. Rates are revised every year on 1 April, partly to keep up with inflation.

Where it came from

Tolls go back thousands of years. The Arthashastra, an ancient Indian book on running a kingdom, describes an officer in charge of tolls collected at city gates. The Romans charged fees at some bridges, and medieval towns in Europe collected tolls to fix their roads.

In 1663, England passed a law allowing tolls on part of a main road north of London. Over the next 200 years, it built thousands of turnpikes, roads blocked by a pole that was turned aside once you paid. That is where the barrier at a toll plaza comes from.

India set up the National Highways Authority of India by law in 1988, and it began work in 1995. In the early 2000s, it built the Golden Quadrilateral, about 5,800 km of highway joining Delhi, Mumbai, Chennai and Kolkata. Many new highways since then have been paid for partly by tolls.

India's story

Toll roads have grown fast in India. In 2021-22, national highways had 733 toll plazas. By 2025-26, there were 1,209. Rajasthan had the most, 172, followed by Uttar Pradesh with 141.

For years, every vehicle stopped at a booth, and drivers paid in cash. At busy plazas, long queues wasted time and fuel. So India brought in FASTag, a sticker on the windscreen with a tiny chip. A reader at the plaza talks to it using radio waves and takes the money from a linked account, a bit like a UPI payment. FASTag became compulsory on national highways in February 2021.

Newer plazas go further, with no barrier at all. A sensor on a metal frame above the road, called a gantry, reads the tag while cameras photograph the number plate, much like traffic cameras. The first of these opened at Choryasi in Gujarat on 1 May 2026. If a payment fails, the owner gets an e-notice and has 72 hours to pay before the toll is doubled.

The numbers that matter

₹70,278 crore. The toll collected on national highways in 2025-26. In 2021-22, it was about ₹33,929 crore, so it has roughly doubled in four years.

98%. The share of highway tolls paid by FASTag. Cash at the booth has almost vanished.

11.86 crore. The number of FASTags issued by early 2026. That is roughly one tag for every 12 people in India.

₹15. What each crossing costs with the ₹3,000 annual pass for private cars, which covers 200 crossings. Without the pass, a single crossing can cost several times more.

1.1 crore. The vehicles that passed through India's first six barrier-free plazas in their first three months, without stopping.

What people argue about

Supporters say tolls are fair because the people who use a road pay for it, not every taxpayer. Toll money also lets roads be built faster, because companies can borrow against future tolls instead of waiting for the government's budget.

Critics say drivers already pay road tax when they buy a vehicle, and tax on every litre of fuel, so tolls can feel like paying twice. They also complain when tolls continue on roads that are full of potholes, or when plazas sit close together. Indian rules say that on roads built with public money, the toll drops to 40% once the building cost has been recovered. Transport groups point out that trucks pay the most, and those costs can show up in the price of things the trucks carry.

Myths and mix-ups

"Tolls only pay for building the road." They also pay for repairs, lighting, cranes, ambulances and patrols, which go on for as long as the road is open.

"Everyone pays at a toll plaza." On national highways, motorbikes and scooters do not pay tolls. Ambulances and fire engines are also let through free.

"No barrier means no toll." At barrier-free plazas the cameras still see every vehicle. Drivers who do not pay get a notice, and the amount doubles if they ignore it.

What it means for you

For a family road trip, tolls are part of the cost, along with fuel and food. Keeping the FASTag topped up saves time at plazas. A family that drives the same highway often may save money with an annual pass.

Tolls also touch you when you are not travelling. Trucks carry vegetables, milk, medicines and school books across India, and part of what they pay in tolls ends up in what those things cost. Smooth surfaces, good lights, clear signs and ambulances on call also matter for road safety. Good roads help the whole economy, because goods and people move faster.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Once a toll road is paid for, the toll always stops.

Truth: Some tolls end when the building cost is paid back, but many continue to pay for repairs, lighting, ambulances and future widening. The rules differ from road to road and country to country.
