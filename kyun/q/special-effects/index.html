How do films make special effects?

One of cinema's first famous special effects began, so the story goes, with an accident. A camera jammed in a Paris street, and a bus seemed to turn into a hearse.

The short answer

Special effects are tricks that show things on screen that could not really be filmed, or would be too dangerous or costly. There are two main kinds. Practical effects happen in front of the camera: models, make-up, puppets, fake snow and wires that lift actors. Visual effects, or VFX, are added later on a computer.

Most modern films mix both. An actor might stand in front of a green screen holding a real sword, while a computer adds the castle, the dragon and the storm. Done well, you never notice the join.

Surprising but true

Actors are often filmed in front of bright green screens because that shade of green is very unlike human skin, so computers can cut it away without cutting the actor.

A magician with a camera

Georges Méliès was a stage magician in Paris. According to his own story, his camera jammed one day while filming a street. When it started again, the scene had changed, so on screen a bus seemed to become a hearse. He realised a camera could do magic tricks no stage could.

Méliès used stopping and starting, double exposures (filming twice on the same strip) and painted sets. In A Trip to the Moon (1902), a rocket lands in the eye of the Man in the Moon. That image is still one of the best known in film history.

Models, mattes and monsters

For decades, films relied on clever handmade work. Small models of ships, buildings and spacecraft were filmed close up so they looked huge. Artists painted scenery on glass, called matte paintings, and filmed through the glass so real actors appeared inside painted cities.

In King Kong (1933), the giant ape was a model less than half a metre tall, moved a tiny bit between each frame. This is the same stop-motion idea used in animation. Monster suits, rubber masks and puppets filled films for years after.

Green screens

To put actors in a place that does not exist, film crews shoot them in front of a plain screen of one colour, usually bright green or blue. Software then picks out every pixel of that colour and replaces it with another picture. This is called chroma key. TV weather forecasters use the same method to stand in front of moving maps.

Green is popular because it is far from any human skin tone, and digital cameras pick up green especially clearly. Blue is used when a character is wearing green. An actor who wears a green shirt by mistake ends up with a hole in their body.

Computers take over

Computer graphics, or CGI, grew in the 1980s and 1990s. In Jurassic Park (1993), computer-made dinosaurs looked real enough to stun audiences, though most dinosaur shots used robot puppets. If you want to know how big a real one was, see T. rex compared.

Later, motion capture let actors wear suits covered in dots while cameras tracked their movements. Computers used that data to move a digital creature, so a human performance drives a character who is not human at all. Today, Indian films such as Baahubali (2015) used hundreds of VFX artists, and Indian studios also work on many big Hollywood films.

Effects you hear

Some effects are sounds. Footsteps, rustling clothes and creaking doors are often recorded later by Foley artists, named after the American sound expert Jack Foley. Squeezing cornflour in a bag can sound like walking on snow, and snapping celery can sound like breaking bones.

In films made in Hollywood and in Mumbai, the same scene may have dozens of these sounds layered together. When they are missing, a scene can feel strangely empty, even if the pictures look perfect.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Today all special effects are done by computers.

Truth: Many films still use real models, make-up, puppets, fake rain and controlled explosions, called practical effects. Computers are often used to clean up or add to them, not to replace them.

Try this

Try the oldest trick in cinema. Put a phone on a stack of books so it cannot move, and start recording a friend standing still. Pause, let your friend step out of the frame, then carry on recording. When you play it back, your friend vanishes.
