What are salt flats?

Bolivia's Salar de Uyuni is so flat that its height changes by less than a metre across an area bigger than Goa and Delhi put together.

The short answer

A salt flat is a wide, flat stretch of ground covered with a crust of salt. Most are the beds of ancient lakes that dried up. Rivers had been carrying tiny amounts of dissolved salt into these lakes for thousands of years. When the water evaporated, the salt stayed, and layer by layer it built a white floor that can be many metres thick.

The biggest is Salar de Uyuni in Bolivia, high in the Andes mountains, at about 10,500 km². Others include the Bonneville Salt Flats in Utah, USA, and the salt marshes of the Rann of Kutch in Gujarat, India.

Surprising but true

After rain, a thin layer of water turns Uyuni into the world's biggest mirror, and satellites have used its flatness to check their measuring instruments.

The world's biggest mirror

Uyuni sits about 3,650 m above sea level, higher than most of the Himalayan hill stations. Its surface is astonishingly level. Across the whole salar, the height changes by less than a metre.

In the rainy season, a few centimetres of water lie on top. With no wind, the water reflects the sky perfectly, and the horizon disappears. Tourists take photos where the clouds seem to be under their feet. Because it is so flat and so bright, scientists have used Uyuni to check the measuring instruments on satellites that bounce lasers off Earth's surface.

Hotels made of salt

Around the edges of Uyuni, some hotels are built from blocks of salt cut from the flat. Walls, beds, tables and chairs are all salt. Guests are politely asked not to lick the walls.

Underneath the crust is something far more valuable: brine, very salty water rich in lithium. Lithium goes into the rechargeable batteries in phones, laptops and electric cars. Bolivia has some of the largest known lithium resources on Earth, and companies and governments are working out how to extract it without harming the water supply of nearby communities.

A racetrack in Utah

The Bonneville Salt Flats in Utah are what's left of a giant ancient lake, Lake Bonneville, that once covered much of the area around 15,000 years ago. Today the salt is hard and smooth enough to drive on. Since the early 1900s, people have raced cars there to set land speed records, and a race week is still held most summers.

The salt is also slowly shrinking. Some has been pumped away for industry, and racers worry that the crust is now thinner than it used to be.

India's white desert

The Great Rann of Kutch in Gujarat is different from Uyuni. It is a salt marsh that floods with seawater and rain during the monsoon, then dries out into a crust of white salt in winter. Under a full moon, the white ground glows so brightly that the area has become famous for its winter festival, the Rann Utsav.

Next door, the Little Rann is home to the Indian wild ass, found nowhere else in the world. Salt workers called Agariyas have harvested salt from shallow pans here for generations, evaporating brine pumped from underground, a process that turns on simple evaporation.

Salt flats beyond Earth

Scientists have spotted what look like old salt deposits on Mars, left behind when its lakes dried up billions of years ago. On Earth, salt flats can trap tiny bubbles of ancient water inside their crystals. If life ever existed on Mars, some researchers think salty places like these are a good spot to look for traces of it.

That idea links Uyuni and the Rann to one of the biggest questions in science. And the salt in them shares a story with the salt in the ocean, explained in why the sea is salty.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Salt flats are just dry, dead deserts where nothing lives.

Truth: Many salt flats are full of life at the edges or in their shallow lakes. Uyuni's lagoons host flamingos, and the Rann of Kutch is home to the Indian wild ass and huge flamingo breeding colonies.

Try this

Stir two spoonfuls of salt into half a cup of warm water until it dissolves. Pour a thin layer onto a dark plate and leave it on a sunny windowsill for a few days. When the water evaporates, you will have your own mini salt flat, with crystals you can see with a magnifying glass.
