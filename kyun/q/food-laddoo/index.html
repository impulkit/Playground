Why are laddoos made for festivals?

In 2026, one laddoo in Hyderabad sold at auction for 39 lakh rupees. In 1994 the same tradition began with a price of 450.

The short answer

A laddoo is a ball of flour, seeds or grated coconut, held together with ghee and sugar or jaggery. It is cheap to make in big batches, easy to share, and it stays good for days without a fridge. That made it perfect for festivals, when a family might feed dozens of guests and send sweets to every neighbour.

Laddoos also carry meaning. In Hindu tradition, the laddoo and its cousin the modak are Lord Ganesha's favourite sweets. Laddoos are handed out at weddings, after exam results and when a baby is born. Saying "laddoo" in many Indian homes is a short way of saying "good news".

Surprising but true

The Tirupati temple kitchen makes, on average, nearly 3 lakh laddoos a day, and the laddoo has its own legal name protection, like Darjeeling tea.

A sweet that keeps

Before fridges, food spoiled quickly in Indian summers. Laddoos solved that. They contain little water, and sugar and ghee slow down the microbes that make food go bad. A tin of besan laddoos can last a week or more.

Ghee and sugar were once expensive luxuries. Making a plate of laddoos was a way to say "this day matters". Each region has its own version. Til laddoos made with sesame and jaggery are eaten at Makar Sankranti in January. Motichoor laddoos, built from hundreds of tiny fried drops of batter, turn up at Diwali and weddings. In Kerala and Tamil Nadu, coconut laddoos are common.

The round shape helps too. A ball has no corners to crumble, packs neatly into a box and fits in one hand, which is useful when you are eating standing up in a crowd.

The laddoo with its own passport

The most famous laddoo in India is made at the Tirumala temple near Tirupati in Andhra Pradesh. Records say the temple started offering laddoos to pilgrims on 2 August 1715. Today its kitchen turns out nearly 3 lakh a day on average, using gram flour, ghee, sugar, cashews, raisins and cardamom.

In 2009, the Tirupati laddoo got a geographical indication, or GI tag. That legal label means only the temple can sell a laddoo under that name, the same kind of protection given to Darjeeling tea and Basmati rice. For many pilgrims, carrying a box of Tirupati laddoos home to relatives is part of the trip.

The 39-lakh laddoo

Every year during Ganesh Chaturthi, people in Balapur, on the edge of Hyderabad, auction the giant laddoo offered to their Ganesha. The tradition began in 1994, when a local farmer won it for 450 rupees. Bids climbed year after year. In 2026, the winning bid was 39 lakh rupees, about 8,600 times the first price.

Buyers believe the laddoo brings good luck, and the money goes to local causes. Other neighbourhoods in Hyderabad now hold their own auctions, sometimes for even bigger sums.

Where did laddoos come from?

Nobody knows for certain. The word comes from old Sanskrit names for round sweets. One popular story says the ancient physician Sushruta rolled sesame, jaggery and peanuts into balls to help patients take medicine. That story is told often, but historians have found little solid evidence for it.

What we do know is that ball-shaped sweets have been made across India for many centuries. Cooks used whatever grew nearby: sesame in the north, coconut in the south, rice flour in Bengal and Odisha. Laddoos made with millets are coming back too, as people look for older grains.

There is a bit of maths hiding in every laddoo. Of all shapes, a sphere holds the most stuff inside for the least outside surface. That is why a laddoo dries out slowly, and why soap bubbles and raindrops pull themselves into round shapes too.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Laddoos are just festival treats and were never meant to be everyday food.

Truth: Some laddoos are traditional everyday foods. Gond, methi and dry-fruit laddoos are made in many homes in winter, and are often given to new mothers to help them build strength.

Try this

With an adult's help, roll a no-cook laddoo. Mash a handful of soft dates, mix in crushed peanuts or desiccated coconut, and press small balls in your palms. Compare how well they hold together with and without a spoon of ghee.
