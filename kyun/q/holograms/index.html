What is a hologram?

Cut a true hologram in half, and each half still shows the whole picture, just from a narrower view.

The short answer

A hologram is a kind of picture that captures depth. When you move your head, you can see around the objects in it, as if they were really there behind the glass. An ordinary photo can't do that. It stays flat whichever way you look.

The difference is in what each one records. A photo records how bright and what colour the light is at each point. A hologram also records the shape of the light waves, which carry information about how far away each part of the scene was. When light shines on the hologram later, those waves are rebuilt, and your eyes see the scene in three dimensions.

Surprising but true

The "holograms" of singers that appear on stage at concerts usually aren't holograms at all. Most use a stage trick first shown in London in 1862.

Waves that overlap

Light travels as waves, with peaks and dips, a bit like ripples on a pond. When two sets of ripples cross, they make a pattern. Where peaks meet peaks, the water rises higher. Where peaks meet dips, they cancel out.

To make a hologram, scientists split a beam of laser light in two. One beam lights up the object, then bounces off it onto a special photographic plate. The other beam goes straight to the plate. The two beams overlap, making a fine pattern of light and dark stripes, far too small to see. That pattern is the hologram. When the right light shines through it later, the pattern bends the light into the same waves that came off the object.

An idea waiting for the laser

In 1947, the Hungarian-born engineer Dennis Gabor, working in England, came up with the idea while trying to improve electron microscopes. He named it hologram, from Greek words meaning "whole message". But he couldn't make good ones. Ordinary light is a jumble of different waves, and holograms need light where all the waves march in step.

That kind of light arrived with the laser in 1960. Within a few years, scientists in the USA and the Soviet Union made the first sharp 3D holograms. Gabor won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1971.

What is a laser?Astronauts left mirrors on the Moon in 1969. Scientists still fire lasers at them to measure the Moon's distance to within a few centimetres.

Holograms in your wallet

You've probably held a hologram today. The shiny, rainbow-coloured stickers on bank cards, passports, concert tickets, medicine packets and some brand-name products are holograms. They're very hard to copy with an ordinary printer or photocopier, so they help show something is genuine.

These security holograms are made differently from laboratory ones. They work in ordinary light, and their rainbow colours shift as you tilt them. They're pressed into thin metal foil like tiny grooves, millions at a time.

The ghost on stage

In the film Star Wars (1977), a small robot projects a glowing, flickering message from Princess Leia into thin air. It became the most famous "hologram" in the world, and inspired scientists and engineers for decades. Real holograms can't float in empty air like that, because the light has to come from a surface.

So what about the singers who appear on stage at concerts, sometimes years after they've died? These usually use a trick called Pepper's ghost. A bright video is shown on a screen hidden below or above the stage and reflected in a sheet of clear glass or film angled towards the audience. The reflection seems to float. The trick was made famous by the scientist John Henry Pepper in a London show in 1862, to make a ghost appear in a play, and theatres soon copied it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The glowing 3D singers and figures shown at concerts are holograms.

Truth: Most use an old trick called Pepper's ghost, reflecting a bright video off a sheet of clear glass or film so it seems to float on stage. Real holograms are usually small and need special lighting.

Try this

Look closely at a shiny security sticker on a box, a bank card or a ticket. Tilt it slowly and watch how the picture or colours change. Then look at an ordinary photo and tilt it. Notice which one seems to have depth or change as you move.
