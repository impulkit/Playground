How did the Mongols build the biggest land empire?

At its height, the Mongol Empire stretched from the coast of China to Eastern Europe. Officials carrying a metal pass could get fresh horses at relay stations across it.

The short answer

The Mongols were nomadic herders from the grasslands, or steppe, of Mongolia. In 1206 a leader named Temujin united the Mongol tribes and took the title Genghis Khan. He and his sons and grandsons then conquered China, Central Asia, Persia, Russia and parts of Eastern Europe.

By the late 1200s the Mongol Empire covered about 23 to 24 million square kilometres, the largest connected land empire in history. Their success came from expert horse archers, fast-moving armies organised in units of ten, ruthless use of fear, and a willingness to borrow skills from the peoples they conquered.

Surprising but true

The word "Mughal", as in India's Mughal Empire, comes from the Persian word for Mongol. Babur, the first Mughal emperor, was descended from Genghis Khan through his mother.

Riders of the steppe

Mongol children learned to ride almost as soon as they could walk. Herding animals across the open steppe made them tough, patient and expert archers. Their bows, made of wood, horn and sinew glued together, were short enough to shoot from the saddle and powerful enough to fire arrows a long way.

Each soldier brought several horses and switched between them. Mongol armies could ride fast and far, live on dried meat and mare's milk, and appear where no one expected them.

An army built on tens

Genghis Khan reorganised his army into units of 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000 men. He broke up old tribal groups and mixed men from different tribes, so their loyalty was to the khan, not to their clan.

Leaders were promoted for skill and loyalty rather than noble birth. Scouts and spies gathered information before an attack. A favourite trick was the fake retreat. Riders pretended to flee, drew the enemy into a trap, then turned and surrounded them. When cities resisted, Mongol armies used captured Chinese and Persian engineers to build siege machines.

Fear and destruction

The Mongol conquests were often terrifying. Cities that surrendered might be spared. Cities that resisted were often destroyed, and many of their people killed, so that the next city would give in without a fight.

In 1258 Mongol forces captured Baghdad, then one of the world's great centres of learning, and destroyed much of it. Medieval writers gave huge death tolls for these campaigns, some probably exaggerated, but historians agree that millions died across Asia and Europe.

Roads, passes and trade

Once in control, the Mongols kept the roads safe. They set up the yam, a network of relay stations with fresh horses and supplies, a little like a postal system. Officials carried a paiza, a metal tablet that worked like a passport.

During this "Mongol peace", merchants, missionaries and ideas moved along the Silk Road more easily than before. The Venetian traveller Marco Polo spent years at the court of Kublai Khan, grandson of Genghis, who founded the Yuan dynasty in China. Paper money, gunpowder and printing ideas spread west, though disease may have travelled along the same routes.

The edges of the empire

Not every campaign succeeded. Kublai Khan's fleets tried to invade Japan in 1274 and 1281 and were wrecked by storms. The Japanese called the winds kamikaze, divine winds. In 1260, an army of the Mamluks of Egypt defeated the Mongols at Ain Jalut, in today's Israel.

India was also defended. The Delhi Sultanate, especially under Alauddin Khilji around 1300, beat back several Mongol invasions. By the 1260s, the empire had split into four khanates ruled by different branches of the family. Two centuries later, a prince called Babur, descended from Genghis Khan, founded the Mughal Empire in India.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Mongols won only because they had huge numbers.

Truth: Mongol armies were often smaller than their enemies'. They won through speed, discipline, clever tactics, good spies and the skill of horse archers trained from childhood.
