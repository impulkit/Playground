What happens to lost teeth around the world?

In Spain a little mouse called Ratoncito Pérez collects lost teeth, and in India many children throw their teeth onto the roof for a sparrow or crow.

The short answer

Children lose 20 baby teeth, usually between about ages 6 and 12. Almost every culture has a custom for what to do with them. In the US, UK and Canada, a tooth fairy swaps a tooth under the pillow for a coin or note. In Spain and Latin America, a mouse called Ratoncito Pérez does it. In India, Greece and many other countries, children throw their teeth onto the roof and ask a bird for a strong new one.

These customs share one wish: that the new tooth will grow in strong and straight. Many link teeth to animals with famously tough teeth, such as mice and rats.

Surprising but true

Ratoncito Pérez was made famous by a story written in 1894 for an eight-year-old Spanish king who had just lost a tooth.

Mice with a mission

Mice and rats have front teeth that never stop growing. That may be why so many tooth customs feature them. In France and Belgium, La Petite Souris, the little mouse, takes teeth and leaves a gift.

In Spain, the mouse has a name and a home. In 1894, the writer Luis Coloma wrote a story for the young King Alfonso XIII, who was eight and had just lost a tooth. In the story, Ratoncito Pérez lived in a biscuit tin in Madrid. Today a small plaque marks the spot on Calle del Arenal, and there's a little museum about the mouse nearby.

Up on the roof

In many parts of India, children throw a lost tooth onto the roof. Often they call to a sparrow or a crow, asking it to take the old tooth and bring a new, strong one. In some families the tooth is buried in the ground or tucked into a crack in a wall.

Greek children throw their teeth on the roof too. In Japan, an old custom says to throw a lower tooth up onto the roof and an upper tooth down under the floor, so new teeth grow towards the old ones. In parts of the Middle East, children throw their teeth up toward the Sun.

Where have all the house sparrows gone?The little brown bird that once nested in every Indian home has become scarce in many big cities. Nobody is completely sure why.

Where the tooth fairy came from

The tooth fairy is fairly new. The idea grew in the US in the early 1900s. A short play called The Tooth Fairy appeared in 1927, and the custom spread through books, cartoons and ads in the decades after.

It may have mixed older European customs of burying teeth or giving them to mice with stories of fairies. Today the tooth fairy is popular across the US, the UK, Canada and Australia, and children in Indian cities increasingly know her too.

The going rate

Each year a dental insurance company, Delta Dental, asks American parents how much the tooth fairy leaves. In its 2026 poll, the average was $5.84 per tooth, up from $5.01 the year before. The first tooth often earns more.

Back in 1998, the average was only about $1.30. Like everything else, the tooth fairy's prices go up with inflation. Some economists even joke that her payments track the stock market.

Why we lose teeth at all

A baby's jaw is small, so baby teeth are small too. As the face grows, there's room for bigger, stronger adult teeth. They grow underneath and press against the roots of baby teeth, which slowly dissolve. That's why a lost baby tooth has hardly any root.

Humans get just two sets of teeth. Sharks keep growing new ones all their lives, losing thousands. Elephants get six sets of molars and can starve if the last set wears out.

Mice and rats, meanwhile, have it the other way round. Their front teeth keep growing forever, and they must gnaw constantly to stop them getting too long, which is why so many children's stories trust them with lost teeth.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Baby teeth don't matter much, because they fall out anyway.

Truth: Baby teeth hold space for adult teeth and help children chew and speak. A baby tooth lost too early to decay can make adult teeth come in crooked, so brushing them matters.

Try this

Make a lost-tooth map. Ask friends, relatives and neighbours what they did with their baby teeth and where they grew up. Mark each custom on a world map or a map of India. Notice which traditions involve mice, birds, roofs or the ground.
