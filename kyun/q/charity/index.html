How do charities help people?

In 2014, India became the first large country to require big companies by law to spend part of their profits on helping society.

The short answer

A charity is an organisation set up to help people, animals or the environment, rather than to make a profit. Charities collect money, food, clothes and time from donors and volunteers, and use them to run schools, feed hungry people, care for the sick, rescue animals, or help after floods and earthquakes.

Some charities are tiny, like a group of neighbours running a free tuition class. Others are huge and work in dozens of countries. The best ones study what people really need, work alongside them, and check whether their help is making a difference.

Surprising but true

Andrew Carnegie, a steel tycoon who grew up poor in Scotland, paid to build more than 2,500 public libraries around the world.

Giving in many traditions

Almost every culture and religion encourages giving. In Hinduism, daan means giving without expecting anything back. Sikh gurdwaras run langar, free community kitchens that serve anyone who comes, whatever their background. The langar at the Golden Temple in Amritsar feeds tens of thousands of people a day.

In Islam, zakat, usually 2.5% of a person's savings each year, is one of the five pillars of faith. Many Muslims also give to the poor before Eid. In Christianity and Judaism, there is a long tradition of giving a tenth of one's income.

From kings to companies

Rulers and wealthy families have funded wells, rest houses and temples for centuries. In the 1800s and 1900s, rich industrialists began giving away huge fortunes. Andrew Carnegie, who arrived in America as a poor boy and became one of the richest men alive, paid for more than 2,500 public libraries around the world.

How did libraries begin?One of the oldest libraries ever found was made of clay. When enemies burned it, the fire baked the tablets hard and helped them survive.

In India, the Tata family set up trusts in the early 1900s that still fund hospitals, science institutes and scholarships. In 2014, India became one of the first countries, and by far the biggest, to make some company giving compulsory. Tiny Mauritius had done it in 2009. Larger companies must now spend 2% of their average profits on causes such as education, health and the environment.

Big organisations

In 1859, a Swiss businessman named Henri Dunant saw thousands of wounded soldiers left without care after a battle in Italy. His ideas led to the founding of the Red Cross in 1863. Today the Red Cross and Red Crescent help people in conflicts and disasters worldwide.

Other charities focus on one problem. Kailash Satyarthi, who shared the Nobel Peace Prize in 2014, founded a movement that has rescued thousands of children from forced labour in India.

Does the help work?

One famous test came from economists Abhijit Banerjee and Esther Duflo, who studied ways to fight poverty in India and elsewhere by running careful experiments, much like testing a medicine. They shared the 2019 Nobel Prize in economics with Michael Kremer for this work, and many charities now use similar methods.

Good intentions are not enough. Sometimes donations of old clothes or food arrive where they are not needed, or hurt local sellers. So many charities now test their work, comparing places that receive help with places that do not.

People also argue about charity itself. Some say it can never replace fair laws and good government. Others point out that charities often reach people that governments miss. Even famous figures are debated. Mother Teresa, who cared for the dying in Kolkata, is admired by millions, while some critics questioned the medical care in her homes. Most people agree that giving, done thoughtfully, can change lives.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A good charity spends nothing on staff or offices.

Truth: All organisations need some running costs, such as trained workers, transport and accounts. What matters most is whether the charity actually helps people and can show it.

Try this

With your family or class, choose one small cause, like a local animal shelter or a school library. Find out from the people who run it what they actually need, then collect only those items. Asking first is what good charities do too.
