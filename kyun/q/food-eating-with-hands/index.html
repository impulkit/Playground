Why does food taste better eaten with hands?

Billions of people eat with their fingers every day, from dal-chawal in Chennai to injera in Addis Ababa and burgers in Texas.

The short answer

Many people say food tastes better eaten with their hands, and science suggests there may be something in it. Flavour isn't made only by your tongue. Your brain builds it from taste, smell, sight, sound and touch all at once. When your fingers feel the warmth, softness and texture of food before it reaches your mouth, your brain gets extra information and gets ready to enjoy it.

Eating with hands also changes how you eat. You can mix rice and dal just the way you like, pick out bones and feel when food is too hot. Many people find it slower, more careful and more satisfying. For billions of people, it is simply the normal, best way to eat.

Surprising but true

In Ethiopia and Eritrea, feeding someone a bite of food with your own hand, called gursha, is a sign of friendship and respect.

Your brain builds flavour

Oxford scientist Charles Spence studies what he calls gastrophysics, the science of how our senses shape what we eat. His experiments show that crisps taste fresher when the crunch is louder, and that the weight and colour of a plate change how food seems to taste.

Touch is part of this too. Your fingertips are packed with sensors, among the most sensitive parts of your body. They tell you about temperature, texture and wetness. Some researchers think that touching food before eating it makes us pay more attention, which can make the first bite more satisfying. The research is still young, and results differ from person to person.

Eating with hands around the world

In India, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh and Pakistan, many people eat most meals by hand. In north India, people often eat rotis with just their fingertips, tearing a piece to scoop up dal or sabzi. In south India, rice is mixed with sambar or rasam using the whole hand and pushed into the mouth with the thumb.

In Ethiopia and Eritrea, meals are served on injera, a large spongy flatbread. Everyone tears off pieces and uses them to pick up stews from a shared plate. In West Africa, balls of fufu are dipped in soup, and in the Middle East, Malaysia and Indonesia, hand-eating is common too.

The right hand rule

In many hand-eating cultures, only the right hand touches food. The left hand is kept for washing and other jobs. Even left-handed children are often taught to eat with their right hand, though some families are more relaxed about it today.

Washing is central. Many homes and restaurants in India have a basin near the dining area, and people wash before and after every meal. Soap breaks up the oily layer that holds germs on skin, which is how soap fights germs.

Banana leaf manners

In south India, festive meals are often served on a banana leaf. Rice, dal, sambar, vegetables, pickles and payasam are placed in a particular order. After eating, many people fold the leaf towards themselves to show they enjoyed the meal, a custom that varies by region and community.

It is a whole way of eating, explored in South Indian meals. Eating from a leaf with your hands means no plates or cutlery to wash, and the leaf can be composted.

Americans do it too

Americans might think hand-eating is unusual, until they think about their favourite foods. Burgers, hot dogs, pizza, fried chicken, corn on the cob and tacos are all usually eaten by hand. Barbecue ribs are almost impossible to eat any other way.

Etiquette guides say hand food should be eaten by hand, because forcing a knife and fork onto a burger is awkward and messy.

Hands, spoons and choices

Hand-eating isn't the old way waiting to be replaced by cutlery. It is a different tradition with its own rules and skills. Many families mix both, eating rice by hand and using a spoon for curd or ice cream.

Knives, forks and spoons have their own long and surprising history. The fork, in particular, was once seen in Europe as a ridiculous luxury, a story told in when people started using forks.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Eating with your hands is dirty and unhygienic.

Truth: Hands that have been washed well with soap and water are perfectly clean for eating. Spoons and forks can also carry germs if they're not washed. Washing hands before and after a meal is part of the tradition in hand-eating cultures.

Try this

With a grown-up's OK, eat the same food twice, such as rice and dal or a piece of fruit, once with a spoon and once with clean hands. Close your eyes for a few bites each time. Notice the temperature, texture and how much you take in each mouthful, then decide which you enjoyed more.
