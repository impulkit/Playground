Who was Marie Curie?

Her notebooks are still so radioactive, about 90 years after her death, that they are kept in lead-lined boxes.

The short answer

Marie Curie was a Polish-born scientist who worked in France. With her husband Pierre, she discovered two new chemical elements, polonium and radium, in 1898. She also gave a name to the strange energy that some materials give off on their own: radioactivity.

She was the first woman to win a Nobel Prize, and she won a second one eight years later. Her work changed how scientists think about the atom and led to new ways of treating cancer. It also slowly harmed her health, because nobody yet knew how dangerous radiation could be.

Surprising but true

Marie Curie is still the only person to have won Nobel Prizes in two different sciences, physics and chemistry.

A secret university

Curie was born Maria Skłodowska in Warsaw in 1867. Poland was then ruled by Russia, and Polish universities did not accept women. Maria and her sister Bronisława studied at the "Flying University", a secret school that moved from house to house to avoid the authorities.

The sisters made a deal. Maria worked as a governess and sent money to help Bronisława study medicine in Paris. Then Bronisława helped Maria. In 1891, aged 24, Maria arrived at the Sorbonne. She was so poor that she sometimes fainted from hunger while studying, but she finished first in her class in physics.

A shed full of rocks

In Paris she met Pierre Curie, a physicist, and they married in 1895. The next year, Henri Becquerel found that uranium gave off invisible rays. Marie decided to study them for her doctorate.

She noticed that a uranium ore called pitchblende was far more active than its uranium alone could explain. Something else must be hiding inside. The Curies worked in a leaky old shed, grinding and boiling tonnes of ore. In 1898 they announced polonium, named after Poland, and then radium, which glowed faintly blue in the dark. It took them almost four more years of hard labour to collect a tenth of a gram of pure radium salt. That is about the weight of a few grains of rice, but it proved radium was real.

In 1903 the Curies and Becquerel shared the Nobel Prize in Physics. In 1906, Pierre was killed in a street accident in Paris. Marie took over his job and became the first woman professor at the Sorbonne. In 1911 she won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry on her own.

Little Curies on the front line

When the First World War began in 1914, Curie realised that X-ray machines could help surgeons find bullets and broken bones in wounded soldiers. She helped equip about 20 cars with X-ray machines and power generators. Soldiers called them "petites Curies", little Curies. She learned to drive and often operated the machines herself, with her teenage daughter Irène.

Around 200 more X-ray rooms were set up in field hospitals. Historians estimate that over a million wounded soldiers were X-rayed.

The cost of discovery

The Curies carried test tubes of radium in their pockets and kept them in desk drawers. They had no idea how harmful radiation was. Marie Curie died in 1934 of aplastic anaemia, a blood disease almost certainly caused by years of exposure.

Her daughter Irène Joliot-Curie went on to share the 1935 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with her husband. In 1995, Marie and Pierre's remains were moved to the Panthéon in Paris. She was the first woman honoured there for her own achievements. Her papers are still radioactive, because radium has a half-life of about 1,600 years.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Marie Curie discovered X-rays.

Truth: X-rays were discovered by the German physicist Wilhelm Röntgen in 1895. Curie studied radioactivity, a different thing. She did use X-ray machines during the First World War, driving mobile X-ray units to help army doctors.

Try this

Play the half-life game. Put 50 coins in a box, shake it and tip them out. Remove every coin showing heads, then put the rest back. Repeat and write down how many are left each round. Roughly half disappear each time, just as half of a radioactive substance changes in each half-life.
