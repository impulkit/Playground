What was the Underground Railroad?

In 1849, a man named Henry Brown mailed himself to freedom in a wooden box, and spent 27 hours inside it.

The short answer

The Underground Railroad was a secret network of people and safe houses in the United States in the 1800s. It helped enslaved Black people escape from the Southern states, where slavery was legal, to the North and to Canada, where they could live free.

It was not underground, and it had no trains. The name was a kind of code. Guides were called conductors. Safe houses were stations. The people escaping were sometimes called passengers or cargo. Using railway words let helpers talk about it without giving it away.

Nobody knows exactly how many people it helped. Historians estimate somewhere between about 30,000 and 100,000.

Surprising but true

The Underground Railroad was neither underground nor a railroad. It borrowed train words as a secret code.

How an escape worked

There was no single route and no map. A journey might cover more than 1,000 km (over 600 miles), mostly on foot, mostly at night. People hid by day in barns, attics, cellars and church basements. They waded through streams so dogs could not follow their scent. Many used the North Star to keep heading the right way.

Helpers came from many backgrounds. Free Black communities did much of the work. Quakers, a Christian group that opposed slavery, ran many stations. Each helper usually knew only the next stop, so if one was caught, the whole chain stayed safe. Being caught meant terrible punishment for those escaping, and fines or prison for those helping.

Why doesn't the North Star move?All night the stars wheel slowly across the sky, except one, which sits almost perfectly still above the North Pole.

The man in the box

Some escapes needed more than walking. In 1849, Henry Brown, enslaved in Richmond, Virginia, had a friend nail him inside a wooden crate about the size of a small cupboard. It was labelled "dry goods" and sent by train and steamboat to Philadelphia.

The trip took 27 hours. At times the box was set down upside down, with Brown on his head. Anti-slavery workers finally pried open the lid. Brown reportedly climbed out and greeted them politely, as if he had just stepped off a train. He became known as Henry "Box" Brown, and toured Northern towns and later Britain telling his story, sometimes with a copy of the box on stage.

Harriet Tubman, the famous conductor

Harriet Tubman escaped slavery in Maryland in 1849. Then she did something astonishing: she went back. Over about eleven years she returned roughly 13 times and guided about 70 people to freedom, including members of her own family.

She travelled in winter, when nights were long. She sometimes set off on Saturday nights, because notices about runaways could not appear in newspapers until Monday. She later said she "never lost a passenger".

Why Canada?

In 1850, the US government passed the Fugitive Slave Act. It said that people who had escaped could be captured even in free Northern states and sent back. Anyone who helped them could be fined or jailed. Suddenly the North was not safe.

So many freedom seekers kept going, across the border into Canada, where slavery had been abolished in 1834 under British law. Towns such as St Catharines in Ontario became new homes. Tubman herself lived there for several years.

Remembering the stories

Because the network was secret, few people wrote anything down at the time. One who did was William Still, a free Black man in Philadelphia. He interviewed hundreds of people who passed through, including Henry Brown, and published their stories in 1872.

The network grew quieter after the Civil War, which ended slavery in the United States in 1865. Today the US National Park Service keeps a list of hundreds of real Underground Railroad sites. And one of the people who escaped slavery, Frederick Douglass, used his freedom to become one of the most powerful speakers in American history.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Families sewed secret codes into quilts to show escaping people the way.

Truth: This idea became popular from a 1999 book, but historians have found no evidence from the time that quilt codes were used. The real network relied on word of mouth, songs, trusted helpers and the stars.

Try this

On a clear night, with an adult, look north and find the Big Dipper (in India it is called the Saptarishi). Follow the line made by its two end stars to a fairly bright star on its own. That is the North Star, which people escaping slavery used as a guide.
