What is science fiction?

In 1905, a Bengali woman wrote a story about a land where women run everything, cook with solar power and fly in air-cars. It is still read as science fiction today.

The short answer

Science fiction is storytelling that asks "what if?" about science and technology. What if we could travel through time? What if robots could think? What if we met life on another planet? The story then follows that idea to see what might happen to ordinary people.

It isn't the same as fantasy. Fantasy uses magic and doesn't need to explain itself. Science fiction usually tries to stay within what science might one day allow, even when it bends the rules.

Its worlds can be far in the future, on distant planets or in a slightly strange version of today. Many of the best stories are less about machines than about the humans using them.

Surprising but true

In 1945, the writer Arthur C. Clarke described satellites hanging in fixed spots above Earth to beam signals around the world, years before anyone launched a satellite.

A monster and a summer storm

Many people call Frankenstein, by the English writer Mary Shelley, the first modern science fiction novel. She began it at 18, during a cold, rainy summer in Switzerland in 1816, after her friends challenged each other to write ghost stories. It was published in 1818.

Its scientist, Victor Frankenstein, uses electricity and chemistry to bring a creature to life, then abandons it. The book asks a question that still matters: are inventors responsible for what they create?

Later in the 1800s, the French writer Jules Verne sent readers under the sea and to the Moon. In England, H. G. Wells wrote The Time Machine in 1895 and The War of the Worlds, about a Martian invasion, in 1898.

Science fiction from Bengal

India's story starts early too. In 1896, the scientist Jagadish Chandra Bose wrote a short Bengali tale about stopping a cyclone with a bottle of hair oil, which calmed the waves. It's often counted among the first science fiction stories in an Indian language.

In 1905, Begum Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain wrote Sultana's Dream, in English. In her imagined Ladyland, women run the country, collect energy from the Sun and travel by flying cars, while men stay at home. It was both science fiction and a sharp joke about how women were treated.

Later, Satyajit Ray wrote about Professor Shonku, a forgetful inventor who travels the world with his cat.

Is there life anywhere else?Nobody has found life beyond Earth yet. But for the first time, we know exactly where to look and have machines that can search.

Pulp magazines and big ideas

The term "science fiction" became popular through American magazines in the 1920s and 1930s, especially those run by the editor Hugo Gernsback. The world's top science fiction prizes, the Hugo Awards, are named after him.

Writers in those magazines dreamed up space travel, death rays and thinking machines. Isaac Asimov wrote stories about robots obeying "Three Laws", the first being that a robot may not harm a human. Engineers working on real robots and AI still argue about those laws.

When fiction got there first

Sometimes writers do guess well. In 1945, the British writer Arthur C. Clarke published an article describing satellites placed high above the equator, where they would circle at the same speed Earth turns. Each would seem to hang in one spot, beaming radio and TV signals. Today, such satellites carry television and weather data around the world.

Other guesses missed. Many old stories gave us flying cars and robot butlers by the year 2000, but nobody predicted that everyone would carry a phone with the world's knowledge in their pocket.

Stories on screens

Science fiction is now huge in films, TV shows, comics and games, from Star Trek and Star Wars to Indian films like Koi... Mil Gaya, about a boy who befriends an alien. In the late 1990s, Doordarshan viewers followed Captain Vyom, a space hero, every week. The props change with each decade. The questions underneath, about who we are and what we should build, stay much the same.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Science fiction is about predicting the future.

Truth: Some stories guess right, but most writers aren't trying to predict. They use made-up worlds and machines to ask questions about people today, like how we treat others, use power or deal with change.

Try this

Pick one real invention you use, such as a phone, a lift or a water filter. Write a half-page story set 100 years from now, where that invention has changed in a way nobody expected. Start with a person having an ordinary morning, and let the strange part creep in.
