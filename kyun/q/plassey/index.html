What happened at the Battle of Plassey?

On 23 June 1757, in a mango grove in Bengal, a small East India Company army defeated a much larger one, mostly because many of the Nawab's own soldiers never joined the fight.

The short answer

The Battle of Plassey was fought on 23 June 1757 near the village of Palashi, about 150 km north of Kolkata. On one side was Siraj ud-Daulah, the young Nawab of Bengal. On the other was the British East India Company, led by Robert Clive.

The Company won, and Siraj was killed soon after. The battle was short and not very bloody. But it put a ruler chosen by the Company on Bengal's throne. Within a few years, the East India Company was collecting Bengal's taxes, and it went on to take control of much of India.

Surprising but true

Fewer than about 25 of the Company's soldiers are thought to have been killed at Plassey, yet the battle changed who controlled one of the richest regions in the world.

Why Bengal mattered

In the 1700s, Bengal was one of the richest places on Earth. Its farmers grew rice, sugar and indigo, and its weavers made fine cotton and silk cloth sold across Asia and Europe. Bengal's textiles were so popular that European countries sometimes tried to ban them to protect their own weavers.

The Nawab of Bengal was in theory a governor under the Mughal emperor, but by then he ruled almost independently from his capital, Murshidabad. British, French and Dutch companies all had trading posts in Bengal, and each wanted special rights to trade without paying taxes.

A young Nawab and an angry company

Siraj ud-Daulah became Nawab in 1756, when he was in his early twenties. He was angry that the British were strengthening the walls of their fort in Calcutta without his permission and misusing their trading rights. In June 1756, his army captured the city.

Clive sailed up from Madras with soldiers and retook Calcutta in early 1757. He also attacked the French post at Chandernagore. Meanwhile, some of the most powerful people at Siraj's court were plotting against him. They included Mir Jafar, his army commander, and the Jagat Seths, a family of bankers so rich that they lent money to rulers.

The day of the battle

Clive made a secret deal: if Mir Jafar's troops held back, the Company would make him Nawab. On the morning of 23 June, Clive's army of about 3,000 men, most of them Indian soldiers called sepoys, lined up in a mango grove. Siraj's army was many times bigger.

The Nawab's guns opened fire, and for hours the two sides traded shots. Around midday, heavy monsoon rain swept over the field. The Company's men covered their gunpowder. Much of the Nawab's powder got soaked. One of Siraj's loyal commanders, Mir Madan, was killed, and Siraj was persuaded to pull back.

Mir Jafar's large force never attacked. By the afternoon, the Nawab's army was breaking up, and Siraj fled. Days later he was captured and killed.

What came after

Mir Jafar became Nawab, but the real power lay with the Company. He paid huge sums to Clive and other Company officers. When later Nawabs resisted, the Company fought again, winning the Battle of Buxar in 1764. In 1765, the Mughal emperor gave the Company the right to collect taxes in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa.

From that base, the Company built armies, fought the Mughals, Marathas and Mysore, and grew into a ruling power. Its rule lasted until the revolt of 1857, after which the British Crown took over directly.

Remembering Plassey

Today Palashi is a quiet place in West Bengal's Nadia district, with a small memorial. Many Indians see Siraj ud-Daulah as a ruler who stood up to foreign power and was betrayed, and in Bengali the name Mir Jafar has come to mean a traitor. British accounts once told Plassey as a great military victory. Most historians now agree it was won more by deals and money than by fighting.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The British won Plassey because their army was much stronger in battle.

Truth: The Company's force was small. It won mainly because key commanders and bankers had secretly turned against the Nawab, and much of his army never fought.
