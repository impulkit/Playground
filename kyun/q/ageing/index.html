Why do people get old?

A Greenland shark swimming today may have been born before the Taj Mahal was finished. Why do we wear out so much faster?

The short answer

Your body is made of trillions of cells. Every day, tiny bits of damage build up inside them. Copying mistakes creep into their DNA, proteins get bent out of shape, and waste piles up. Cells can repair much of this damage, but not all of it.

Over many years, the unrepaired damage adds up. Some cells stop dividing, some die, and others stop working well. Skin gets thinner and wrinkles, bones weaken, eyes and ears become less sharp, and the body heals more slowly. That slow wearing-down is ageing.

Why bodies don't simply repair everything for ever is a deeper puzzle. Scientists are still arguing about it.

Surprising but true

The oldest person whose age has been carefully checked, Jeanne Calment of France, lived to 122 years and 164 days. She died in 1997.

Cells that stop dividing

In 1961, the American scientist Leonard Hayflick noticed something about human cells grown in a dish. They divided about 40 to 60 times, then stopped. This became known as the Hayflick limit.

Part of the reason lies at the tips of your chromosomes, the packages that hold your DNA. These tips, called telomeres, work a bit like the plastic caps on shoelaces. Each time a cell divides, its telomeres get a little shorter. When they get too short, the cell stops dividing. Cells that stop but don't die can also send out signals that cause swelling and damage nearby.

Why evolution allows it

From evolution's point of view, what matters most is surviving long enough to have children and raise them. In the wild, most animals are killed by predators, disease, accidents or hunger long before they grow old. So there was little pressure for bodies to keep themselves perfect into old age.

Some scientists think genes that help us grow strong when young may cause harm later in life. Others think the body simply puts its energy into growing and having children rather than into perfect repair. Most think both ideas are partly true.

Animals that age slowly

Lifespans in nature vary hugely. A mayfly may live only a day as an adult. A mouse lives two or three years. But some animals age very slowly. Bowhead whales can live over 200 years. In 2016, scientists studying the eyes of Greenland sharks estimated that one of them may have been around 400 years old. That estimate could be off by more than a century either way.

One tiny jellyfish, Turritopsis dohrnii, can turn itself back into a young stage when it is hurt or stressed. It is sometimes called the immortal jellyfish, which you can read about in jellyfish that never die.

Living longer than ever

People today live much longer on average than their great-grandparents did. In India around 1950, average life expectancy at birth was only about 35 to 40 years. Today it is about 70. In the USA it is in the high 70s.

The biggest reason is that far fewer children die young, thanks to vaccines, clean water, better food and medicines like antibiotics. This has changed the shape of the population: many countries now have more older people than ever before.

Can ageing be slowed?

Some things clearly help people stay healthier as they age: being active, eating well, sleeping enough, not smoking and staying connected with friends and family. These don't stop ageing, but they can delay many of the problems that come with it.

Scientists are testing whether drugs or special diets can slow ageing itself. In worms, flies and mice, some have added years, or the equivalent, to lifespans. Whether the same works in people isn't known yet. For now, the oldest humans on record have lived to just over 120, and nobody has beaten Jeanne Calment.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In the old days, most people died by the time they were 30 or 40.

Truth: Average life expectancy was low mainly because so many children died young. People who survived childhood often lived into their 60s, 70s or beyond, even centuries ago.
