Why does thunder rumble?

Thunder is the sound of air being heated, in a split second, to several times hotter than the surface of the Sun.

The short answer

Thunder is the sound made by lightning. A lightning bolt heats the air around it to about 30,000 °C in a tiny fraction of a second. The air expands so fast that it pushes outward in a shock wave, like an explosion. That wave reaches your ears as thunder.

It rumbles because a lightning bolt is long, often several kilometres. Sound from the near end reaches you first, and sound from the far end arrives a few seconds later. Echoes off hills, buildings and clouds stretch the noise out even more.

Surprising but true

A lightning bolt can heat the air around it to about 30,000 °C, roughly five times hotter than the surface of the Sun.

Air that explodes

Lightning is a giant spark of electricity, a much larger version of the small shock you sometimes feel from static electricity. The channel it travels through is only a few centimetres wide, but the electric current in it is enormous.

In about a millionth of a second, the air in the channel becomes several times hotter than the surface of the Sun. Hot air takes up more room than cold air, and this air has no time to move politely out of the way. It bursts outward, squashing the air around it. That pressure wave spreads out in all directions as sound.

Crack or rumble?

If lightning strikes very close, you hear a sharp crack or a loud bang, because the sound from the nearest part of the bolt arrives all at once. From farther away, the sound has travelled a long way and bounced off many things. The high-pitched parts fade, leaving a low, rolling rumble.

The shape of the bolt matters too. Lightning zigzags and branches. Each twist sends sound towards you from a slightly different distance, so the thunder arrives as a long series of booms that blur together into one rumble.

Counting the distance

Light travels about a million times faster than sound. You see a lightning flash almost instantly, but its thunder takes time to arrive. Sound in air travels about 340 metres a second, so it covers about a kilometre every 3 seconds, or a mile every 5.

Count the seconds between flash and thunder and divide by 3 to get the distance in kilometres. It is simple speed and distance maths. If the gap is 30 seconds, the storm is about 10 km away.

When thunder roars

Thunder can usually be heard up to about 15 km away, and lightning can strike that far from the storm too. The US National Weather Service has a simple rule: "When thunder roars, go indoors." If you can hear thunder, you are close enough to be struck.

The safest places are inside a proper building or a car with a metal roof. Trees, open fields and water are the most dangerous places in a storm. In India, lightning is especially common in the weeks before and during the monsoon, and the India Meteorological Department sends out warnings when thunderstorms are expected.

Gods of thunder

For most of history, nobody knew what caused thunder, and people explained it through stories. In the Vedas, Indra, king of the gods, wields the vajra, a thunderbolt weapon, to release the rains. In Norse myths, Thor's hammer was said to cause thunder. Zeus in Greek stories threw lightning bolts.

About 2,000 years ago, the Roman poet Lucretius suggested that thunder might come from clouds crashing together, an idea that lasted a long time. Scientists showed in the 1700s and 1800s that lightning is electricity, and that thunder follows from it.

Thunder without rain

Sometimes you can see lightning flicker on the horizon on a summer night without hearing any thunder. People call it heat lightning, but it is ordinary lightning from a storm too far away for its thunder to reach you. Sound fades and bends in the air over long distances, so the light arrives and the sound doesn't. Your ears simply can't catch it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Thunder is caused by clouds bumping into each other.

Truth: Clouds are soft masses of tiny droplets and ice, and they can't bang together. Thunder is made by lightning, which heats the air so fast that it bursts outwards in a shock wave.

Try this

Make your own thunder in a paper bag. Blow up a paper bag, twist the neck shut and pop it by clapping your hands on it. The sudden burst of squeezed air spreading outwards makes the bang, a tiny version of what lightning does. Then pop one in a corridor or stairwell and listen for the echo that makes it rumble.
