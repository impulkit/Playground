Who lives in Antarctica?

Nobody is born and raised in Antarctica, yet every winter about a thousand people stay on through months of total darkness.

The short answer

No one lives in Antarctica permanently. There are no towns, no farms and no native people. Instead, about 70 research stations run by around 30 countries dot the continent, mostly near the coast.

In the short summer, a few thousand scientists, engineers, cooks, doctors and pilots live and work there. In winter, when the Sun doesn't rise for weeks and it is too stormy for most planes to land, about a thousand people stay behind. They study ice, weather, rocks, animals and space, then go home after a few months or a year. The only permanent residents are penguins, seals and a few hardy insects and mosses.

Surprising but true

The coldest air temperature ever measured on the ground, minus 89.2 °C, was recorded at Russia's Vostok Station in Antarctica in 1983.

The coldest, windiest, driest place

Antarctica holds the world record for cold. On 21 July 1983, Russia's Vostok Station measured minus 89.2 °C, colder than the inside of a home freezer by about 70 degrees. Winds rushing down from the high ice can blow faster than a speeding car.

Strangely, Antarctica is also a desert. Very little snow falls each year in the interior, but because it almost never melts, it piles up over hundreds of thousands of years. That has built an ice sheet so heavy it pushes the rock beneath below sea level.

India on the ice

India sent its first expedition to Antarctica in 1981. Its first base, Dakshin Gangotri, was built in 1983, and was later buried by snow and turned into a supply base. Its main stations today are Maitri, opened in 1989, and Bharati, opened in 2012.

Indian scientists study the ice, the ozone layer, the weather and the rocks. Antarctica and India were once joined, as part of the old supercontinent of Gondwana, so some Antarctic rocks match rocks in eastern India.

A continent shared by treaty

In 1959, twelve countries signed the Antarctic Treaty. It says Antarctica may be used only for peaceful purposes and science. Military bases and weapons tests are banned, and scientists are expected to share what they find. A later agreement, the Madrid Protocol of 1991, banned mining too. Today over 50 countries have signed it.

The United States runs the largest station, McMurdo, which can hold over a thousand people in summer. It has a harbour, an airstrip, a fire station and even a coffee shop. The US also runs the Amundsen-Scott station at the South Pole itself, named after the two explorers who raced there in 1911 and 1912, a story you can read in polar explorers.

Life on a station

Daily life can feel like living on a spaceship. People share small rooms, eat together, and take turns washing dishes. Fresh fruit and vegetables run out in winter, so some stations grow salad in greenhouses under lamps.

Going outside means layers of thermal clothing, goggles and checking in by radio. People celebrate midwinter in June with special dinners and greetings sent between stations from different countries.

A library made of ice

The ice is a record of the past. Snow that fell long ago traps tiny bubbles of air, and as more snow piles on top, those bubbles are sealed in. Scientists drill out long cylinders of ice, called cores, and read the air from hundreds of thousands of years ago, as explained in ice ages.

This matters for the future too. If all of Antarctica's ice melted, which would take a very long time, seas would rise by tens of metres.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Antarctica is just a giant sheet of floating ice, like the Arctic.

Truth: Antarctica is a real continent of rock, with mountains and valleys. It is buried under an ice sheet that is over 4 km thick in places. The Arctic, in contrast, is mostly a frozen ocean.

Try this

Fill two cups with the same amount of cold water. Wrap one in a woolly sock or scarf and leave the other bare. Put both in the fridge for an hour and feel which stays less cold. Antarctic clothing uses layers of trapped air in the same way.
