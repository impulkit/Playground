How do astronauts train for space?

To practise spacewalks, NASA astronauts spend hours underwater in a pool 12 m deep that holds a full-size copy of parts of the International Space Station.

The short answer

Astronauts train for years before they fly. They learn how a spacecraft works, how to fix it and what to do if something goes wrong. They practise moving and working without weight, survive in the wild in case they land somewhere remote, and study science for the experiments they will run.

Most astronauts spend about two years in basic training. After they are picked for a particular mission, they train for another year or two with their crew.

Much of it is like school. There are classes, exams and endless practice. The rest is more like an adventure camp, with diving, flying, camping in snow and learning to work as a team when things go wrong.

Surprising but true

In training planes that dive in giant arcs, astronauts float weightless for about 20 to 25 seconds at a time. One flight can repeat this dozens of times.

Floating in a giant pool

Spacewalks are some of the hardest jobs in space. To practise them, NASA built the Neutral Buoyancy Laboratory in Houston, Texas. It is a pool 12 m (40 feet) deep, holding about 23 million litres of water, enough to fill about nine Olympic swimming pools.

Inside sit full-size copies of parts of the space station. Astronauts in real spacesuits are lowered in. Divers add small weights and floats until each astronaut neither sinks nor rises. This trick, called neutral buoyancy, is the closest thing to weightlessness you can keep up for hours.

A practice session can last six hours. Astronauts often spend several hours in the pool for every hour they will spend on a real spacewalk.

The vomit comet

Some training happens in the air. A special plane climbs steeply, then curves over the top of an arc and dives. For about 20 to 25 seconds at a time, everyone inside floats.

The plane repeats the arc again and again on one flight. Many passengers feel sick, which is why the plane has the nickname "vomit comet". Astronauts use these seconds to practise eating, drinking and moving around without weight.

Astronauts also ride a centrifuge, a machine that spins them around fast to pile on the heavy feeling of a rocket launch. And they learn to fly jets, which teaches them to stay calm and make quick choices.

What happens to the body in space?Astronauts come home a few centimetres taller, with weaker bones and puffy faces, and some have to relearn how to walk.

Surviving the landing

Capsules sometimes come down far from where they were meant to. So astronauts practise surviving in forests, deserts, snow and the sea.

They learn to climb out of a bobbing capsule into a life raft, build shelters and keep warm until rescuers arrive. Russian training includes winter camps in the forest. Crews practise these skills together, because getting along matters as much as any skill when you are stuck in a small space for months.

Learning languages

On the International Space Station, crews from many countries live together. NASA astronauts learn Russian, and Russian cosmonauts learn English. Many controls on the Russian Soyuz spacecraft are labelled in Russian, so astronauts riding it must read them quickly.

Crews also study every system of the spacecraft they will live in, from the toilet to the oxygen supply, until they know them by heart. Up there, there is no repair shop to call.

India's astronauts in training

In 2024 India named four Indian Air Force pilots as astronaut-designates for its Gaganyaan programme: Prasanth Nair, Ajit Krishnan, Angad Pratap and Shubhanshu Shukla. In 2020 and 2021 they trained at the Gagarin Cosmonaut Training Centre near Moscow, where they practised survival in snow, water and desert.

Since then they have trained at ISRO's own facility in Bengaluru, and some trained in the USA. In 2025 Shukla put it all to use, joining the short list of Indian astronauts with about 18 days aboard the International Space Station. The rest of the group continue training for India's first crewed Gaganyaan flight.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Astronauts float in space because there is no gravity up there.

Truth: At the height of the space station, gravity is still about 90% as strong as on the ground. Astronauts float because the station and everyone in it are falling around the Earth together, all the time.

Try this

Try putting on a pair of thick gloves, like oven mitts, and then tying your shoelaces or picking up coins. Spacesuit gloves are stiff and puffy, and astronauts train for hours to do fiddly jobs in them.
