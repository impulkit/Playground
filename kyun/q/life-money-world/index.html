What do coins and notes look like around the world?

On the island of Yap in the Pacific, some money is a stone wheel taller than a grown-up, and it never moves even when it changes owners.

The short answer

Money comes in a huge range of shapes, sizes and materials. Some coins have holes in the middle. Some notes are made of plastic you can see through. Swiss banknotes are designed to be read standing up, like a phone screen. All US dollar notes, from $1 to $100, are exactly the same size, while Indian notes get bigger as their value goes up.

Every country decorates its money with things it is proud of: leaders, animals, buildings, inventions and art. Look closely at any note and you'll find tiny details meant to stop forgers, and to help people who can't see well.

Surprising but true

An Indian banknote shows its value in 17 languages: Hindi and English on the front, and 15 more in a panel on the back.

Notes that speak 17 languages

Turn over an Indian banknote and you'll find a panel with its value written in 15 languages, from Assamese to Urdu. Add Hindi and English on the front, and that makes 17. Indian notes also show a famous place on the back: the Red Fort, Hampi's stone chariot, the Rani ki Vav stepwell, and, on the ₹2,000 note withdrawn in 2023, the Mangalyaan spacecraft.

The rupee got its own symbol, ₹, in 2010. It was designed by D. Udaya Kumar, who mixed the Devanagari letter "र" with the Roman "R".

Plastic, cotton and holes

US dollars are printed on a blend of about 75% cotton and 25% linen. That's why a forgotten note can survive the washing machine. In 1988, Australia brought out the first polymer notes, made of thin plastic film with a clear window. They last several times longer than cotton notes.

Coins vary just as much. Japan's 5-yen and 50-yen coins have holes in the middle, and so do some coins from Denmark. In the past, Chinese coins had square holes so people could thread them on a string.

Money you can't carry

On Yap, an island in the western Pacific, people used giant stone discs called rai. Some are more than 3 metres across, taller than a grown-up, with a hole in the middle for a pole. They were carved from rock on islands hundreds of kilometres away and brought back by canoe.

Big rai stones were so heavy that they stayed where they were. When one changed owners, everyone simply remembered who owned it. That's a little like a bank today, keeping a record instead of moving cash.

Huge numbers, little value

In 2009, Zimbabwe printed a note for 100 trillion dollars. That's 100 followed by 12 zeros. Prices had been rising so fast that the money was almost worthless. Reports at the time said one note was not enough for a bus ride in the capital, Harare. This kind of runaway inflation shows that a note is only worth what people trust it to buy.

Money for everyone's hands

Indian coins and notes are designed to help people with low vision. Each note has a different size and raised marks you can feel, and the RBI launched a free phone app called MANI in 2020 that reads a note's value aloud. Coins of different values have different shapes, sizes and edges.

Canada puts raised dots on its notes. US notes, which are all the same size, have been criticised for being hard to tell apart, and the government has promised to add features to help.

A world going cashless

In many places, people now pay with phones and cards more than cash. India's UPI payments handle billions of transactions every month, from shopping malls to street vendors with a QR code taped to their cart. Sweden is one of the most cashless countries anywhere.

Still, notes and coins aren't disappearing yet. They work when phones die and networks fail. They're also tiny works of art. Each new note is designed over years, with hidden threads, colour-changing ink and watermarks, and making one that is hard to copy is a science of its own.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Banknotes are made of paper, like a notebook.

Truth: Most notes are made of cotton, sometimes mixed with linen, which is far tougher than wood-pulp paper. Many countries now use thin plastic film instead.

Try this

Collect any coins you can find at home, including old or foreign ones. Sort them by size, then by weight using a kitchen scale, then by the pictures on them. Close your eyes and try to tell coins apart by touch alone, the way people with low vision do.
