How did wolves become dogs?

Every dog on Earth, from a Chihuahua to a Rajapalayam hound, comes from wolves that lived alongside Ice Age hunters.

The short answer

Dogs come from grey wolves. Sometime before about 15,000 years ago, during the last Ice Age, some wolves began living near groups of human hunters. Over many generations, the wolves that were calmer around people did better. They got more food, had more pups and passed on their friendlier nature.

Slowly, those wolves changed. Their faces became shorter, their teeth smaller and their behaviour softer. They became dogs.

Nobody knows exactly where it happened. Europe, Siberia and East Asia have all been suggested, and it may have happened more than once. What is clear is that dogs were the first animal people tamed, thousands of years before cows, goats or cats, and before the first farms.

Surprising but true

Dogs have a small muscle above each eye, which most wolves lack, that lets them make "puppy-dog eyes" at people.

Who tamed whom?

For a long time, people imagined Stone Age hunters taking wolf pups home and raising them. That may have happened. But many scientists now think the wolves did much of the work themselves.

Hunters left bones and scraps around their camps. Wolves brave enough to come close, but not so aggressive that people drove them off, got an easy meal. Over time, these camp wolves became a separate group. In return, they may have warned people of danger and helped track animals. This is sometimes called self-domestication.

The oldest burial that is clearly a dog was found at Bonn-Oberkassel in Germany. About 14,000 years ago, a young dog was buried there with two people. It had been seriously ill as a puppy, and it seems people nursed it for weeks before it died.

The fox experiment

In 1959, a Russian scientist named Dmitry Belyaev began a famous experiment in Siberia. He bred silver foxes, keeping only the ones that were least afraid of people.

After a few dozen generations, the foxes wagged their tails, licked their keepers and whined for attention. Some had floppy ears, curly tails and white patches, just like many dogs. Choosing for friendliness alone seemed to bring along a whole set of changes. It gave scientists a clue to how wolves might have turned into dogs.

Made to understand us

Dogs are unusually good at reading people. Even young puppies will follow a pointing finger to find hidden food. Dogs also look at our faces for clues, and they have a small muscle above each eye that lifts the inner eyebrow. That creates the sad "puppy-dog eyes" that people find so hard to resist. Most wolves don't have it.

Dogs changed in their insides too. When people started farming, dogs living with them ate more grain scraps. Over time, many dogs gained extra copies of a gene that helps digest starch, something wolves do poorly.

And they kept their superb sense of smell. A dog's nose has hundreds of millions of smell sensors, compared with about five million in yours, as explained in how noses work.

Dogs in history and stories

In 1925, sled dog teams raced medicine across frozen Alaska to the town of Nome, to stop an outbreak of a deadly disease. In 1957, a Moscow street dog named Laika became the first animal to orbit Earth, aboard Sputnik 2. She did not survive the flight.

In India, the Mahabharata ends with a dog. As Yudhishthira climbs towards heaven, only a faithful dog stays with him. He refuses to enter heaven without it, and the dog turns out to be the god Dharma in disguise.

Most modern breeds are young, created in the last 200 years. India has its own old types, like the Rajapalayam and the Mudhol hound, and the hardy Indian street dog, whose roots may go back thousands of years. All of them still share most of their DNA with wolves, like the wolves of Yellowstone.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Dogs see the world only in black and white.

Truth: Dogs do see colour, just fewer colours than most people. They see blues and yellows well, but reds and greens look much alike to them, a bit like a person with red-green colour blindness.

Try this

If you have a friendly pet dog, hide a treat under one of two upturned cups while it can't see. Then let it watch you point clearly at the right cup. Many dogs follow your finger straight away. Wolves, even ones raised by people, usually find this much harder.
