How does your tongue taste things?

Your taste buds are replaced every week or two, and most of what you call "taste" actually happens in your nose.

The short answer

Your tongue is covered in little bumps called papillae. Tucked into many of them are taste buds, tiny clusters of cells shaped a bit like garlic bulbs. An adult has a few thousand.

When you chew, food mixes with saliva. Chemicals from the food dissolve and wash into small openings in the taste buds. There they touch receptor cells, which are tuned to different kinds of chemicals. The receptors send signals along nerves to your brain, which decides whether you're tasting something sweet, sour, salty, bitter or savoury.

Your nose then adds most of the detail, which is how you can tell a mango from a banana.

Surprising but true

Chilli "heat" isn't a taste at all. It switches on the same nerve sensors that warn you about real heat.

Five basic tastes

Sweet tells you about sugars, which mean energy. Salty helps you find the salt your body needs. Sour often warns of unripe fruit or food that's gone off. Bitter can be a warning too, because many poisonous plants taste bitter.

The fifth taste is umami, a rich, savoury flavour. In 1908, the Japanese chemist Kikunae Ikeda worked out what gave a seaweed broth its deep taste. It was a substance called glutamate. He named the taste umami, from the Japanese for "delicious". Tomatoes, cheese and mushrooms are full of it.

The map that wasn't

For most of the 20th century, many school books showed a "tongue map". It put sweet at the tip, bitter at the back, and sour and salty along the sides. It's wrong. In 1901, a German scientist found that some parts of the tongue are very slightly more sensitive to some tastes. Later writers turned this into a strict map.

Every part of the tongue with taste buds can detect all five tastes. Taste buds also grow on the roof of your mouth and in your throat. A catfish goes further: it has taste buds all over its skin, so it can taste the water around its whole body.

Why does chilli feel hot?Chilli does not actually burn you. It tricks the same nerves that warn you about a hot stove.

Mostly it's your nose

Try eating with a blocked nose during a cold, and food seems to lose its flavour. That's because "flavour" is taste and smell working together. As you chew, smells drift up from the back of your mouth into your nose. Your brain blends them with taste so smoothly that it feels as if everything comes from your tongue.

Hot, cold and tingly

Your mouth feels more than tastes. A chilli burns, mint feels cool, and fizzy drinks prickle. These are touch and temperature nerves, not taste buds. The chemical in chillies, capsaicin, fools the heat sensors into firing. Menthol in mint fools the cold sensors.

Six tastes in Ayurveda

Long before modern chemistry, traditional Indian medicine described tastes in its own way. Ayurveda lists six tastes, called rasas: sweet, sour, salty, pungent, bitter and astringent. Astringent is the dry, puckering feeling of raw banana or strong tea. A traditional thali often tries to include all six in one meal. Scientists don't count pungent and astringent as true tastes, but the idea of balancing flavours is very old.

Taste changes as you grow

Children are often more sensitive to bitter flavours than adults, which may be why bitter gourd or some green vegetables taste so strong to them. Many children also prefer sweeter foods. As people get older, they usually come to enjoy more bitter and sour foods. Taste buds wear out and get replaced every week or two, which is why a burnt tongue soon recovers.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Different parts of your tongue taste different things, with sweet at the tip and bitter at the back.

Truth: Every area of the tongue that has taste buds can sense all five tastes. The famous "tongue map" came from a misreading of a German study from 1901 and was copied into textbooks for decades.

Try this

Cut an apple and a raw potato into small cubes of the same size. Close your eyes, pinch your nose shut and ask a friend to give you one cube at a time. Can you tell which is which? Then let go of your nose and try again.
