How do robots help surgeons?

In 2001, surgeons in New York removed a patient's gall bladder in France, more than 6,000 km away, by controlling a robot over a high-speed cable link.

The short answer

Surgical robots are tools that let surgeons operate through a few small holes instead of a large cut. The surgeon sits at a console a few metres from the patient, looks into a 3D viewer and moves hand controls. Robotic arms beside the operating table copy those movements with thin instruments and a tiny camera inside the body.

The robot doesn't decide anything. What it adds is precision. It can shrink a big hand movement into a tiny one, filter out shaking, and bend its "wrists" in ways human hands can't inside a small space. The best-known system, da Vinci, was approved in the United States in 2000 and is now used in many countries, including India.

Surprising but true

A surgical robot can filter out the tiny natural shake in a surgeon's hands, so its instruments stay steadier than any human fingers.

Keyhole surgery, upgraded

Before robots, surgeons were already doing keyhole surgery. They pushed long, thin tools and a camera through small cuts and watched a screen. Patients healed faster, with less pain and smaller scars. But it was awkward. The tools were stiff, the picture was flat, and moving a hand left made the tool's tip go right, like using chopsticks through a letterbox.

A robot fixes much of this. Its instruments have joints near the tip that can turn and twist. The camera gives a magnified 3D view, a bit like looking through powerful lenses. And the controls move the natural way.

Steady hands

Everyone's hands shake very slightly, even a top surgeon's. A robot's computer can filter that out. It can also scale movements, so moving a hand 3 cm moves the instrument just 1 cm. This helps with delicate work like stitching tiny blood vessels or removing a tumour close to a nerve.

Robots are often used for operations on the prostate, the kidneys, the womb and the heart, where the space is small and the work is fiddly. Many big hospitals in Indian cities now have surgical robots, and some Indian companies have started making their own.

An operation across the ocean

On 7 September 2001, a team led by the French surgeon Jacques Marescaux sat in New York and removed the gall bladder of a 68-year-old woman in Strasbourg, France. A robot in Strasbourg copied their movements, sent over a high-speed fibre-optic link. Surgeons stood by in France in case anything went wrong. It was called Operation Lindbergh, after the pilot who first flew alone across the Atlantic.

The hardest part was the delay. If the video arrives late, the surgeon's hands get ahead of what they can see. Fast networks such as 5G and fibre may make long-distance surgery more common, especially for places without specialists.

From Sushruta to silicon

Surgery is old. In ancient India, Sushruta described dozens of surgical instruments and operations, including repairing noses. Over the centuries, doctors added anaesthesia, which keeps patients asleep, and germ-killing methods that made operations far safer.

Robots are the latest step. They are expensive, though. A single system can cost crores of rupees, plus costly instruments for every operation. Doctors still argue about which operations really benefit, because for some, robotic and ordinary keyhole surgery give similar results.

Learning to drive a robot

Surgeons train for many hours on simulators before using a robot on patients, a bit like pilots in a flight simulator. Researchers are now testing robots that can do small tasks by themselves, such as stitching, under a surgeon's eye. For now, the robot is like a very steady, very precise pair of hands that always waits for instructions.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In robotic surgery, a robot operates on you by itself.

Truth: Today's surgical robots are tools, not doctors. A trained surgeon controls every movement, and other doctors and nurses stand beside the patient throughout. Fully self-operating surgery robots are still being researched.

Try this

Hold a pencil at arm's length and try to keep its tip perfectly still against a dot on paper for 30 seconds. Notice the small wobble. Now rest your wrist on the table and try again. Surgical robots remove that wobble electronically, which is one reason surgeons use them.
