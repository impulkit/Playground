What are some Japanese folk legends?

In Japanese folklore, if you meet a river monster called a kappa, the smart move is to bow politely.

The short answer

Japan has thousands of old stories about spirits, shape-shifters and brave children. Many of the creatures are called yokai, a word for mysterious beings that can be scary, silly or helpful. There are river imps, magic foxes, long-nosed mountain spirits and even haunted umbrellas.

The best-known tales include Momotaro, about a boy born from a peach, and Urashima Taro, about a fisherman who visits a palace under the sea. Another is The Tale of the Bamboo Cutter, about a moon princess found in a bamboo stalk. That last one was written down about a thousand years ago and is thought to be the oldest surviving Japanese story told in prose.

Surprising but true

In 2020 a mermaid-like spirit from an 1846 Japanese print, said to keep away sickness, became a national symbol again and even appeared in government health posters.

The polite river monster

A kappa is a small green creature with a beak, a shell like a turtle and a dish of water on top of its head. It lives in rivers and ponds and pulls careless people into the water. The dish is its weakness. Bow to a kappa and, being well brought up, it bows back. The water spills out and it loses its strength.

Kappa love cucumbers more than anything. That's why a cucumber sushi roll in Japan is called kappa maki.

Foxes with many tails

In Japanese folklore, a kitsune is a fox with magic powers, especially shape-shifting. The older and wiser a fox becomes, the more tails it grows, up to nine. Some kitsune are tricksters. Others are messengers of Inari, the spirit of rice and farming.

At Fushimi Inari Shrine in Kyoto, stone foxes guard paths lined with thousands of bright orange gates, stretching up a mountain. If you know the Pokémon Ninetales, you've already met a kitsune.

A peach, a turtle and a princess

Momotaro was found inside a giant peach floating down a river. When he grew up, he set off to fight a gang of ogres on a faraway island, sharing his millet dumplings with a dog, a monkey and a pheasant, who joined him. The city of Okayama claims him and has his statue outside its station.

Urashima Taro saved a turtle and was rewarded with a visit to the Dragon Palace under the sea. He stayed three days. When he returned home, 300 years had passed. He opened a box he'd been told never to open, and turned into an old man.

The moon princess Kaguya-hime grew up on Earth, turned down every suitor with impossible tasks, and finally returned to the Moon. In 2013 Studio Ghibli turned her story into a film.

A street full of monsters

In 1776 an artist named Toriyama Sekien published an illustrated catalogue of yokai, giving many of them the looks they still have today. In the 1960s the manga artist Shigeru Mizuki made them famous again with his comic GeGeGe no Kitaro, about a one-eyed yokai boy. His home town, Sakaiminato, now has a street lined with more than a hundred bronze yokai statues.

Old spirits, new jobs

Tengu are mountain spirits, sometimes shown with a red face and a very long nose, sometimes with a crow's beak and wings. Japanese Buddhism also has Karura, its name for Garuda, the bird king of Indian stories. Some scholars think crow-like tengu borrowed a little from him.

In 2020, during the COVID-19 pandemic, people rediscovered Amabie, a long-haired mermaid creature from an 1846 woodblock print. In the story, she rose from the sea and told people to share her picture to protect against disease. Drawings of Amabie spread online, and Japan's health ministry used her in posters.

Yokai are everywhere in manga and anime. And the strangest part? Kappa and kitsune have cousins all over Asia, from shape-shifting foxes in China and Korea to the many-headed serpents in the story of dragons.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Yokai are all evil demons.

Truth: Yokai can be scary, silly, helpful or simply strange. Some bring good luck, like the house spirit zashiki-warashi, said to bless families. Many are closer to mischief-makers than villains.

Try this

Invent your own yokai. Pick an ordinary object at home, like an old umbrella or a squeaky door, and give it a personality, a power and one weakness. Japanese folklore really has an umbrella spirit, a one-eyed hopping umbrella called kasa-obake. Draw yours and tell its story to someone.
