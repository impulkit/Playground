How does a telescope work?

A telescope's real job isn't making things bigger. It is catching far more light than your eye ever could.

The short answer

A telescope is a light bucket. Its big lens or curved mirror collects light from a distant object over a much larger area than your eye can. It then brings that light together into a sharp image, which you can look at through a small eyepiece lens or record with a camera.

The pupil of your eye is at most about 7 mm wide in the dark. Even a small backyard telescope with a 100 mm lens collects about 200 times more light. That is why a telescope shows thousands of stars where your eyes see a few, and turns smudges in the sky into galaxies.

Surprising but true

India's largest optical telescope, at Devasthal in Uttarakhand, has a mirror 3.6 m wide. It gathers more than 250,000 times as much light as the pupil of your eye.

Lenses: the first telescopes

The first known telescopes were made in the Netherlands around 1608. Spectacle makers found that two lenses held in a line made faraway things look closer. In 1609 Galileo heard about the idea and built his own. He pointed it at the sky and saw mountains on the Moon, moons around Jupiter and countless stars in the Milky Way.

A telescope that uses lenses is called a refractor, because the glass refracts, or bends, the light. The big front lens bends light to meet at a point. A smaller lens near your eye then spreads out that image so it fills your view.

The largest working refractor is at Yerkes Observatory in Wisconsin, USA, with a lens just over 1 m wide, finished in 1897. Lenses bigger than that sag under their own weight, because they can only be held at the edges.

Mirrors: bigger and better

In 1668 Isaac Newton built a different kind of telescope, using a curved mirror instead of a front lens. A mirror shaped like a shallow bowl reflects light to a point, just as a lens bends it. These are called reflectors.

Mirrors have big advantages. Light bounces off the front surface, so the glass behind it doesn't have to be perfectly clear. A mirror can be supported from behind, so it can be made much larger. Nearly every big research telescope today is a reflector.

India's largest is the Devasthal Optical Telescope in the hills of Uttarakhand, with a mirror 3.6 m across, working since 2016. In Chile, astronomers are building the Extremely Large Telescope, whose main mirror, 39 m wide, is made of 798 hexagonal pieces. That is nearly twice the length of a cricket pitch, or about three school buses parked end to end.

Seeing more than light

Not all telescopes look at the light our eyes can see. Radio telescopes use giant metal dishes to collect radio waves from space. Near Pune, India's Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope uses 30 dishes, each 45 m across, spread out over about 25 km. Working together, they act like one enormous telescope.

Other telescopes sense infrared, X-rays or ultraviolet light. Many of those signals are blocked by Earth's air, so the telescopes have to go to space. Hubble and the James Webb Space Telescope are the most famous.

Looking back in time

Light takes time to travel. When a telescope catches light from a galaxy millions of light-years away, that light set off millions of years ago. So the farther a telescope looks, the further back in time it sees.

That makes every big telescope a kind of time machine. The bigger the mirror, the fainter and older the light it can catch.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A telescope's main job is to make stars look bigger.

Truth: Stars are so far away that even the biggest telescopes show most of them as points. What a telescope mainly does is gather light, so faint things become visible.

Try this

Hold a magnifying glass between a bright window and a sheet of white paper on the far side of a room. Move the lens slowly until a sharp, small picture of the window appears on the paper. It will be upside down. A lens telescope starts with exactly this trick. Never point a lens or telescope at the Sun.
