How does India decide what to buy for its armed forces?

A new helicopter for the Army can take ten years or more to go from a wish on paper to a machine on a runway. Here is why.

The short answer

India's armed forces need many things: helicopters, ships, radars, trucks, uniforms, spare parts and much more. They cannot just go shopping. Every big purchase follows a long, written process, starting with a meeting of the Defence Acquisition Council, led by the Defence Minister.

First, the council agrees the forces truly need something. Then come tests, price talks and a contract. Today, the rules say India should first try to buy things designed and made inside the country.

Surprising but true

For many years, India bought more weapons from other countries than any other nation on Earth. Now it is trying to make most of them at home, and even sells some abroad.

How it works

Imagine your family needs a new fridge. First you agree you need one. Then you decide what size, compare brands, check reviews, bargain on price and only then pay. Buying for the armed forces is similar, but with far more steps and far more money.

Step 1: the need. The Army, Navy or Air Force explains what it needs and why. The council gives "Acceptance of Necessity", a first yes.

Step 2: the request. The ministry writes down exactly what the equipment must do and asks companies to offer it.

Step 3: the tests. Shortlisted machines are tried out, sometimes in deserts, mountains and at sea, to see if they really work.

Step 4: the price. A committee bargains to get a fair deal. The cheapest offer that passes every test usually wins.

Step 5: the contract. For the biggest deals, the top ministers in the Cabinet give final approval. Only then is a contract signed, and payments begin as items are delivered.

The rules also rank where things should come from. Top of the list is equipment designed, developed and made in India. Next comes equipment made in India with help from a foreign partner. Buying a finished product from abroad comes last, and needs a strong reason. This order was set up to help Indian factories grow, and to keep more of the money inside the country.

Where it came from

After independence in 1947, India bought most of its major weapons from abroad, first from Britain and later mostly from the Soviet Union. A few Indian factories made guns and bullets, and Hindustan Aircraft, founded in Bengaluru in 1940, built and repaired planes.

In 1958, India set up the Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO) to design its own equipment. In the 1980s, a big argument over payments in a deal for Swedish guns led to much stricter buying rules. India's current rulebook, the Defence Acquisition Procedure, came in 2020.

That same year, the government began publishing lists of items that the forces must buy only from Indian makers. The lists now include hundreds of items, from small parts to big systems.

India's story

India has a long border, a large coastline, a huge economy to protect and an army of more than 10 lakh soldiers. That makes defence one of the biggest items in the government's budget.

For years, India was the largest buyer of weapons from other countries. Recently, it has made more at home. The Tejas fighter jet, designed in India, first flew in 2001. The aircraft carrier INS Vikrant, built in Kochi, joined the Navy in 2022. Indian companies now make drones, radars, artillery guns and helicopters like those flown by the Air Force.

India also exports defence equipment, from bulletproof jackets to missiles, to dozens of countries. In 2024-25, those sales reached about ₹23,622 crore, the highest ever.

The numbers that matter

  • - ₹2.12 lakh crore: the value of new items given first approval in October 2026. That works out to roughly ₹1,500 for every person in India.
  • - About 82%: the share of that value meant to go to companies in India.
  • - About ₹6.81 lakh crore: India's defence budget for 2025-26. That was about ₹13 of every ₹100 the central government planned to spend.
  • - Second: India's place among the world's arms importers in 2020 to 2024, after Ukraine, says SIPRI, a research group that tracks weapons sales.
  • - 10 years or more: how long some big purchases take, from first approval to delivery.

What people argue about

How much to spend is a constant debate. Some say a country with India's borders must stay strong and ready. Others point out that every rupee spent on defence is a rupee not spent on schools, hospitals or roads, and say the balance matters.

There is also the question of build or buy. Buying from abroad, or importing, is often faster and brings the latest technology. Building at home creates jobs, saves foreign money and means India cannot be cut off from spare parts in a crisis. But home-made projects have sometimes run years late, so the forces wait longer.

People also argue about speed. Some say the many steps keep deals honest. Others say the process is so slow that equipment can be old-fashioned by the time it arrives.

Myths and mix-ups

"Defence buying is only about weapons." Much of it is trucks, radars, boats, communication sets, clothing and spare parts. The October 2026 list even included a weather radar.

"Approval means the deal is done." A first approval can still be changed, delayed or dropped if tests fail or prices are too high.

"Made in India means every part is Indian." Many Indian-made machines still use some imported parts, such as engines. The aim is to raise the Indian share step by step.

What it means for you

Defence spending comes from taxes, so it is partly your family's money. Decisions about it shape what else the government can afford.

Buying from Indian factories also creates jobs, not just for soldiers but for engineers, welders, software writers and small workshops making parts in places like Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Pune and Kanpur. Some startups now build drones and sensors for the forces.

And much of this equipment also helps in peacetime. The same helicopters and boats rescue people during floods, cyclones and earthquakes. A weather radar can warn of a storm for villages, not just soldiers. So the next time you read about a big defence deal, ask three questions: what is being bought, who will make it, and when will it actually arrive?

Myth or fact?

Myth: When the news says "₹2 lakh crore approved", all that money is spent straight away.

Truth: That number is the estimated value of everything on the list. The money is paid bit by bit over many years, only after contracts are signed and equipment is delivered.

Try this

Make a "needs list" for your school with a pretend budget of ₹1,000. Write down five things, like books, a football and a fan. Decide which you can make yourselves, which you must buy, and what comes first. You have just done what a defence council does, on a tiny scale.
