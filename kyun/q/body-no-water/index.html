How long can you go without water?

In 1979 an Austrian teenager was forgotten in a police cell for 18 days and survived, reportedly by licking water droplets off the walls.

The short answer

Most people can survive only about three days without any water. In cool weather, resting in the shade, some have survived a week or more. In a hot desert, working hard, it could be less than a day. That is why doctors and rescuers often quote a rough "rule of three": about three minutes without air, three days without water and three weeks without food.

Water is in every cell of your body. You lose it all the time, by breathing out, sweating and going to the toilet. Without new water, your blood thickens, your kidneys struggle and your brain and heart can't work properly.

Surprising but true

About a fifth of the water you take in each day doesn't come from drinks at all. It is hidden in food such as fruit, vegetables, rice and dal.

A body made of water

About half to 60 percent of an adult's body weight is water, and babies are even wetter. Your brain, muscles, kidneys and blood are mostly water. Even bones contain some.

Water carries food and oxygen around your body, cools you through sweat and helps your joints move smoothly. Your kidneys use it to flush out waste. A healthy adult loses around two to three litres a day, and on a hot day in Rajasthan or Arizona, much more.

What happens when you run low

Your body notices quickly. When you lose just 1 or 2 percent of your water, you feel thirst. Your mouth dries, and your urine turns darker as the kidneys save water. Many people get headaches and find it harder to concentrate.

At 5 percent or so, people feel weak, dizzy and confused. Over about 10 percent is a medical emergency. The blood becomes thick and hard to pump, and organs begin to shut down. Doctors treat severe dehydration with fluids given through a drip.

The forgotten prisoner

In April 1979, an 18-year-old named Andreas Mihavecz was placed in a police holding cell in Austria after a car crash in which he was a passenger. Three police officers each thought someone else was looking after him. He was forgotten for 18 days.

He survived, and Guinness World Records lists his case as the longest known survival without food and water. Reports say he licked condensation, the droplets that form on cold surfaces, off the walls, which may have given him just enough water. The case is extreme, and doctors would never expect most people to last that long.

Survivors under rubble

After earthquakes, rescuers sometimes find people alive under fallen buildings many days later. They often survive because the space is cool, they don't move much, and they may find small amounts of water from pipes or rain.

These stories show how much survival depends on conditions. A person sitting still in a cold, damp space loses water slowly. A person walking through a hot desert can lose over a litre an hour in sweat.

Desert champions

Some animals handle dry conditions far better than us. Camels can lose a quarter of their body water and recover, and can drink around 100 litres in about 10 minutes. The kangaroo rat of North American deserts may never drink at all, getting water from its food.

In India, the chinkara gazelle of the Thar Desert can also go long periods without drinking, getting moisture from plants and dew.

Drinking enough

You don't need to count glasses. For most healthy kids, drinking when thirsty, with meals and after exercise, is plenty. Watery foods like watermelon, cucumber and curd help too.

On very hot days, especially during heatwaves, drink more and rest in the shade. And the water in your glass has been around for billions of years, travelling through clouds, rivers and maybe a dinosaur or two, in the endless water cycle.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Everyone must drink exactly eight glasses of water a day.

Truth: There's no strong science behind the exact number. How much water you need depends on your size, the weather and how active you are, and food and other drinks count too. For most healthy people, drinking when thirsty works well.

Try this

For one day, keep a water diary. Write down every drink you have and every watery food, such as fruit, curd, dal or soup. Notice how your body tells you it needs water, such as dry lips or darker urine. Remember to drink when you're thirsty.
