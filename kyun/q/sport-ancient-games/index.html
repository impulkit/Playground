What games did children play long ago?

A board game from Iraq, about 4,500 years old, was still being played by a community in Kochi, Kerala, within living memory.

The short answer

Children thousands of years ago played many games we would recognise. They spun tops, threw pebbles and bones, rolled marbles and played chasing games. They had dolls, whistles, toy animals on wheels and board games with dice.

Archaeologists find these things in old houses, rubbish heaps and tombs. A toy cart from the Indus cities, more than 4,000 years old, would not look strange in a child's hands today. Some games have hardly changed at all. Others vanished for centuries and were only worked out again by scholars reading ancient rule books.

Surprising but true

Children in the Indus cities more than 4,000 years ago played with clay toy carts, whistles shaped like birds and six-sided dice much like ours.

The 4,500-year-old board game

In the 1920s, the archaeologist Leonard Woolley dug up the royal tombs of Ur, a city in Mesopotamia, in modern Iraq. Among the gold and jewels were wooden boards decorated with shell and red and blue stone. Each had 20 squares in an odd shape, like two blocks joined by a narrow bridge.

It was a race game for two players, but the rules were lost. Then Irving Finkel, a curator at the British Museum, translated a clay tablet written by a Babylonian scribe in 177 BCE that explained how to play. Today you can play the Royal Game of Ur again, and museum visitors often do. Finkel also found that Jewish families in Kochi, Kerala, played a version of it into the 20th century.

Games in the tombs of pharaohs

In ancient Egypt, the favourite was senet, a board of 30 squares played about 5,000 years ago. At first it was a simple race game. Later, Egyptians saw it as a journey through the afterlife, and they buried boards with the dead. The young pharaoh Tutankhamun had several in his tomb, including a beautiful one of ebony and ivory.

Egyptian children also had balls made of leather or reeds, wooden toy animals with moving jaws and spinning tops.

Toys from the Indus cities

At Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, archaeologists have found thousands of small clay objects: bulls with nodding heads, toy carts with wheels, bird-shaped whistles, marbles and rattles. They also found cubic dice with dots from one to six, a little different in arrangement from ours.

Some of these may have had religious or adult uses. But many are small and cheerful, the kind of thing made for a child to push, blow or shake.

Games India gave the world

India has a long history of board games. Pachisi, a race game played on a cross-shaped cloth with cowrie shells as dice, was a royal favourite. At Fatehpur Sikri, a courtyard is laid out like a giant pachisi board, and stories say the emperor Akbar played using people as pieces. A simpler version reached Britain and became ludo.

Snakes and ladders began in India as a game about life. Ladders were good deeds that lifted you up, and snakes were bad deeds that dragged you down. Chess grew from an Indian game called chaturanga, about 1,500 years ago.

Games with sticks and stones

Many of the oldest games needed nothing you had to buy. Five stones, or knucklebones, was played in ancient Greece with sheep's ankle bones, and Indian children still play it with pebbles, under names such as gutte. Hopscotch, tug of war and kite flying are all centuries old, and the yo-yo appears on Greek vases painted about 2,500 years ago.

In Indian villages, gilli danda needs just two pieces of wood: a long stick and a short one tapered at both ends. Strike the short one so it flips up, then whack it as far as you can. Some historians think it may share an ancestor with cricket and baseball, though nobody can prove it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Children long ago had no toys or games and spent all their time working.

Truth: Many children did work, but archaeologists have found toys and game boards in almost every ancient civilisation, from clay carts in the Indus cities to dolls and balls in Egypt.

Try this

Collect five small, smooth pebbles and play five stones. Throw one pebble up, pick up another from the floor with the same hand, and catch the falling one. Then try picking up two at a time, then three. It is the same game children played in ancient Greece with sheep's ankle bones.
