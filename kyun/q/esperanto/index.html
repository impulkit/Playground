Can someone invent a language?

In 1887, an eye doctor in Warsaw published a small booklet containing a brand-new language. Today some children grow up speaking it.

The short answer

Yes. Most of the world's 7,000 or so languages grew slowly and naturally over thousands of years. But people have invented hundreds of languages on purpose, and a few have real speakers.

The most successful is Esperanto. It was created by Ludwik Lejzer Zamenhof, an eye doctor, and published in 1887. He wanted an easy, neutral second language that anyone could learn, so people from different countries could talk as equals. He signed his booklet "Doktoro Esperanto", meaning "Doctor Hopeful", and the language took that name.

Other invented languages were made for stories, such as Elvish in Tolkien's books and Klingon in Star Trek. Klingon was built by a language expert, Marc Okrand, with deliberately strange sounds and grammar so it would feel alien. Fans have since translated Hamlet into it. And in at least one famous case, a group of children invented a new language without even trying.

Surprising but true

Some children learn Esperanto from birth, because their parents met through it and had no other shared language. There may be around a thousand of these native speakers.

A boy who heard four languages

Zamenhof grew up in Białystok, a town that was then in the Russian Empire and is now in Poland. On its streets he heard Russian, Polish, German and Yiddish. The different groups often distrusted each other, and he came to believe that not understanding one another's languages made things worse.

As a teenager he began building a language. He chose words mostly from European languages, especially Latin, French and German, so that many people would recognise them. Then he made the grammar as simple as possible, with no exceptions.

Rules with no exceptions

In English, the past tense of "go" is "went", and of "see" is "saw". Learners have to memorise hundreds of oddities like these. Esperanto has none.

Every noun ends in -o: hundo is dog. Every adjective ends in -a: granda is big. Verbs end in -as for now, -is for the past and -os for the future: mi kantas is "I sing", mi kantis "I sang", mi kantos "I will sing". Every letter always makes the same sound, so you can read any word aloud correctly once you know the alphabet. "Hello" is saluton.

Supporters say this makes Esperanto much quicker to learn than most languages. Critics point out that it is easier mainly for speakers of European languages, since its words come from them.

Who speaks it now?

Esperanto never became the world's second language. Still, it has a green star flag, a world congress held almost every year since 1905, thousands of books and songs, and speakers on every continent. UNESCO passed resolutions supporting it in 1954 and 1985. Since 2015, millions of people have tried free online courses in it.

Estimates of how many people speak it well vary hugely, from tens of thousands to about two million. A few families use it at home, so it even has native speakers.

Languages invented by children

The most surprising invented language had no single inventor. In Nicaragua in the late 1970s and 1980s, new schools brought together hundreds of deaf children who had never been taught a sign language. They began signing to each other on buses and in playgrounds.

Over a few years, their gestures turned into a full language with its own grammar, now called Nicaraguan Sign Language. Younger children who joined made it richer and more regular. Scientists studied it closely, because it showed something rarely seen: a brand-new human language being born.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Esperanto failed, and nobody speaks it any more.

Truth: It never became everyone's second language, as its creator hoped. But it is still used for meetings, books, music and online chats around the world, and there are children who speak it from birth.

Try this

Invent a mini-language with a friend. Make up ten words and two rules, such as "every action word ends in -ka" and "add -zo for more than one". Then try to pass secret notes and see if the rules make them easy to read.
