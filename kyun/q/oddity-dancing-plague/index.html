What was the dancing plague?

In the summer of 1518, a woman in Strasbourg began dancing in the street, and within weeks dozens of people could not stop dancing either.

The short answer

The dancing plague was a strange outbreak in the city of Strasbourg in 1518. It began in July, when a woman known in later accounts as Frau Troffea started dancing in the street and kept going for days. Others soon joined her. Within weeks dozens of people were dancing, some for days on end, often looking distressed rather than happy.

City records and chronicles describe it, so it really happened. What caused it is less clear. Most historians today think it was a mass psychogenic illness, a kind of panic that spreads through a frightened community, rather than a germ or a poison.

Surprising but true

To cure the dancers, the city leaders first hired musicians and built a stage, thinking more dancing would wear the sickness out. It seems to have made things worse.

A city under strain

Strasbourg in 1518 was a hard place to live. Several bad harvests had left people hungry. Diseases like smallpox and a new illness, syphilis, were spreading. Many families were poor and in debt.

People also believed that saints could punish sinners with disease. One saint, St Vitus, was said to be able to curse people with a compulsion to dance. In such a place, fear and exhaustion could spread from person to person very quickly.

The cure that backfired

Doctors of the time blamed "hot blood". The city council decided the dancers must dance it out. They cleared a hall and a market space, built a wooden stage, and paid musicians and strong dancers to keep the sufferers moving.

It didn't work. More people joined. Some reports say up to 400 people danced at some point that summer, although historians treat that number with caution. The council then changed its mind. It banned public music and dancing and sent the dancers to a shrine of St Vitus in the hills near Saverne. By September, the outbreak had faded. At the shrine, the dancers were given small crosses and red shoes, and led around the altar. Whether the ritual helped or the panic simply burned itself out, nobody can say.

Not the only time

Strasbourg's outbreak was not the first. In 1374, groups of dancers moved through towns along the River Rhine, including Aachen and Cologne, in a similar frenzy. Smaller outbreaks were reported in other parts of Europe for about 300 years, often in times of famine or disease, like the years after the Black Death.

The later medical term "St Vitus' dance" came from this history. Today doctors use other names for the different conditions it once described.

What caused it?

One old idea blamed ergot, a fungus that grows on damp rye. Eating bread made with infected rye can cause hallucinations and twitching. But ergot also cuts off blood flow to the arms and legs, making it hard to dance for hours, let alone days. Most historians now doubt it. Some fungi do affect the mind, as the story of mushrooms shows, but ergot doesn't fit the facts well.

The historian John Waller argues it was a mass psychogenic illness. People who were starving, frightened and convinced they had been cursed fell into a trance-like state. Seeing others dance made more people believe they were cursed too. Doctors know that stress and fear can produce real physical symptoms.

Catching behaviours today

Similar outbreaks still happen. In 1962, uncontrollable laughter spread from a girls' boarding school in Tanganyika, now Tanzania, to nearby villages. Several schools had to close for a time. In 2001, rumours of a "monkey man" attacking people at night caused panic across parts of Delhi, though no such creature was ever found.

These cases show how powerful the mind can be, and how feelings move through a crowd. On a gentler scale, you see the same thing every time one person yawns and the whole room follows, or when laughter spreads around a classroom.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Fifteen people a day were dancing to death in Strasbourg in 1518.

Truth: That figure appears in some modern retellings, but historians have not found it in records from the time. Some dancers may have died of exhaustion, but the true number of deaths, if any, is unknown.
