Why does iron rust?

In Delhi stands an iron pillar about 1,600 years old that has barely rusted. Scientists spent decades working out why.

The short answer

Rust forms when iron reacts with oxygen and water. The iron atoms give up some of their electrons to oxygen, a kind of reaction called oxidation. The result is a new substance, iron oxide, mixed with water. That's the flaky, reddish-brown crust we call rust.

Iron needs both oxygen and water to rust. A nail kept completely dry, or completely sealed under oil, barely changes. Leave it out in damp air, and rust appears within days.

Unlike many other metals, iron has a problem. Its rust is crumbly and flakes away, which exposes fresh iron underneath to more air and water. So once rusting starts, it can keep eating deeper and deeper until a strong iron bar crumbles.

Surprising but true

Mars is red because of rust. Its dusty surface is full of iron oxide, the same compound that forms on an old bicycle left out in the rain.

Salt makes it worse

Rust is really a tiny electrical process. Charges have to move through the water on the iron's surface. Pure water doesn't carry charge very well, but salty water does. That's why iron rusts much faster near the sea.

In coastal cities like Mumbai, Chennai and Miami, railings, cars and old buildings rust quickly in the salty, damp air. In places with cold winters, such as Chicago or Toronto, salt spread on icy roads speeds up rust on cars. Ships face the hardest test of all, sitting in salt water for years. That's why their steel hulls need thick paint and regular care to stay afloat, as you can read in floating ships.

How to stop rust

The simplest way is to keep water and air away from the iron. Paint, oil and grease all form a barrier. The Eiffel Tower in Paris is repainted roughly every seven years to protect its iron.

Another way is galvanising, coating steel with a thin layer of zinc. Zinc reacts with air first and protects the iron below, even if the coating gets scratched. Many buckets, roofing sheets and lamp posts are galvanised. You can often spot the zinc as a dull grey surface with a faint pattern, like frost on a window.

Stainless steel contains chromium, at least about 10% of the mix. The chromium forms a thin, invisible layer on the surface that seals the steel. That's why steel tiffins, kitchen sinks and spoons stay shiny for years.

The pillar that won't rust

In the Qutub complex in Delhi stands an iron pillar more than 7 metres tall. It carries an inscription in Sanskrit, and it is usually dated to the reign of Chandragupta II in the Gupta period, around 400 CE. Despite standing in the open for about 1,600 years, it shows very little rust.

Scientists, including R. Balasubramaniam of IIT Kanpur, studied it closely. They found the iron contains an unusually high amount of phosphorus and very little sulphur. Over time, this helped a thin, tightly sticking protective layer form on the surface. Delhi's fairly dry climate helps too.

Rust beyond Earth

The red colour of Mars comes from iron oxide in its rocks and dust. Billions of years ago, scientists think, the planet had liquid water, which may have helped rust its iron-rich surface.

Not every metal "rusts" the way iron does. Copper turns green over time, which is why the Statue of Liberty in New York, made of thin copper sheets, is pale green instead of the shiny brown it was in 1886. That green layer, called a patina, protects the copper underneath, unlike iron's flaky rust.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You get tetanus from the rust on a rusty nail.

Truth: Tetanus is caused by bacteria that live in soil and dust, not by rust itself. Rusty objects lying outdoors are often dirty, which is why a deep cut from one is a reason to tell an adult and ask a doctor about a tetanus vaccine.

Try this

Put a plain steel paper clip (not coated in plastic) into each of three small glasses. Leave one dry, cover one with tap water, and cover one with water mixed with a spoon of salt. Check them every day for a week and draw what you see. Then ask an adult to help you throw the water away and wash your hands.
