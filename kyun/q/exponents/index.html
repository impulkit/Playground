What are powers and exponents?

Fold a sheet of paper in half 42 times and, in theory, the stack would reach past the Moon.

The short answer

An exponent, or power, is a short way to write repeated multiplication. Instead of writing 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2, you write 2⁵. The big number, 2, is called the base. The small raised number, 5, is the exponent. It tells you how many 2s to multiply together. 2⁵ is 32.

Powers grow very fast, which is why they are useful for describing huge and tiny numbers. Scientists use them to write the distance to stars, the size of atoms and the number of cells in your body without filling a page with zeros.

Surprising but true

In the old legend of the chessboard and the rice, the grains on the last square alone would be 2 to the power 63, about 9 quintillion grains, far more rice than the world grows in a year.

Squares and cubes

You already know two powers. 5² is read "five squared", and it equals 25, the area of a square with sides of 5. 5³ is "five cubed", and equals 125, the number of small cubes in a big cube 5 on each side. That is where the names come from.

There are a couple of odd rules. Any number to the power 1 is just itself. And any number, except 0, to the power 0 is 1. That sounds strange, but it follows a pattern: 2³ is 8, 2² is 4, 2¹ is 2, and each step halves the answer. Halve once more and you get 2⁰ = 1.

The chessboard and the rice

An old story from India tells of the clever inventor of chess. The king was so pleased that he offered any reward. The inventor asked for rice: one grain on the first square of the chessboard, two on the second, four on the third, doubling each time, for all 64 squares.

The king laughed at such a small request. But the numbers grow as powers of 2. The 11th square has 1,024 grains. The 21st has over a million. The 64th square alone would need 2⁶³ grains, about 9 quintillion. All 64 squares together come to more rice than the whole world would harvest in hundreds of years. The story is a legend, but the maths is real.

How does rice feed half the world?More than half the people on Earth eat rice almost every day, and most of it grows in fields flooded like shallow lakes.

Folding to the Moon

Paper folding shows the same explosion. A sheet of paper is about 0.1 mm thick. Fold it once and it is 2 layers thick. Fold it 10 times and it would be 2¹⁰, or 1,024, layers: about 10 cm. Fold it 42 times and it would be 2⁴² layers, more than 400,000 km, past the Moon, which is about 384,000 km away.

In real life you can't fold paper that many times, because each fold needs far more paper than the last. In 2002, an American student named Britney Gallivan set a record of 12 folds, using a very long strip of paper. In 2011, students at a school in Massachusetts managed 13 folds with a strip of toilet paper about 16 km long.

Powers of ten

Powers of 10 are especially handy. 10² is 100, 10³ is 1,000, and 10⁶ is a million. The exponent tells you how many zeros follow the 1. In India, 1 lakh is 10⁵ and 1 crore is 10⁷.

Scientists write big numbers as a small number times a power of 10. The Sun is about 1.5 × 10⁸ km away, or 150 million km. A light-year is about 9.5 × 10¹² km. For tiny things, they use negative powers: a typical bacterium is about 10⁻⁶ m, a millionth of a metre, across.

Exponents also describe things that double, like bacteria splitting in a warm lunchbox or money growing with compound interest. Something that doubles every 20 minutes can go from one cell to over a million in less than seven hours, because 2²⁰ is more than a million.

Myth or fact?

Myth: 2³ means 2 × 3, which is 6.

Truth: 2³ means 2 multiplied by itself three times, 2 × 2 × 2, which is 8. The exponent counts how many times the number appears in the multiplication.

Try this

Take a sheet of newspaper and fold it in half as many times as you can. Count the layers after each fold: 2, 4, 8, 16. Most people can't get past 7 or 8 folds, because by then the stack is over a hundred layers thick. Write the number of layers as a power of 2.
