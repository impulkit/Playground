What was the Iron Age?

An iron pillar in Delhi has stood out in sun and monsoon rain for about 1,600 years, and it still hasn't rusted away.

The short answer

The Iron Age is the period when iron became the main metal for tools and weapons, taking over from bronze. It began at different times in different places. In West Asia and around the Mediterranean, iron spread widely after about 1200 BCE. In India, iron was in use at least 3,000 years ago, and maybe a lot earlier.

Iron changed the world because its ore is common. Bronze needed tin, which was rare and had to be carried long distances. With iron, almost any community with ore, charcoal and skill could make its own ploughs, axes and swords. Farming spread into new lands, forests were cleared and kingdoms grew.

Surprising but true

Tutankhamun was buried with a dagger made of iron that came from a meteorite, long before Egyptians could smelt iron from rock.

A metal that is hard to tame

Iron melts at about 1,538 °C, much hotter than the copper used to make bronze. Early furnaces couldn't reach that. So smiths learned a different trick. They heated iron ore with charcoal in a clay furnace until it formed a glowing, spongy lump called a bloom. Then they hammered it again and again to squeeze out the waste.

Before people learned this, the only iron they had came from meteorites that fell from space. In 2016, scientists showed that a dagger buried with the Egyptian king Tutankhamun, about 3,300 years ago, was made of meteorite iron.

When iron came to India

Archaeologists have found iron objects at many old sites in India. At places such as Malhar and Raja Nala ka Tila in Uttar Pradesh, iron has been dated to around 1800–1500 BCE. In southern India, people buried their dead in stone-ringed graves called megaliths, often with iron knives, swords and tools beside them.

In January 2025, Tamil Nadu's archaeology department released a report on finds at Sivagalai and other sites, with dates from before 3000 BCE. If confirmed, that would make Indian iron-working much older than once thought. Some archaeologists welcomed the results, while others asked for more samples and testing before rewriting the textbooks.

Why does iron rust?In Delhi stands an iron pillar about 1,600 years old that has barely rusted. Scientists spent decades working out why.

The pillar that won't rust

Near the Qutub Minar in Delhi stands an iron pillar about 7 metres tall and weighing around 6 tonnes. An inscription on it is linked to a king named Chandra, thought by most historians to be Chandragupta II of the Gupta dynasty, about 1,600 years ago.

Iron normally rusts when it meets air and water. Yet the pillar has stayed solid. Metallurgists at IIT Kanpur found that its iron contains a lot of phosphorus. Over time this helped a thin, tight protective layer form on the surface, which slows further rusting. The smiths who made it had no idea of this chemistry, but their methods gave just the right result.

Steel from South India

Indian smiths also became famous for wootz, a very fine steel. Steel is iron with a small, carefully controlled amount of carbon, which makes it much harder and tougher. Wootz was made by heating iron and plant material in sealed clay pots, then cooling it slowly.

Cakes of wootz from South India and Sri Lanka travelled to the Middle East, where smiths forged them into "Damascus" swords with wavy patterns on the blade. These swords were famous for being strong yet flexible. Ancient Tamil poems mention iron weapons too.

Iron around the world

The Hittites of Anatolia, in today's Turkey, were among the early masters of iron. In West Africa, the Nok people of Nigeria smelted iron more than 2,000 years ago. In China, smiths learned to build furnaces hot enough to melt iron and pour it into moulds, making cast iron, by about 500 BCE.

Historians often say the Iron Age ended when written history began in each region. In a way, though, we never left it. Iron, mostly as steel, is still the metal that holds up our bridges, buildings and trains.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Everyone in the world entered the Iron Age at the same time.

Truth: Iron-working began at different times in different places, and spread slowly. Parts of West Asia used iron by about 1200 BCE, Britain from about 800 BCE, and some places, such as Australia, didn't have it until Europeans arrived.

Try this

Put an iron nail in each of three small glasses. Cover one with water, one with salty water and leave one dry. Check them every day for a week. Which rusts first? This is why the Delhi pillar not rusting is such a puzzle.
