What was apartheid?

For over 40 years, South Africa's laws decided where people could live, which school they could attend and whom they could marry, all by the colour of their skin.

The short answer

Apartheid was a system of laws in South Africa that separated people by race and kept power in the hands of the white minority. It began in 1948 and lasted until the early 1990s.

Under apartheid, every person was classified by law as White, Black, Coloured (mixed race) or Indian. Black South Africans, the large majority, were forced into poorer areas, given worse schools, and could not vote for the national government. After years of resistance and pressure from around the world, apartheid was taken apart, and in 1994 Nelson Mandela became the country's first Black president.

Surprising but true

India cut off trade with South Africa in 1946, two years before apartheid even became official, in protest at how Indians there were treated.

Laws for every part of life

Racial unfairness in South Africa went back to colonial times. Gandhi had fought against it there as a young lawyer. But in 1948 the National Party won the election, with only white people voting, and built a much stricter system.

New laws came one after another. The Population Registration Act put every person into a racial group. The Group Areas Act decided which race could live in each part of a town, and whole neighbourhoods were bulldozed. Black adults had to carry a passbook and show it to police on demand. Beaches, buses, parks and even park benches were marked "Whites only".

Homelands and schools

The government set up so-called homelands for Black South Africans, often on poor land far from jobs. Millions of people were forced to move. Many men had to live in crowded hostels near mines and cities, seeing their families only once a year.

Schools for Black children got far less money. A 1953 law shaped their lessons to train them mainly for low-paid work. In 1976, students in Soweto, near Johannesburg, marched against a rule forcing them to learn in Afrikaans. Police opened fire, and many young people were killed. South Africa now marks 16 June as Youth Day.

Fighting back

Resistance never stopped. The African National Congress (ANC) and other groups organised strikes, boycotts and marches. In 1960, at Sharpeville, police shot dead 69 people protesting against the pass laws. The government banned the ANC, and some members turned to armed struggle.

Mandela was arrested in 1962, and in 1964 he was sentenced to life in prison. He spent 27 years behind bars, 18 of them on Robben Island. Others, such as Archbishop Desmond Tutu, spoke out loudly and won support abroad.

The world says no

Many countries refused to trade with South Africa or sell it weapons. South Africa was shut out of the Olympics and most international sport, including cricket from 1970. Students in the USA and Britain pushed their universities and banks to stop investing there.

India had been one of the earliest and firmest opponents. It cut trade ties in 1946 and spoke against apartheid at the United Nations for decades.

A new South Africa

By the late 1980s, the economy was suffering and the protests were growing. In 1990 President F. W. de Klerk lifted the ban on the ANC and freed Mandela. After four tense years of talks, South Africans of all races queued for hours to vote on 27 April 1994, now celebrated as Freedom Day.

A Truth and Reconciliation Commission later let victims tell their stories in public. South Africa's new constitution, adopted in 1996, is often praised as one of the most protective of equal rights in the world, though deep gaps in wealth from the apartheid years remain.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Apartheid ended because the South African government decided on its own that it was wrong.

Truth: It ended after decades of protest, strikes, boycotts and armed struggle inside South Africa, together with sanctions and sports bans from around the world. Talks between the government and Mandela's party then set up a new, democratic system.
