What is Kalamkari?

Indian painted cottons were once so popular in Europe that France banned them to protect its own weavers.

The short answer

Kalamkari is a way of decorating cotton cloth with natural dyes. The name comes from Persian words: kalam means pen and kari means work. It is made mainly in Andhra Pradesh, in two towns with two very different styles.

In Srikalahasti, near Tirupati, artists draw every line by hand with a bamboo pen. Their cloths show gods, goddesses and scenes from the Mahabharata and Ramayana, often with writing in Telugu. In Machilipatnam and nearby Pedana, on the coast, designs are stamped with carved wooden blocks. These show flowers, vines and birds, with a strong Persian flavour.

Surprising but true

The English word "chintz" comes from the Hindi word chheent, meaning spotted or sprinkled, after the flowery Indian cottons that Europeans went wild for.

A pen made from bamboo

A Kalamkari pen is a sharpened bamboo stick with a pad of wool or cloth wrapped near the tip. The pad soaks up dye and feeds it slowly to the point, like the ink in a fountain pen.

For black outlines, artists use a liquid made by leaving rusty iron scraps to soak with jaggery and water for days. It smells strong, but it makes a deep, lasting black. Many fine lines in a Srikalahasti cloth are drawn freehand, with no tracing.

In Machilipatnam the work is different. Craftsmen carve designs into blocks of wood, often teak, and stamp them onto the cloth one after another. A single design may need several blocks, one for the outline and one for each colour. Lining them up perfectly takes a steady hand and a good eye. The fine details are often still added by hand with a kalam.

Dyes that need a helper

Plant dyes tend to wash out of cotton unless something holds them in place. That helper is called a mordant. In Kalamkari, the cloth is first soaked in a mixture made from myrobalan fruit and milk. Then alum is painted wherever red is wanted. When the cloth is boiled with a red-dye root, the colour sticks only where the alum is.

Blue comes from indigo, yellow from flowers and fruit rinds, and green from yellow laid over blue. Between each stage, the cloth is washed in flowing water and dried in the sun. A single piece can go through more than a dozen steps and take weeks.

The cloth that conquered Europe

For centuries, painted and printed cottons from India's east coast were traded across Asia, Africa and Europe. Machilipatnam was a busy port, and its block-printed designs were shaped by buyers from Persia and the court of Golconda, near today's Hyderabad.

When European traders, including the East India Company, brought these bright, washable cloths home in the 1600s, people loved them for clothes, curtains and bedcovers. Europe had nothing like them. Local silk and wool weavers feared for their jobs. France banned imported Indian printed cottons in 1686, and Britain passed its own laws against them in 1700 and 1721.

Kalamkari today

Indian dyers kept their secrets for a long time, and European factories eventually learned to copy the look with machines. Kalamkari almost faded away, but it was revived in the 20th century with help from craft organisations and designers.

Today you might see it on sarees, kurtas, wall hangings and cushion covers. Some makers now use chemical dyes to save time, while others stick to the slow natural process. Both the Srikalahasti and Machilipatnam styles have a GI tag, a government label that protects a product linked to one place, like Darjeeling tea.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Kalamkari is a kind of painting with ordinary paints on cloth.

Truth: Kalamkari uses dyes, not paints. The colour soaks into and bonds with the cotton fibres, helped by substances called mordants. That is why good Kalamkari can be washed for years.

Try this

Ask an adult for an old cotton handkerchief and a used tea bag. Dab a pattern on the cloth with the wet tea bag, let it dry, then rinse it under the tap. Some colour stays and some washes away, which is the puzzle every Kalamkari dyer has to solve.
