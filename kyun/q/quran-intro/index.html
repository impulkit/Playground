What is the Quran?

Millions of people around the world, many of them children, have learned the whole Quran by heart, all 114 chapters of it, in Arabic.

The short answer

The Quran is the holy book of Islam. Muslims believe it is the word of God (Allah in Arabic), revealed to the Prophet Muhammad through the angel Jibril, known in English as Gabriel, over about 23 years. It is written in Arabic and has 114 chapters, called surahs.

For about 2 billion Muslims around the world, the Quran is a guide for faith and daily life. It speaks about God and about the prophets who came before Muhammad, such as Ibrahim (Abraham), Musa (Moses) and Isa (Jesus). It also teaches about honesty, prayer, charity, justice and kindness to parents, orphans and strangers.

Surprising but true

Pages of a Quran kept at the University of Birmingham in England were carbon-dated in 2015 to between about 568 and 645 CE, making them among the oldest Quran pages known.

How the Quran came to be

According to Muslim belief, the first revelation came in about 610 CE, when Muhammad was meditating in a cave called Hira on a mountain near Mecca. The angel told him "Iqra", meaning "Read!" or "Recite". Revelations continued for the rest of his life, in Mecca and later in Medina.

His companions memorised the verses and wrote them on whatever they had: parchment, palm leaves, flat bones. After his death in 632 CE, the first caliph, Abu Bakr, had them gathered together. Around 650 CE, the third caliph, Uthman, had standard copies made and sent to major cities. The text Muslims read today follows that version.

India has one of the largest Muslim populations in the world, and the Quran has been translated into many Indian languages, including Urdu, Malayalam, Tamil, Bengali, Gujarati and Hindi. Old handwritten copies, some decorated with gold, are kept in libraries such as the Khuda Bakhsh Oriental Library in Patna and the Raza Library in Rampur.

What's inside

Each surah has a name, often taken from a word inside it, like Al-Baqarah (The Cow) or Maryam (Mary). After the short opening chapter, the surahs are arranged roughly from longest to shortest, not in the order they were revealed. The longest has 286 verses. The shortest has just three.

The opening chapter, Al-Fatiha, has seven verses. Muslims recite it in every unit of their daily prayers, which means many people say it at least 17 times a day. Almost every surah begins with the words Bismillah ir-Rahman ir-Rahim, "In the name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful".

A book to be heard

The word "Quran" means "the recitation". It was first shared aloud, and it is still meant to be heard. Reciters train for years to chant it with exact pronunciation and rhythm, following rules called tajwid.

A person who has memorised the whole book is called a hafiz. Many children in India, Pakistan, Indonesia, Egypt and elsewhere begin learning as young as six or seven, often finishing in a few years. During the holy month of Ramadan, many Muslims try to read or hear the entire Quran, and mosques hold special night prayers where it is recited in parts.

Beautiful writing

Because the Quran is so honoured, writing it out became an art. Islamic religious art usually avoids pictures of people, so calligraphers made the words themselves beautiful, in flowing or angular styles of Arabic script. Verses decorate mosques from Spain to India, including the gateways of the Taj Mahal, where the letters grow larger near the top so that they look the same size from the ground.

Muslims treat printed copies with care. Many wash before touching one, keep it on a high shelf and never place it on the floor. Translations exist in more than 100 languages. Most Muslims see them as explanations of the meaning, while the Arabic text is considered the Quran itself. Its teachings shape festivals like Eid and the pilgrimage to Mecca, which millions of Muslims make every year.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Quran is only for people who speak Arabic.

Truth: Most Muslims do not speak Arabic as their first language. Many learn to read the Arabic text for prayer and recitation, and also read translations in their own language, such as Urdu, Malayalam, Bengali, English or Indonesian.

Try this

Look at a page of Arabic writing, in a book or online, and notice that it runs from right to left and the letters join up like joined-up handwriting. Try copying one short word slowly with a pencil. Then find out how a friend or neighbour who reads Arabic, Urdu or Persian learned to read the script.
