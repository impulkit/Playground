What do Bhimbetka's cave paintings show?

On the rock walls of Bhimbetka in central India, people painted rhinos, bison, dancers and hunters, and some of these pictures are thought to be more than 10,000 years old.

The short answer

The rock shelters of Bhimbetka, in Madhya Pradesh, hold some of the oldest known paintings in India. They show animals like bison, deer, tigers, elephants, rhinos and boars. They show people hunting with bows and spears, dancing in lines, riding horses and elephants, gathering honey and playing drums.

The paintings were made over a very long time. The oldest are thought to date from the late Stone Age, perhaps more than 10,000 years ago. Others are much younger, from the time of early kings, with horse riders and soldiers. Together they form a picture book of human life in central India stretching across thousands of years.

Surprising but true

Bhimbetka was noticed by an archaeologist looking out of a train window in 1957, when he saw rock formations that reminded him of painted caves he had seen in Spain and France.

Found from a train window

Bhimbetka lies in the hills of the Vindhya range, about 45 km south of Bhopal. Local people knew the rocks well. But in 1957, the archaeologist Vishnu Shridhar Wakankar was travelling by train when he spotted strange rock formations on the hills. They reminded him of painted caves in Spain and France.

He went back to explore and found shelter after shelter filled with paintings. Over the following years, he and other researchers recorded about 700 rock shelters in the area. In 2003, UNESCO made Bhimbetka a World Heritage Site. The name is often linked to Bhima, a hero of the Mahabharata, and means something like "Bhima's sitting place".

Animals and hunters

Some of the oldest paintings show big animals in thick lines, such as rhinos and bison, drawn larger than life. One famous rock, nicknamed the Zoo Rock, is covered with elephants, deer, bison and other animals, painted by different people over time.

The rhino paintings are a surprise. Today, India's one-horned rhinos live mainly in Assam, far to the northeast, as in Kaziranga. The paintings suggest rhinos once lived in central India too.

Hunting scenes show people with bows and arrows, spears and traps. Some animals are drawn with patterns inside their bodies, like an X-ray, showing lines or dots.

Who made the very first paintings?Long before paper, pencils or paintbrushes, people were painting on rock. Some of their pictures are more than 50,000 years old.

Dancers and daily life

Bhimbetka's people didn't only paint hunts. Lines of figures hold hands and dance. Others play drums. Some paintings show people collecting honey from bee hives, which is still done in Indian forests today. Mothers carry children, and groups gather around what might be a fire.

In younger paintings, people ride horses and elephants and carry swords and shields. Those show a later age, when kingdoms and armies had arrived. The walls show a change from hunting and gathering to farming and warfare.

Red and white paint

The painters used mainly red and white, with some yellow and green. Red came from ochre and haematite, iron-rich rocks ground into powder. White came from limestone or chalky clay. They probably mixed these with water, plant gum or fat.

Brushes might have been made from twigs or plant fibres, chewed at the end. Some paintings were done with fingers. Because the shelters are protected from rain, many paintings have survived for thousands of years, though some have faded.

Why they matter

Bhimbetka also has signs of humans living there long before any paintings. Stone tools found here suggest people and their ancestors used these shelters for a very long time, perhaps hundreds of thousands of years. Some researchers believe small cup-shaped marks carved into rock here are among the oldest human-made marks in the world.

Like the painted caves of Lascaux in France, Bhimbetka shows that people have always wanted to make pictures. Thousands of years after Bhimbetka's first painters, artists in western India covered the Buddhist caves of Ajanta with far richer colours. How they did it is a story of its own.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Cave paintings were just doodles by bored people.

Truth: Many paintings were carefully made, sometimes high up on walls that needed climbing, and repeated over generations. Experts think they recorded hunts, rituals, dances and beliefs, though we can't be sure of their exact meaning.

Try this

Make earth paint. Rub a piece of brick or red soil on rough paper or a stone to get a red powder. Mix a little with water and a drop of glue, then paint an animal on a piece of cardboard using your finger or a twig. Leave it in a shady spot and check how well it lasts over a week.
