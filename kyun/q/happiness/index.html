What makes people happy?

A study that has followed the same families since 1938 found that the best clue to a happy, healthy old age wasn't money or fame. It was good relationships.

The short answer

Researchers who study happiness keep finding the same big ingredients: warm relationships with family and friends, good health, enough money to feel safe, and a sense that your life has purpose. Feeling free to make your own choices, and living in a fair, trusting community, matter too.

Some things matter less than people expect. Extra money makes a big difference when a family is poor. Once people are comfortable, each extra rupee or dollar adds less. Fame and new possessions give a quick lift that soon fades. Genes play a part too, which is why some people seem naturally cheerful.

Surprising but true

Bhutan's king said in the 1970s that "Gross National Happiness" mattered more than how much money the country made, and Bhutan still measures it today.

The study that has lasted a lifetime

In 1938, researchers at Harvard University began following a group of young men: students at the university and boys from poor Boston neighbourhoods. They asked about their health, work and families, and kept asking for decades. Later, the study added their wives and children.

When the men were in their 80s, researchers looked back at who had stayed healthy and happy. The strongest clue wasn't wealth, cholesterol or career success. It was how satisfied people had been with their relationships at age 50. The current director, psychiatrist Robert Waldinger, sums it up simply: good relationships keep us happier and healthier.

Does being thankful make you happier?In one well-known study, students who wrote down five things they were thankful for each week ended up feeling better about their lives than students who listed their hassles.

Getting used to things

People are good at adjusting. A new bicycle feels wonderful for a few weeks, then it's just your bicycle. Psychologists call this hedonic adaptation. A 1978 study found that lottery winners, after a while, were not much happier than people who hadn't won.

This is one reason some psychologists suggest spending on experiences, like a family trip or learning to swim, more than on things. Studies by Thomas Gilovich at Cornell University found that people often stay happier about experiences for longer. Memories of a trip can even grow fonder with time, and they are usually shared with other people.

It works the other way too. People often recover from setbacks better than they expect. That doesn't mean bad things don't hurt. It means our feelings tend to drift back towards our usual level over time.

Measuring a country's mood

Every year, the World Happiness Report asks people in more than 140 countries to picture a ladder from 0 to 10. The top is the best possible life for them, the bottom the worst. Then it asks which step they're on now.

In 2026, Finland came top for the ninth year running, and India ranked 116th. Nordic countries do well partly because people trust each other and their governments, and have good support when things go wrong. Bhutan, a small Himalayan kingdom, took a different path: in the 1970s its king said "Gross National Happiness" was more important than national income, and the country still surveys its people's wellbeing.

Small things that help

Scientists have tested everyday habits that seem to lift mood. Spending time with friends, getting enough sleep and moving your body all help. So does time outdoors among trees or water. Doing something kind for someone else often makes the helper feel good too, a finding studied in the science of kindness.

In 2018, Delhi's government schools added a daily "Happiness Class", where children talk about feelings, practise paying attention and do activities about helping others. Many schools in the USA run similar social and emotional learning lessons.

Feeling sad, angry or worried sometimes is a normal part of being human. Happiness isn't about feeling great all the time. If sadness hangs around for weeks, though, it's worth talking to a parent, teacher or doctor, just as you would about a pain that won't go away.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If you just had the right things, like a new phone or a big house, you'd be happy forever.

Truth: New things give a burst of pleasure that usually fades within weeks, as people get used to them. Lasting happiness has more to do with relationships, health, purpose and how we spend our time.

Try this

For one week, write down three good things each evening, however small, like a funny joke at lunch or a warm chapati. Also write why each one happened. At the end of the week, read the list and notice how you feel. Some studies have found this simple habit lifts people's mood.
