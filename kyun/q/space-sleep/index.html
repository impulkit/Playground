How do astronauts sleep?

Astronauts on the space station see about 16 sunrises and 16 sunsets every day, and they sleep in bags tied to the wall so they don't float into anything.

The short answer

On the International Space Station, astronauts sleep in small private cabins about the size of a telephone booth. Each has a sleeping bag strapped to the wall, a pillow, a laptop and a few personal photos. The astronaut zips into the bag, which stops them from drifting around while they float.

Because there is no gravity pulling them down, it doesn't matter if they sleep upright, sideways or upside down. Their body feels the same. But sleep in space has strange problems. The Sun rises every 90 minutes. The station hums with fans and machines. And floating arms drift up in front of the body like a sleepwalking cartoon character unless they are tucked into the bag.

Surprising but true

Without a fan blowing, an astronaut could wake up with a headache, because the air they breathe out can collect in a bubble around their face.

Sixteen sunrises a day

The space station circles Earth about once every 90 minutes. That means astronauts pass from daylight into darkness and back again about 16 times a day. If they followed the Sun, they would never sleep properly.

So the station runs on a fixed clock, Coordinated Universal Time, close to London time. Lights are dimmed at "night" and brightened in the "morning". Since 2016, NASA has used special LED lights that change colour, bluer in the morning to wake people up and warmer in the evening to help them feel sleepy. They help keep the astronauts' body clocks on track.

Floating in a sleeping bag

Most astronauts say floating in a sleeping bag is very comfortable. There is no mattress to press on their back, and nothing aches. Some like to strap their head to a pillow so it doesn't nod around. Others use straps across their body so they feel held, like a blanket.

Floating arms are a common surprise. When the body relaxes, the arms rise in front, like a zombie. Many astronauts tuck them in or hold them with straps. Some astronauts have said they dreamed of floating, even on Earth after coming back.

What is your body clock?Deep in your brain, a cluster of about 20,000 nerve cells keeps time like a clock, telling your body when to feel sleepy, hungry and alert.

Breathing in your sleep

On Earth, warm air rises. When you breathe out in bed, the used air drifts away. In space, there is no rising and sinking, because nothing is heavier or lighter. Breathed-out carbon dioxide can pool in a cloud around your head.

That is why the station's cabins have fans that keep fresh air flowing. Astronauts who forget to switch on the fan can wake up with a headache or feeling short of breath. Noise from the fans is so constant that many astronauts wear earplugs.

Not enough sleep

NASA plans for astronauts to sleep about 8.5 hours a night. But a big study in 2014 found that astronauts on the space station and the Space Shuttle often slept only about six hours. Excitement, noise, busy schedules and the light show outside all play a part.

Too little sleep makes people slower and more forgetful, which can be risky when working with complicated machines. So astronauts track their sleep, and scientists study it closely, partly to plan for long trips to Mars.

Sleeping on a long trip

A trip to Mars could take six to nine months each way. Scientists are thinking about how to help crews sleep well for years at a time, with better lights, quieter cabins and even better sleeping bags.

India's astronauts will face these questions too. In 2025, Shubhanshu Shukla spent about 18 days on the space station during the Axiom-4 mission, the first Indian to live aboard it. India's Gaganyaan programme plans to send its own crew to orbit in a capsule built in India. Wherever they go, astronauts will need to sleep, eat and, yes, use a toilet, each of which in orbit becomes a puzzle, as the story of the space toilet shows.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Astronauts sleep lying down in beds like on Earth.

Truth: In orbit there is no up or down, so lying down means nothing. Astronauts zip into sleeping bags fixed to a wall, usually upright, and the bag keeps them in place while they float.

Try this

Try a space-style sleep experiment. For one night, keep a notebook by your bed and write down when you fell asleep, when you woke, and how rested you felt, on a scale of 1 to 5. The next night, make the room as dark as possible with a sleep mask or thick curtains. Compare the two.
