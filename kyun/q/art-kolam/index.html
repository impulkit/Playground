What is kolam?

Before sunrise, in millions of homes in south India, someone draws a new pattern of rice flour on the doorstep, knowing ants will eat it by noon.

The short answer

A kolam is a pattern drawn on the ground at the entrance of a home, usually in white rice flour. In Tamil Nadu and nearby states, women and girls have done it at dawn for generations. They sweep the doorstep, sprinkle water to settle the dust, and then let the flour trickle from between their fingers into lines, loops and curves.

By the evening, feet, wind and ants have worn it away. The next morning, a new one appears. It is part welcome, part prayer and part art, made fresh every single day. Its northern cousin is rangoli.

Surprising but true

Computer scientists and mathematicians have studied kolam patterns since the 1970s, because some of them follow rules like a computer program.

Drawing with your fingers

Watching an expert draw kolam is like watching someone write very fast in a script you can't read. A pinch of flour is held between thumb and fingers, and the hand moves steadily while the flour falls in an even white line. A large design can take only a few minutes.

Many kolams begin with a grid of dots, called pulli. Some designs connect the dots with straight lines into stars and flowers. Others, called sikku or neli kolams, loop one continuous line around every dot without ever touching them, like a thread wound round pegs. A good sikku kolam often has no beginning and no end.

Food for a thousand souls

The rice flour matters. Ants, sparrows and other small creatures come and eat it. For many families, drawing a kolam is a way of feeding other living things at the start of the day.

Kolams also send messages. A doorstep with no kolam can be a sign that the family is in mourning. During festivals, kolams grow bigger, with colour and flowers added. In the Tamil month of Margazhi, from mid-December to mid-January, whole streets fill with giant designs, and some towns hold kolam competitions. At Pongal, designs show pots of rice boiling over.

What are Onam and Pongal?On Pongal morning in Tamil Nadu, families watch a pot of rice and milk on the fire, waiting for it to boil over. When it does, everyone shouts "Pongalo Pongal!"

The maths in the flour

Kolam isn't only beautiful. It is full of symmetry, repeats and rules. In the 1970s, the computer scientist Gift Siromoney and colleagues in Chennai studied kolams. They showed that some could be described by step-by-step rules, the same kind of instructions used in computer programs. A design that looks like art can also be a recipe. Later researchers used kolams to study algorithms, patterns and knots.

A single-line kolam has to obey strict rules: go around every dot, never cross a dot, and close the loop. Kolam artists learn these rules by eye and hand, not by formula, from mothers and grandmothers. Some can draw designs with hundreds of dots from memory.

A tradition that keeps changing

In crowded cities, many apartment doorways are tiny. Some families now use kolam stickers, stencils or rollers that print a pattern in one go. Others still get up early with a bowl of flour, even on a narrow landing.

Kolam has also spread with Tamil families to Malaysia, Singapore, Sri Lanka and the United States, where it appears at festivals and in school projects about patterns. Artists have drawn kolams on canvas, on walls and in galleries.

And the creatures it was made to feed are still on the job. Watch a fresh kolam for an hour and you may see a line of ants carrying off the flour grain by grain, back to a nest hidden in the cracks.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Kolam and rangoli are exactly the same thing.

Truth: They are close cousins. Kolam is the Tamil tradition, usually white line drawings of rice flour made every morning. Rangoli is a wider word used in many parts of India, often for colourful powder designs made for festivals.

Try this

On paper, draw a grid of 3 rows of 3 dots. Now try to draw one single line that loops around every dot without lifting your pencil and without touching any dot. That is a simple sikku kolam. Try a 4 by 4 grid next.
