Why does Rajasthan have so many forts?

The wall around Kumbhalgarh fort snakes over the Aravalli hills for about 36 km, long enough to run almost a full marathon along it.

The short answer

For hundreds of years, Rajasthan was split into many kingdoms ruled by Rajput clans, such as the rulers of Mewar, Marwar and Amber. Neighbouring kingdoms often fought each other, and armies from outside the region invaded too. Every ruler needed a stronghold, and a stronghold showed off his power.

The land helped. The Aravalli hills, some of the oldest mountains on Earth, give steep, rocky ridges that are hard to climb. In the west, the Thar Desert made attacks difficult for any army without enough water. Builders placed forts on hilltops, ridges and desert rises, and surrounded them with thick walls. Many still stand, from Chittorgarh to Jaisalmer.

Surprising but true

Thousands of people still live inside Jaisalmer Fort, making it one of the few "living forts" left in the world.

A wall you can see from far away

Kumbhalgarh, built mostly in the 1400s by Rana Kumbha of Mewar, has a wall that runs for about 36 km over the hills. In places it is wide enough for several horses to ride side by side. Inside are dozens of temples, wells and palaces.

Chittorgarh, also in Mewar, is one of the largest forts in India, spread over a long hilltop about as big as several hundred football fields put together. It was besieged three times in its long history, and its stories of courage are still told in songs.

What was life like in a castle?Medieval castles were cold, smoky and crowded, and the toilets often emptied straight down the outside wall.

Built to survive a siege

An attacking army often didn't try to climb the walls. It surrounded the fort and waited for the people inside to run out of food or water. So builders planned for long sieges.

Forts stored grain in deep stores and collected every drop of rain. Amber Fort near Jaipur, and many others, used channels, step wells and large tanks to keep water through the dry months. Gates came in a series, one after another, often on a winding uphill path. Iron spikes on the doors stopped elephants from ramming them, and sharp bends meant no enemy could charge straight in. Narrow slits in the walls let archers, and later gunners, shoot out while staying hidden.

The golden fort in the desert

Jaisalmer Fort rises out of the Thar Desert like a giant sandcastle. It was founded in 1156 by Rawal Jaisal, and is built from yellow sandstone that glows gold in the evening. It sat on trade routes where camel caravans carried goods between India and lands to the west.

What makes it unusual today is that people never left. Thousands still live inside, with homes, shops, temples and small hotels in its narrow lanes. That brings problems: modern water pipes and drains have leaked into the old walls, and parts have crumbled. Conservation groups are working to protect it.

From war to world heritage

Forts lost their military purpose in the 1800s, when much of Rajasthan came under British influence and cannons got stronger. Many royal families turned parts of their forts into museums or hotels. Mehrangarh in Jodhpur, founded in 1459, now holds collections of palanquins, armour and paintings.

In 2013, UNESCO listed six of the state's hill forts as a World Heritage Site: Chittorgarh, Kumbhalgarh, Ranthambore, Gagron, Amber and Jaisalmer. They joined India's long list of World Heritage Sites, chosen because they show how Rajput builders used hills, rivers and desert for defence. Gagron is unusual: rivers surround it on three sides, so it is a hill fort and a water fort at once. Ranthambore's fort now stands inside a tiger reserve. Visitors climbing to its gates sometimes spot a tiger resting near the old walls, as if it has taken over guard duty.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Forts were only military bases where soldiers lived.

Truth: Many Rajasthan forts held palaces, temples, markets, homes and huge water tanks. Kings ruled from them, and towns grew up inside and around their walls. Jaisalmer Fort still has homes and shops inside.

Try this

Build a model fort from cardboard, clay or sand on a mound of earth or a pile of books. Add a winding entrance path, a wall with lookout points, and a water tank inside. Then ask a friend where they'd try to attack it, and see if your design holds up.
