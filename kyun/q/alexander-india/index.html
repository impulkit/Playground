What happened when Alexander reached India?

Alexander the Great marched from Greece to the Punjab without losing a major battle. Then his own soldiers refused to go any further.

The short answer

Alexander, a young king from Macedonia in northern Greece, crossed the Hindu Kush mountains into the Indus region in 327–326 BCE. He had already conquered the vast Persian Empire. Some local rulers, such as the king of Taxila, joined him rather than fight.

In 326 BCE he defeated King Porus at the Hydaspes river, today's Jhelum, in a hard battle against war elephants. But when his army reached the Beas river, the exhausted soldiers refused to march east. Alexander turned south, sailed down the Indus to the sea and left. He died in Babylon in 323 BCE, aged just 32.

Surprising but true

Alexander's favourite horse, Bucephalus, died after the battle with Porus, and Alexander founded a city named after him beside the Jhelum river.

Battle at the river

Porus ruled the land beyond the Jhelum. He lined up his army on the far bank, with elephants in front. Horses panic at the sight and smell of elephants, so Alexander could not simply charge across.

According to the Greek writers, Alexander tricked Porus. For many nights he made noise as if about to cross, until the Indian army stopped reacting. Then, in a monsoon storm, he crossed upstream with part of his army. The battle was fierce. Some elephants, wounded and frightened, trampled soldiers on both sides. Porus, a very tall man on a huge elephant, kept fighting until he was badly wounded.

"Treat me like a king"

The Greek historian Arrian tells a famous story. When Porus was brought to Alexander, Alexander asked how he wished to be treated. Porus is said to have replied, "Like a king."

We can't be sure he said exactly this, since Arrian wrote more than 400 years later. He relied on earlier accounts by Alexander's officers, which are now lost. But Alexander did let Porus keep ruling and gave him more land. Alexander needed friends to guard the region after he moved on. Near the battlefield he founded two towns, one named Bucephala after his beloved horse, Bucephalus, who died around this time.

The army says no

Alexander wanted to keep going east. He had heard of rich kingdoms on the Ganga, probably the Nanda kingdom of Magadha, with an even larger army and thousands of elephants.

His soldiers had been marching and fighting for about eight years and had travelled thousands of kilometres. The monsoon rains soaked them for weeks. At the Beas river, they refused to cross. After sulking in his tent for three days, Alexander gave in. On the way home, he was badly wounded by an arrow while attacking a town of the Malli people. Later, many of his troops suffered terribly crossing the desert of Gedrosia, in today's Baluchistan.

What Alexander left behind

Indian sources of the time do not mention Alexander at all. To them, he was one of several invaders from the northwest, and he stayed only about two years. His soldiers did not build roads or temples that lasted, and his garrisons soon melted away.

His visit still mattered. Within a few years, a young leader called Chandragupta, guided by his teacher Chanakya, founded the Mauryan Empire and took over the northwest. In about 305 BCE, Alexander's general Seleucus made peace with Chandragupta and received 500 elephants in exchange for land. Greek kings ruled parts of Afghanistan and the Punjab for two more centuries. Their art mixed with Indian ideas, and in Gandhara, early Buddhist sculptures show the Buddha in robes that fall in Greek-style folds.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Alexander conquered India.

Truth: Alexander only reached the north-western edge of the subcontinent, in what is now Pakistan and Indian Punjab. He turned back at the Beas river and never saw the Ganga plains.
