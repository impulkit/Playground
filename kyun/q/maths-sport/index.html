How is maths used in sport?

A baseball team with little money once used statistics to find bargain players, and basketball teams changed how they shoot after doing some simple multiplication.

The short answer

Maths turns up in almost every sport. Scorers keep statistics, coaches study data to plan tactics, engineers use physics to design better bats, balls and shoes, and broadcasters use geometry to track the ball. Even setting a fair target in a rain-hit cricket match needs a formula.

Some of the maths is simple, like adding runs or timing a race. Some is complicated, like the computer models that predict where a cricket ball would have gone if it hadn't hit the batter's pad. Either way, numbers help players train smarter, help fans understand the game, and help referees and umpires make fairer decisions.

Surprising but true

When rain shortens a one-day cricket match, the new target is set by a mathematical formula, first used in international cricket in 1997 and named after the statisticians who designed it.

Rain, cricket and a formula

In a one-day cricket match, rain can wipe out overs halfway through. How do you set a fair target for the team batting second? Simply scaling down by overs is unfair, because a team with ten wickets in hand can bat much more boldly than a team with two.

In 1997, two English statisticians, Frank Duckworth and Tony Lewis, created a method that counts both overs and wickets as "resources". The fewer resources a team has, the lower its target. In 2014, an Australian statistician, Steven Stern, updated it for modern high-scoring games, and it became the Duckworth–Lewis–Stern, or DLS, method. Players often joke about not understanding it, but it is used in every rain-affected international one-day and T20 match.

Moneyball and the three-pointer

Around 2002, the Oakland Athletics, a baseball team with far less money than its rivals, began picking players using statistics instead of scouts' hunches. They found that some skills, like getting on base, were undervalued and cheap to buy. The team won many games on a small budget, and the writer Michael Lewis told the story in his book Moneyball.

Basketball went through its own number revolution. Analysts pointed out that a three-point shot, even if it goes in less often, can be worth more on average than a two-pointer. In recent years, teams in the NBA have taken far more threes than teams did in the 1990s.

Physics in flight

When Neeraj Chopra won India's javelin gold at the Tokyo Olympics, his best throw flew 87.58 m. A throw like that is a lesson in angles and speed. In a world without air, the best angle to launch a thrown object is 45 degrees. Real javelins are affected by air pushing on them, and top throwers usually release at a lower angle, often around 35 degrees.

The same thinking applies to a long jumper's take-off, a footballer's free kick curling around a wall, and a spin bowler's flight. Coaches film athletes with high-speed cameras and use software to measure angles and speeds, then suggest tiny changes.

The third umpire's maths

Ball-tracking systems such as Hawk-Eye use several cameras around the ground. Each camera records the ball's position many times a second. A computer combines the views to work out exactly where the ball was in three dimensions, then predicts its path. In cricket, that prediction helps decide leg-before-wicket appeals. In tennis, it shows whether a ball landed in or out.

These systems are very accurate but not perfect. That is why cricket's review system has an "umpire's call" zone: if the predicted path only clips the stumps, the umpire's original decision stands. It is maths admitting its own margin of error, which good statistics always should.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Statistics take the human skill out of sport.

Truth: Numbers can show patterns that people miss, but players still have to perform under pressure, and coaches still have to read the game. Most teams now use data alongside the judgement of experienced players and coaches.
