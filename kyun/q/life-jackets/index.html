How does a life jacket keep you afloat?

A good life jacket can flip an unconscious person onto their back so their face stays out of the water, without them doing anything at all.

The short answer

Anything in water is pushed upward by a force called buoyancy. The push equals the weight of the water the object shoves aside. If that push is bigger than the object's weight, it floats.

The human body is almost as dense as water, so most people float only just, with little more than the top of the head above the surface. That is not enough to breathe easily, especially in waves. A life jacket adds a lot of volume but very little weight. It shoves aside extra water, which gives you an extra upward push, and it places that push where it lifts your head and chest highest.

Surprising but true

Some life jackets inflate by themselves. A small tablet inside dissolves when it gets wet, releasing a spring that punctures a cartridge of carbon dioxide, which fills the jacket in seconds.

Archimedes and the bath

The idea goes back to Archimedes, a Greek thinker who lived in Syracuse over 2,200 years ago. According to an old story, he noticed the water level rise as he got into a bath and realised the water pushed back on his body. Whether or not the bath story is true, he did write down the rule: a floating object is pushed up by a force equal to the weight of water it displaces.

That rule explains why a huge steel ship floats, why ice floats on a lake, and why a life jacket works.

What is inside

Most life jackets are filled with closed-cell foam. Each tiny bubble in the foam is sealed off from its neighbours, so water cannot soak in and the foam keeps its lift even if it is torn. Older jackets used cork or kapok, a fluffy fibre from the seed pods of the kapok tree.

Inflatable life jackets are slim collars that lie flat until needed. Pull a cord and a small cylinder of carbon dioxide fills them. Automatic ones do it themselves when they get wet. They are comfortable for sailors who wear them all day, but they need regular checks, and they are not usually recommended for small children or weak swimmers.

Built to turn you over

A life jacket is different from a buoyancy aid. A buoyancy aid, the kind worn for kayaking, helps a conscious swimmer stay up. A proper life jacket is designed to roll an unconscious person onto their back.

It does this with its shape. Most of the floatation sits on the chest and behind the head. Your body's own weight hangs lower, so it settles below the floatation like the keel of a boat. Even face down, the jacket tips you over. Life jackets are rated in newtons, a unit of force. A 100-newton jacket suits calm, sheltered water. A 150 or 275-newton jacket is for the open sea, where heavy clothes and rough waves need more lift.

Lifeboats and a cork vest

In 1854, Captain John Ross Ward of the Royal National Lifeboat Institution in Britain designed a vest of cork pieces for lifeboat crews, who rowed out into storms to rescue sailors. It was one of the first life jackets made for everyday use.

When the Titanic sank in 1912, it carried life jackets for everyone on board, but most people who entered the freezing water died of cold, not from sinking. That is why modern safety rules also focus on lifeboats, survival suits and fast rescue.

Wearing it right

A life jacket only works if it fits and is fastened. Children's jackets often have a strap between the legs, so the jacket cannot slide up over the head. It should feel snug: if an adult lifts it by the shoulders, it should not rise past the ears.

In India, ferries, boat rides on lakes and houseboat trips in Kerala are supposed to provide life jackets for every passenger, and many boating accidents have led to stricter rules. In the USA, children on boats must usually wear them. Coast Guard figures show again and again that most people who drown in boating accidents were not wearing one.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Good swimmers don't need life jackets.

Truth: Cold water, strong currents, waves, tiredness or a blow to the head can overwhelm even strong swimmers. Lifeboat crews and experienced sailors wear life jackets on the water for exactly this reason.

Try this

Fill a basin with water. Drop in a small toy that sinks. Now tie it to a piece of sponge or a sealed empty bottle cap and try again. Add more or less floating material until the toy just bobs at the surface. You have built a life jacket for it.
