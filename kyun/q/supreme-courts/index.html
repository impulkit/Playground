What do supreme courts do?

In the 1980s, India's Supreme Court began treating ordinary letters and postcards from citizens as real court cases.

The short answer

A supreme court is the highest court in a country. Its decisions are final: there is nowhere higher to appeal. Lower courts across the country must follow its rulings.

Its biggest job is to protect the constitution. If a government action or a law goes against it, the supreme court can cancel it. Supreme courts also settle disputes between states, and between citizens and the government.

India's Supreme Court sits in New Delhi and started work in 1950. The US Supreme Court sits in Washington, D.C., and first met in 1790. Both are watched closely, because their decisions can change how millions of people live.

Surprising but true

In 1973, India's Supreme Court heard a case with 13 judges sitting together, the largest bench in its history. It decided that Parliament cannot change the basic structure of the Constitution.

Guardians of the constitution

The Constitution of India gives every citizen fundamental rights, such as equality and freedom of speech. Article 32 lets anyone go straight to the Supreme Court if those rights are broken. Dr B.R. Ambedkar called this article "the very soul of the Constitution and the very heart of it".

In the USA, the court gave itself a similar power in 1803, in a case called Marbury v. Madison. It ruled that judges can strike down laws that conflict with the US Constitution. This power is called judicial review.

The case of 13 judges

In 1973, a religious leader from Kerala named Kesavananda Bharati challenged a land law. The case grew into a huge question: can Parliament change any part of the Constitution it likes?

Thirteen judges heard it, the largest bench ever. By seven votes to six, they ruled that Parliament can amend the Constitution but cannot destroy its basic structure, such as democracy, the rule of law and fundamental rights. This idea still guides Indian law.

A ruling that changed schools

In 1954, in Brown v. Board of Education, the US Supreme Court ruled that keeping Black and white children in separate public schools broke the Constitution. All nine justices agreed. It was a turning point for the American civil rights movement, though many schools resisted for years.

Justice by postcard

In the late 1970s and 1980s, Indian judges noticed that the poorest people almost never reached the Supreme Court. Lawyers cost too much. So judges like P.N. Bhagwati began treating letters about injustice as petitions. This grew into public interest litigation, which lets anyone go to court on behalf of people who can't.

Cases about prisoners, bonded labourers and polluted rivers reached the court this way. Critics say the court sometimes goes too far into decisions better left to governments, a debate that continues.

How the courts are run

India's Supreme Court can have up to 34 judges, who retire at 65. They usually hear cases in small groups called benches. Senior judges have a big say in choosing new ones, through a group called the collegium. The court has a huge backlog, with tens of thousands of cases waiting.

The US court has nine justices, who serve for life. The president nominates them and the Senate must approve. They hear only around 60 cases a year, chosen from thousands of requests.

A new statue

For centuries, Lady Justice has been shown blindfolded, holding scales and a sword. In 2024, a new statue in India's Supreme Court library showed her with open eyes, holding the Constitution instead of a sword. News reports said the change was meant to show that the law is not blind, and that justice in India rests on the Constitution.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Supreme courts make the laws.

Truth: Laws are made by parliaments, such as India's Parliament or the US Congress. Supreme courts decide what laws mean and whether they follow the constitution. If a law breaks the constitution, the court can strike it down.

Try this

Hold a mini court at home. Pick a small, friendly dispute, like who gets the last mango. One person argues each side, and a "judge" listens to both and gives a decision with reasons. Swap roles. Notice how hard it is to be fair when you already have an opinion.
