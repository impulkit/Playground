What is insurance?

Lloyd's of London, one of the most famous insurance markets in the world, began in the 1680s as a coffee shop where ship owners swapped news.

The short answer

Insurance is a way of sharing risk. Lots of people each pay a small, regular amount, called a premium, into a shared pool run by an insurance company. When something bad happens to one of them, such as a house fire or a stay in hospital, the company pays out from the pool to help cover the cost.

It works because most bad things don't happen to most people in the same year. If you had to pay for a big hospital bill alone, it could wipe out your savings. With insurance, you pay a little each year and swap a small, certain cost for protection against a big, uncertain one.

Surprising but true

After the Great Fire of London in 1666, early insurance companies ran their own fire brigades, and put metal badges on the houses they had insured.

The maths behind it

Insurance companies are experts in probability. They study huge amounts of data. How many houses catch fire each year? How likely is a 40-year-old driver to crash? From this, they work out how much to charge.

Say 1,000 families each pay ₹500. That makes a pool of ₹5 lakh. If, on average, two homes a year need ₹2 lakh of repairs, the pool pays ₹4 lakh and has ₹1 lakh left for running costs and savings. People with higher risks, such as drivers who have crashed before, usually pay more.

Old ideas about sharing risk

People have shared risk for thousands of years. The Code of Hammurabi, carved in Babylon around 1750 BCE, included rules for merchants who borrowed money for trading trips. If robbers took the goods, the merchant might not have to repay the loan.

In the 1600s, ships carried valuable cargo across dangerous seas. Merchants and ship owners in London gathered at a coffee house run by Edward Lloyd to hear the latest shipping news. Some agreed to share the losses if a ship sank, in return for a fee. That coffee house grew into Lloyd's of London, which still insures ships, planes and much more.

Fire marks and fire brigades

In 1666, the Great Fire of London destroyed more than 13,000 houses. Afterwards, the first fire insurance companies appeared. Each company had its own team of firefighters. Insured houses carried a metal badge, called a fire mark, so the firefighters knew which buildings were theirs.

In America, Benjamin Franklin helped found a fire insurance company in Philadelphia in 1752. It still exists, making it one of the oldest insurers in the USA.

Kinds of insurance

Today there's insurance for almost everything. Health insurance helps pay for doctors and hospitals. Life insurance pays money to a family if someone who earns for them dies. Car insurance pays for damage after accidents, and in both India and the USA most drivers must have at least some by law. Farmers can insure crops against drought or floods.

In some countries, the government provides health insurance for everyone, paid for through taxes. In India, a scheme called Ayushman Bharat PM-JAY, started in 2018, covers hospital care of up to ₹5 lakh a year for each poorer family that qualifies. Since 2024 it also covers everyone aged 70 or over, whatever their income. The way countries pay for healthcare is a big topic of debate around the world.

Making a claim

When something goes wrong, you make a claim: you tell the insurer what happened and send evidence, such as photos, bills or a police report. The company checks the details and pays out if your policy covers that kind of loss.

Insurers check claims carefully, because some people try to cheat. Lying to get insurance money is a crime. Reading the small print matters too. A policy might cover fire but not floods, or a stolen bike only if it was locked. Like any promise, insurance is only as good as its rules, and both sides need to understand them.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Insurance is a waste of money if you never make a claim.

Truth: You pay for protection against a loss you couldn't afford, the same way you wear a seatbelt on every trip even though most trips are safe. If nothing goes wrong, that's good news, not wasted money.
