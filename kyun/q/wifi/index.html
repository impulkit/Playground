How does Wi-Fi work?

Wi-Fi sends your videos and messages through the air as invisible radio waves, using almost the same frequency as a microwave oven.

The short answer

Wi-Fi lets phones, laptops and other devices connect to the internet without wires, using radio waves.

At the heart of it is a box called a router. It connects to the internet through a cable. The router turns data from the internet into radio signals and sends them out through the air. Your device picks up those signals with a tiny antenna and turns them back into data. When you send something, like a message, the same thing happens in reverse.

It all happens millions of times a second, so web pages and videos appear almost instantly.

Surprising but true

Wi-Fi doesn't stand for anything. The name was made up by a branding company in 1999 because it sounded catchy, a bit like "hi-fi".

Radio waves carrying data

Radio waves are a kind of light our eyes can't see. Like ripples on a pond, they have a frequency, which means how many waves pass a point each second. Wi-Fi mostly uses waves at 2.4 gigahertz (2.4 billion waves a second) and 5 gigahertz. Newer routers also use 6 gigahertz.

To carry information, the router changes the waves in tiny, precise ways, shifting their strength and timing. Each change stands for a group of 0s and 1s, the binary language of computers. Your device reads the changes and rebuilds the data.

The 2.4 gigahertz band reaches further and passes walls better. The higher bands carry more data but fade faster.

Taking turns

In a busy home, many devices share one router. A phone streams a cricket match, a laptop joins a video call, a games console downloads an update. They can't all talk at once on the same channel, or their signals would jam.

So Wi-Fi uses rules for taking turns. Each device listens first, and only sends when the air is clear. Modern routers can also talk to several devices at once by splitting their channel into smaller pieces. Large buildings like airports and stations use many routers so no single one gets overloaded.

The microwave connection

A microwave oven uses radio waves at about 2.45 gigahertz, very close to Wi-Fi's 2.4. In the oven, those waves are very strong and trapped inside a metal box, where they make water molecules in food jiggle and heat up.

A little of that energy can leak out, which is why some people notice their Wi-Fi slowing down when the microwave is on. Wi-Fi itself is far weaker, thousands of times less powerful than an oven, and health agencies have found no evidence that normal Wi-Fi harms people.

How it was invented

In 1997, engineers agreed on a shared set of rules for wireless networks, called IEEE 802.11. Because every company followed the same rules, devices from different makers could talk to each other.

A key problem was echoes. Indoors, radio waves bounce off walls and furniture and arrive at slightly different times, blurring the signal. A team at CSIRO, Australia's national science agency, led by the radio astronomer John O'Sullivan, developed ways to deal with this, based partly on techniques from studying signals from space. In 1999, a group of companies gave the technology a catchier name: Wi-Fi.

Wi-Fi in everyday life

Wi-Fi now connects billions of devices, from phones to smart TVs to doorbells. Many cities, cafes, airports and libraries offer free Wi-Fi. In 2016, Mumbai Central became one of the first Indian railway stations to offer free high-speed Wi-Fi, and hundreds of stations followed.

Because Wi-Fi travels through the air, anyone nearby can try to listen. That's why home networks use passwords and encryption, which scrambles data so only the right devices can read it. On public Wi-Fi, it's wise to avoid entering private information unless the website is secure.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Wi-Fi stands for "wireless fidelity".

Truth: The name was invented in 1999 by a branding firm and doesn't stand for anything. A slogan mentioning "wireless fidelity" was used briefly afterwards, which is where the confusion began.

Try this

With an adult's permission, take a phone or tablet around your home and watch the Wi-Fi symbol, which shows signal strength. Draw a map of your home and mark strong, medium and weak spots. See if walls, the kitchen or a metal cupboard make a difference.
