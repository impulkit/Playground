What are the floating islands of Loktak Lake?

In Manipur, a lake is covered with floating islands, and on the biggest of them lives a deer that seems to dance as it walks.

The short answer

Loktak Lake, in the hills of Manipur, is the largest freshwater lake in Northeast India. Much of its surface is covered with phumdis, floating islands made of tangled grass, roots, soil and rotting plants. Some are small enough for one person to stand on. Others are huge. The largest join together into a floating national park, Keibul Lamjao, about 40 square kilometres in size.

People live on and around the lake, fishing from wooden boats and building huts on the phumdis. The lake also feeds a hydroelectric power project and provides water for farms across the valley.

Surprising but true

Keibul Lamjao, on Loktak Lake, is described as the world's only floating national park.

Ground that wobbles

A phumdi looks like solid land covered in tall grass. But step on one and it sinks and sways a little, like a waterbed. Most of a phumdi's thickness sits below the water, with only a small part sticking out on top, a bit like an iceberg.

Phumdis can be more than a metre thick. In the dry season, some sink down and touch the lake bed, where their roots soak up nutrients. When the rains come and the water rises, they float up again. This yearly rise and fall keeps them healthy.

The dancing deer

Keibul Lamjao was set up to protect the sangai, a brow-antlered deer found nowhere else in the wild. Its antlers sweep forward over its eyes in a long curve. In 1951, it was even declared extinct, until a small group was found again on the phumdis in 1953.

Sangai pick their way across the floating ground with high, careful steps. Manipuris call it the dancing deer, and it appears in songs and in the graceful movements of Manipuri dance. It's Manipur's state animal. A count in 2016 found about 260, so it remains endangered.

What is Manipuri dance?In Manipuri dance, the drummers dance too, leaping and spinning in the air while still playing their drums.

Circles on the water

From above, parts of Loktak look as if someone drew circles all over it. These are athaphums, rings of phumdi cut and arranged by fishing families. Fish gather inside the rings, sheltered by the plants, and are caught with nets.

Many families live in phumshongs, small huts built on floating islands. Children paddle boats to school, and fishers spend days on the water. Life here follows the lake, much like many other fishing villages across India. Loktak's fish, plants and snails feed thousands of people around it.

A lake under pressure

In 1983, the Ithai barrage was built where water leaves the lake, to keep water levels steady for a hydroelectric dam project. It made electricity, but it also stopped the natural rise and fall of the water.

Without that seasonal dip, many phumdis stopped touching the lake bed. They grew thinner and weaker. In Keibul Lamjao, thin phumdis can sink under the deer's weight, and their grassland is shrinking. Too many phumdis in other parts of the lake block boats and choke the water.

Loktak was named a Ramsar wetland of international importance in 1990, and in 1993 it was put on a list of Ramsar sites under threat. Scientists and the Manipur government are trying to balance power, fishing and wildlife. Ideas include opening the barrage gates at certain times to let the water rise and fall again, and clearing phumdis from the open parts of the lake. Some scientists also want a second home for the sangai elsewhere, so that one bad flood can't wipe out the whole species.

Loktak is among the great wetlands of Asia. And it isn't the only strange landscape in India's Northeast: a few hundred kilometres away in Meghalaya, people grow bridges out of living tree roots.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The floating islands of Loktak are made by people, like rafts.

Truth: Phumdis form naturally from plants, soil and rotting matter that tangle together over many years. People do shape some of them, cutting them into rings for fish farming or building huts on them, but the islands themselves grow on their own.
