Who was Chanakya?

A teacher who, legend says, was thrown out of a royal court swore revenge, and helped topple a whole dynasty.

The short answer

Chanakya was a Brahmin scholar who, according to tradition, lived around 300 BCE. He is said to have taught at Taxila, a famous centre of learning in what is now Pakistan.

The best-known story says he was insulted at the court of the Nanda king of Magadha and vowed to bring the dynasty down. He found a bright young man, Chandragupta, trained him and helped him take the throne. This began the Mauryan Empire. Chanakya is also known as Kautilya, the name on a famous Sanskrit book about running a kingdom, the Arthashastra.

Surprising but true

The Arthashastra was lost for centuries. A palm-leaf copy turned up in Mysore in 1905 and was recognised by a librarian named R. Shamasastry.

A book lost and found

For centuries, people knew the Arthashastra only from mentions in other works. Then, in 1905, a bundle of palm-leaf manuscripts was handed to the Oriental Library in Mysore. The librarian, R. Shamasastry, realised he was holding the lost text.

He published it in Sanskrit in 1909 and in English in 1915. Scholars around the world were startled. Here was a detailed handbook of government from ancient India, covering topics most people thought only modern states worried about. The find changed how historians saw early India. It showed that thinkers there had written carefully about money, law and power, as well as about religion and poetry.

What the book says

The Arthashastra is long, with 15 books inside it. It explains how to collect taxes, run mines and markets, keep weights and measures honest, store grain against famine and protect forests and elephants.

It also has a hard side. It describes networks of spies in disguise, as monks, traders or students, who report on officials and enemies. It discusses secret messages, poisons and how to win wars by tricks as well as battles. Its main aim is a strong, wealthy and safe kingdom, and it is frank about the methods.

Clever, or ruthless?

Writers often compare Kautilya with Niccolò Machiavelli, an Italian who wrote The Prince in 1513, another blunt guide for rulers. Both describe power as it works, not as people wish it worked.

But the Arthashastra also says a king's happiness lies in the happiness of his people. It sets out rules for fair wages, punishments for officials who cheat and help for orphans and the old. So readers find both cold strategy and real concern for good government in it.

Who was the real Chanakya?

No inscription from his own time names him. The stories about his life come from Buddhist, Jain and Hindu texts written much later, and they disagree about details. A famous Sanskrit play, Mudrarakshasa, shows him outwitting a rival minister, but it was written many centuries after him.

Some scholars think Chanakya and Kautilya may have been joined into one figure over time. The book itself mentions earlier teachers and seems to have grown in stages. So the man is partly history and partly legend.

Chanakya today

His name is everywhere in India. A collection of short sayings called Chanakya Niti is quoted in homes and offices, though it was put together long after him. In Delhi, the area where many embassies stand is called Chanakyapuri, a fitting name for a place full of diplomats.

TV serials and films have made him a hero of clever strategy. Students of politics around the world still read the Arthashastra as one of the oldest guides to statecraft.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Chanakya wrote every word of the Arthashastra exactly as we read it today.

Truth: Tradition credits him, but the text we have seems to have been edited and added to over a long time. Many scholars think it took its final form a few centuries after the Mauryas.
