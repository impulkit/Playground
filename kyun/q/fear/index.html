What happens when you are scared?

When something startles you, your body can start reacting before your thinking brain has even worked out what the danger is.

The short answer

Fear is your body's alarm system. When you sense danger, such as a loud bang, a growling dog or a sudden drop, a part of your brain called the amygdala sounds the alarm. Within moments, your body gets ready to act. Your heart pounds, you breathe faster, your muscles tense and your senses sharpen.

This response helped our ancestors survive. Someone who froze or ran at the first sign of a lion was more likely to live long enough to have children. Today, the same system kicks in before an exam or a scary film. It isn't always helpful, but it is completely normal, and there are good ways to calm it down.

Surprising but true

A woman known to scientists as S.M., whose amygdalae were damaged by a rare disease, feels almost no fear, even when she holds a live snake.

The fast road and the slow road

Your eyes and ears send signals to a relay station deep in the brain. From there, the signals take two paths. One goes straight to the amygdala, quickly and roughly. The other goes up to the outer layer of the brain, where you think things through, which is slower but more accurate.

The scientist Joseph LeDoux called these the "low road" and the "high road". The low road lets you jump back from a curved stick before you even know whether it's a snake. A split second later, the high road catches up and tells you it's just a stick, and you feel a little silly. It's better to be silly than bitten.

What your body does

Once the alarm sounds, your nervous system and glands get to work. Glands on top of your kidneys release adrenaline, a hormone that speeds up your heart and breathing. Your liver releases sugar into the blood for extra energy. Your pupils widen to let in more light.

Blood is sent to your arm and leg muscles and away from your stomach, which is why fear can feel like butterflies or a knot in your belly. You might sweat, shake or get goosebumps. In 1915, the American scientist Walter Cannon called this the "fight or flight" response. Scientists now add a third option: freeze, staying very still and hoping not to be noticed.

What is courage?Brave people feel fear just like everyone else. Courage is what they do while their heart is still pounding.

Born scared, or taught?

Babies seem to come ready to fear a few things, such as loud noises and falling. In a famous 1960 experiment called the visual cliff, crawling babies were placed on a glass table with what looked like a sudden drop underneath. Most would not crawl over the "edge", even when their mothers called them.

Many other fears are learned. In experiments, young monkeys raised in a lab weren't afraid of snakes at first. After watching a video of another monkey reacting with terror to a snake, they quickly became afraid too. Interestingly, they did not learn to fear flowers the same way, which suggests our brains are primed to learn some fears faster than others.

A life without fear

Scientists learned a lot from a woman known as S.M. A rare disease damaged both sides of her amygdala. Researchers took her to a pet shop with snakes and spiders, to a "haunted house" and showed her scary films. She stayed calm and curious, and even tried to touch a snake.

It sounds like a superpower, but it isn't. S.M. has walked into dangerous situations that most people would avoid. Fear, uncomfortable as it is, keeps us safe.

Taming the alarm

Sometimes fear rings too loudly, or at things that aren't really dangerous. A fear that's very strong and hard to control, like a terror of lifts or dogs, is called a phobia. Psychologists often help people face such fears in small, safe steps until the alarm quietens down.

For everyday fears, slow breathing helps, especially breathing out for longer than you breathe in. Naming the feeling ("I'm nervous") can calm the amygdala a little. Talking to a trusted adult helps too. And some people enjoy a safe scare, on a roller coaster or with a ghost story, because the rush is followed by relief.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Brave people don't feel fear.

Truth: Brave people feel fear too. Courage means acting well even though you are scared. Firefighters, soldiers and astronauts train hard so that they can think clearly while their bodies are full of fear.

Try this

Practise "box breathing", a trick some pilots and athletes use. Breathe in slowly through your nose while you count to four, hold for four, breathe out for four and wait for four. Do it four times, then notice whether your heartbeat feels slower. It's a handy tool before a test or a match.
