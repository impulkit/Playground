How do some people swim in icy water?

In 2007, a swimmer crossed 1 km of open sea at the North Pole in water colder than freezing, and came out alive.

The short answer

A few people can swim in water close to freezing because they train slowly for years and their bodies learn to cope. Their first gasp of shock gets smaller. Their blood vessels clamp down harder to save heat for the heart and brain. Many also carry a bit of extra body fat that works like a wetsuit.

They also follow strict rules. They swim for short times, with boats and doctors nearby, and they know the warning signs of danger. For anyone else, icy water is extremely dangerous. This is a sport for trained adults, never something to try alone.

Surprising but true

Sea water can stay liquid below 0 °C because salt lowers its freezing point, so polar swimmers can be in water at about minus 1.7 °C.

The first minute is the scariest

When cold water hits your skin, your body panics. You gasp without meaning to. Your breathing races, your heart pounds and your blood pressure jumps. This is called cold shock. If your head is under water at that moment, you can breathe in water.

Lifeguards teach the 1-10-1 rule. About 1 minute to get your breathing under control. About 10 minutes of useful movement before your arm and leg muscles get too cold to work. Up to about 1 hour before the body's core cools enough to cause dangerous hypothermia. The numbers are rough guides, but the message is clear: stay calm, float first.

How bodies get used to cold

Researchers at the University of Portsmouth in England found that after just a handful of short, careful dips, people's gasp response became noticeably weaker. Regular cold swimmers often shiver less and feel less panic.

Their blood vessels near the skin squeeze shut quickly, so warm blood stays deep inside. Some may also have more brown fat, a special kind of fat that burns energy to make heat. Babies have lots of it. A layer of ordinary fat under the skin helps too, the way blubber keeps a seal or a penguin warm in polar seas.

Swims that sound impossible

In 1987, American swimmer Lynne Cox swam about 4 km across the Bering Strait, from an Alaskan island to one belonging to the Soviet Union, in water of around 4 °C. It was a swim between two rival countries during the Cold War, and leaders of both praised it.

In 2007, British swimmer Lewis Pugh swam 1 km at the North Pole, in a gap in the sea ice. The water was about minus 1.7 °C. It stays liquid below zero because salt water freezes at a lower temperature than fresh water. In 2015, Indian swimmer Bhakti Sharma swam about 2.25 km in Antarctic waters close to 1 °C. It took her about 41 minutes.

The hidden danger afterwards

Getting out is not the end. Swimmers can keep cooling for several minutes after leaving the water, as cold blood from the arms and legs flows back to the core. This is called afterdrop. Ice swimmers dress fast in warm layers and sip warm drinks, but avoid very hot showers right away, which can make them faint.

Signs of serious cold include confused speech, clumsy hands and shivering that suddenly stops. Ice swimming clubs make sure everyone watches for them. Many people swear a short cold dip lifts their mood, and scientists are still testing why. For now, the safest way to enjoy cold water is with an adult, a lifeguard and a warm towel waiting. Some animals have gone much further than any human. One small North American frog lets most of its body freeze solid in winter. In spring it thaws out and hops away, a trick explained in the story of the wood frog. The body's own heater, and why 37 °C matters so much, is just as clever.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ice swimmers are just people who don't feel the cold.

Truth: They feel it hard. Through slow training their bodies react less wildly, but they still risk hypothermia, which is why they swim for short times, with safety boats and support.

Try this

Make a blubber glove. Put a spoonful of ghee or vegetable shortening inside a zip-lock bag, and slide a second bag inside it so your hand stays clean. Put that hand and your bare hand into a bowl of cold water with ice cubes for a few seconds and feel the difference.
