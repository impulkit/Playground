What is the solar wind?

The Sun is always blowing a wind into space, and it loses about a million tonnes of itself to that wind every second.

The short answer

The solar wind is a stream of electrically charged particles that flows out from the Sun in every direction, all the time. It is mostly protons and electrons, the tiny pieces that atoms are made of. It comes from the Sun's outer atmosphere, the corona, which is so hot, over a million degrees, that the Sun's gravity can't hold on to all of it.

The wind usually travels at about 400 km per second, and fast streams can reach 800 km per second or more. At those speeds, it reaches Earth in about two to four days.

It is extremely thin. Near Earth, there are only a few particles in each cubic centimetre. Yet it shapes the tails of comets, lights up the auroras and fills a giant bubble around the whole solar system.

Surprising but true

NASA's Parker Solar Probe, which flies through the solar wind close to the Sun, was the first NASA spacecraft named after a living person.

A wind nobody could see

In the 1950s, a German astronomer named Ludwig Biermann noticed something odd about comets. Their gas tails always pointed away from the Sun, and sometimes rippled as if blown. He suggested the Sun was pushing out a steady stream of particles.

In 1958, an American physicist, Eugene Parker, worked out the maths and called it the "solar wind". Many scientists didn't believe him, and his paper was nearly rejected. In 1962, NASA's Mariner 2 spacecraft, on its way to Venus, measured the wind directly. Parker was right.

In 2018, NASA launched the Parker Solar Probe, which flies closer to the Sun than any spacecraft before it. It was the first NASA spacecraft named after a living person. Parker, then 91, watched the launch. He died in 2022.

Earth's invisible shield

Earth has a magnetic field, made by molten iron swirling deep inside the planet. It acts like a shield, steering most of the solar wind around us.

Some particles leak in near the North and South Poles. They crash into gases high in the air and make them glow, creating the aurora, the northern and southern lights.

Mars has no global magnetic field. NASA's MAVEN spacecraft has found that the solar wind strips gas from the top of the Martian atmosphere. Over billions of years, this helped turn Mars from a world with rivers into a cold, dry desert.

Space weather

Sometimes the Sun throws out a huge cloud of particles called a coronal mass ejection. When one hits Earth, it can cause a geomagnetic storm. In March 1989, a storm like this caused a blackout across the Canadian province of Quebec, leaving millions of people without power for about nine hours.

Storms like these can damage satellites, confuse GPS and disturb radio signals. Astronauts on the space station have to be careful too. NOAA's Space Weather Prediction Center in the USA issues warnings, a bit like a weather forecast for space. India's Aditya-L1 observatory, sitting about 1.5 million km from Earth towards the Sun, carries instruments that measure the solar wind before it reaches us.

The bubble around the Sun

The solar wind blows outwards until it runs into the thin gas between the stars. The bubble it carves out is called the heliosphere, and it surrounds all the planets.

In 2012, Voyager 1 became the first spacecraft to cross its edge, about 18 billion km from the Sun. Voyager 2 followed in 2018. Both are now sending back measurements from interstellar space, beyond the reach of the wind that has blown past every planet.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Space between the planets is completely empty.

Truth: It is far emptier than any vacuum made on Earth, but it is filled with the thin, fast solar wind, along with dust and magnetic fields. There are usually a few particles in every cubic centimetre near Earth.

Try this

Hang a few strips of tissue paper from a pencil and hold it in front of a fan on low, then walk slowly around the fan in a circle. Notice that the strips always point away from the fan, whichever way you are walking, just like a comet's tail in the solar wind.
