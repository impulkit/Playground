Did badminton begin in India?

British officers played an early form of badminton in Pune, but the game is named after a grand house in England.

The short answer

Partly. Badminton as we know it took shape in the 19th century, among British army officers stationed in India and among wealthy families in England. The officers played a version in Poona, now Pune, and the game was even called "Poona" for a while.

The name badminton comes from Badminton House, a country estate of the Duke of Beaufort in England, where the game was played in the 1870s. People had been hitting shuttlecocks back and forth with paddles for centuries before either.

Surprising but true

A badminton shuttlecock can leave the racket at over 500 km/h, making the smash the fastest shot in any racket sport.

An ancient pastime

Games with a feathered shuttle are very old. In China, people played jianzi, kicking a weighted shuttle with their feet, for over 2,000 years. In Europe, children played battledore and shuttlecock, keeping a shuttle in the air with small wooden bats.

Similar games were played in India and Japan. What none of these had was a net, a court and a way to score points against an opponent.

Poona and Badminton House

The idea of a net seems to have come together among British officers in India in the 1860s. Rules for the game were drawn up in Poona around the 1870s, and players took it back to England.

At about the same time the Duke of Beaufort's family and guests played it at Badminton House. Visitors called it "the Badminton game", and the name stuck. In 1893 the Badminton Association of England published official rules. The first All England Championships followed in 1899.

A feather rocket

A competition shuttle has 16 goose or duck feathers fixed into a rounded cork base. The feathers overlap slightly, like the blades of a fan, which makes the shuttle spin as it flies.

The shape gives it a strange flight. A hard smash leaves the racket at enormous speed, but the feathers create so much drag that it slows sharply. In 2023, India's Satwiksairaj Rankireddy set a Guinness World Record with a smash measured at 565 km/h. That is faster than a Formula One car, yet the shuttle reaches the other side of the court quite gently. It also always flips to fly cork-first. The feathers act a little like those on an arrow, and much like the flight feathers that steady birds in the air.

India's badminton stars

In 1980 Prakash Padukone became the first Indian to win the All England Championships, then the most important tournament in the sport. Pullela Gopichand won it in 2001 and later opened an academy in Hyderabad that trained many of India's top players.

Saina Nehwal won India's first Olympic badminton medal, a bronze in London in 2012. P. V. Sindhu won silver in Rio in 2016 and bronze in Tokyo, becoming one of India's most successful Olympians. In 2022 India's men won the Thomas Cup, the world team championship, for the first time.

A backyard game and an Olympic one

Badminton is the rare sport that works both in a garden with no lines and in a packed indoor arena. Across India and Asia, families play it on rooftops and streets in the evening, while in China, Indonesia, Denmark and Japan it is a major professional sport.

It joined the Olympics in 1992. Professional matches are played indoors, because the lightest breeze can push a shuttle off course. Sports scientists say top players can change direction hundreds of times in a single match, while most people in the crowd are just trying to follow the shuttle.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Badminton was invented in India.

Truth: British officers in India helped shape the modern rules in the 1860s and 1870s. But people in China, Japan, India, Greece and Europe had played shuttlecock games for many centuries before that.

Try this

Drop a shuttlecock and a small ball from the same height and watch which lands first. Then drop the shuttle feathers-down. It flips over and falls cork-first every time, because of where its weight sits.
