Why do countries trade?

In 1956, a trucking businessman loaded 58 metal boxes onto a ship in New Jersey. That idea, the shipping container, changed how the whole world shops.

The short answer

Countries trade because no country can make everything it needs well and cheaply. India grows tea, writes software and makes medicines, but it does not have enough oil. Saudi Arabia has plenty of oil but little farmland. By trading, each gets things it could not easily make at home.

Trade also lets countries focus on what they do best. If one country is very good at making cars and another at growing coffee, both can end up with more cars and more coffee by specialising and swapping. That idea is called comparative advantage, and it is one of the oldest in economics.

Surprising but true

India imports nearly 90% of the crude oil it uses (2024–25), mostly by sea, because it does not produce nearly enough of its own.

Comparative advantage

In 1817, the British economist David Ricardo used a famous example. England and Portugal could both make cloth and wine. Ricardo showed that even if Portugal were better at making both, each country gained by doing more of what it did relatively best and trading for the rest.

Think of a cricket team. Your best batter might also be a decent bowler, but the team does better if she concentrates on batting and leaves bowling to others. Countries are similar.

Old roads, old ships

Trade is far older than countries as we know them. Thousands of years ago, the people of the Indus Valley traded beads and cotton with Mesopotamia. Silk, porcelain and paper moved west along the Silk Road, while horses, glass and silver moved east.

What was the Silk Road?Silk from China reached Rome, and Buddhism reached China, along roads that almost nobody travelled from end to end.

India's coasts were famous for spices, especially black pepper from Kerala. The search for a sea route to the spice markets drove Vasco da Gama to sail around Africa to Kozhikode in 1498.

The box that changed everything

Until the 1950s, ships were loaded by hand, sack by sack and crate by crate. It took days and cost a lot. In 1956, the American businessman Malcom McLean sent a ship called the Ideal X from New Jersey to Texas carrying 58 truck-sized metal boxes. Containers could be lifted straight from ship to lorry to train.

Standard containers cut the cost of shipping dramatically. Today, giant container ships, some longer than three football fields, carry most of the world's manufactured goods, and about 80% of world trade by volume moves by sea. A phone you buy may contain parts from a dozen countries before it is put together.

India and America

India exports software and IT services, medicines, refined fuels, jewellery, rice and textiles. India makes a large share of the world's generic medicines, and is sometimes called a "pharmacy of the world". It imports crude oil, gold, electronics and machinery. The United States is one of India's biggest trading partners, buying Indian software services and medicines and selling aircraft, machinery and energy.

Rules and arguments

The World Trade Organization, set up in 1995, writes rules for trade between its members and settles disputes. Countries still argue constantly. Some put taxes called tariffs on imports to protect their own farmers or factories. Supporters say tariffs save jobs. Critics say they raise prices for everyone and invite other countries to hit back.

Most economists think open trade has helped lift hundreds of millions of people out of poverty. They also recognise that when cheap imports arrive, some factory towns lose jobs, and those people need help to find new work. How to balance these is one of the oldest debates in politics, and one that markets alone do not settle.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In trade, one country always wins and the other loses.

Truth: When both sides trade freely, each usually gains, because each gets something it values more than what it gave. But within a country, some workers and businesses can lose out when cheaper imports arrive.

Try this

Pick five things in your home, like a toy, a phone charger, a packet of tea and a T-shirt, and look for "Made in" on the label or box. Mark each country on a map. Then think about which of them could easily have been made in your own country, and which could not.
