How did the Cholas build the Brihadeeswara temple?

A thousand years ago, without cranes or steel, Chola builders raised a granite tower as tall as a 20-storey building in Thanjavur.

The short answer

In about 1010 CE, the Chola king Rajaraja I finished a temple to Shiva in his capital, Thanjavur. Its tower, called a vimana, rises about 66 m. For centuries it was one of the tallest buildings in India.

The builders used granite, a stone so hard that steel tools blunt on it. They had no cranes, no engines and no steel girders. What they had were thousands of workers, elephants, ropes, wooden rollers and very clever ideas about stacking stone so that gravity holds it together.

Surprising but true

The nearest good granite is roughly 50 km from Thanjavur, so almost every stone in the temple was dragged across the flat Kaveri delta.

A king with big plans

The Cholas ruled much of South India, and their ships sailed as far as Southeast Asia. Rajaraja wanted a temple that showed this power. He named it Rajarajeswaram. Today most people call it the Brihadeeswara, or "big lord", temple.

Its walls carry long inscriptions in Tamil. They list gifts of gold, land and cattle. They even name about 400 temple dancers, along with the house and share of land each one received. It is like finding a pay sheet from a thousand years ago.

Moving a mountain of stone

No granite hills sit next to Thanjavur, which lies in the flat, rich delta of the Kaveri river. Most historians think the stone came from quarries around 50 km away.

Workers probably split blocks by cutting rows of holes and hammering in wedges. Then they dragged them on wooden rollers or sledges, pulled by people and elephants. Some blocks weigh many tonnes, heavier than a loaded autorickshaw.

Guides often say the temple holds more than 1 lakh tonnes of granite. Nobody weighed it, so treat that as a rough figure. Even so, if each sledge carried a few tonnes, that means tens of thousands of trips. Most accounts say the building took only about seven years, so huge crowds of people and animals must have been hauling at once.

Up, up and up

The tower is hollow inside for much of its height. It narrows as it climbs, in 13 storeys, like a stepped pyramid. Each layer of stone presses down on the one below and locks it in place. In most places, no mortar was used.

The biggest puzzle is the capstone. It is often said to weigh about 80 tonnes, about as heavy as 13 elephants. A local story says workers built an earth ramp about 6 km long, starting at a village now called Sarapallam, "the scaffold's hollow". The stone was then hauled up the slope. Nobody has found firm proof of the ramp, but it is a sensible guess. Others suggest levers, wooden scaffolds and slow lifting, a few centimetres at a time.

Giants inside the gates

A huge Nandi, the bull who carries Shiva, sits facing the shrine. It is carved from one stone, about 5 m long. Inside, the linga stands about 3.7 m tall, taller than two grown-ups.

The walls hold carved dancers showing the poses of Bharatanatyam. Chola painters also covered inner walls with murals, which were hidden under later paint until scholars found them in the 20th century. Underneath a layer from the 1600s, Rajaraja's own painters had been waiting all along, with Shiva, dancers and the king himself.

Still alive after 1,000 years

Unlike many ancient buildings, Brihadeeswara is still a working temple. Prayers have been said there for a thousand years. It has survived earthquakes, wars and centuries of heavy rain.

In 1954, it appeared on India's ₹1,000 note. In 2010, its 1,000th birthday was marked with a special coin. And the same Chola workshops produced something just as surprising in metal: the dancing Shiva statues of Chola bronze casting.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The shadow of Brihadeeswara's tower never falls on the ground at noon.

Truth: It does. Anyone can see the tower's shadow on the courtyard on a sunny day. The story is popular, but the Sun obeys the same rules in Thanjavur as everywhere else.
