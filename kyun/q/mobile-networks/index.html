How does a mobile network connect your call?

When you call someone across India, your voice leaps by radio to a tower, races through cables, and finds one phone among more than a billion.

The short answer

A mobile network divides land into many small areas called cells, each served by a tower with antennas, called a base station. Your phone talks by radio to the nearest tower.

From the tower, your call travels through cables to the network's central computers. These look up where the person you're calling is, then send your call to the tower nearest them. That tower passes it by radio to their phone. All of this happens in about a second, which is why the phone rings almost as soon as you dial.

If you move while talking, your call is handed from one tower to the next without dropping.

Surprising but true

The first call on a handheld mobile phone was made in New York in 1973 by Martin Cooper of Motorola. He rang a rival engineer at another company to tell him.

Cells like a honeycomb

Engineers often draw a mobile network as a map of hexagons, like a honeycomb, with a tower in each. Real cells are messier, shaped by hills, buildings and roads, but the idea is the same. Each cell uses radio frequencies that are different from its neighbours', so calls don't interfere.

This lets frequencies be reused again and again across a country. In crowded cities, cells are small, sometimes just a few hundred metres across, so each tower serves fewer people. In the countryside, one tower can cover many kilometres.

Why are honeycomb cells hexagons?Bees don't know geometry. But they build the best shape anyway.

Finding the right phone

Your SIM card holds a code that identifies you to the network. Every so often, even when you're not using it, your phone quietly tells nearby towers it's there. The network keeps a record of which area each phone is in.

When someone calls you, the network checks that record and asks the towers in your area to call your phone. Your phone answers, and the call is connected. For a call to another network or another country, the call passes between networks through exchanges and cables.

Voice turned into numbers

Your voice is turned into a stream of numbers by your phone. The phone squeezes this data to make it smaller, then sends it in tiny bursts by radio. At the other end, the numbers are turned back into sound.

This happens fast enough that the delay is usually too short to notice. Today, calls, messages and internet data all travel the same way, as packets of digital information.

From 1G to 5G

The first mobile networks, in the 1980s, carried voices as analogue radio signals. They were called 1G later on. Phones were large and expensive. The second generation, 2G, in the 1990s, was digital and brought text messages. 3G and 4G brought fast internet, so phones could show maps and videos. 5G, spreading through the 2020s, is faster again and can connect many more devices at once.

Before all this, phones used wires, as you can read in the history of the telephone.

Calling across India

India's first mobile phone call was made on 31 July 1995. Jyoti Basu, then chief minister of West Bengal, called the telecom minister, Sukh Ram, in Delhi from Writers' Building in Kolkata. Calls were costly at first, and mobile phones were a luxury.

Prices fell sharply over the next two decades. Today India has more than a billion mobile connections, and mobile data is among the cheapest in the world. Towers stretch from Himalayan villages to islands in the Bay of Bengal. In places no tower reaches, such as ships at sea, people rely on satellites instead.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Most phone calls travel through satellites in space.

Truth: Most calls go by radio only to the nearest tower. From there they travel through cables, mostly fibre-optic, across land and under the sea. Satellites are used mainly in remote places, at sea and in the air.

Try this

Draw a map of your town or neighbourhood and cover it with a pattern of hexagons, like a honeycomb. Put a dot for a "tower" in the middle of each. Now trace a route for a car journey and mark each point where the car's phone would hand over from one tower to the next.
