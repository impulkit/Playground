What was Carthage?

Carthage was a sea-trading city in North Africa that nearly beat Rome, once with an army that marched elephants over the Alps.

The short answer

Carthage was a city on the coast of North Africa, in what is now Tunisia. It was started by sailors from Tyre, a port in today's Lebanon, traditionally in 814 BCE. These sailors were Phoenicians, famous traders who carried goods all around the Mediterranean Sea.

Carthage grew rich and powerful. Its ships sailed to Spain, Sicily and beyond, and it ruled a trading empire for hundreds of years.

Then it ran into another rising power across the sea: Rome. The two fought three long wars, called the Punic Wars. In 146 BCE the Romans captured Carthage and burned it.

Surprising but true

Carthage's round war harbour had an island in the middle with sheds for well over a hundred warships, and the admiral could watch the whole fleet from a tower.

A city built on a story

Romans told a famous legend about how it began. A princess named Elissa, also called Dido, fled Tyre and landed in Africa. A local king said she could have as much land as an ox hide could cover.

Elissa cut the hide into very thin strips and laid them end to end around a whole hill. That hill, the Byrsa, became the heart of Carthage.

Nobody knows if any of this happened. What archaeologists can say is that people were living at Carthage by the 700s BCE, which fits the old tradition quite well.

Merchants and sailors

Carthage made its money from the sea. It traded tin and silver from Spain, grain from African farms and a costly purple dye made from sea snails. Its people spoke Punic, a form of the Phoenician language, and wrote with the Phoenician alphabet. A Carthaginian captain named Hanno is said to have sailed far down the west coast of Africa, around 500 BCE.

The city had two linked harbours. The outer one was for merchant ships. The inner one was round, with an island in the middle. Around its edges and on the island stood sheds for well over a hundred warships, and from a tower in the centre the admiral could see them all. You can still see the outline of that round harbour beside the sea today.

Hannibal and the elephants

The most famous Carthaginian is a general named Hannibal. In 218 BCE, during the Second Punic War, he marched an army from Spain, over the Pyrenees and then across the snowy Alps into Italy. He brought war elephants with him.

Many soldiers and most of the elephants died on the way. Even so, Hannibal beat the Romans in battle after battle. At Cannae in 216 BCE he surrounded and crushed a much larger Roman army.

Which elephants did he use? Historians think most were a smaller kind of African elephant that once lived in North Africa and has since died out there. A Roman writer says Hannibal's bravest elephant was called Surus, which may mean "the Syrian", so it might have been an Asian elephant.

He never captured Rome itself, though. The Romans sent an army to attack Carthage, and Hannibal had to hurry home. In 202 BCE a Roman general named Scipio defeated him at Zama.

The end, and after

Carthage lost its empire but kept trading and grew wealthy again. Some Romans could not stand that. A senator called Cato the Elder was said to end his speeches with the words "Carthage must be destroyed."

In 149 BCE Rome attacked. After a siege of about three years, the city fell in 146 BCE. Most of the survivors were sold into slavery.

The story did not end there. Roughly a hundred years later the Romans built a new Carthage on the same spot, and it became one of the largest cities in Roman Africa. Its ruins now sit among the houses of a seaside suburb of Tunis, where archaeologists still dig.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Romans ploughed salt into the ground at Carthage so that nothing would grow there again.

Truth: No ancient writer says this. The story seems to have appeared about two thousand years later, in the 1800s and 1900s. The Romans did burn the city, but they soon farmed the land around it and later rebuilt the city itself.
