What kinds of homes do people live in around the world?

In an Australian opal-mining town, many people live underground in homes dug into the rock, and stay cool while it is over 40 °C outside.

The short answer

People build homes from whatever is nearby and whatever the weather demands. In the Arctic, it has been snow. In the Mongolian grasslands, felt and wooden poles. In the Assam floodplains, bamboo raised on stilts. In the Thar desert, mud and cow dung that keep rooms cool.

There are homes that float, homes that move, homes underground and homes hanging off cliffs. Many look strange to outsiders, but each one is a clever answer to a local problem. In crowded cities, most people now live in flats and apartments, in towers that can hold more people than a whole village.

Surprising but true

Luke Skywalker's childhood home in the first Star Wars film was a real underground house hotel in Matmata, Tunisia.

Homes under the ground

In Coober Pedy, a small town in the Australian outback, summer days often go above 40 °C. Many people there live in "dugouts", homes carved into the hillside rock. The rock keeps the inside around 23 to 25 °C all year, with no air conditioner. The town even has underground churches and a hotel.

In Matmata, Tunisia, families dug pit homes into the ground, with rooms opening onto a round courtyard. One was used as Luke Skywalker's home in the first Star Wars film in 1977, and fans still visit. Underground homes are among the oldest ideas in housing, a story that runs back to the first shelters humans ever made.

Homes that move

In Mongolia, many herding families live in a ger, a round tent of felt stretched over a wooden frame. A family can take it down, load it onto a few animals or a truck, and put it up again in a few hours. The door traditionally faces south, away from cold winds.

In India, the Raika camel herders of Rajasthan and the Gaddi shepherds of the Himalaya move with their animals and use light shelters on the road. The life of India's nomads follows the seasons.

How do people live in the Thar Desert?Very little rain, scorching summers, and yet the Thar is one of the most crowded deserts on Earth.

Homes on water

On Dal Lake in Srinagar, hundreds of carved wooden houseboats float near the shore. A story often told is that, under Dogra rule, British visitors weren't allowed to buy land in Kashmir, so they built homes on the water instead. Many houseboats now take tourists.

In Kerala, the kettuvallam, once a rice barge, has become a floating holiday home. The Bajau people of South-East Asia have lived on boats and stilt houses over the sea for centuries. Some Bajau divers can hold their breath for minutes at a time while fishing.

Snow, bamboo and barrel huts

The igloo is famous, but it was mainly a short-stay shelter. Inuit hunters could build one from blocks of packed snow in an hour or two. Snow is full of tiny air pockets, so it traps heat well.

In Assam, the Mising people build chang ghar, bamboo houses on stilts, to stay dry when the Brahmaputra floods. In the Nilgiri hills, the Toda people built barrel-shaped huts of bamboo and grass with a door so small you crawl in, keeping heat inside.

Clever cooling

Before electric fans, homes in hot places used tricks. Thick mud walls soak up heat in the day and release it at night. Courtyards pull in breezes. In old Gujarat and Rajasthan houses, jaalis, carved stone screens, let air in while blocking sun. Some homes hang wet cloths in doorways, so moving air cools as water dries, the same science as evaporation cooling.

Clay, mud and burnt brick remain among the world's most common walls. Fired bricks were already being used in the cities of the Indus Valley more than 4,000 years ago.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Inuit people always lived in igloos all year round.

Truth: Igloos were mostly used as temporary winter shelters, especially while travelling or hunting. Many Inuit families lived in houses made of stone, earth and whale bone, or in skin tents in summer. Today most live in ordinary modern houses.
