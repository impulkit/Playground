Where does sugar come from?

The word "sugar" comes from Sanskrit, because Indians were the first people to turn sugarcane juice into crystals.

The short answer

Most sugar comes from two plants: sugarcane, a giant grass that grows in warm places, and sugar beet, a pale root grown in cool countries. Like all green plants, they make sugar from sunlight, water and air through photosynthesis. They simply store much more of it than most plants.

At a sugar mill, cane is crushed to squeeze out its juice. The juice is cleaned, boiled down until it is thick and syrupy, and spun in machines so that sugar crystals separate from the dark syrup, called molasses. Further cleaning turns brown sugar into white. The same white sugar, sucrose, can come from cane or beet.

Surprising but true

When Alexander the Great's army reached India in 326 BCE, his officers wrote about a reed that made honey without bees.

The first sugar crystals

Sugarcane was first farmed in New Guinea, about 8,000 years ago, and spread slowly to South and Southeast Asia. People chewed the canes for their sweet juice.

India was the first place where people learned to boil cane juice and make it into solid sugar that could be stored and traded. Many historians date this to around the Gupta period, about 1,600 years ago, though some say earlier. Indian traders sold it far and wide. The English word sugar comes from the Sanskrit sharkara, through Persian and Arabic, and many other languages have similar borrowed words.

Gur, khand and mishri

India still makes plenty of unrefined sugar. Jaggery, or gur, is cane juice boiled in big open pans until it sets into golden or brown blocks. It keeps some of the cane's minerals and molasses, which give it a deep, toffee-like taste.

Khand is a coarse, partly refined sugar, and mishri is large, clear crystal sugar often offered at temples. In Uttar Pradesh and Maharashtra, India's biggest cane-growing states, the crushing season fills villages with the smell of boiling juice.

Sugar's dark history

From the 1500s onwards, European countries set up huge sugar plantations in Brazil and the Caribbean. The work of cutting cane and boiling juice was brutally hard. Millions of Africans were taken by force and enslaved to do it.

After slavery was abolished in the British Empire in the 1830s, plantation owners recruited workers from India on harsh contracts, called indenture. Over the next 80 years, more than a million Indians went to places like Mauritius, Fiji, Trinidad and Guyana, many to work on sugar estates. Their descendants form large communities there today.

Sugar from a root

In 1747, a German chemist named Andreas Marggraf found that beetroots contained the same sugar as cane. The first beet sugar factory opened in 1801 in what is now Poland. Beet sugar meant cool countries could make their own sugar, and today a large share of Europe's sugar comes from beet.

Why is sugar bad for teeth?Sugar doesn't rot your teeth by itself. Tiny bacteria living on your teeth eat it and spit out acid.

How much is too much

Sugar gives quick energy, but bacteria in your mouth love it too. They turn it into acid that eats into tooth enamel, as you can see in why sugar harms teeth.

The World Health Organization advises that added sugars should make up less than a tenth of the energy a person eats in a day, and ideally less than a twentieth. A single can of a sugary fizzy drink can hold about seven teaspoons of sugar or more. Fruit has natural sugars too, but it comes with fibre and water, which make it far harder to overeat.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Eating sugar makes children hyperactive.

Truth: Careful studies, where neither children nor parents knew who had eaten sugar, found no difference in behaviour. Parents who believed their child had eaten sugar were more likely to describe them as hyper. Too much sugar is still bad for teeth and health.

Try this

Stir as much sugar as will dissolve into half a glass of room-temperature water. Pour a little onto a dark saucer and leave it on a sunny windowsill for several days. As the water evaporates, small sugar crystals will start to form. Look at them with a magnifying glass.
