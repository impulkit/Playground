Who gets to name a dinosaur?

One dinosaur is named after a school in Harry Potter, another after an irritated scientist, and India has one named after Rabindranath Tagore.

The short answer

The scientists who first describe a new kind of dinosaur get to name it. They must publish a careful description in a scientific journal. They also pick one particular fossil to be the "name-bearer", the specimen that the name will always belong to.

Names follow an international rule book. Every species gets two parts, like Tyrannosaurus rex or Rajasaurus narmadensis. The first part names the group, the second the species. Within the rules, the scientists can choose almost anything: a description, a place, a person or even a joke.

Surprising but true

The word "dinosaur" is younger than the first dinosaur bones found in India. It was invented in 1842.

The first names

The first dinosaur ever named was Megalosaurus, "great lizard", described by William Buckland in England in 1824. Back then, nobody had a word for the whole group. In 1842, the British anatomist Richard Owen grouped a few giant fossil reptiles together and called them Dinosauria. It meant "terrible lizards", in the sense of fearfully great.

That makes the word younger than India's first dinosaur find. Bones were dug up near Jabalpur in 1828, fourteen years before there was a word for them.

Rules for naming

Names are usually built from ancient Greek or Latin words, which scientists all over the world can share. Triceratops means "three-horned face". Velociraptor means "swift thief". The rule book is the International Code of Zoological Nomenclature, which covers every animal, from ants to whales.

The name-bearing fossil, called the holotype, is kept in a museum or university so that anyone can study it later. When someone digs up a similar skeleton, they compare it with the holotype to decide whether they have the same species or a new one. A name with no holotype behind it is like a label stuck on an empty box.

The code's most important rule is priority. If two people name the same animal, the first published name wins. During the Bone Wars of the 1870s and 1880s, two rival Americans rushed out so many names that some animals were named twice. One of those duplicates caused the most famous naming muddle of all.

Why do names have meanings?Almost every name was once an ordinary word. Aditya means sun, Smith meant a metal worker, and Johnson meant "son of John".

The Brontosaurus story

In 1877, Othniel Marsh named a long-necked dinosaur Apatosaurus. Two years later he named a bigger skeleton Brontosaurus, "thunder lizard". In 1903, another scientist decided they were the same kind of animal. Apatosaurus was published first, so Brontosaurus was officially dropped.

The public kept the name anyway. It appeared in films, books and even on a US postage stamp in 1989. Then, in 2015, a team compared dozens of long-necked skeletons in detail. They argued that Brontosaurus was different enough to be its own genus again. After more than a century, the thunder lizard was back.

Honouring people and places

Many names celebrate where a dinosaur was found. Rajasaurus narmadensis, "royal lizard from the Narmada", was named in 2003 from bones found in Gujarat. Barapasaurus tagorei, "big-leg lizard", honours the poet Rabindranath Tagore. It was dug up around his birth centenary.

Scientists try not to name dinosaurs after themselves, which is seen as bad manners. Naming one after a colleague or a hero is fine.

Names with a joke

Some names have stories behind them. Irritator, a fish-eating dinosaur from Brazil, got its name because fossil dealers had stuck extra plaster on its skull to make it look longer. Removing it irritated the scientists. Dracorex hogwartsia, "dragon king of Hogwarts", was named in 2006. Its skull belongs to the Children's Museum of Indianapolis, and young visitors helped suggest the name. And one armoured dinosaur from Montana is called Zuul, after a monster in the film Ghostbusters.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Brontosaurus never existed and was just a mistake.

Truth: For about a century, scientists thought Brontosaurus was the same animal as Apatosaurus, so the newer name was dropped. In 2015, a large study found enough differences to bring Brontosaurus back as its own animal. Some scientists still disagree.

Try this

Invent a name for a new dinosaur from your town. Use two parts, like real names. The first might describe it (such as "swift" or "horned") and the second might honour a place or person. Look up the Greek or Latin words in a dictionary, or make up a good reason for each part.
