Why do some people feel sick in cars?

Your eyes say you're sitting still. Your inner ear says you're swinging round a bend. Your brain can't decide, and your stomach suffers.

The short answer

Motion sickness happens when your senses send your brain messages that don't match. Your inner ear has a balance system that feels every turn, bump and swing of the car. But if you're looking at a phone or a book, your eyes tell your brain that you're sitting still.

The brain gets confused by this disagreement. Scientists aren't fully sure why, but the confusion sets off feelings of sickness: a sweaty face, yawning, a headache, a queasy stomach and sometimes vomiting. It can happen in cars, buses, boats, planes, on fairground rides and even while playing video games. Children between about 2 and 12 get it most.

Surprising but true

The word "nausea" comes from the Greek word "naus", meaning ship. People have been seasick for as long as they have sailed.

Your built-in balance system

Deep inside each ear, beyond the parts that hear, is the vestibular system. It has three tiny curved tubes filled with fluid, set at different angles. When your head turns, the fluid lags behind and bends tiny hair-like sensors. They tell your brain which way you're turning.

Two small sacs nearby contain tiny crystals that shift when you speed up, slow down or tilt. Together, these parts work like a motion sensor in a phone. Your brain combines their messages with what your eyes see and what your muscles and joints feel. Normally they all agree, and you barely notice your sense of balance at all.

When the senses disagree

In a car, your inner ear feels the swaying. If you look out of the front window, your eyes see the road curving too, and everything matches. But if you look down at a book, your eyes see a still page. Your brain gets two different stories.

One old idea suggests the body treats this confusion like a sign of poisoning, since some poisons can scramble the senses. Vomiting would get rid of the poison. It's a neat explanation, but scientists still argue about it.

Ghat roads and stormy seas

Winding mountain roads are hard on sensitive stomachs. Many Indian families know the feeling on hill roads in Himachal, the ghat roads up to Ooty or the twisting climb to Darjeeling. The constant left-right swing of hairpin bends is a strong trigger.

At sea, the slow up-and-down rolling of a boat is the classic cause. Many sailors feel sick for the first few days, then their brains adjust. When they get back on land, some feel as if the ground is still moving.

In space and in headsets

Astronauts get a version called space sickness. In space there's no "down", so the inner ear gives strange signals. More than half of astronauts feel ill during their first few days, before their brains adapt.

Virtual reality can cause the opposite problem. Your eyes see you flying or running, but your inner ear says you're standing still. That mismatch makes some people feel just as sick as in a car.

What helps

Looking out at the horizon helps, because it lets your eyes see the same movement your ears feel. Sitting where the motion is smallest helps too: in the middle of a boat, over the wing of a plane, or facing forwards in a car or train. Fresh air, small light meals before travelling and not reading on the move can all make a difference.

Some people find that ginger settles their stomach, and there's some evidence it helps a little. Doctors can suggest medicines for long trips, and it's best to ask a doctor or pharmacist before giving any to a child. Most children grow out of travel sickness as they get older.

There are also small tricks people swear by. Some listen to music or close their eyes. Others count telephone poles out of the window, which keeps their eyes on the real movement outside. On long bus journeys, sitting near the front, where the swing is smaller, can make the trip far easier.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Motion sickness is all in your head, and only weak people get it.

Truth: It's a real response of the brain and body that almost anyone can have in strong enough motion. Sailors, pilots and astronauts get it too. Some people are simply more sensitive than others.

Try this

Fill a clear bowl with water and drop in a few tiny bits of paper. Turn the bowl quickly a quarter turn and stop. Watch the water keep swirling after the bowl has stopped. The fluid in your inner ear does the same thing, which is why the room seems to spin after you twirl around.
