Why did Pokémon become so popular?

Pokémon began with a boy near Tokyo who spent his summers catching beetles, and grew into one of the world's biggest entertainment brands.

The short answer

Pokémon took off because it mixed three things children already loved: collecting, battling and trading with friends. You catch creatures, train them, and swap them with other players to fill your Pokédex, a kind of catalogue.

The first games came out in Japan in 1996 for Nintendo's Game Boy. Within a few years there was a cartoon, a trading card game, films and toys, each pulling fans into the others. Pokémon is often listed as the highest-earning media franchise in history, though such totals are rough estimates. The idea behind it began with a boy who loved bugs.

Surprising but true

Pikachu's tail has never had a black tip, even though millions of fans clearly remember one.

A boy and his beetles

Satoshi Tajiri grew up in Machida, a suburb of Tokyo, in the 1970s. He spent hours in fields and ponds catching insects, studying them and comparing them with friends. As the suburb grew, many of those fields were built over.

Tajiri later became a video game fan and started a game magazine, which grew into a studio called Game Freak. He wanted city children to feel the thrill he had felt as a collector. When he saw the Game Boy's link cable, he imagined creatures crawling along it from one player to another. It took his small team about six years to finish the first games.

Why do we need insects?Australia once had a cow dung problem so big that it shipped in beetles from other continents to clean it up.

Gotta catch 'em all

The two first versions, Red and Green, each had Pokémon the other was missing. That simple trick meant no one could finish alone. Children had to meet, talk and trade, and the game spread through playgrounds by word of mouth.

The creatures also had types, like water, fire and grass, which work a bit like rock, paper, scissors. Water beats fire, fire beats grass, and grass beats water. Easy to learn, but with 151 Pokémon there were endless combinations to argue over.

From Game Boy to TV screens

In 1997 an anime series started in Japan, following a boy called Satoshi (renamed Ash in English) and his Pikachu. It reached American TV in 1998, and the trading card game soon followed. Each part fed the others: the cartoon made you want the cards, and the cards sent you back to the game.

In India, Hindi-dubbed episodes ran on channels including Cartoon Network and later Hungama. For many Indian children, the cartoon came first, years before they ever touched a Game Boy.

Pokémon Go

In 2016, the company Niantic released Pokémon Go for phones. It used GPS and the phone's camera to place Pokémon in the real world, in parks, at bus stops and outside temples. Millions of people walked around their towns staring at their screens.

The game had problems too. Some players wandered into unsafe places or private property while chasing a rare creature, and some cities asked for game spots to be moved.

Why it lasted

Many crazes vanish after a few years. Pokémon kept adding new regions and new creatures, and there are now more than 1,000. Each new pair of games brings fresh creatures to learn, which gives older fans a reason to come back and younger ones a fair place to start. Parents who played Red and Green in the 1990s now play with their own children.

The heart of it has not changed much: a world full of creatures waiting to be found, and someone to show them to.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pikachu has a black tip on its tail.

Truth: It never has. Pikachu's ears are black-tipped, but its tail is yellow with a brown patch at the base. So many people remember it wrongly that it is often given as an example of a shared false memory.
