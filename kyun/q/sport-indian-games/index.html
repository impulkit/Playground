What traditional games do Indian children play?

Ludo, snakes and ladders and chess all began as Indian games, long before they came back to India in cardboard boxes.

The short answer

Indian children have played street and courtyard games for centuries, often with nothing more than a stick, a few stones or a chalk line. Some are team chasing games, like kho-kho and kabaddi. Others need a bit of gear: a stick and a wooden peg for gilli-danda, marbles for kancha, a spinning top called a lattu, or a stack of flat stones for lagori.

There are quieter games too. Board games like pachisi, chaupar and pallanguzhi were played on cloth, wood or floors scratched with chalk. Several Indian games travelled the world and came back as famous brands.

Pallanguzhi, popular in Tamil Nadu and Kerala, uses a wooden board with two rows of seven cups and a handful of seeds or shells. Players scoop and sow the seeds around the board, capturing as they go. It belongs to a family of counting games called mancala, played from Africa to Southeast Asia, and it quietly trains children to count and plan ahead.

Surprising but true

The Mughal emperor Akbar is said to have played pachisi on a giant board laid into a courtyard at Fatehpur Sikri, using people as the pieces.

Games that conquered the world

About 1,500 years ago, Indian players moved pieces representing an army's four parts, elephants, chariots, horses and foot soldiers, across a board. The game was chaturanga. It travelled to Persia and then Europe and slowly became modern chess.

Pachisi, played with cowrie shells as dice, travelled to England, where a simpler version was sold as Ludo from the 1890s. And gyan chaupar, also called mokshapat, became snakes and ladders. In the old boards, ladders rose from good deeds and snakes dropped from bad ones.

Run, chase, breathe

Kabaddi is a tag game where a raider runs into the other team's half while chanting "kabaddi, kabaddi" without taking a breath. Tag someone and get back safely, and you score. Since 2014, the Pro Kabaddi League has made it one of India's most-watched sports on TV.

In kho-kho, chasers sit in a line facing alternate directions. Only one can chase at a time, and they swap by tapping a teammate's back and shouting "kho". In January 2025, New Delhi hosted the first Kho Kho World Cup, and India won both the men's and women's titles.

What is gilli-danda?All you need is a stick and a smaller stick, and children across India have played this game for centuries, with dozens of names for it.

Stones, marbles and tops

Lagori goes by many names: pithoo in the north, lagori in Karnataka, satoliya in some places, seven stones in English. A team knocks over a pile of seven flat stones with a ball, then tries to rebuild it before being hit.

Kancha, or marbles, is played by flicking glass balls with a bent finger to strike others. Lattu are wooden tops wound with string and thrown so they spin on the ground. Good players can scoop a spinning lattu onto their palm. Stapu or kith-kith, a hopping game on chalk squares, is close to American hopscotch.

Why grandparents still remember them

Most of these games cost almost nothing. A stick from a tree or a few stones from a lane is enough. Rules change from one street to the next, and children argue about them as fiercely as umpires.

The government's Khelo India programme now includes traditional sports like mallakhamb and kalaripayattu, and some schools teach old games again. Kids in other countries play cousins of the same games, from tag to marbles to hopscotch, which you can see in playground games around the world. Even the emperor Akbar liked a good board game. At Fatehpur Sikri, a courtyard is laid out like a huge pachisi board, where he is said to have played using people as the pieces.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Traditional Indian games are dying out and nobody plays them anymore.

Truth: Many are thriving. Kabaddi has a hugely popular professional league, kho-kho got its first World Cup in 2025, and games like lagori, kancha and gilli-danda are still played in lanes and schoolyards across India.

Try this

Play lagori with friends in a safe open space. Stack seven flat stones or plastic lids, then take turns knocking the pile down with a soft ball. The thrower's team must rebuild the pile while the other team tries to tag them with the ball. Agree on the rules before you start.
