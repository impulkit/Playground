How is origami used in space?

The Webb space telescope's sunshield is the size of a tennis court. It flew into space folded up like a paper map.

The short answer

Spacecraft and satellites have a packing problem. A rocket's nose is only a few metres wide, but solar panels, sunshields and antennas often need to be much bigger. They have to travel folded and open out once they are in space.

Origami, the Japanese art of folding paper, turns out to be full of maths that solves this. Engineers study how creases meet, which folds open easily and which jam. Then they design panels that fold flat into a small bundle and spread out again with a single pull, with no tearing and no tangles.

Surprising but true

In 2002, a high-school student in California folded a single long sheet of paper in half 12 times, breaking the old "seven folds" rule.

The fold that opens in one move

Around 1980, the Japanese astrophysicist Koryo Miura worked out a clever pattern of slanted, zigzag creases. A sheet folded this way collapses into a neat little block. Pull two opposite corners and the whole thing springs open in one smooth motion. Push them together and it folds up again. The trick is that the creases are not at right angles. Each zigzag is slightly tilted, so all the panels move together like one piece.

It is now called the Miura fold. In 1995, Japan launched a science satellite called the Space Flyer Unit with a solar panel folded in this pattern. Some city maps in Japan are folded the same way, so they open with one tug instead of a wrestling match.

Webb's giant sunshield

The James Webb Space Telescope, launched in 2021, has to stay extremely cold to see faint heat from distant galaxies. It is protected by a sunshield made of five thin layers, each about the size of a tennis court.

That is far too big for any rocket. So engineers folded the sunshield and the telescope's 6.5-metre gold mirror to fit inside the nose of an Ariane 5 rocket. After launch, the telescope unfolded itself over about two weeks, using hundreds of moving parts. Each fold had been planned and tested on Earth many times.

Engineers at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory have also tested an origami-style design for a "starshade". It would be a giant flower-shaped screen, flown far in front of a telescope, to block a star's glare so that faint planets around it could be seen.

The maths in the creases

Unfold any flat origami model and look at the creases. They follow strict rules. At any point where creases meet, the number of "mountain" folds and "valley" folds always differs by exactly two. This is called Maekawa's theorem, after the Japanese mathematician Jun Maekawa.

There is also a rule about angles. Go round the point and add up every other angle between the creases. For the model to fold flat, those angles must add up to 180 degrees.

The American physicist Robert Lang left his job with lasers to design origami full time. He has helped engineers plan folding telescope lenses and even airbags, using the same rules.

The seven-fold limit that wasn't

Every fold doubles the thickness. After 7 folds you have 128 layers. After 10 folds you have over 1,000. Soon the paper is thicker than it is wide and refuses to bend. If you could somehow fold an ordinary sheet 42 times, the stack would reach past the Moon.

People used to say seven folds was the limit. In 2002, Britney Gallivan, a high-school student in California, worked out a formula for how long a strip must be to fold it a given number of times. Then she proved it. Using a strip of thin paper more than a kilometre long, she folded it in half 12 times. The doubling that stopped her is the same reason big numbers grow so fast.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Nobody can fold a piece of paper in half more than seven times.

Truth: With a big enough sheet, you can. Britney Gallivan worked out the maths and folded a very long strip of paper in half 12 times in 2002.

Try this

Take a sheet of newspaper and fold it in half as many times as you can, counting each fold. Then try with a long strip of tissue or toilet paper. Notice how quickly the layers pile up, because each fold doubles them.
