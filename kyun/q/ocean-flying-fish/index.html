How do flying fish fly?

A fish chased by a tuna bursts out of the sea, spreads fins like wings and glides for the length of half a football field before splashing down.

The short answer

Flying fish don't really fly like birds. They glide. When a predator chases one, the fish swims fast towards the surface, up to about 60 km/h. It shoots out of the water and spreads its big, stiff pectoral fins, the fins just behind its head, like the wings of a glider.

Before leaving the water completely, it often beats the lower lobe of its tail against the surface very fast, like an outboard motor, to gain extra speed. Then it sails through the air for up to about 50 m, roughly half a football field, before splashing back down or touching the water to push off again.

Surprising but true

In 2008, a Japanese TV crew filmed a flying fish staying in the air for 45 seconds, using its tail to bounce off the waves again and again.

Built for gliding

A flying fish's body is shaped like a torpedo, which helps it slice through water. Its pectoral fins are huge for a fish of its size, and some species also have large pelvic fins near the tail, giving them four "wings". These are called four-winged flying fish.

The lower part of the tail is longer than the upper part. That lets the fish keep its tail in the water while the rest of its body is in the air. Its eyes seem adapted to see well both underwater and in air, which is rare for a fish.

Escape from below, danger from above

Flying fish leap mainly to escape predators such as tuna, marlin and dolphinfish. Underwater, a hunter loses track of a fish that suddenly vanishes into the air. When the fish lands, it may be many metres away.

But the air has its own hunters. Seabirds such as frigatebirds sometimes snatch flying fish in mid-glide. Some flying fish land on the decks of ships at night, attracted by lights or carried by the wind, and sailors have long collected them for breakfast.

The longest flights

Most glides last a few seconds and stay within a metre or two of the waves, though gusts can lift a fish several metres up. By touching the water with its tail and beating again, a flying fish can stay up much longer, skipping like a stone across a pond. In 2008, a Japanese TV crew on a ferry near the island of Yakushima filmed one gliding for 45 seconds, the longest recorded flight.

Scientists have tested flying fish in wind tunnels and found they glide about as efficiently as some birds, such as hawks. Their fins hold out flat and stiff, which gives them steady lift, much like a well-made paper plane.

Fish on the menu

About 70 kinds of flying fish live in warm oceans around the world, including the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal. Fishermen in India catch them too. In Barbados, flying fish are so loved that the island is called the land of the flying fish. The national dish is cou-cou and flying fish, and the fish appears on its coins.

In Japan, flying fish eggs are tiny, crunchy, orange beads called tobiko, often sprinkled on sushi. Flying fish lay sticky eggs on floating seaweed and other drifting things, and fishers in some places catch them by floating bundles of leaves for the fish to lay on.

Gliding without wings

Flying fish are not the only animals that glide. Flying squirrels, flying lizards and even flying snakes in South and South-East Asia leap from trees and sail through the air. None of them can climb higher by flapping. Only birds, bats and insects can stay up by flapping. And baby spiders have an odder trick still: they let out silk threads and drift on the wind for kilometres, one of many ways to fly without wings.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Flying fish flap their fins like birds flap their wings.

Truth: Flying fish glide. They hold their fins out stiffly, like a paper plane. All their power comes from the tail, before and sometimes during the glide.

Try this

Make two paper planes from the same paper, one with wide, flat wings and one with narrow, pointed wings. Launch each gently from the same height, without throwing hard, and measure how far they glide. Wide, flat wings, like a flying fish's fins, usually glide further.
