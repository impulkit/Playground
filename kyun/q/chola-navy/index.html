How did the Cholas sail to Southeast Asia?

In about 1025 CE, a Chola king from Tamil Nadu sent an army by sea across more than 2,000 km of ocean to attack ports in Malaysia and Sumatra.

The short answer

The Cholas ruled much of south India from the 800s to the 1200s CE. They grew rich on rice from the Kaveri delta and on trade across the Bay of Bengal. Their kings, Rajaraja I and his son Rajendra I, built a strong navy.

In about 1025 CE, Rajendra sent a fleet across the Bay of Bengal to attack Srivijaya, a kingdom that controlled the sea passage between Sumatra and the Malay Peninsula. The ships rode the monsoon winds. They raided Kadaram, in today's Kedah in Malaysia, and other ports in Sumatra and southern Thailand. We know about it mainly because the Cholas carved a proud list of the places they attacked on temple walls.

Surprising but true

Some of the clearest evidence for the Chola raid is a list of conquered ports carved in Tamil on temple walls in Thanjavur, far from the sea.

A sea full of traders

A thousand years ago, the Indian Ocean was one of the world's great trading zones. Ships carried pepper, cotton cloth and gems from India eastwards. They came back with Chinese silk and porcelain, and with camphor and spices from the islands.

Chola ports such as Nagapattinam were busy with merchants. Tamil trading groups, known by names like the Five Hundred of Ayyavole, travelled as far as Sumatra and Myanmar. A Tamil inscription left by one of these guilds in 1088 has been found at Barus, on the west coast of Sumatra. Archaeologists digging at old Tamil ports have found broken Chinese pottery from this time, a sign of how far goods travelled.

Wind for an engine

Sailing ships depended on the wind. Around the Bay of Bengal, the monsoon makes that wind dependable. From about June to September it blows from the southwest, pushing ships from India towards Southeast Asia. From about November it turns and blows the other way.

So voyages followed a calendar. Sailors left with one monsoon, traded or fought, and waited for the wind to turn before sailing home. We don't have drawings of Chola warships, so historians argue about how big they were. They were probably wooden ships with sewn or pegged planks and large sails, able to carry soldiers, horses and supplies.

Why go so far

The Cholas were already used to fighting overseas. Rajaraja's forces had taken the northern part of Sri Lanka in the 990s, and his inscriptions claim the Maldives too, which they called the "twelve thousand ancient islands". Holding these islands helped protect the sea routes around the tip of India.

Srivijaya, ruled from Sumatra, controlled the Strait of Malacca, the narrow sea lane between India and China. Ships passing through often had to stop and pay. Some historians think the Cholas wanted to break that hold and keep their own merchants' route to China open.

Relations had not always been bad. Earlier, Rajaraja had allowed a Srivijaya king to build a Buddhist monastery at Nagapattinam, and the Cholas sent missions to China from 1015. What went wrong is not fully known. Some historians think trade disputes turned the two powers into rivals.

Words carved in stone

Rajendra's inscriptions list the places his fleet attacked: Kadaram, Malaiyur, Ilamuridesam in northern Sumatra, and Nakkavaram, the Nicobar Islands. They boast of capturing Srivijaya's king and his treasure, including a jewelled gate.

The raid does not seem to have led to lasting rule, and trade carried on soon after. But it showed that an Indian kingdom could send an army across open sea. Rajendra already had another proud title, the one who took the Ganga, from an earlier campaign in the north. He built a new capital, Gangaikonda Cholapuram, whose great temple still stands. Elsewhere in the region, monuments like Borobudur in Java were already two centuries old, and stories of the Chola fleet spread along every trade route in the eastern seas.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Cholas conquered and ruled a big empire in Southeast Asia.

Truth: Most historians think Rajendra's expedition was a powerful raid, not a lasting conquest. The Cholas defeated Srivijaya and seized treasure, but local kings soon ruled again, and trade between the two sides carried on.
