Why does a clock have 12 hours?

Our 12-hour clock goes back to ancient Egyptians counting stars at night, and our 60 minutes go back even further, to Babylon.

The short answer

A clock face shows 12 hours because the day is split into two halves of 12, one for the morning and one for the afternoon and night. That gives 24 hours in total.

The habit of using 12 began in ancient Egypt, more than 3,000 years ago. The Egyptians split the night into 12 parts by watching groups of stars rise, and the daytime into 12 parts too.

Minutes and seconds come from a different place. The Babylonians, in what is now Iraq, counted in sixties. Greek astronomers later borrowed their system, and that is why an hour has 60 minutes and a minute has 60 seconds.

Surprising but true

For a short time after 1793, revolutionary France used decimal time, with 10 hours in a day and 100 minutes in each hour. People hated it, and it was dropped within about two years.

Counting stars in the dark

On clear desert nights, Egyptian priests watched the sky. They picked out 36 groups of stars, now called decans, that rose one after another through the year.

On any given night about 12 of these groups rose between dusk and dawn. So the night was divided into 12 parts. Sundials then split the daylight into parts as well, and in time the day had 12 hours too.

Those hours were not all the same length. A summer night's hours were short and a winter night's hours were long, because each one was simply a twelfth of the dark.

Why the Babylonians loved 60

Most of us count in tens, probably because we have ten fingers. The people of Mesopotamia counted in sixties instead, a system called sexagesimal.

Sixty is a very friendly number. It can be divided exactly by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20 and 30. That makes sharing easy, with no awkward fractions. The same reason explains why 12 is useful: you can split it into halves, thirds, quarters and sixths.

Some people think counting by twelves came from fingers too. Touch your thumb to the three sections on each of your other four fingers and you get exactly 12.

India's own clock

Ancient India had its own way to divide the day. A full day and night held 60 ghatis, each about 24 minutes long. Each ghati was split again into 60 smaller parts called palas.

People measured ghatis with a water clock. A small bowl with a hole in the bottom was floated in a bigger pot of water. It slowly filled and sank, marking one ghati each time. Some temples and royal courts kept these water clocks running all day. You can still find ghatis in traditional Indian calendars.

a.m. and p.m.

With two sets of 12 hours, you need a way to tell them apart. English uses a.m. and p.m., short for the Latin words ante meridiem and post meridiem. They mean "before midday" and "after midday".

The meridian here is the line in the sky the Sun crosses at noon, when it is highest. That is why noon itself is the confusing case. It is neither before nor after midday, so some people write "12 noon" just to be clear.

Clocks that tried to change

In 1793, during the French Revolution, France tried to make time decimal like its new metric system. A day had 10 hours, each of 100 minutes, each of 100 seconds. Clockmakers built special faces for it. But people were used to the old hours, and France gave up decimal time within about two years.

Today many places use a 24-hour clock. Indian Railways, airlines and armies all write times like 18:30 instead of 6:30 p.m., because it avoids mixing up morning and evening. Most kitchen clocks still show 12, a habit Egyptian stargazers started long ago.

Myth or fact?

Myth: There have always been 60 minutes in an hour, and the hours have always been the same length.

Truth: For most of history, an hour was a twelfth of the daylight, so summer hours were long and winter hours short. Equal hours became normal only once mechanical clocks spread in the 1300s and later.

Try this

Count to 12 on one hand the old way. Use your thumb to touch each section of your four fingers, three sections per finger. That makes 12. Then use the fingers of your other hand to keep track of each 12, and see how high you can count on two hands.
