How does a watermelon grow so big?

The heaviest watermelon on record weighed 159 kg, more than two grown men, and it grew from a seed smaller than your fingernail.

The short answer

A watermelon is a giant water balloon built by a plant. The vine spreads its leaves across the ground and catches sunlight. Through photosynthesis, the leaves turn sunlight, air and water into sugar. That sugar, plus huge amounts of water drawn up by the roots, pours into the growing fruit.

Inside the fruit, cells don't just multiply. They also swell, like tiny balloons filling at a tap. Once a flower is pollinated, a typical watermelon goes from marble-sized to ripe in roughly five or six weeks, sometimes gaining a good fraction of a kilo in a day.

Most of what you crunch into is water, about 92%. The rest is mainly sugar, fibre and the red pigment lycopene, the same one that colours tomatoes.

Surprising but true

A watermelon is about 92% water, so most of that weight is water the plant pulled up from the soil.

It starts with a bee

A watermelon vine grows two kinds of yellow flowers. Male flowers make pollen. Female flowers have a tiny striped bump behind the petals, a baby watermelon in waiting. A bee has to carry pollen from one to the other, often visiting the same flower several times.

If too little pollen arrives, the fruit grows lopsided or drops off. That's why many farmers in the USA rent beehives when their melons flower. Without bees and other pollinators, there would be very few watermelons.

Out of Africa

Wild watermelons first grew in Africa. For a long time people thought the ancestor was a bitter melon from southern Africa. A study of watermelon DNA published in 2021 pointed instead to a melon from Sudan, in the north-east, called the Kordofan melon. Its flesh is pale and fairly sweet.

People in the Nile valley were growing watermelons more than 4,000 years ago, and paintings of them appear in Egyptian tombs. Over centuries, farmers chose the sweetest, reddest fruits for seed. Traders carried them to India, China and, after 1492, the Americas.

Today China grows well over half the world's watermelons. In north India, farmers have long grown tarbooz on the sandy beds of rivers like the Ganga and Yamuna. They plant in the dry weeks before the monsoon, when the water drops and leaves the banks bare.

How giants are grown

The 159 kg record holder was grown by Chris Kent of Tennessee and weighed in 2013. Contest growers use special seed, give each vine plenty of room and space, and often pinch off all but one fruit so the plant puts everything into it. They water carefully, because a sudden flood of water after a dry spell can make a big melon split.

An ordinary market watermelon weighs more like 5 to 10 kg. That's still heavy enough to need two hands and a strong shopping bag.

Seeds, no seeds and square ones

Seedless watermelons aren't made with chemicals or tricks. In the 1930s, the Japanese scientist Hitoshi Kihara found a way to cross two kinds of watermelon plant so that the fruit can't form proper seeds. You may still see soft white seed coats inside. Growers plant normal seeds nearby so bees can bring in pollen.

In Japan, some farmers grow watermelons inside glass boxes so they come out square. They stack neatly in a fridge, but they're often picked before they are ripe and sold as decorations.

A drink you can chew

In hot weather, a watermelon is a good way to take in water without a glass. Street sellers in India stack them on carts in April and May. In the American South, a cold slice is a classic July picnic. Either way, every bite is mostly rain and river water that a plant spent the summer collecting.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If you swallow a watermelon seed, a watermelon will grow in your stomach.

Truth: Seeds need soil, light and air to sprout. Your stomach is dark and full of acid, so a swallowed seed simply passes through your body.

Try this

Weigh a slice of watermelon on a kitchen scale, then leave it on a plate in a sunny window, covered with a net, for a day or two. Weigh what's left. The difference is mostly water that escaped into the air.
