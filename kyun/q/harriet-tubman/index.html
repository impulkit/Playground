Who was Harriet Tubman?

She escaped slavery alone, walking about 145 km by night, then went back again and again to lead others out.

The short answer

Harriet Tubman was born into slavery in Maryland, escaped to freedom in 1849, and then risked her life to guide others out. She became the best-known "conductor" on the Underground Railroad, a secret network of routes and safe houses that helped enslaved people escape to the North.

During the Civil War she worked for the Union army as a nurse, a scout and a spy. Later she campaigned for women's right to vote and cared for poor and elderly people. She never learned to read, yet she planned journeys across hundreds of kilometres of enemy country and, by her own account, never lost a person she was guiding.

Surprising but true

During the Civil War, Tubman helped lead an armed raid on the Combahee River in South Carolina that freed more than 700 enslaved people in a single night.

A hard childhood

She was born around 1822 and named Araminta Ross. Her family called her "Minty". As a small child she was hired out to other families, made to check muskrat traps in icy water and to look after babies through the night.

When she was a teenager, an overseer threw a heavy weight at another enslaved person, and it struck her head instead. She was badly hurt. For the rest of her life she had headaches and sudden sleeping spells.

Running for freedom

In 1849, fearing she would be sold away from her family, she escaped. She travelled mostly at night, about 145 km, to Pennsylvania, where slavery was illegal. She took a new name, Harriet, after her mother, and her husband's surname, Tubman.

"I had crossed the line," she said later. "I was free; but there was no one to welcome me." So she went back.

The conductor

Over about eleven years she made around 13 trips into Maryland. She brought out her parents, brothers and many others. She used disguises, sang songs with hidden messages, and moved in winter, when nights were long. Helpers, Black and white, hid people in barns, cellars and attics. In Wilmington, Delaware, a Quaker shopkeeper named Thomas Garrett gave her shoes, money and shelter many times. In Philadelphia, William Still recorded the stories of hundreds of people who passed through, which is one way historians know what happened.

She followed rivers and, on clear nights, the North Star. After 1850, a new law meant escaped people could be captured even in northern states, so she guided some of them all the way to Canada.

Soldier and spy

When the Civil War began in 1861, during Abraham Lincoln's presidency, Tubman joined Union forces in South Carolina. She nursed soldiers and organised a network of scouts. In June 1863 she helped guide Union gunboats up the Combahee River. Enslaved people ran to the boats, and more than 700 were freed. She was one of the first women in US history to help lead an armed raid.

She was paid very little for her war service, and she spent years asking the government for the pension she was owed. After the war, slavery ended across the country, the goal she had worked for since her escape.

Her later years

Tubman settled in Auburn, New York. She spoke at meetings for women's right to vote, and opened a home for elderly Black people on her land. She died in 1913, aged around 90, and was buried with military honours.

Her home in Auburn and places in Maryland where she grew up are now national historical parks. In 2016 the US Treasury announced plans to put her portrait on the 20-dollar note, but in 2026 it said it was no longer working on the change.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Harriet Tubman rescued more than 300 people from slavery.

Truth: The number 300 came from an early biography. Historians who studied records believe she led about 70 people out herself and gave directions to dozens more who escaped on their own.

Try this

On a clear night, with an adult, look for the Big Dipper (in India, the Saptarishi). The two stars at the end of its "bowl" point towards the North Star. Practise finding it, and notice that it stays in nearly the same place while other stars move.
