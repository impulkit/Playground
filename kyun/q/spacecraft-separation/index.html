How does a spacecraft split in two before coming home?

Before a crew capsule can come home, it has to break free from the rest of its spacecraft in a split second, cleanly, every single time.

The short answer

Most crew spacecraft are built in two halves. On top sits the crew module, the capsule where astronauts ride. Below it is the service module, which holds the engines, fuel tanks and power systems.

Before coming home, the two halves must part. Only the capsule returns to Earth. To split them, engineers use small, carefully timed explosive devices, called pyrotechnics, that cut the links between them in a fraction of a second.

Surprising but true

The service module, the half of a spacecraft that carries the engines and fuel, is never meant to come back. After the split, it falls into the air and burns up.

Why split at all?

A spacecraft in orbit races around Earth at about 28,000 km an hour. When it dives back into the air, the air in front of it gets squashed and rubbed, and the outside heats up to thousands of degrees. You can feel a tiny version of this heat from friction by rubbing your palms together fast.

The crew module has a heat shield on its base to protect the people inside. The service module has none. So it is let go and burns up, while the capsule slows down, opens its parachutes and splashes into the sea. NASA's Apollo astronauts came home from the Moon in exactly the same way in 1969 and the years after.

What is India's Gaganyaan mission?India has sent probes to the Moon, Mars and the Sun. Gaganyaan is its plan to launch its own astronauts on its own rocket.

A clean break

The two halves are joined by bolts, clamps and umbilical lines, the cables and pipes that carry electricity and fluids between them. At the moment of separation, every one of these must be cut at once. If one link holds on, the capsule could tumble.

The cut also sends a sharp jolt and shaking, or vibration, through the capsule. So engineers test it on the ground first, in a rig covered in sensors, and check that computers, navigation equipment and the heat shield all come through unharmed. In October 2026, ISRO ran exactly these tests for Gaganyaan, India's spacecraft for carrying people.

Robots go first

New crew spacecraft usually fly without people first. For Gaganyaan's first flight, a human-shaped robot called Vyommitra was chosen to ride in the capsule and report back on what astronauts would feel.

Engineers also drop capsules from aircraft to test their parachutes, and practise turning a capsule upright after it lands in the water. Each test removes one more risk before astronauts climb aboard.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Spacecraft come back to Earth in one piece, just like they went up.

Truth: Most spacecraft drop parts on the way. Rockets shed stages going up, and capsules leave their service module behind before coming down. Only a small part actually lands.
