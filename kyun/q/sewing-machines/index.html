How does a sewing machine stitch?

A sewing machine needle never goes all the way through the cloth. Its secret is a second thread waiting underneath, and a hook that catches a loop.

The short answer

A sewing machine joins cloth with two threads instead of one. The top thread runs from a spool down to the needle. The bottom thread sits on a tiny reel called a bobbin, under the cloth.

Each time the needle stabs down, it carries a loop of top thread just below the fabric. A spinning hook grabs that loop and swings it around the bobbin, wrapping it around the bottom thread. Then the needle rises, a lever pulls the top thread tight, and the two threads lock together in the middle of the cloth. This is called a lockstitch. A home machine can make several hundred of them a minute.

Surprising but true

The hole in a sewing machine needle is near its sharp point, the opposite end from a hand-sewing needle.

Loop, catch, pull

The parts move together like a team of gears and levers, all driven by one turning shaft. As the needle reaches its lowest point and starts to rise, friction in the cloth holds back a little of the thread. That makes a small loop beside the needle.

A pointed hook spins past and snags it. Some machines use a hook that whirls in a circle, others a shuttle that rocks back and forth. Either way, the loop is pulled wide and slipped around the bobbin case. Below the needle, small metal teeth called feed dogs grip the cloth and push it forward by one stitch length. Then everything repeats.

Turn the stitch-length dial and the feed dogs push the cloth further each time. For a zigzag stitch, the needle swings from side to side between stitches. Zigzags stretch with the fabric and stop cut edges from fraying.

Many inventors, one big fight

People tried for decades to build a machine that sewed. In 1830, a French tailor named Barthélemy Thimonnier patented a machine that made a simple chain of loops. The French army ordered uniforms from his workshop. Then a crowd of tailors, afraid for their jobs, broke in and smashed the machines.

In the USA, Walter Hunt built a lockstitch machine in the 1830s but never patented it. Elias Howe patented his version in 1846. A few years later Isaac Singer built a more practical machine with a needle that moved straight up and down and a foot pedal to drive it. Howe sued Singer and others for copying his ideas. In the end, the makers agreed to share their patents and pay each other fees.

What was the Industrial Revolution?About 250 years ago, machines began doing work that hands had done for thousands of years, and the way people lived changed faster than ever before.

A machine in every home

Early machines were expensive. Singer's company let families pay a little each month instead of all at once, one of the first big uses of this kind of hire purchase. By the late 1800s, sewing machines were among the most common machines in homes around the world.

In India, the treadle machine, worked by rocking a foot plate, became the tool of the neighbourhood darzi, or tailor. A company in Kolkata began making sewing machines under the name Usha in the 1930s. Many tailors still work on foot-powered machines because they need no electricity and can be repaired almost anywhere.

Factory speed

Most clothes you wear were stitched on industrial machines. Some make 5,000 or more stitches a minute, so fast that the needle is a blur. Special machines sew buttonholes, attach buttons or trim and bind the edge of fabric at the same time.

A T-shirt might pass through several machines, each run by a different worker. Garment hubs like Tiruppur in Tamil Nadu, and factories in Bangladesh, China and Vietnam, sew clothes for shops across the world. A pair of jeans can have over a dozen separate seams, and each one is held together by the same loop-and-hook trick.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A sewing machine just copies hand sewing very quickly, with one needle and one thread going in and out.

Truth: A hand stitch uses one thread that passes right through the cloth. The common machine stitch uses two threads, one above and one below, that twist together inside the cloth. Inventors only succeeded once they stopped trying to copy a human hand.

Try this

Make a model lockstitch with two colours of wool and a piece of thin card with holes punched in a row. Push a loop of the top colour down through a hole, pass the bottom colour through that loop underneath, then pull the top thread back up so the two lock in the middle of the card. Repeat along the row.
