How has medicine changed over time?

Before 1846, there was no reliable way to put a patient to sleep for surgery. The best surgeons were simply the fastest.

The short answer

For most of history, medicine meant herbs, rest, prayer and a few brave operations. Healers in Egypt, India, China and Greece wrote careful notes about illnesses and remedies, and some of their cures worked. But nobody knew that tiny living things called germs caused many diseases.

Most of the huge changes are less than 250 years old. Vaccines arrived in 1796. Painless surgery came in the 1840s, and clean operating rooms soon after. Germs were identified in the late 1800s, and antibiotics followed in the 1940s. Together these changes more than doubled how long the average person lives.

Surprising but true

In 1900 the average person in the world lived to about 32. By 2023 it was about 73, mostly because far fewer children died young.

Old books, sharp knives

Ancient doctors were careful observers. An Egyptian scroll from about 3,600 years ago describes how to treat broken bones and wounds. In India, the Sushruta Samhita, written down well over 1,500 years ago (experts disagree on the exact date), describes more than a hundred surgical tools. It explains how to remove cloudy lenses from eyes and rebuild a damaged nose with skin from the cheek, an early kind of plastic surgery linked to the healer Sushruta.

In Greece, Hippocrates and his followers taught that illness had natural causes, not angry gods. In Persia, Ibn Sina finished his Canon of Medicine in about 1025. Students in Europe and the Middle East used it for about 600 years. In India, the Charaka Samhita laid out ideas about diet, digestion and daily habits that are still part of Ayurveda.

Long mistakes

Many old ideas were wrong. In Europe, doctors believed the body held four fluids, or humours, that had to stay in balance. To fix an "excess" of blood, they cut patients and let them bleed. When George Washington fell ill in 1799, his doctors drained a large amount of his blood. He died within a day.

Surgery was terrifying. With no way to stop pain, the best surgeons were the fastest. Wounds often became infected afterwards, because no one cleaned their hands or tools. Hospitals could be dirty, crowded places. Florence Nightingale's work with wounded soldiers in the 1850s helped turn nursing into a trained profession and made cleanliness a hospital rule.

The great turning points

In 1796, the English doctor Edward Jenner scratched material from a cowpox sore into a boy's arm. The boy became protected against smallpox. This was the first vaccine, and it led to smallpox being wiped out worldwide in 1980.

In 1846, a dentist in Boston put a patient to sleep with ether gas during a public operation. The patient felt nothing, and the news spread around the world within months. In 1867, the British surgeon Joseph Lister began using carbolic acid to kill germs on wounds and tools, and far fewer of his patients died from infections.

Microscopes helped scientists like Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch prove that germs cause disease. In 1897, Ronald Ross, working in Secunderabad, showed that mosquitoes spread malaria. He won a Nobel Prize for it in 1902.

How did microscopes reveal a hidden world?In the 1670s, a Dutch cloth seller peered through a lens smaller than a pea and saw tiny creatures swimming in a drop of pond water. Nobody had ever seen them before.

Seeing inside and fighting back

In 1895, X-rays let doctors see bones without cutting. In 1928, Alexander Fleming noticed that a mould had killed the bacteria on one of his dishes. That mould gave us penicillin, which by the 1940s was saving soldiers and children alike.

Since then, medicine has added blood transfusions, heart surgery, organ transplants and scans that show the brain at work. India was declared free of polio in 2014, after years of vaccination drives that reached every village. Doctors still make mistakes, and new diseases still appear. But a child born today, in India or the USA, can expect to live more than twice as long as a child born in 1900.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Doctors have always washed their hands and cleaned their tools before treating patients.

Truth: Clean hands and tools only became normal in the late 1800s. In the 1840s, Ignaz Semmelweis showed that handwashing saved mothers' lives in a Vienna hospital, but many doctors refused to believe him at first.

Make a "medicine through time" strip. Fold a long paper into six panels and draw one big change in each one, such as herbal remedies, ancient surgery, the first vaccine, anaesthesia, antibiotics and X-rays. Ask an older relative what medicines or treatments they remember changing in their lifetime.
