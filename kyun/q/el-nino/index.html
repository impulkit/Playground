What are El Niño and La Niña?

When the sea off Peru warms by a degree or two, farmers in India, fishers in Peru and skiers in California can all feel it months later.

The short answer

El Niño and La Niña are two opposite moods of the tropical Pacific Ocean. They swap back and forth every few years, and they change the weather in many parts of the world.

Normally, steady winds called trade winds blow from east to west along the equator. They push warm surface water towards Indonesia and Australia, while cold water rises up from the deep off the coast of Peru. In an El Niño, the trade winds weaken. Warm water spreads back east, and the eastern Pacific becomes unusually warm. In a La Niña, the trade winds blow harder than usual, and the eastern Pacific becomes colder.

Scientists call the whole cycle ENSO, short for El Niño–Southern Oscillation.

Surprising but true

One of the key discoveries about El Niño was made by a British scientist working in India, who was trying to predict the monsoon.

A Christmas warming

Fishers on the coast of Peru noticed long ago that, every few years, the sea grew warm around Christmas and fish became scarce. They called it El Niño, Spanish for "the boy", referring to the Christ child. The cooler opposite later got the name La Niña, "the girl".

The fish disappear for a reason. Off Peru, cold water rising from the deep carries nutrients that feed plankton, which feed huge shoals of anchovies. In an El Niño, warm water caps the sea like a lid and stops the rising cold water. In 1972–73, the anchovy catch collapsed, hurting fishing boats and the farmers worldwide who used fishmeal as animal feed.

A scientist in India

In 1904, a British mathematician named Gilbert Walker became head of the weather service in India. A failed monsoon in 1899 had led to a terrible famine, and he wanted to forecast the rains. Walker and a team of assistants, doing calculations by hand, compared weather records from all over the world.

In the 1920s, he found a giant seesaw in air pressure: when it was high over the Pacific, it tended to be low over the Indian Ocean, and the other way round. He called it the Southern Oscillation. Decades later, scientists realised it was the air half of El Niño. That is why the cycle carries his word "oscillation" in its name.

What it does to India and the USA

El Niño years often bring a weaker monsoon to India and a higher risk of drought. Some of the driest years, such as 2009 and 2015, came during El Niño. La Niña years often bring good rains. But El Niño is only one player. Temperatures in the Indian Ocean, a pattern called the Indian Ocean Dipole, can strengthen or cancel its effect.

In the USA, El Niño winters tend to be wetter across the south, from California to Florida, and milder in the north. La Niña often brings drought to the southwest. La Niña years also tend to have more Atlantic hurricanes, because winds high above the ocean are calmer and let the storms grow.

El Niño can bring drought and bushfires to Australia and Indonesia, and floods to Peru's normally dry coast.

Watching the Pacific

Today, NOAA and other agencies watch the Pacific with a network of floating buoys, ocean robots and satellites. They measure water temperatures and winds every day. Computer models use this data to forecast El Niño or La Niña several months ahead, which helps farmers in India decide what and when to plant.

El Niño also nudges the whole planet warmer for a while, because heat stored in the ocean escapes into the air. Strong El Niños helped make 2016 and 2023 two of the hottest years ever recorded, on top of long-term warming.

Quick facts

Names
Spanish for "the boy" and "the girl"
How often
roughly every 2 to 7 years
Typical length
about 9 to 12 months, sometimes longer
Scientific name for the whole cycle
ENSO (El Niño–Southern Oscillation)
Early pioneer
Gilbert Walker, India Meteorological Department, 1920s

Myth or fact?

Myth: El Niño is a storm.

Truth: El Niño is not a single storm but a change in ocean temperatures and winds across the tropical Pacific. It shifts weather patterns around the world for months at a time.

Try this

Fill a long, clear plastic box with water and add a few drops of food colouring at one end. Blow gently and steadily across the surface from that end through a straw for a minute. Watch the coloured water pile up at the far end, the way trade winds push warm water west across the Pacific.
