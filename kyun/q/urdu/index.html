How did Urdu develop?

Urdu was born in the markets and army camps of north India, where Persian, Arabic and Turkish words met the everyday speech of Delhi.

The short answer

Urdu grew out of the everyday speech of the region around Delhi, starting roughly 800 years ago. When Turkic and Persian-speaking rulers set up the Delhi Sultanate in the 1200s, soldiers, traders and officials needed a common tongue. Local speech borrowed thousands of words from Persian, Arabic and Turkic.

Over centuries this mixed language became a rich literary language, written in the Perso-Arabic script. Its flowing Nastaliq letters slant from upper right to lower left, and each letter changes shape depending on its neighbours. That made Urdu hard to set in metal type, so many Urdu newspapers were copied out by hand by calligraphers until computers learned the style in the 1980s. Today it is the national language of Pakistan and one of India's 22 scheduled languages.

Surprising but true

The name Urdu comes from a Turkic word for an army camp, the same root as the English word horde.

A language with many names

For a long time the language had no fixed name. People called it Hindavi, Hindi, Dehlavi or Rekhta, which means "scattered" or "mixed". The name Urdu came later. It is short for a phrase meaning "the language of the exalted camp", referring to the royal city of Delhi.

The poet Amir Khusrau, who lived around 1300, wrote mostly in Persian but also composed riddles and songs in Hindavi. Many people see him as an early voice of the language.

The Deccan connection

Surprisingly, some of the earliest Urdu poetry was written far from Delhi, in the kingdoms of the Deccan such as Golconda and Bijapur. There it was called Dakhani. In about 1700 a poet known as Wali Dakhani visited Delhi, and his verses made Delhi's own poets take the language seriously.

Ghazals in Delhi and Lucknow

In the 18th and 19th centuries, Urdu poetry flowered in Delhi and Lucknow, even as Mughal power faded. The favourite form was the ghazal, a poem of linked couplets about love and loss. Mir Taqi Mir and Mirza Ghalib are the giants. Ghalib's old house in Ballimaran, a narrow lane in Old Delhi, is now a small museum.

Later, Muhammad Iqbal wrote "Saare Jahan Se Achha" in 1904. It is still sung by Indian schoolchildren, and its tune is played at the Beating Retreat ceremony after Republic Day.

Hindi and Urdu: twins with two scripts

In everyday speech Hindi and Urdu are very close. Their grammar is almost the same, and a Hindi speaker in Lucknow and an Urdu speaker in Karachi can chat easily. Linguists often call the shared spoken language Hindustani. The big differences are the script, Devanagari or Nastaliq, and the formal vocabulary. Formal Hindi borrows from Sanskrit, while formal Urdu borrows from Persian and Arabic.

In the 19th century, under British rule, the two drifted apart as each was linked with a religious community. That division hardened at the Partition of India in 1947. Yet the great writer Premchand began writing his stories in Urdu before switching to Hindi.

Urdu in your ears

If you watch Bollywood films, you already know a lot of Urdu. Words like ishq, khwaab and mohabbat fill film songs, because lyricists such as Sahir Ludhianvi and Gulzar have drawn heavily on Urdu poetry. India's 2011 Census counted about 5 crore Urdu speakers, with large communities in Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Telangana and Maharashtra. Several states, including Telangana, Bihar and Uttar Pradesh, use it as an official language alongside another. Many more people use its words every day without thinking about where they came from.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Urdu came to India from Arabia or Persia.

Truth: Urdu grew up inside India. Its grammar and most basic words come from the speech of the Delhi region. Persian and Arabic gave it much vocabulary, but it was born in Indian cities and camps, and spoken by people of many faiths.
