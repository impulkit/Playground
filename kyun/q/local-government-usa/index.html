How do American towns govern themselves?

Every March, in many small towns in Vermont, residents gather in a school gym or town hall and vote on the town's budget themselves, by show of hands.

The short answer

In the USA, power is split into layers. There's the federal government in Washington, D.C., then the 50 state governments, and below them a huge number of local governments. These include counties, cities, towns and villages, plus school districts and special districts that run things like water supply, fire services or public libraries.

Local governments handle the things you see every day: rubbish collection, street repairs, parks, local police and fire services, building rules, and most public schools. Many are led by elected mayors and councils. In some small towns, the residents still vote on decisions directly, in a town meeting.

Surprising but true

The USA has about 90,000 separate local governments, including counties, cities, towns, school districts and special districts for things like water and fire services (2022 count).

The town meeting

The oldest form of American local democracy comes from New England, in the north-east. Early English settlers in the 1600s, like the Pilgrims, held meetings where the men of the community decided local matters together. Women and many poorer residents were left out at first.

The tradition survives. In Vermont, the first Tuesday in March is Town Meeting Day. In many towns, residents meet in a school gym or town hall, argue about the budget, a new fire truck or a road, and vote. It is about as close as modern America gets to the direct democracy of ancient Athens.

Counties, parishes and boroughs

Almost every state is divided into counties, which run courts, keep records like births and land deeds, and often manage roads and jails. There are about 3,100 counties and similar areas in all. Texas alone has 254.

Louisiana calls its counties parishes, a name left over from its French and Spanish Catholic past. Alaska calls them boroughs. A county may be led by an elected board and a sheriff, who heads local law enforcement. Americans elect many local officials that other countries would appoint, including sheriffs and, in most states, some judges and prosecutors.

Then there are special districts, set up for a single job. Some run airports or buses, some manage water, and in Florida several exist just to control mosquitoes. Each can have its own board and even collect its own taxes.

Mayors and councils

Cities usually have a mayor and a city council. In some, like New York and Chicago, the mayor is powerful and runs city departments directly. In others, the elected council hires a professional city manager to run things day to day.

City governments can be huge. New York City has more people than most American states and a budget larger than many countries'. Others are tiny. A town of a few hundred people might have a part-time mayor who also runs a shop.

School boards and local taxes

Most American public schools are run by local school districts, each with an elected school board. There are around 13,000 of them. They hire superintendents, set school calendars and choose some of what is taught.

Much of the money comes from property taxes paid by local homeowners. This means schools in richer areas often have more money than those in poorer ones, a long-running debate in American life. India's panchayats and city councils do some of the same jobs, though Indian states play a bigger part in running schools. Turnout in local elections is often low, even though their decisions shape daily life most directly, from potholes to policing.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In the USA, most decisions about your daily life are made in Washington, D.C.

Truth: Many everyday matters, such as rubbish collection, local roads, parks, zoning and most public schools, are decided by counties, cities, towns and school boards. Local elections often affect daily life more directly than national ones.
