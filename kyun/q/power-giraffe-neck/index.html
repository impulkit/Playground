How does blood reach a giraffe's head?

A giraffe's heart weighs about 11 kg and pumps blood at roughly twice the pressure of yours, just to push it two metres up the neck.

The short answer

A giraffe's brain sits about 2 metres above its heart. Getting blood up that far is like pumping water to the top of a tall ladder. So the giraffe has a powerful heart, weighing about 11 kg, with very thick muscle walls.

That heart pumps blood at roughly twice the pressure found in a healthy human. If you had pressure that high, it would damage your body. Giraffes have special tricks to cope with it, from tight leg skin to valves in the neck.

The neck itself is a marvel. Like you, a giraffe has only seven neck bones. Each one is just enormous, up to about 25 cm long.

Surprising but true

Scientists who design space suits have studied giraffe legs, because the tight skin there works like built-in compression socks.

A pump built for height

Your heart pushes blood at around 120 units of pressure with each beat, measured in a scale doctors call millimetres of mercury. A giraffe's heart pushes at roughly twice that. Much of that extra pressure is used up just lifting blood against gravity up the long neck.

Here is the maths. A column of blood 2 metres tall presses down with about 150 of those units. So the heart must push at least that hard just to get blood level with the brain, and then a bit more to keep it flowing.

The walls of the left side of the giraffe heart are thick and strong. In 2016, scientists who read the giraffe's genes found changes in several linked to heart growth and blood pressure.

Built-in compression socks

High pressure creates a new problem at the other end. Blood in the legs sits far below the heart, and gravity adds even more pressure there. In a person, that would make fluid leak out of blood vessels and the ankles would swell up like balloons.

Giraffes don't swell. Their leg skin is very tight and thick, with a tough layer underneath, so it squeezes the vessels like compression socks. The walls of their leg arteries are also thick.

In the 1980s and 1990s, NASA scientist Alan Hargens studied giraffes for exactly this reason. Astronauts in space suits and in low gravity have trouble with fluid shifting around their bodies. Giraffes showed one natural way to keep it in place.

The drinking problem

The most dangerous moment for a giraffe's head is when it bends down to drink. Its head swings from 2 metres above the heart to more than a metre below it. Blood should rush down and flood the brain.

It doesn't, thanks to several tricks. Valves inside the big neck veins stop blood flowing backwards. Stretchy arteries in the neck soak up surges like a balloon. Scientists also think a network of small vessels near the base of the brain helps smooth out pressure changes, although they still argue about how much it does.

Giraffes spread their front legs wide to reach the water. They are clumsy and slow to stand up again in this position, so they drink quickly and look around often for lions.

Long necks, short sleep

Giraffes live in the dry grasslands and open woodlands of Africa, from Niger and Chad in the west to Kenya and Tanzania, and down to South Africa. Their height lets them browse leaves from the tops of acacia trees, using a dark tongue about 45 cm long to wrap around branches.

They don't sleep much, often just a few hours a day in short bursts, sometimes standing up. Male giraffes swing their necks like hammers in fights called necking. A tall male can reach about 5.5 m, as high as a double-decker bus stood on top of a car.

You can see giraffes in the wild on safari in Kenya. And the strangest part? Some dinosaurs had necks several times longer than a giraffe's, and scientists still debate how they got blood to their heads, as you can read in the long necks of sauropods.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Giraffes are silent animals with no voice.

Truth: Giraffes snort, grunt and hiss. A study of recordings from zoos found they also make a deep humming sound at night, though scientists are still working out what it means.

Try this

Hold a small water bottle at the level of your heart. Now lift it as high above your head as you can and hold it there for 30 seconds, then lower it. Your arm feels the work of lifting. A giraffe's heart does that kind of work with blood every second.
