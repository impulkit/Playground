Where do idioms come from?

When a cricket fan says a piece of news "hit me for six", they are using a phrase that began on a cricket ground.

The short answer

An idiom is a group of words that means something different from the words on their own. "Spill the beans" has nothing to do with beans. "It's a piece of cake" is not about cake. You only understand an idiom if someone has taught you, or if you have heard it enough times to guess.

Most idioms started as plain descriptions of real things: ships, horses, games, farms and kitchens. Over time people used them for other situations, and the original picture faded. Some came from famous writers. Others came from sport. Many are so old that nobody knows who said them first.

The word itself comes from the Greek idioma, meaning something peculiar to one place or person. That fits. Idioms are the private jokes of a language, and you have to be inside to get them.

Surprising but true

Nobody knows where "raining cats and dogs" comes from, even though English speakers have used it for over 350 years.

Your brain on idioms

Young children often take idioms literally. Tell a five-year-old that you have "butterflies in your stomach" and they may look worried. By about eight or nine, most children understand that the words are a picture. The butterflies are really that fluttery feeling of fear before a test or a match.

Adults who learn a new language find idioms among the hardest parts. You can know every word in "kick the bucket" and still have no idea that it means to die.

Borrowed from sport

Sport is a rich source, because millions of people watch the same game and share its words. In cricket, a batter who hits the ball over the boundary without a bounce scores six runs. So when bad news "hits you for six", it knocks you right off your feet. Being "on a sticky wicket" means you are in a tricky spot, just as a batter struggles on a damp, uneven pitch.

Boxing gave English "saved by the bell" and "throw in the towel", which is what a boxer's team once did to give up a fight. American baseball added "touch base", "off base" and "a whole new ball game".

How did cricket become India's favourite sport?The first cricket match recorded in India was played by sailors on a Gujarat beach.

Gifts from writers

A playwright speaking to big crowds every night could launch a phrase across a whole country. William Shakespeare is the earliest known writer of "wild-goose chase", in Romeo and Juliet. Back then it meant a horse race where riders had to follow the leader's twists and turns, like geese flying in a line. Today it means a pointless hunt.

He also used "break the ice" in The Taming of the Shrew. That one is older than him. It first described ships cutting a path through frozen water so other boats could follow. Opening a channel became a way of saying "starting the conversation".

Idioms in every language

Every language grows its own. Hindi has "naach na jaane aangan tedha": someone who can't dance blames the crooked courtyard. Another, "oont ke munh mein jeera", means a cumin seed in a camel's mouth, for a portion far too small to matter. An English speaker might say "a drop in the ocean" for the same thing.

These are hard to carry across languages. Word for word, a translation of "it's raining cats and dogs" makes no sense in Kannada or Spanish. Good translators look for a phrase that does the same job in the new language, even if the picture is completely different.

When the story is a guess

Because idioms are fun, people love inventing tales about them. You may hear that "raining cats and dogs" comes from animals falling off roofs, or that "saved by the bell" comes from coffins with bells. Neither story has real evidence.

Experts who build dictionaries hunt for the oldest written example of a phrase. Sometimes they find it in a play, a newspaper or a letter. Often the trail goes cold, and the honest answer is "we don't know". That hasn't stopped anyone from using the phrase. People were saying cats and dogs came down from the sky long before anyone thought to ask why.

Myth or fact?

Myth: "Raining cats and dogs" comes from pets sliding off thatched roofs in heavy rain.

Truth: There is no evidence for this. The phrase has been in use since at least the 1600s, and word experts still don't know where it began.

Try this

Pick an idiom in Hindi, Tamil or any language your family speaks, and translate it word for word into English. Read it to a friend and ask them to guess what it means. Then try the same with an English idiom going the other way.
