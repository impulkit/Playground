What happened to the people on the Mary Celeste?

In December 1872 sailors found a ship drifting in the Atlantic with food, clothes and cargo aboard, and not one person on it.

The short answer

On 4 December 1872, the crew of a ship called the Dei Gratia spotted another sailing ship zigzagging across the Atlantic, east of the Azores islands. She was the Mary Celeste. Nobody was at the wheel. When they rowed over and climbed aboard, they found the ship sound, the cargo nearly all there and about six months of food in the stores. The ten people who should have been on board were gone.

They were never found. The lifeboat was missing, and so were the captain's navigation tools. That one clue tells us most of what we know: the crew almost certainly climbed into the small boat and left. Why they would swap a good ship for a tiny open boat in the middle of the ocean is the real mystery.

Surprising but true

The famous detail of warm tea and half-eaten breakfasts on the tables was invented by a young Arthur Conan Doyle in a made-up story, three years before he created Sherlock Holmes.

Who was on board

The ship had a troubled past. She was built in Nova Scotia, Canada, in 1861 and first named Amazon. Her first captain fell ill and died on her early voyages, and she later ran aground and was rebuilt before being renamed Mary Celeste.

The captain was Benjamin Briggs, an experienced sailor from Massachusetts. With him were his wife Sarah, their two-year-old daughter Sophia, and seven crewmen. They had left New York on 7 November, heading for Genoa in Italy with 1,701 barrels of industrial alcohol, the strong kind used in factories, not for drinking.

The last entry in the ship's log was written on 25 November, near Santa Maria island in the Azores. The ship was found ten days later, still sailing roughly the right way, hundreds of kilometres further on. For more than a week, an empty ship had steered itself across the sea, pushed by wind and ocean currents.

The clues

The men of the Dei Gratia noticed some odd things. There was about a metre of water sloshing in the bottom of the hull. One of the pumps had been taken apart. A rod used to measure water in the hold lay on the deck. Clothes, the crew's belongings and the captain's furniture were still in place. Nine of the alcohol barrels were empty.

The Dei Gratia crew sailed the ship to Gibraltar and claimed a reward for saving it. A court looked hard at whether they had done something wrong, found no proof, and gave them about a sixth of the ship's value.

The best guesses

One idea is that alcohol fumes leaked from the barrels. If Briggs smelled them, or heard a bang, he may have feared an explosion and ordered everyone into the lifeboat, tied on a rope, to wait at a safe distance. In 2006 a chemist at University College London built a model hold and showed that alcohol vapour can burn in a sudden, fairly cool flash that leaves little mark.

Another idea is simpler. With a broken pump and a confusing water reading, Briggs may have thought the ship was sinking fast. Land was close. Leaving would have seemed sensible. If the rope snapped or the wind picked up, a small boat could never have caught a sailing ship again.

How a story became a legend

In 1884 a young doctor named Arthur Conan Doyle wrote a short story about the ship, calling it the Marie Celeste. He added warm tea, a cat asleep and half-eaten breakfasts. None of it was true, but many readers believed it. Three years later Doyle created Sherlock Holmes, and his made-up version of the mystery is still repeated today.

The ship's own ending was strange too. In 1885 her last captain ran her onto a reef near Haiti on purpose, hoping to cheat an insurance company. He was caught. And the Mary Celeste is still dragged into stories about the Bermuda Triangle, even though she was found on the other side of the Atlantic, thousands of kilometres away.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The crew vanished so suddenly that their breakfast was still warm on the table.

Truth: No warm meals were found. That detail came from an 1884 short story by Arthur Conan Doyle, which many readers took to be true. The real ship was messy and wet, with a missing lifeboat, which points to a planned escape.
