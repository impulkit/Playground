Why is the Mississippi River so important?

A stream you can wade across in Minnesota grows into a river that drains about 40 percent of the lower 48 US states.

The short answer

The Mississippi is the main river of the United States. It begins as a small stream flowing out of Lake Itasca in Minnesota and runs about 3,700 kilometres south to the Gulf of Mexico. Along the way, big rivers such as the Missouri and the Ohio pour into it.

Its basin, the whole area whose rain ends up in the river, covers about 40 percent of the 48 joined-up states. That makes it the drain for the American heartland, the flat farmland where much of the country's maize and soybeans grow. For thousands of years it has been a highway for boats, a source of food and a dividing line between east and west. It has also flooded towns and farms again and again.

Surprising but true

A drop of water that sets off from the river's source at Lake Itasca takes roughly three months to reach the Gulf of Mexico.

From a trickle to a giant

At Lake Itasca, the young river is so shallow and narrow that visitors hop across it on stepping stones. By the time it reaches St Louis, after the Missouri joins it, it carries a vast load of mud from the Great Plains. The Missouri brings so much of it that people nicknamed that river the "Big Muddy".

Near its mouth, below New Orleans, the river slows and drops that mud. Over thousands of years it has built a huge delta, pushing new land into the sea in a shape that looks like a bird's foot from above.

A river highway

Long before Europeans arrived, the river joined up Native peoples across the middle of the continent. Around 1050 to 1350 CE, near today's St Louis, a city called Cahokia grew up with a giant earthen mound at its centre. Thousands of people lived there, and it traded along the river with places hundreds of kilometres away. It is one of the largest ancient sites of the Native nations north of Mexico.

Today, barges pushed by tugboats carry grain, coal and steel along the river. One barge can hold as much grain as dozens of lorries, so moving goods by water is cheap. Much of the grain the USA sells to other countries goes down the river to ports near New Orleans.

Mark Twain's river

Samuel Clemens grew up in Hannibal, Missouri, a town on the riverbank, in the 1840s. As a young man he trained as a steamboat pilot, which meant memorising every bend, sandbar and snag over more than a thousand kilometres of water.

When he became a writer he called himself Mark Twain, after a cry used by boatmen measuring the depth. His books The Adventures of Tom Sawyer and Adventures of Huckleberry Finn are full of the river: rafts, islands, storms and the life of river towns. In Life on the Mississippi, he wrote about how the river kept changing its course.

Floods and a river that wants to move

The Mississippi has always flooded. In 1927, after months of heavy rain, it burst through its banks in many places and covered about 70,000 square kilometres, roughly the size of Assam. Hundreds of thousands of people had to leave their homes.

After that, engineers built thousands of kilometres of levees, high earth banks along the river. They also built a set of gates in Louisiana called the Old River Control Structure. Left alone, the Mississippi would probably have switched much of its flow into a shorter, steeper channel, the Atchafalaya River, decades ago. The gates hold it in place, because ports and cities depend on the river staying where it is.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Mississippi is the longest river in the USA.

Truth: By most measurements, its biggest branch, the Missouri, is longer. Rivers wander and their sources are hard to pin down, so the figures vary, but the US Geological Survey lists the Missouri as about 320 km longer.

Lay a long sheet of paper on a slight slope, like a tilted tray. Sprinkle a line of sand or soil at the top and drip water slowly onto it with a spoon. Watch how the water finds a path, bends, and drops sand where it slows down at the bottom, like a tiny river and delta.
