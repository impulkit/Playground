How are paints and brushes made?

Every paint, from a cave painter's red to the poster colours in your school bag, is just coloured powder mixed with something sticky.

The short answer

All paint has two main parts. The first is a pigment, a powder that gives the colour. The second is a binder, a sticky liquid that holds the powder together and glues it to the surface. Change the binder and you get a different kind of paint.

Brushes are simpler. They are bundles of animal hair or plastic fibres, squeezed into a metal collar and fixed to a wooden handle. The kind of hair decides how the brush behaves: stiff and scratchy, or soft and springy.

Surprising but true

For centuries, the deep blue called ultramarine was made by grinding up lapis lazuli, a stone mined in the mountains of Afghanistan, and it could cost more than gold.

Colours from rocks, plants and bugs

The oldest pigments came from the ground. Cave painters used red and yellow ochre, which are earth coloured by iron, and black from charcoal. In India, the painters of the Ajanta caves used earth colours, soot and, for some blues, lapis lazuli.

Some colours were very hard to get. Ultramarine blue came from lapis lazuli mined in Afghanistan and carried west along trade routes, and in Renaissance Europe it was often kept for the robes of the most important figures. Other pigments came from plants, or even insects: carmine red is made from tiny cochineal bugs that live on cactus.

Many old pigments were also dangerous. Some whites contained lead, and a bright green once made with arsenic was used in wallpaper. Modern paints for children are made to be much safer.

Colours from a lab

In the early 1700s, a colour maker in Berlin was trying to make a red. Something went wrong with his ingredients, and he got a deep blue instead. It became known as Prussian blue, the first modern pigment made by chemistry. It was cheap, and soon painters everywhere were using it.

More discoveries followed in the 1800s and 1900s: bright yellows from chromium and cadmium, and later many colours made from oil-based chemicals. Today most paint colours are made in factories, which is why even a small school paint box can have dozens of bright shades.

Which great inventions happened by accident?A messy lab, a melted chocolate bar and a glue that didn't stick properly all led to inventions now used by millions of people.

Choosing the glue

The binder changes everything. Egg yolk makes tempera, which medieval painters used. Linseed oil, pressed from flax seeds, makes oil paint, which dries slowly so artists can blend colours for days. Gum arabic, from acacia trees, makes watercolour. Acrylic paint, which appeared in the mid-1900s, uses a kind of plastic that dries quickly and becomes waterproof.

For a long time, painters mixed their own paints and stored them in pigs' bladders tied with string. In 1841, an American painter named John Goffe Rand patented a squeezable metal tube. Paint could now be carried easily, and painting outdoors became much simpler, which suited the Impressionists who loved painting in natural light.

Bristles and hairs

Brushes for oil paint are often made from hog bristles, which are stiff enough to push thick paint around. Soft watercolour brushes are made from fine hairs, such as sable, which hold lots of water and come to a sharp point. Today many brushes use nylon or other synthetic fibres instead.

Indian miniature painters made some of the finest brushes of all. For the tiny details in Mughal miniatures, artists used brushes made from just a few hairs, traditionally taken from a squirrel's tail or a kitten's fur. With them they could paint single eyelashes on a face no bigger than a fingernail.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Paints are coloured by dyes, like food colouring.

Truth: Most artists' paints are coloured by pigments, which are tiny solid grains that do not dissolve. The binder glues them to the paper or canvas. Dyes, which dissolve completely, are used more for cloth and inks.
