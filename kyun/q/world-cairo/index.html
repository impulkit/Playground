What is Cairo like today?

In Cairo you can eat breakfast near a 4,500-year-old pyramid and ride Africa's oldest metro line to work before lunch.

The short answer

Cairo is the biggest city in the Arab world and one of the largest in Africa, home to about 2.2 crore people in its greater area (2024 estimate). That is about as many as live in and around Mumbai. It sits on the River Nile, where the green river valley opens into the wide delta, with desert pressing in on both sides.

Today's Cairo is a city of honking traffic, rooftop gardens, ancient mosques, glass towers and markets that have been trading for 600 years. And the pyramids of Giza are not far away in the desert. They are on the edge of town.

Surprising but true

Cairo gets only about 25 mm of rain in a whole year, less than Mumbai can get in a single hour during the monsoon.

A city on a strip of green

From space, Egypt looks like a brown sheet with one green ribbon running down it. That ribbon is the Nile, and Cairo is the knot at the top. Almost all Egyptians live within a few kilometres of the river, because everywhere else is desert.

Cairo gets about 25 mm of rain in a whole year. Some years it barely rains at all. Every glass of water, every field of beans and every swimming pool depends on the Nile. Its water fell as rain thousands of kilometres away, in the mountains of Ethiopia and East Africa.

Old Cairo and very old Cairo

The pyramids are about 4,500 years old, but the city of Cairo itself is much younger. It was founded in AD 969 by the Fatimid rulers, who named it al-Qahira, "the victorious". Its Al-Azhar mosque, begun soon after, has been a centre of Islamic learning for over 1,000 years.

Old Cairo has so many mosques that people call it the city of a thousand minarets. The Khan el-Khalili market dates back to the 1380s. Its narrow lanes sell spices, brass lamps, perfume bottles and tiny cups of sweet mint tea.

Further south lies Coptic Cairo, home to some of Egypt's oldest churches. The Hanging Church gets its name because it was built on top of the gatehouse of an old Roman fortress, so its floor seems to float above the ground. Egypt's Christians, called Copts, make up roughly a tenth of the country's people.

How did people decode hieroglyphs?For about 1,400 years, nobody on Earth could read Egyptian hieroglyphs. A broken stone found by soldiers in 1799 changed that.

A brand-new museum for very old treasures

For over a century, Egypt's greatest treasures were crammed into a pink building in central Cairo. In November 2025, the Grand Egyptian Museum opened in full beside the Giza plateau. It is the largest museum in the world for a single civilisation.

For the first time, all of Tutankhamun's treasures are shown together, about 5,000 objects, from his golden mask to his sandals. A giant statue of Ramesses II, as tall as a four-storey house, greets visitors in the entrance hall. In 2018 it was carried there on a special truck, crawling through the streets at walking pace.

Getting around a giant city

Cairo's metro opened in 1987, the first in Africa. Its lines carry millions of people a day, and some carriages are set aside for women only. Above ground, microbuses, tuk-tuks and taxis squeeze through some of the busiest roads anywhere.

To ease the crowding, Egypt is building a New Administrative Capital about 45 km east of Cairo, with government buildings, a huge mosque and Africa's tallest skyscraper. Many ministries have already moved there.

The city that recycles

On the eastern edge of Cairo lives a community called the Zabbaleen, which means "garbage people". For decades they have collected rubbish from homes across the city by donkey cart and truck, then sorted it by hand. They reuse or sell a huge share of it, far more than many official recycling systems manage.

The city is old, new and noisy all at once. And under its streets and suburbs, archaeologists keep finding tombs, which is why every new building project in Cairo might turn into a dig for mummies.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The pyramids stand alone in the middle of an empty desert.

Truth: The Giza pyramids sit right at the edge of a huge city. Houses, roads and even a pizza shop look straight at the Sphinx, and the desert begins only on the far side.

Measure 25 mm on a ruler. That is all the rain Cairo gets in a year. Put a cup outside the next time it rains where you live, and see how long it takes to collect that much.
