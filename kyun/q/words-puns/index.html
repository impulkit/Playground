Why are puns funny?

About 900 years ago, an Indian poet wrote a single poem that tells the Ramayana and the Mahabharata at the same time, using puns in every verse.

The short answer

A pun is a joke that plays with a word that has two meanings, or two words that sound alike. "I used to be a banker, but I lost interest." The sentence makes sense one way, then suddenly makes sense another way too.

Scientists think puns are funny because of that sudden switch. Your brain reads the sentence, expects one meaning, hits a surprise, and then solves the puzzle. That quick "aha" can feel good, and it can make you laugh.

Puns also make people groan. That groan is part of the game. It says: "I see what you did there, and I wish I didn't."

Surprising but true

Every year since 1978, people have gathered in Austin, Texas, to compete in a pun championship.

Two meanings at once

Your brain stores many meanings for many words. "Bark" can be a dog's sound or a tree's skin. "Light" can mean not heavy or not dark. Normally you pick the right meaning from the context so fast you don't notice.

A pun forces you to hold two meanings at once. Researchers who study humour call this incongruity: something doesn't fit, until suddenly it does. Children usually start enjoying puns in the early school years, around the time they understand that words can have more than one meaning. Before that, puns just sound like mistakes.

Shakespeare's double meanings

Shakespeare loved puns. By some counts there are more than 3,000 in his plays. In Romeo and Juliet, Mercutio is fatally wounded in a sword fight. Even then he jokes: "Ask for me tomorrow, and you shall find me a grave man." He means serious, and he means a man in his grave.

People in Shakespeare's time found puns clever and stylish. Later, some critics in the 1700s and 1800s looked down on them as cheap. Today, newspaper headlines, adverts and children's books use them everywhere.

Poems that tell two stories

Sanskrit poets took puns further than almost anyone. They called the art shlesha, meaning "embrace", because two meanings hold each other in one line. A single verse might describe a king and the Moon at the same time, with every word fitting both.

Around 900 years ago, a poet called Kaviraja wrote the Raghavapandaviya. Read one way, each verse tells part of the Ramayana. Read another way, the very same words tell the Mahabharata. Writing it meant choosing every word in the language of Sanskrit to carry two jobs.

Indian folk tales are full of wordplay too. Clever heroes like Tenali Raman and Birbal often win by twisting the meaning of a king's command.

Puns you can eat

In Chinese, many words sound alike, so puns are part of everyday life and art. The word for fish, yu, sounds like the word for abundance. That is why fish is served at Lunar New Year. The word for bat sounds like the word for good fortune, so bats appear on old Chinese plates and silk as lucky symbols.

Because Chinese characters show meaning as well as sound, a pun can be written with one character but heard as another. Some New Year decorations play on this, hanging a sign for "good fortune" upside down, because "upside down" sounds like "arrived".

Pun champions

Since 1978, Austin, Texas, has hosted the O. Henry Pun-Off World Championships. Contestants stand on stage and fire puns at each other on a set topic, sometimes for minutes without stopping. The audience groans loudest at the best ones.

Puns are a little like riddles. Both hide a second meaning in plain sight and reward the listener who finds it. And the strangest part? Some of the world's oldest riddles, from ancient Greece to India, work exactly the same way.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Great writers avoid puns because they are a low, lazy kind of joke.

Truth: Some of the greatest writers in history loved puns. Shakespeare packed his plays with them, and Sanskrit poets turned double meanings into a high art called shlesha.

Try this

Pick a word with two meanings, like "bat", "bark" or "light". Write one sentence where both meanings could work at once. Try it on someone and watch their face for the half-second pause before they get it, then groan or laugh.
