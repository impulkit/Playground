How do chameleons change colour?

A chameleon doesn't change colour mainly to hide. Most of the time it's showing off, picking fights or warming up.

The short answer

For a long time, scientists thought chameleons changed colour by spreading out or bunching up pigments in their skin, like ink spreading on paper. In 2015, researchers at the University of Geneva found that the real trick is far cleverer.

Under a chameleon's outer skin is a layer of cells called iridophores. These are packed with tiny crystals of a substance called guanine, arranged in a neat grid. When the chameleon relaxes, the crystals sit close together and reflect blue light, which mixes with yellow pigment above to look green. When it gets excited, the skin stretches, the crystals spread apart and they reflect yellow, orange and red instead.

Surprising but true

Chameleons change colour by stretching or relaxing a layer of tiny crystals in their skin, which changes which colours of light bounce back.

Talking in colour

Chameleons mostly change colour to communicate. A male panther chameleon facing a rival turns from green to bright yellow or orange in a minute or two. The brightest, fastest-changing male usually wins without a fight.

Females use colour to say "not interested" to males, often turning dark with bright spots. Chameleons darken when cold, because dark skin soaks up more sunlight, and turn paler when hot. A sick or stressed chameleon may go dull and dark. So a chameleon's skin works more like a mood display than an invisibility cloak.

Colour without paint

Colour from tiny structures is called structural colour. It's how a peacock's tail, a butterfly's wing and a soap bubble get their shimmering colours, without blue or green pigment. A soap bubble changes colour as its film gets thinner, and a chameleon's crystals do something similar as the spacing changes.

Chameleons also have a deeper layer of crystals, bigger and less ordered, which reflects infrared light. Scientists think it may help keep them from overheating in strong sun.

A tongue like a catapult

A chameleon's tongue is another superpower. It can shoot out more than its own body length and grab an insect in a fraction of a second. Small chameleons have the fastest tongues for their size. In 2016, a scientist filmed one tiny species, Rhampholeon spinosus, and found its tongue sped up at more than 250 times the pull of gravity. A fighter pilot blacks out at about 9.

The tongue works like a catapult. Muscles squeeze around an elastic sheath, storing energy, then let go all at once. The sticky tip grips prey with mucus far thicker than human saliva.

Eyes that look two ways

Chameleon eyes sit in turret-like cones and can move separately. One can look forward while the other looks behind. When it spots food, both eyes swivel to lock on, judging distance for the strike.

There are more than 200 species. Nearly half live on the island of Madagascar. One of them, Brookesia nana, described in 2021, has males about 2 cm long including the tail, small enough to sit on a fingertip. India has the Indian chameleon, which lives in dry forests and scrubland from Gujarat to Tamil Nadu and in Sri Lanka.

Masters of colour, but not the only ones

Chameleons aren't the fastest colour changers. Their change takes a minute or two, and only some species, like the panther chameleon, put on dazzling shows. Cuttlefish and octopuses can change in less than a second, using muscles to stretch pigment sacs. Stranger still, octopuses seem to do it while being colourblind, a puzzle that keeps scientists studying how an octopus changes colour.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Chameleons change colour to match whatever background they are on.

Truth: Chameleons are naturally well camouflaged, but they don't copy backgrounds. A chameleon on a red cloth won't turn red. They change colour mostly to send signals to other chameleons, to show their mood, and to control their temperature.

Try this

Look at a CD, the inside of a shell, or a soap bubble in light and tilt it slowly. Watch the colours shift even though nothing is painted on it. That kind of colour, made by tiny structures bending light, is the same kind of trick a chameleon uses.
