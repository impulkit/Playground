What are lassi and chaas?

Both drinks start as curd, which is milk that billions of tiny bacteria have already half-digested for you.

The short answer

Lassi and chaas are both drinks made from curd, the soft, sour, set milk that Indian families often make at home. The difference is mostly in how thick they are and what goes in.

Lassi is thick and creamy. Curd is whisked with a little water, then sweetened with sugar or flavoured with salt. In Punjab, it is often served in a tall steel glass or a clay cup, sometimes with a slab of malai on top. Chaas is thin and salty. More water is added, plus roasted cumin, ginger, green chilli or coriander. It is the everyday drink after a heavy lunch across much of western and southern India.

Surprising but true

Traditional buttermilk has almost no butter in it. It is the thin, sour liquid left behind after the butter has been churned out.

Milk with helpers

Curd is made by tiny living things. When a spoon of old curd goes into warm milk, it carries millions of bacteria, mostly a kind called Lactobacillus. Overnight, they eat the milk's natural sugar, called lactose, and turn it into lactic acid.

The acid makes the milk proteins clump together into a soft, wobbly solid. It also makes it harder for harmful germs to grow, which is one reason sour milk foods kept better before fridges. This kind of change, made by microbes, is called fermentation. The same process gives us idli batter, cheese and bread.

The bacteria work best when they are warm, at about the temperature of your own body or a little above. That is why curd sets in a few hours in May but can take all night in a north Indian winter. Some families wrap the pot in a cloth or tuck it near the stove to help. If the milk is too hot, it kills the bacteria, and nothing sets at all.

Are all germs bad?Right now, about as many bacteria live in and on you as there are cells in your body, and most of them are on your side.

Where the butter went

In many Indian villages, butter is made by churning curd with a wooden churner called a madhani or ravai. Turning it back and forth for a long time knocks the fat droplets together until they form lumps of white butter. These are lifted out, and some are slowly heated to make ghee.

What is left in the pot is real buttermilk: thin, tangy and low in fat. That is the original chaas. Today, most people just thin curd with water instead. In the United States, "buttermilk" in a carton is usually milk soured with added bacteria, used in pancakes and fried chicken more than as a drink.

One drink, many names

Chaas changes its name, and its flavour, from state to state. In Gujarat it is chaas, often with a pinch of black salt. In Tamil Nadu it is neer mor, spiced with curry leaves, ginger and asafoetida. In Karnataka it is majjige, and in Maharashtra taak. In Punjab and Haryana it is chhachh, drunk in large brass glasses in summer.

Lassi changes too. Mango lassi is popular in Indian restaurants from London to New York, and many Americans think of it as India's national drink. In Amritsar, lassi shops pour it so thick that a spoon can almost stand in it.

From kitchen to carton

Today, big dairies pack chaas into pouches and bottles. Amul, the farmers' cooperative from Gujarat, sells a spiced buttermilk that sits in shop fridges across India. Many smaller dairies do the same.

The drinks are cooling in a hot climate partly because they are mostly water with a little salt, which helps the body replace what it loses in sweat. Nutrition experts also point out that curd drinks contain protein, calcium and, if they have not been heated, living bacteria. Whether you drink it sweet or salty is a question families argue about happily.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Drinking cold lassi or curd will give you a cold.

Truth: Colds are caused by viruses, which spread from person to person. A cold drink cannot give you one, though some people find cold food uncomfortable when they already have a sore throat.

Try this

With an adult's help, make two glasses. In one, whisk three spoons of curd with a glass of cold water, a pinch of salt and a pinch of roasted cumin powder. In the other, whisk three spoons of curd with half a glass of water and a spoon of sugar. Taste both and decide which is chaas and which is lassi.
