Can people talk by whistling?

On a steep island in the Atlantic, children learn at school to whistle whole sentences across valleys too deep to shout over.

The short answer

Yes. In dozens of places around the world, people can turn an ordinary spoken language into whistles and hold real conversations with them. These are called whistled languages.

They are not separate languages. They are a way of whistling an existing one, such as Spanish, Turkish or Greek. The whistler copies the rise and fall of the voice and the way sounds change from vowel to consonant. A trained listener can follow it word by word.

Whistled speech usually grows up where people live far apart in steep mountains or thick forests. A whistle carries much farther than a shout, often several kilometres in good conditions, so a farmer can ask a neighbour across the valley if the goats have wandered over.

Surprising but true

A study of whistled Turkish found that listeners used both halves of the brain fairly evenly to understand it, while spoken language leans heavily on the left half.

Silbo on La Gomera

La Gomera, one of Spain's Canary Islands off the coast of Africa, is cut by deep ravines. Walking from one side of a valley to the other can take a long time. For centuries, islanders whistled messages across instead, using a whistled form of Spanish called Silbo Gomero.

By the late 20th century, with roads and phones arriving, fewer young people were learning it. In 1999, the island made Silbo a school subject. In 2009, UNESCO added it to its list of the world's Intangible Cultural Heritage, which honours living traditions rather than buildings.

Silbo whistlers often put a finger or two in the mouth to make the sound louder and cup a hand to aim it.

How a whistle can carry words

When you speak, your voice makes a mix of many pitches at once. A whistle is much purer, one main pitch that can slide up and down. That narrow, high sound cuts through wind and the noise of rivers and travels a long way before it fades.

Some languages, such as the Mazatec language of Mexico or Hmong in Southeast Asia, are tonal: the pitch of a word changes its meaning. Whistlers of those languages mainly whistle the tones. In non-tonal languages like Spanish and Turkish, whistlers turn vowels into different pitches and show consonants as quick jumps, dips or breaks in the whistle.

A whistled sentence is harder to understand than a spoken one, but experienced listeners can recognise a surprising amount.

Bird village, Turkey

In the mountains near Turkey's Black Sea coast lies Kuşköy, whose name means "bird village". Villagers there whistle Turkish to talk across the steep hazelnut slopes. UNESCO listed it in 2017 as a tradition in urgent need of protection, because mobile phones have made it less necessary.

Scientists tested whistlers from the area. Spoken language is handled mostly by the left half of the brain. But when people listened to whistled Turkish, both halves did a fairer share of the work. Whistles, it seems, are processed a bit like music and melody.

A whistling village in India

In the hills of Meghalaya, the Khasi village of Kongthong is famous for a related tradition. Each child is given a unique tune by their mother, which works as a name. People call each other across the forest by whistling or humming these tunes.

It is not a full whistled language, since it is used for names and calls, not whole sentences. But it shows the same idea: in hills where shouting fades, a tune can carry a message to exactly the right person.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Whistled languages are just a few simple signals, like a referee's whistle.

Truth: Many are full languages. Whistlers turn the sounds of an ordinary spoken language into whistles, so they can say almost any sentence, not just "come here" or "danger".

Try this

With a friend, agree on a few whistled "words", such as a rising whistle for "yes", a falling one for "no", two short ones for "come here". Stand at opposite ends of a playground or a long corridor and test how far your messages carry. Then try with talking instead and compare.
