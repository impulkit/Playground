Why is aluminium everywhere?

In the 1850s aluminium was so hard to make that it cost more than silver. Today it wraps your sandwich and gets thrown away without a thought.

The short answer

Aluminium is everywhere because it is light, it doesn't rust away, it carries electricity well and it is easy to shape. It weighs about a third as much as steel for the same size. You'll find it in drink cans, foil, window frames, bicycles, pressure cookers, phones, power lines and aeroplanes.

It is also the most common metal in the Earth's crust, about 8% by weight. The catch is that it never sits around as pure metal. It is locked in rock, mostly an ore called bauxite, and getting it out takes a lot of electricity.

Surprising but true

When the Washington Monument was finished in 1884, its tip was capped with a small pyramid of aluminium, then a rare and costly metal chosen to show off.

Dearer than silver

Aluminium was first made as a metal in the 1820s, using expensive chemicals. For decades, it came out in tiny lumps. Emperor Napoleon III of France is said to have served his most honoured guests with aluminium cutlery while lesser guests used gold. The story may be partly legend, but aluminium really was a luxury.

The Washington Monument in the US capital was finished in 1884. Builders topped it with a small aluminium pyramid, about 23 centimetres tall. It was one of the largest single pieces of the metal ever cast at that time, and it was put on show in a New York jewellery shop before going up.

The year it became cheap

In 1886, two young men working on opposite sides of the Atlantic found the same answer within weeks of each other. Charles Martin Hall in Ohio and Paul Héroult in France both used electricity to pull aluminium out of its oxide.

Their method is still used. Aluminium oxide is dissolved in a bath of molten mineral at about 950 °C, and a huge electric current passes through it. Pure aluminium collects at the bottom like a silver pool. Within a few years, the price had fallen to a small fraction of what it had been.

From red earth to soft drink can

Bauxite is a reddish, crumbly rock that forms in hot, rainy places when other minerals wash out of the soil. India has large deposits, especially in the hills of Odisha, where the company NALCO runs mines and refineries. Australia, Guinea and Brazil are other big sources.

First, bauxite is crushed and cooked with chemicals to make alumina, a white powder like flour. About two tonnes of alumina make one tonne of aluminium. The smelting step uses so much power that aluminium plants are often built near big power stations or dams. In 2024, China made more than half of the world's aluminium, and India was in second place.

Why it doesn't rust away

Iron rusts, flaking and crumbling until it is gone. Aluminium reacts with air even faster, but in a useful way. In a split second, it grows a skin of aluminium oxide only a few millionths of a millimetre thick. This tough, see-through layer seals the metal underneath and stops it reacting any further.

That's why an aluminium ladder can sit in the rain for years and still look fine. It's also why aluminium is used in planes and ships, where weight and corrosion both matter.

The can that comes back

Aluminium can be melted and reused again and again without getting worse. Recycling it uses only about 5% of the energy needed to make new metal from bauxite. A large share of all the aluminium ever made is still in use today, in buildings, cars and cables.

In India, kabadiwalas, the people who buy old newspapers and scrap, have long paid for old aluminium pots and cans. Melted down and rolled out again, an old can may be back on a shop shelf within a few months, holding a cold drink.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Aluminium foil has a shiny side and a dull side because one side is meant for cooking.

Truth: Both sides work the same. Foil is so thin that, in the last stage of rolling, two sheets go through the rollers together. The sides touching the polished rollers come out shiny and the sides touching each other come out dull.

Try this

Collect a few clean empty drink cans, a steel spoon and a fridge magnet. Test which things the magnet sticks to. Aluminium isn't magnetic, which is one way recycling plants sort aluminium cans from steel ones.
