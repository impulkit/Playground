Which dogs changed history?

In 1925, teams of sled dogs raced medicine about 1,085 km across frozen Alaska in under six days to save a town from a deadly disease.

The short answer

Dogs have been working beside humans for thousands of years, so plenty have shaped history. Laika was the first animal to orbit Earth, in 1957. Sled dogs Togo and Balto helped carry medicine to a sick town in Alaska in 1925. Hachiko, a loyal dog in Tokyo, became a symbol of devotion in Japan.

Other dogs saved lives with their noses or their courage. Barry the St Bernard rescued travellers lost in the Alps. In Mumbai, a police dog named Zanjeer helped find hidden weapons and explosives after the 1993 bomb blasts.

Surprising but true

The famous statue of Balto in New York's Central Park honours the final leg of the 1925 serum run, but another dog, Togo, ran the longest and most dangerous stretch.

The race to Nome

In January 1925, a deadly disease called diphtheria broke out in Nome, a small town in Alaska. Children were most at risk. The only medicine, called serum, was hundreds of kilometres away. Winter had shut the sea, and the railway stopped far short of Nome.

So the serum went by dog sled, in a relay. About 20 drivers, called mushers, and roughly 150 dogs carried it about 1,085 km in about five and a half days, through blizzards and temperatures far below -40°C. A trip that normally took weeks was done in under six days.

The lead dog Togo and his musher Leonhard Seppala covered the longest and most dangerous leg, across sea ice that could crack. Balto led the last team into Nome. Newspapers made Balto a star, and a statue of him was put up in New York's Central Park that same year.

The first dog in orbit

On 3 November 1957, the Soviet Union launched Sputnik 2 carrying Laika, a stray dog from the streets of Moscow. She became the first animal to orbit Earth. Scientists wanted to know if a living thing could survive launch and weightlessness.

There was no way to bring her back. For decades, Soviet officials said she lived several days. In 2002 a scientist revealed she had died within hours, as her capsule overheated. Three years later, in 1960, two dogs named Belka and Strelka orbited Earth and came home safely. Their flight helped pave the way for the first human in space in 1961, part of the story of animals in space.

Which animals have gone to space?The first animals in space weren't dogs or monkeys. They were fruit flies, launched from New Mexico in 1947, and they came back alive.

Hachiko, the dog who waited

Hachiko was an Akita dog in Tokyo. Every evening he met his owner, a university professor, at Shibuya railway station. In 1925 the professor died suddenly at work and never came home.

Hachiko kept going to the station every day for about nine years, until he died in 1935. Commuters fed him and newspapers told his story. Today a bronze statue of him stands outside Shibuya station, one of Tokyo's most famous meeting points.

Noses that save lives

A dog's nose has hundreds of millions of scent receptors, far more than ours, as explained in a dog's nose. That makes dogs superb searchers.

Barry, a St Bernard at a mountain monastery in the Swiss Alps in the early 1800s, is credited with saving about 40 people. Mountain teams still use dogs to find people buried in snow.

In March 1993, bombs went off across Mumbai. A Labrador police dog named Zanjeer helped search the city. He sniffed out grenades, rifles and explosives hidden in scooters and suitcases. When he died in 2000, Zanjeer was given a funeral with full police honours.

A soldier dog

In the First World War, a stray dog named Stubby was smuggled to France by an American soldier. He learned to warn troops of gas attacks and incoming shells. He became one of the most famous dogs of the war and met three US presidents.

Dogs first lived with people when early humans tamed wolves, at least 15,000 years ago, though scientists still debate exactly when and where. That ancient friendship is told in domestic dogs.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Balto ran the whole serum run by himself and saved Nome.

Truth: It was a relay of about 20 drivers and around 150 dogs. Balto led the final team into Nome, so he became famous. Togo, led by musher Leonhard Seppala, ran the longest and most dangerous section.

Try this

Test your nose against a dog's. Ask an adult to hide a cotton ball dabbed with vanilla or orange peel somewhere in one room. Try to find it by smell alone. A trained dog's nose is thousands of times more sensitive, which is why police use them to search.
