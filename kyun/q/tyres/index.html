Why do tyres have patterns?

The grooves on a car tyre are drains. At highway speed in heavy rain, a tyre can push away several litres of water every second.

The short answer

The patterns on a tyre, called the tread, are mainly there to deal with water. When a road is wet, the grooves give water somewhere to go, so the rubber can still touch the road. Without them, a tyre could float on a thin layer of water and lose its grip.

Treads also help on mud, gravel and snow, where the blocks of rubber bite into the ground. That's why a tractor tyre looks so different from a racing car tyre. Each pattern is designed for a job.

Your shoes work the same way. Trainers and school shoes have grooved soles for the same reason, so you don't slip on a wet bathroom floor or a muddy playground. A smooth leather sole feels fine on carpet and skids on a rainy pavement.

Surprising but true

Early car tyres were pale, not black. Rubber turned black when makers started adding a sooty powder called carbon black, which makes tyres much tougher.

Squeezing out the rain

Picture a tyre rolling over a wet road. Water gets trapped between the rubber and the road. The tread's grooves act like drains, squirting that water out to the sides and back.

If the grooves can't clear the water fast enough, the tyre starts riding on top of it. This is called aquaplaning, or hydroplaning in the US. The steering suddenly feels light and the brakes do little, because the tyre has almost no friction with the road. It's most dangerous at high speed on roads flooded by a monsoon downpour.

Grooves, blocks and slits

A tyre's tread has a few parts. Long grooves running around the tyre carry water away. Cross grooves link them to the sides. Between the grooves sit blocks of rubber that grip the road.

Many tyres also have sipes, tiny slits cut into the blocks. When the tyre flexes, the sipes open slightly and grab at wet or icy surfaces. Winter tyres in snowy places like Canada or Himachal can have hundreds of them. Off-road tyres have big, chunky blocks with wide gaps that dig into mud and then fling it out.

Why racing tyres are smooth

On a dry track, grooves are a waste. Every groove means less rubber touching the road. So Formula One cars use slicks, smooth tyres with no tread, when it's dry. They grip so hard that they get hot and sticky, almost like chewing gum.

If it starts to rain, teams rush the cars into the pits and swap to grooved wet-weather tyres. A slick on a wet track would aquaplane almost at once.

From cushions of air to black rubber

In 1845 a Scottish engineer, Robert William Thomson, patented a tyre filled with air, but it didn't catch on. In 1888 John Boyd Dunlop, a vet in Belfast, made air-filled tyres for his son's tricycle to give a smoother ride. They soon spread to bicycles and then cars.

Early rubber tyres were pale. Makers found that mixing in carbon black, a fine soot, made rubber much tougher. Tyres have been black ever since. In India, the company MRF started in 1946 by making toy balloons in Madras, now Chennai, before it moved on to tyres.

When the pattern wears out

Every time you drive, a tiny layer of rubber wears away. As the tread gets shallower, the grooves can't carry as much water. Many countries, including the UK and most of Europe, set a legal minimum tread depth of 1.6 millimetres, about the thickness of a coin. Most US states use a similar limit.

Many tyres have small bars, called wear indicators, moulded into the bottom of the grooves. When the tread wears down level with them, it's time for new tyres. Worn tyres take much longer to stop on a wet road, even with good brakes.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Tyres are black because rubber is naturally black.

Truth: Natural rubber from trees is milky white or pale yellow. Tyres are black because makers add carbon black, a fine soot that makes the rubber stronger and helps it last longer.

Try this

Find an old shoe with a smooth sole and a trainer with deep grooves. Splash some water on a smooth floor tile or tray and push each shoe sideways across it with your hand, then try on a dry patch. Notice which one slips more on the wet surface, and why.
