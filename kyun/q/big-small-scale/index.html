How small is an atom?

Line up about a million atoms side by side and they would only just stretch across a single human hair.

The short answer

An atom is about a tenth of a billionth of a metre across, depending on the type. That size is so small that ordinary words stop working, so scientists use the nanometre, a billionth of a metre. Most atoms are between 0.1 and 0.5 nanometres wide.

To picture it, take a single human hair. It is very roughly a tenth of a millimetre thick. You could lay something like a million atoms side by side across it. A full stop at the end of this sentence could hold billions of billions of them.

Everything you can touch, from a cricket ball to your own skin, is built from atoms like these.

Surprising but true

An atom is almost entirely empty space. If an atom were as big as a cricket stadium, its nucleus would be about the size of a pea in the middle.

Shrinking by tens

The easiest way to travel down to an atom is in steps of ten.

Start with your hand, about 10 centimetres wide. Ten times smaller is a fingernail's width, about 1 centimetre. Ten times smaller again is a grain of sand, about a millimetre. Ten more is the width of a hair. Ten more is a single cell in your body. Keep going: a bacterium, then a virus, then a large molecule, then a small one.

After about nine steps from your hand, you arrive at an atom. Each step divides the size by ten, so nine steps divide it by a thousand million. Scientists write this as 10⁻¹⁰ metres.

In 1977, the American designers Charles and Ray Eames made a short film called Powers of Ten. It zooms out from a picnic to the edge of the known universe, then back into a single atom, one step of ten at a time.

Mostly nothing

An atom has a tiny centre called a nucleus, made of protons and neutrons. Around it, much further out, are even lighter particles called electrons.

The nucleus is about 100,000 times smaller across than the whole atom. If an atom were blown up to the size of a cricket stadium, its nucleus would be roughly a pea on the centre of the pitch. The electrons would be somewhere out in the stands.

Yet almost all the atom's mass is in that pea. The rest is mostly empty space. You don't fall through your chair because the electrons in your atoms and the chair's atoms push each other away.

Seeing the unseeable

For a long time, atoms were an idea. Around 2,400 years ago, the Greek thinker Democritus suggested that everything was made of tiny pieces that couldn't be cut, atomos. In India, the philosopher Kanada, of the Vaisheshika school, taught a similar idea of tiny eternal particles called paramanu.

Atoms are far too small to see with an ordinary microscope, because they are smaller than the waves of light. Then, in 1981, two scientists at IBM in Zurich, Gerd Binnig and Heinrich Rohrer, built the scanning tunnelling microscope. It feels a surface with an extremely sharp tip and maps the bumps, one atom at a time. They won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1986.

In 1989, IBM scientist Don Eigler used one of these microscopes to move 35 single atoms of xenon into the letters "IBM".

From tiny to huge

Scientists use the same powers of ten to go upwards. Your body contains about 30 to 40 lakh crore cells, each made of countless atoms. A strand of DNA is only about 2 nanometres wide but, stretched out, the DNA in one of your cells would be about 2 metres long.

Going up instead of down, the Earth is about 10⁷ metres across. Our galaxy is about 10²¹ metres. Somewhere in the middle of all those steps, at about one or two metres, is you.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Atoms are tiny, solid little balls.

Truth: Atoms are mostly empty space, with a tiny heavy nucleus in the middle and electrons spread out in a fuzzy cloud around it. Pictures of atoms as hard balls, or as little solar systems, are just handy drawings.

Try this

Draw a line 10 cm long and divide it into ten equal parts of 1 cm. Then try to divide one of those into ten parts of 1 mm each. You can probably manage one more step with a sharp pencil. To reach the size of an atom, you'd need to keep dividing by ten about seven more times.
