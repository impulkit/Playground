How did Hindi grow?

Hindi grew from the everyday speech of the country around Delhi. Today it is one of the most spoken languages on Earth.

The short answer

Hindi grew slowly over more than a thousand years. Its deep roots go back to Sanskrit, through everyday spoken languages called Prakrits and later forms called Apabhramsha. The standard Hindi used today grew from Khari Boli, the speech of the region around Delhi and Meerut.

Over the centuries it picked up words from Persian, Arabic, Turkish, Portuguese and English. It is written in the Devanagari script, the same one used for Sanskrit, Marathi and Nepali.

In the 2011 census, about 53 crore people in India gave Hindi as their mother tongue, and many more speak it as a second language. That makes it one of the most widely spoken languages in the world.

Surprising but true

Some everyday Hindi words, like almari (cupboard) and chabi (key), came from Portuguese.

From Sanskrit to Khari Boli

Languages change a little with every generation, like a river slowly shifting its course. The Sanskrit of ancient texts gave way, in everyday speech, to simpler Prakrits. These in turn changed into many regional languages across north India: Braj, Awadhi, Bhojpuri, Maithili and others.

Some of the greatest poetry in the Hindi tradition was written in these regional forms. In the 1500s, Tulsidas wrote the Ramcharitmanas, a retelling of the Ramayana, in Awadhi. Surdas sang of Krishna in Braj Bhasha. Saint-poets such as Kabir and Mirabai used plain, everyday words that farmers, weavers and traders could understand.

Hindi and Urdu, a shared root

In the markets and army camps around Delhi, from about the 1200s onwards, Khari Boli mixed with Persian, the language of the royal courts. The result was a lively common language often called Hindustani. Over time it split into two written styles. Urdu uses a Persian-Arabic script and more Persian and Arabic words. Hindi uses Devanagari and more words drawn from Sanskrit.

In everyday speech, the two are so close that a Hindi speaker and an Urdu speaker usually understand each other easily. The writer Premchand wrote his stories in both. Many Hindi film songs use words that belong equally to the two.

Words from far away

Hindi is a magpie language, collecting shiny words from everyone who came. From Persian came darwaza (door) and dukaan (shop), and through Persian came Arabic words such as kitab (book). Portuguese traders, who arrived in the 1500s, left almari (cupboard), chabi (key), mez (table) and balti (bucket). English has added school, bus, doctor and hundreds more.

The flow goes the other way too. English speakers say pyjamas, shampoo and bungalow without knowing they are using words from India. Many people now mix Hindi and English in a single sentence, a style nicknamed Hinglish, heard everywhere from college canteens to TV adverts.

Becoming an official language

On 14 September 1949, India's Constituent Assembly decided that Hindi, written in Devanagari, would be the official language of the central government, with English continuing alongside it for official work. That date is now celebrated as Hindi Diwas.

The choice was hotly debated, especially by people in states where Tamil, Bengali and other languages are spoken. India's Constitution does not name any national language, and today it lists 22 languages in its Eighth Schedule.

Hindi has also travelled abroad. In the 1800s and early 1900s, many Indian workers sailed to distant British colonies, and forms of Hindi are still spoken by their descendants in Fiji, Mauritius, Suriname and Trinidad.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Hindi is India's national language.

Truth: India's Constitution does not name any national language. Hindi in Devanagari is an official language of the central government, used alongside English, and the Constitution lists 22 languages in all.
