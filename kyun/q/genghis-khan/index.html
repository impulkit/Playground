Who was Genghis Khan?

As a boy, Temujin was abandoned with his family on the freezing steppe and lived on roots and fish. He grew up to rule the largest land empire the world had seen.

The short answer

Genghis Khan was a Mongol leader who united the warring tribes of the Mongolian steppe in 1206 and then launched conquests across Asia. He was born with the name Temujin, probably around 1162. "Genghis Khan" was a title, often translated as "universal ruler", though its exact meaning is debated.

By his death in 1227, his armies had conquered northern China, Central Asia and parts of Persia. His sons and grandsons pushed further, creating the largest connected land empire in history, the Mongol Empire. In Mongolia he is honoured as the founder of the nation. In many places he conquered, he is remembered for terrible destruction.

Surprising but true

In 1221 Genghis Khan chased an enemy prince all the way to the banks of the Indus, then turned back, sparing the Delhi Sultanate from a Mongol invasion at that time.

A hard childhood

Temujin's father, Yesugei, was a chief. When Temujin was about nine, his father was poisoned by members of a rival tribe, the Tatars. His father's followers then abandoned the family.

Temujin's mother, Hoelun, kept her children alive by gathering wild roots and berries and catching fish and small animals. Life on the steppe was harsh and dangerous. As a teenager Temujin was captured by enemies and escaped. Later his wife, Borte, was kidnapped by another tribe, and he gathered allies to rescue her. Step by step, he built up a band of loyal followers.

Uniting the tribes

The steppe was home to many tribes who raided and fought each other. Temujin defeated rivals one by one, including old friends who turned against him. Instead of rewarding only his relatives, he promoted people who were loyal and skilled, even former enemies.

In 1206 a great gathering of Mongol leaders, a kurultai, proclaimed him Genghis Khan. He organised his people into units of ten, a hundred and a thousand, broke up old tribal loyalties, and issued rules known as the Yassa. No complete copy of the Yassa survives, so historians know it only from later accounts.

Writing and law

The Mongols had no written language. Genghis Khan ordered a captured Uyghur scribe to adapt the Uyghur script for Mongolian. This allowed laws, orders and records to be written down. The script, written in vertical columns, is still used today.

He also allowed people of different religions to worship freely. Buddhist, Muslim, Christian and Daoist leaders all visited his court. For merchants, he made the roads safer, which helped trade along the Silk Road.

Conquest and its cost

Genghis Khan's armies attacked the Tangut kingdom of Western Xia and the Jin dynasty of northern China, capturing its capital, today's Beijing, in 1215. In 1219 he invaded the Khwarazmian Empire, in Central Asia and Iran, after its governor killed a group of Mongol-sponsored merchants and the shah killed his envoys.

The campaign was devastating. Great cities such as Bukhara, Samarkand and Merv were captured, and many were destroyed. In 1221 he chased the Khwarazmian prince Jalal ad-Din to the Indus river. The prince famously escaped by riding his horse into the river and swimming across. He sought shelter with Sultan Iltutmish of Delhi, who wisely kept his distance. Genghis Khan turned back, and the Delhi Sultanate was spared for the time being.

A hidden grave and a genetic trail

Genghis Khan died in 1227, during a campaign against the Tanguts. How he died is unclear. He was buried in a secret place, probably in the Khentii mountains of Mongolia, and the location has never been found.

In 2003, geneticists found that about 8 per cent of men in a large region of Asia carry a very similar Y chromosome, the part of DNA passed from father to son. They suggested it might come from Genghis Khan and his male relatives. Other scientists think the evidence is not strong enough to name him. Today his face appears on Mongolian banknotes, and Ulaanbaatar's international airport bears his name.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Genghis Khan was nothing more than a brutal destroyer.

Truth: His wars caused enormous death and destruction. He also created a written script for Mongolian, promoted people by merit, allowed religious freedom and helped connect trade across Asia. Historians try to weigh both sides.
