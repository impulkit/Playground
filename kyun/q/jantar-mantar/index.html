What is Jantar Mantar?

In Jaipur stands a sundial as tall as an eight-storey building, and its shadow creeps fast enough for you to watch it move.

The short answer

Jantar Mantar is the name of five observatories built in the early 1700s by Maharaja Sawai Jai Singh II, the ruler who founded Jaipur. Instead of telescopes, they are filled with giant instruments made of stone, brick and plaster, used to track the Sun, Moon and stars with the naked eye.

The largest and best preserved is in Jaipur. It has about 20 main instruments, including the Samrat Yantra, a sundial about 27 m tall. UNESCO made it a World Heritage Site in 2010. There is another famous Jantar Mantar in Delhi, and smaller ones in Ujjain and Varanasi. The fifth, in Mathura, has disappeared.

The name comes from "yantra", meaning instrument, and "mantra", which can mean formula or calculation.

Surprising but true

The shadow on the giant sundial in Jaipur moves about a millimetre every second, so you can see time passing with your own eyes.

A king who loved the sky

Jai Singh II became ruler of Amber, near today's Jaipur, in 1699, when he was a boy. He grew up studying mathematics and astronomy. He found that the star tables used in his time often gave wrong positions for the planets and the times of eclipses.

Metal instruments were small and could bend or wear. So Jai Singh decided to build huge ones in stone, which he thought would be steadier and more accurate. He collected books on astronomy in Sanskrit, Persian and Arabic, and sent scholars to learn about methods used in other countries. The result was a set of tables of star and planet positions, dedicated to the Mughal emperor Muhammad Shah.

The giant sundial

The Samrat Yantra, or "supreme instrument", is a huge right-angled triangle of stone with a staircase up its sloping side. The slope points at the North Star and is parallel to Earth's axis. Curved scales on each side catch the shadow.

As the Earth turns, the shadow sweeps across the scales at about 1 mm a second. Stand beside it and you can watch it creep, a few centimetres every minute. Marks on the scales let readers tell local time to within a few seconds, more precisely than most clocks of the age.

Why is Jaipur called the Pink City?In 1876, a maharaja painted his whole old city the colour of terracotta to welcome a royal guest. It has stayed pink ever since.

Bowls, rings and zodiac signs

Other instruments are just as unusual. The Jai Prakash Yantra is a pair of bowls sunk into the ground, each one a map of the sky turned inside out. A cross-wire above them casts a small shadow onto the marked surface, showing where the Sun is in the sky.

The Ram Yantra is a pair of round, roofless buildings with pillars and marked floors, used to measure how high a star or planet is and in which direction. The Rashivalaya Yantra is a group of 12 instruments, one for each sign of the zodiac. Astronomers and astrologers of the time used it to measure the positions of the constellations along the Sun's path.

Still standing, still working

After Jai Singh died in 1743, astronomy based on telescopes took over, and naked-eye observatories fell out of use. Some instruments crumbled. In the early 1900s, the Jaipur instruments were restored, and they still work today.

For visitors, the Jaipur observatory is a playground of strange shapes. With a guide, students can see how people measured the sky without any electronics. The Delhi Jantar Mantar, close to Connaught Place, is also well known as a place where people gather for peaceful protests.

Ancient Indian astronomers had calculated the sky with mathematics and careful observation for over a thousand years. Jai Singh's stone instruments were one of the last great efforts of that tradition before the telescope changed everything.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Jantar Mantar is a ruin from ancient times.

Truth: The observatories are less than 300 years old, built in the 1720s and 1730s. Many of the Jaipur instruments were restored in the early 1900s and can still be used to measure time and the positions of stars.

Try this

With a teacher or adult, stand a stick upright in a pot of sand or soil in a sunny spot in the schoolyard or on a balcony. Every half hour, mark the tip of its shadow with a pebble. By the end of the day you will have built a simple sundial, and you can compare it with a clock.
