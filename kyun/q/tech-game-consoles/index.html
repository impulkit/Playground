How do game consoles work?

A modern games console has millions of times more memory than the computer that guided astronauts to the Moon.

The short answer

A games console is a computer built for one job: running games smoothly on a TV. Inside are a few key parts. The CPU is the brain that follows the game's rules. The GPU is a graphics chip that draws the pictures. Memory holds what the game needs right now, and storage keeps the game itself.

You press a button on the controller. The CPU works out what happens, maybe your character jumps. The GPU then draws the new scene, and the console sends it to the screen. This loop repeats 30, 60 or even 120 times every second, so smoothly that it feels like a moving world.

Surprising but true

The best-selling games console ever is still the PlayStation 2 from 2000, with about 160 million sold, and the original Nintendo Switch is close behind (2026).

The brain and the artist

The CPU handles the thinking. It checks where every enemy is, whether you hit the ball, and what the physics says should happen when a car crashes.

The GPU is the artist. Modern game worlds are built from millions of tiny triangles. For every frame, the GPU works out where each triangle sits, how light falls on it and what colour every pixel should be. A GPU has thousands of small processing cores working side by side, all built on silicon chips with billions of tiny switches.

From one dot to whole worlds

The first home console was the Magnavox Odyssey, sold in the USA in 1972. Its inventor, Ralph Baer, had built a test version known as the Brown Box. The Odyssey showed only a few white squares and lines. To add colour, players stuck plastic overlays onto their TV screens.

Then came the Atari 2600 in 1977 and the Nintendo Entertainment System in the 1980s, with games like Super Mario Bros. Each new generation packed more power into the same box under the TV.

Moon computer versus game box

The computer that guided Apollo astronauts to the Moon in 1969 had about 4 kilobytes of working memory. A PlayStation 5 has 16 gigabytes. That is roughly four million times more.

All of it is stored as binary code, long strings of 1s and 0s. A big modern game can take up over 100 gigabytes, about as much as the plain text of 100,000 novels.

The controller in your hands

A controller is a small computer of its own. Each button closes a tiny electrical circuit when pressed. The thumbsticks measure how far you push them, many times a second, and send those numbers to the console by wire or radio.

Many controllers also hide little motors with off-centre weights. When they spin, the controller rumbles, so you feel a crash or an explosion as well as seeing it. Some newer ones can tighten their triggers, so pulling a bowstring in a game really feels harder.

Why consoles are so cheap for what they do

Building a gaming computer with the same power often costs more than a console. Console makers sometimes sell the machine at little or no profit, then earn money on games, subscriptions and online services.

Because every copy of a console is identical, game makers know exactly what hardware they are writing for. That lets them squeeze out performance a general computer can't always match.

Best-sellers and Indian living rooms

As of 2026, the best-selling console ever is still Sony's PlayStation 2 from 2000, with about 160 million sold. The original Nintendo Switch, launched in 2017, had reached about 156 million and was closing in. Its follow-up, the Switch 2, came out in 2025.

In 1990s India, few families could buy an official Nintendo. Instead, many homes had cheap copies of the Famicom, sold as "TV video games" with yellow cartridges claiming hundreds of games in one. Lots of those games were the same handful repeated. Children still played them for hours, humming the tunes that the story of video game music explains.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The console's speed is the only thing that decides how good a game looks.

Truth: The game's design and its software matter just as much. Clever programmers have made older, slower consoles produce beautiful games, while a careless game can look poor on the newest machine.

Try this

Make a flip-book game: on the corners of 20 pages of an old notebook, draw a tiny character jumping over a box, moving it a little on each page. Flip fast and watch it move. A console does the same thing, only 60 times a second.
