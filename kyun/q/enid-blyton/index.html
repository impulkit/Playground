Why are Enid Blyton's books so famous in India?

Generations of Indian children who had never seen an English village knew exactly what a picnic at Kirrin Island should taste like.

The short answer

Enid Blyton was an English writer who wrote more than 600 books for children between the 1920s and 1960s. Her series, such as the Famous Five, the Secret Seven, Malory Towers and The Faraway Tree, have sold hundreds of millions of copies worldwide.

In India, her books became a childhood habit for generations, especially in English-medium schools. They were easy to read, exciting, and came in long series, so a child who liked one had dozens more to find. School libraries, lending libraries and second-hand bookshops kept them in circulation for decades. Many Indian adults say Blyton was the first author they read on their own.

Surprising but true

The famous phrase "lashings of ginger beer" never appears in the Famous Five books. It comes from a 1982 TV parody of them.

Picnics, secret passages and no grown-ups

Blyton's stories are built around a simple, irresistible idea: children having adventures without adults. The Famous Five appeared in 21 books between 1942 and 1963. Three siblings, their cousin George and her dog Timmy explore islands, caves and smugglers' tunnels and catch criminals while the adults are elsewhere. Her books have been translated so often that UNESCO's Index Translationum lists her among the most translated authors in the world.

Her boarding-school stories, Malory Towers and St Clare's, follow girls through midnight feasts, lacrosse matches and quarrels. Her fantasy books, like The Magic Faraway Tree, take readers up a tree to strange lands that drift past at the top.

She wrote in plain words and short chapters, with lots of dialogue. Each chapter ended with a reason to read the next. That made her books ideal for children just starting to read longer stories, much as Harry Potter did later.

Why India took to her

Blyton's books came to India while it was still under British rule, through missionary schools and English-medium education. After independence in 1947, English remained a language of schools and jobs, and her books stayed on library shelves.

They were also cheap and plentiful. Paperbacks were passed between cousins, swapped in school and rented from small lending libraries in many Indian towns. Indian readers learned about scones, ginger beer, potted meat and tinned peaches long before most had tasted them.

Many Indian children also attended schools with houses, prefects and uniforms. The boarding-school world of Malory Towers felt both foreign and familiar.

The criticism

Blyton has been criticised for a long time. In the 1930s to 1950s, the BBC turned down her work for radio, saying it lacked literary value. Later, some libraries removed her books.

Critics pointed to a limited vocabulary and to attitudes that look badly outdated. Some books contain racist stereotypes. Girls are often shown doing the cooking while boys lead, though George, in the Famous Five, insists on being treated like a boy. Many newer editions have been edited to remove offensive words and update names. In 2022, when English Heritage put up a blue plaque at her former London home, it noted these criticisms.

Supporters reply that her books have got millions of children reading for pleasure, which is no small thing.

Still on the shelves

Blyton's books are still sold in India, and new editions keep appearing. Many Indian writers in English, and many readers who later moved on to Ruskin Bond, Tolkien or Harry Potter, started with her.

Children now also read Indian adventure stories set in places they know. But in many Indian homes, a battered Famous Five paperback is still passed down from parent to child. As an English-language book loved in a country far from England, it is a small piece of history in its own right.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Famous Five are always saying "lashings of ginger beer".

Truth: They drink plenty of ginger beer, but the phrase "lashings of ginger beer" is not in the books. It became famous from a 1982 British comedy, "Five Go Mad in Dorset", that poked fun at the stories.
