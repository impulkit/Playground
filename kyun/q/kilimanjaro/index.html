What is Mount Kilimanjaro?

Africa's highest mountain stands only about 340 km south of the equator, and yet ice still clings to its summit, for now.

The short answer

Mount Kilimanjaro is the highest mountain in Africa, at 5,895 m. It rises alone from the plains of northern Tanzania, near the border with Kenya. Unlike the Himalayas, it isn't part of a range. It's a giant volcano, built up by eruptions over hundreds of thousands of years.

From the dry grassland at its foot, it climbs almost 5 km into the sky. That makes it one of the tallest free-standing mountains on Earth. Every year tens of thousands of people try to walk to the summit. You need no ropes or climbing gear on the main routes, but you do need patience, warm clothes and lungs that can cope with thin air.

Surprising but true

Kilimanjaro has lost more than 80% of the ice it had in 1912.

Three volcanoes in one

Kilimanjaro is made of three volcanic cones. Shira, in the west, is the oldest and has mostly worn away. Mawenzi, in the east, is a jagged, broken peak. Kibo, in the middle, is the youngest and tallest, and its crater rim holds Uhuru Peak, the highest point.

Nobody is quite sure where the name Kilimanjaro comes from, though several Swahili and Chagga meanings have been suggested.

Kibo is dormant, not dead. It hasn't erupted for a very long time, but there are still vents near the summit where gas seeps out. The mountain grew as part of the forces that are slowly pulling East Africa apart, the same ones that formed the Great Rift Valley.

From rainforest to ice

Climbing Kilimanjaro is like travelling from the equator to the Arctic in a week. The lower slopes are farmland, where the Chagga people grow bananas and coffee. Above that is thick rainforest with colobus monkeys. Higher still come heath and moorland, then a stony alpine desert with almost no plants.

At the summit, nights fall far below freezing. Strange plants grow on the way up, including giant groundsels that look like cabbages on tree trunks and can be taller than a person.

Thin air and steady feet

The biggest danger is altitude. At the top, each breath holds only about half as much oxygen as at sea level. Climbers who go up too fast can get headaches and sickness. In rare cases it becomes life-threatening. Guides tell everyone "pole pole", Swahili for "slowly, slowly".

The first recorded climb to the summit was in 1889, by the German geographer Hans Meyer and the Austrian climber Ludwig Purtscheller. They were helped by a young local guide, Yohani Lauwo, and other porters whose names were mostly not written down. Today, thousands of Tanzanian porters carry tents, food and water for climbers. A typical group of tourists may have three or four helpers for every climber, and the porters often reach camp first, with tents already up.

The disappearing ice

In 1912, ice covered much of Kibo's summit. Since then more than 80% has vanished. Some of the remaining glaciers look like white cliffs standing on brown rock.

Scientists study why. Warmer temperatures play a part. So does drier air, which means less snow falls to replace the ice, and strong sunshine that turns ice straight into water vapour. Many expect the last of Kilimanjaro's ice to disappear within a few decades.

The writer Ernest Hemingway made the mountain famous in 1936 with a story called "The Snows of Kilimanjaro". Children climbing it in the future may find only bare rock at the top. For the people on its slopes, the bigger worry is water. The melting glaciers supply only a little of it. The forests matter more, because they catch rain and mist and feed the streams that farms depend on, so protecting those trees has become a big part of caring for the mountain.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Places near the equator are always too hot for snow and ice.

Truth: Temperature drops as you go higher. Kilimanjaro, Mount Kenya and the Rwenzori Mountains in Africa, and peaks in the Andes, all have permanent ice even though they sit close to the equator.
