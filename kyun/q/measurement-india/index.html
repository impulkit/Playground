What units did India use before the metre?

Before kilograms, Indian goldsmiths weighed gold against tiny red-and-black gunja seeds, and the tola they built from them is still used in jewellery shops today.

The short answer

For thousands of years, India measured with its own units. Gold and spices were weighed in ratti, masha and tola. Rice and wheat were sold by the ser and the maund. Cloth was measured in gaj, land in bigha, and long distances in kos.

These units grew from everyday things: seeds, hands, arms and footsteps. They worked well inside one town or kingdom. But a ser in one market might not match a ser in the next, and that made trade and taxes messy.

After independence, India switched to the metric system, with grams, metres and litres. The change began with a law in 1956 and took several years. Some old units still survive, especially at the jeweller's.

Surprising but true

The weights of the Indus cities, over 4,000 years old, followed a doubling pattern, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, much like the way computers count in binary today.

The world's oldest standard weights

Long before kings and coins, the Harappan cities of the Indus valley used carefully cut cubes of stone, often a pale banded rock called chert, as weights. They have been found from Gujarat to Pakistan, and they match each other closely.

The smaller weights double each time: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64 units. Larger ones jump in tens. That doubling is the same idea computers use in binary counting.

At Lothal in Gujarat, archaeologists found a scale made of ivory with fine lines cut into it, used for measuring length. Matching weights across a huge area suggests a shared system that traders trusted.

Seeds as weights

Goldsmiths needed tiny, reliable weights. One answer was the seed of the gunja vine, bright red with a black spot. Gunja seeds are fairly even in size, so the ratti, from the Sanskrit raktika, linked to the word for red, became a unit of about a tenth of a gram.

From the ratti came bigger units. Eight ratti made a masha, and twelve masha made a tola. In British India the tola was fixed at 180 grains, about 11.66 grams. The silver rupee coin itself was minted to weigh one tola.

Even today, many people talk about the price of gold "per tola". In practice, jewellers often round it to 10 grams.

Ser, maund and the market

For grain and vegetables, people used the ser and the maund. In British India the standard ser was 80 tola, a little under a kilogram, and 40 ser made a maund, about 37 kilograms. But local sers were often different.

Lengths came from the body too. A hasta was roughly an arm's length from elbow to fingertip. The Mughal emperor Akbar set an official yard, the Ilahi gaz, for measuring land so taxes would be fair. The gaj is still used for cloth and plots in north India.

Annas and pice

Money had its own maths. A rupee was split into 16 annas, each anna into 4 paise, and each paisa into 3 pies. So a rupee held 192 pies. Prices like "two annas" or "four annas" were common in shops and in songs.

On 1 April 1957 India went decimal, with 100 naye paise ("new paise") to the rupee. The word "naye" was dropped in 1964.

Going metric

The Standards of Weights and Measures Act of 1956 made the metric system India's official system. Shops had to switch to kilograms and metres over the next few years. Teachers ran classes and posters explained the new units. For years, many people kept converting in their heads.

Old habits stay, though. Farmers still talk in bigha and acre, gold is still bought by the tola, and people in many towns still say "one ser" when they mean about a kilo. Big numbers are still counted the Indian way, in lakhs and crores.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A bigha is the same size everywhere in India.

Truth: The bigha is a traditional land unit, and its size varies a lot from state to state and even district to district. In some places it's about a third of an acre, in others more than half an acre, so land papers always need checking.

Try this

Ask a grandparent or older neighbour which old units they remember, such as seer, maund, gaj or bigha. Write down what each was used for and roughly how big it was. Then work out each one in grams, metres or square metres, and see whose memory matches the numbers.
