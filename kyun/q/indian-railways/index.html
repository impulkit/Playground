How did railways come to India?

India's first passenger train ran for about 34 km in 1853. Today Indian Railways makes close to 2 crore passenger journeys on a typical day.

The short answer

Railways came to India in the 1850s, built by British companies while the East India Company ruled much of the country. On 16 April 1853, a train of 14 carriages carrying about 400 guests left Bori Bunder in Bombay for Thane, about 34 km away. It was pulled by three steam engines named Sahib, Sindh and Sultan. The trip took about an hour and a quarter.

Within fifty years, lines linked most big cities. After independence in 1947, the railways became Indian Railways, run by the government. It now has about 68,000 km of routes, more than 7,000 stations and around 12 lakh staff, and it carries close to 2 crore passenger journeys on a typical day.

Surprising but true

The world's highest railway arch bridge is in India. The Chenab Bridge in Jammu and Kashmir, opened in 2025, stands about 359 metres above the river, higher than the Eiffel Tower is tall.

Why the British built them

The railways were built mainly to serve British interests. Trains could carry raw cotton, tea, coal and grain from inland India to ports like Bombay and Calcutta, to be shipped to Britain. They also let the army move soldiers quickly. After the revolt of 1857, the British pushed railway building even faster.

Companies building the lines were promised a guaranteed profit, paid from Indian taxes if needed. Much of the steel and many engines came from Britain. Indian workers did most of the hard digging and building, cutting through hills and bridging huge rivers like the Ganga, often in dangerous conditions.

Trains that brought people together

Even so, Indians soon made the railways their own. Trains let pilgrims reach faraway temples, families visit relatives and workers travel to cities for jobs. People from different regions and castes shared crowded carriages. Leaders of the freedom movement, including Gandhi, travelled the country by third-class train to meet ordinary people.

Trains also carried sorrow. During Partition in 1947, millions crossed the new borders, many of them packed into trains, and some of those journeys ended in terrible violence.

Mumbai's lifeline

In Mumbai, the suburban trains carry several million passengers a day. At rush hour, trains built for about 1,800 people can carry far more. People hang from doors, play cards on the way to work and know exactly which carriage stops nearest the station exit.

The same trains help the city's dabbawalas carry lunches across town, sorting boxes at stations and riding the trains between them. Ladies' compartments, first-class carriages and luggage vans all have their own regulars. For many Mumbaikars, a seat by the window on a slow train is a small daily luxury.

How do Mumbai's dabbawalas deliver lunches?Every working day, thousands of men in white caps carry home-cooked lunches across a city of 2 crore people, and almost nothing goes missing.

Mountains and heritage

Some of India's railways climb mountains. The Darjeeling Himalayan Railway, opened in 1881, winds up to over 2,000 metres in loops and zigzags. The Nilgiri Mountain Railway climbs to Ooty, and the Kalka–Shimla line passes through more than a hundred tunnels. Together they are a UNESCO World Heritage Site. So is Mumbai's Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Terminus, a grand Victorian Gothic station finished in 1888.

New tracks

Indian Railways keeps changing. Most lines have been converted to one wide track gauge and electrified. Vande Bharat trains, designed and built in India, began running in 2019. The Chenab Bridge, opened in 2025, carries trains about 359 metres above a river in Jammu and Kashmir. A high-speed line between Mumbai and Ahmedabad, using Japanese bullet train technology, is being built.

The United States has a longer network, mostly for freight. But few countries depend on their trains the way India does.

Myth or fact?

Myth: India has the biggest railway network in the world.

Truth: India's network is the fourth longest, after the United States, China and Russia. It is one of the busiest, though, carrying billions of passengers a year, and Indian Railways is one of the largest employers anywhere.
