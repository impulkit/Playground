How did biryani come to India?

Hyderabad, Lucknow, Kolkata, Kozhikode and Ambur all have their own biryani, and each city will tell you theirs is the real one.

The short answer

Biryani is a dish of fragrant rice layered with spiced meat, fish, eggs or vegetables, then cooked slowly in a sealed pot. Most historians think it grew out of Persian rice dishes like pilaf. Cooks at the courts of the Mughals and the sultans of the Deccan brought them to India from the 1500s onwards.

In India, those dishes met local spices, local rice and local cooks. Over time, every region that took up biryani changed it. That is why there isn't one biryani but dozens. Each tastes of the place where it grew up.

Surprising but true

Kolkata biryani usually contains a whole potato, and a popular story links it to Wajid Ali Shah, the last Nawab of Awadh, who was exiled to Kolkata in 1856.

Persian roots, Indian kitchens

The word biryani is usually traced to Persian. Birinj means rice, and biryan means fried or roasted. Persian cooks made pilafs of rice and meat, and these travelled with soldiers, traders and nobles into India.

At royal courts, cooks had time and money to experiment. They added saffron, rose water, ghee and many spices. The Mughal court and the courts of the Deccan, especially Golconda near Hyderabad, developed rich, layered rice dishes that became biryani.

Older stories

Not everyone agrees that biryani came only from Persia. Old Tamil poems describe oon soru, rice cooked with meat, ghee and spices, eaten by warriors. Arab traders sailed to the Kerala coast for centuries and brought their own rice dishes.

Some food historians think these older traditions mixed with Persian ideas. Others say they were different dishes that later borrowed the name biryani. Both points of view have supporters, and the evidence isn't enough to settle it. Food and recipes often travel along trade routes without leaving clear records.

The secret of dum

Biryani's key cooking method is called dum, meaning breath or steam. The meat is marinated in curd and spices, and the rice is partly boiled. They are layered in a heavy pot, sprinkled with saffron milk, fried onions and ghee. The lid is sealed with a rope of dough.

The pot is cooked slowly on low heat, sometimes with hot coals on the lid. Trapped steam finishes cooking the rice and carries the smells of meat and spice through every grain. When the dough seal is broken at the table, the steam rushes out.

A city for every biryani

In Hyderabad, kacchi biryani layers raw marinated meat with rice and cooks them together on dum. Lucknow's biryani, from the kitchens of Awadh, is gentler and more delicate, made with meat cooked first.

Kolkata biryani is lightly spiced and usually includes a potato and an egg. Many people link it to Wajid Ali Shah, the last Nawab of Awadh, who was exiled to Kolkata in 1856 with his cooks. Malabar biryani from Kozhikode uses short, fragrant rice called kaima. Ambur and Dindigul in Tamil Nadu make spicy versions with short-grain rice too.

India's favourite takeaway

Biryani is now one of the most ordered dishes on food delivery apps in India, year after year. It is cooked in huge pots at weddings and at Eid. Vegetarian versions, with paneer, mushrooms or vegetables, are popular too, though some people insist that only pulao can be vegetarian.

Biryani has followed Indians, Pakistanis and Bangladeshis around the world. You'll find it in Dubai, London, Toronto and New Jersey. There it is cooked by families from many regions, each sure that their grandmother's rice is the best.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Biryani and pulao are the same dish with different names.

Truth: They are close cousins. In a pulao, rice and meat or vegetables usually cook together in one go. In a biryani, the rice is usually part-cooked separately and layered with the meat or vegetables, then finished on dum. Cooks argue happily about the exact line.
