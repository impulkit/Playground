Who were the Mughals?

The family that built the Taj Mahal began with a young prince from Central Asia who lost his own kingdom and wrote a diary about missing melons.

The short answer

The Mughals were a dynasty of emperors who ruled much of the Indian subcontinent from 1526 until the 1700s, and in name until 1857. They were Muslims, descended from the Central Asian conquerors Timur and Genghis Khan.

At its biggest, around 1700, their empire covered almost all of today's India, Pakistan and Bangladesh, and part of Afghanistan. It was one of the richest and most populous states in the world. The Mughals built the Taj Mahal and the Red Fort, supported painters, poets and musicians, and left a mark on everything from Indian food to the Hindi and Urdu languages. After about 1720, their power faded, and the British took over.

Surprising but true

Babur, the first Mughal emperor, kept a frank diary in which he complained that India had no good melons or grapes, and later wrote about how much he loved its rains.

A prince who lost everything

Babur was born in 1483 in Fergana, a valley in today's Uzbekistan. He became a king at 11, lost his kingdom, won Samarkand and lost it again. Eventually he settled in Kabul.

From there he raided India. In 1526, at Panipat near Delhi, his small army defeated the last Delhi Sultan, Ibrahim Lodi, using cannons and guns. Babur wrote an unusually honest diary called the Baburnama in his own Turkic language. He describes battles, gardens, animals and his longing for the fruits of home.

Lost, then found again

Babur died in 1530. His son Humayun lost the empire to the Afghan ruler Sher Shah Suri and spent fifteen years in exile. Sher Shah used his short reign well, building roads, rest houses and a new silver coin called the rupiya, ancestor of today's rupee.

Humayun won back Delhi in 1555 but died months later, falling down the stairs of his library. His tomb in Delhi, with its garden laid out in four parts, inspired the Taj Mahal.

How was the Taj Mahal built so perfectly?It took about 20 years, around 20,000 workers and white marble carried from Rajasthan.

Four great emperors

Humayun's son Akbar ruled for nearly 50 years and made the empire strong. He worked with Rajput kings and tried to bring people of different faiths together.

Jahangir, his son, loved nature and art and had painters record birds, flowers and animals in fine detail. Shah Jahan built the Taj Mahal in Agra as a tomb for his wife Mumtaz Mahal, and a new walled city in Delhi with the Red Fort. Aurangzeb ruled for 49 years and pushed the empire to its largest size. He was a strict Muslim ruler. Historians still argue about his policies, including the return of the jizya tax on non-Muslims and the wars he fought for decades in the Deccan.

Gardens, paintings and food

The Mughals loved gardens divided into four parts by water channels, called charbagh. You can see them at Humayun's Tomb, the Taj Mahal and the Shalimar Bagh in Srinagar.

Court painters made tiny, detailed miniature paintings. Persian, Central Asian and Indian cooking blended into rich dishes such as kormas and kebabs. Many words in Hindi and Urdu came from the Persian spoken at court.

How the empire faded

After Aurangzeb died in 1707, the empire weakened. Provinces broke away, the Marathas grew powerful, and in 1739 the Persian ruler Nader Shah sacked Delhi and carried off the jewel-covered Peacock Throne.

The East India Company gradually took control. By 1857, the emperor Bahadur Shah Zafar, a poet, ruled little beyond the Red Fort. After the revolt that year, the British sent him into exile in Rangoon, where he died in 1862. The dynasty ended there, far from Fergana and far from Delhi.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Mughals were foreigners who never became Indian.

Truth: Babur came from Central Asia, but his descendants were born and buried in India, married into Indian families, spoke Hindustani and shaped Indian food, music, art and language for centuries.

Try this

Look closely at a picture of the Taj Mahal or Humayun's Tomb. Fold a sheet of paper down the middle and draw one half of the building, then try to draw the mirror image on the other side. Mughal builders loved perfect symmetry.
