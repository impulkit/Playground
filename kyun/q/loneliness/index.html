What is loneliness?

You can feel lonely in a full classroom and perfectly happy alone in your room. Loneliness is about the gap between the company you want and the company you have.

The short answer

Loneliness is the sad, empty feeling you get when you have less closeness with other people than you need. It might come from having no one to sit with at lunch. It might come from being surrounded by people who don't seem to get you.

It is different from being alone. Reading by yourself on a quiet afternoon can feel lovely. That's often called solitude. Loneliness is when the alone part hurts. Almost everyone feels it sometimes, after moving house, changing schools, losing a friend or simply having a bad week. It usually passes. When it lasts a long time, it is worth talking to someone about.

Surprising but true

In 2025 the World Health Organization found that teenagers and young adults report loneliness more often than older people do.

A signal, like hunger

Many scientists think loneliness works like hunger or thirst. Hunger is unpleasant on purpose: it pushes you to find food. Loneliness may push you to find people.

For early humans, being part of a group meant safety, shared food and help when hurt. Someone left alone was in danger. So a brain that felt bad when cut off from others had a reason to seek company. That's one way to see why loneliness can feel almost physical, like an ache. It is one of the strongest emotions people describe.

Researchers also say loneliness is more about quality than numbers. One close friend you can really talk to may protect you better than a big group you never feel part of.

What it does to the body

When loneliness goes on for months, it can affect more than mood. People who are lonely for a long time often sleep badly. Their bodies may stay in a state of stress, with more of the hormones that keep us alert for danger.

The World Health Organization's 2025 report said long-term loneliness and isolation are linked to heart disease, depression and dying younger. It estimated that about 1 in 6 people worldwide feel lonely. Governments have started to take it seriously. The UK named a minister for loneliness in 2018, and Japan did the same in 2021.

Is kindness good for you?In one experiment, people given money to spend on someone else ended the day happier than people told to spend it on themselves.

Why young people feel it too

It may sound surprising, but teenagers and young adults report loneliness more than most older people. Growing up involves a lot of change. Friend groups shift. People move away for study or work. Feeling different, or being left out online, can sting.

Phones complicate things. Messaging can keep you close to a cousin in Pune or a friend who moved to Texas. But scrolling through other people's parties can make your own evening feel emptier. Researchers still argue about how much screens add to loneliness and how much they ease it.

What helps

Small steps count. A short chat, a shared joke or an activity with others, like a team, a club or a choir, builds connection without much pressure. Helping someone else often helps the helper too, partly because it gives you a reason to talk.

Noticing loneliness in others matters as well. The new child in class, a grandparent who lives alone, a friend who has gone quiet: a few minutes of empathy can change their day. Real friendship usually starts small.

Some places are trying new ideas. In Britain, doctors can "prescribe" activities like gardening clubs, choirs or walking groups, an approach called social prescribing. Some schools have a buddy bench in the playground. A child who wants company sits there, and others know to invite them to play.

If you feel lonely for a long time, or very low, tell a trusted adult such as a parent, teacher or school counsellor. They can help, and saying it out loud is often the hardest and most useful step.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Only people with no friends get lonely.

Truth: Loneliness is a feeling, not a head count. Someone with lots of friends can feel lonely if nobody seems to understand them, and someone with one close friend may feel fine.

Try this

Make a "connection map". Write your name in the middle of a page, then draw lines to everyone you talked to this week, even briefly, like a shopkeeper or a bus conductor. Put a star next to the ones who made you smile. Pick one person on the map you'd like to talk to more, and say hello to them tomorrow.
