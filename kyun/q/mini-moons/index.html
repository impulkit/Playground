What is a mini-moon?

Every so often, a space rock about as big as a person gets caught by Earth and loops around us for a few months, and almost nobody notices.

The short answer

A mini-moon is a small space rock that Earth's gravity catches for a while. It loops around our planet for a few months or a year or two, then drifts away again.

It is not a real second Moon. Our Moon is 3,474 km across. Mini-moons are usually a few metres wide, small enough to fit inside a classroom.

Surprising but true

When the mini-moon 2026 QQ8 was first reported, experts thought it might be a piece of old rocket. Only careful tracking proved it was a natural space rock.

How does a planet catch a moon?

Most small asteroids go round the Sun in their own orbit. A few have orbits very like Earth's, so they drift alongside us slowly, like two cars on a motorway driving at almost the same speed.

When one of these slow rocks passes close, Earth's gravity can bend its path into big, wobbly loops around our planet, often far beyond our Moon, which is about 384,000 km away. The Sun's pull never stops tugging, though, so after some months the rock slips away again.

Some mini-moons may even be chips of our own Moon, knocked off long ago by crashes.

What is space junk?Millions of bits of old rockets and broken satellites circle Earth faster than bullets. Even a fleck of paint can chip a window.

Rock or rocket?

Old rocket stages also drift around the Sun near Earth, and some get caught too. So when astronomers spot a tiny object looping around us, they must check whether it is natural. They track how sunlight pushes on it: a hollow rocket part gets nudged far more than a solid rock. They also study its colour with a telescope.

Mini-moons so far

Astronomers have found only a handful. In 2006, a rock called 2006 RH120 orbited Earth for about a year. Another, 2020 CD3, stayed for over a year until 2020. In late 2024, a roughly 10-metre rock called 2024 PT5 visited for about two months. In 2026, a 1 to 2 metre rock called 2026 QQ8 was caught, spotted from Hungary and confirmed after it had been looping for months.

Bigger survey telescopes, like the Rubin Observatory in Chile, should find many more. The same sky watch that finds mini-moons also keeps an eye out for larger rocks that could be dangerous. Mars may have caught its two moons in a similar way, but kept them for good.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Earth has only ever had one moon.

Truth: Earth has one permanent Moon, but small space rocks are caught for a few months or years quite often. Scientists think there may be a tiny mini-moon around Earth much of the time. Most are just too small and faint to spot.

Try this

Roll a marble slowly past a bowl so it just catches the rim. Sometimes it circles once or twice inside before rolling out again. That wobbly, short visit is a bit like how Earth catches a mini-moon and then lets it go.
