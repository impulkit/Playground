How does money grow by itself?

In 1790, Benjamin Franklin left £1,000 each to Boston and Philadelphia with orders to leave it alone for 200 years. By 1990, Boston's share had grown to about $4.5 million.

The short answer

Money in a bank grows because the bank pays you interest. Banks lend your money to other people and businesses and share some of what they earn with you. If the interest is added to your savings, then next year you earn interest on the interest too. That's called compound interest.

At first the growth is slow. Then it speeds up, because the pile keeps getting bigger and the interest is a percentage of the whole pile. Given enough years, a small amount can grow into a surprisingly large one, without you adding a single rupee.

Surprising but true

Albert Einstein almost certainly never called compound interest "the eighth wonder of the world". Nobody has found any record of him saying it.

Simple versus compound

Say you save ₹1,000 at 10% interest a year. With simple interest, you get ₹100 every year, always worked out on the first ₹1,000. After 10 years you have ₹2,000.

With compound interest, year one gives ₹100, so you have ₹1,100. Year two gives 10% of ₹1,100, which is ₹110. Year three gives ₹121. The amount added grows every year. After 10 years you have about ₹2,594. After 30 years, simple interest gives you ₹4,000, but compound interest gives you over ₹17,000.

That's the strange power of exponents. The number gets multiplied again and again, not just added to.

The rule of 72

Bankers use a handy trick. Divide 72 by the yearly interest rate, and you get roughly how many years it takes for money to double. At 6% a year, money doubles in about 12 years. At 8%, about 9 years. At 12%, about 6 years.

It isn't exact, but it's close for normal interest rates. It also works backwards for things that grow in a bad way. If prices rise 6% a year, they double in about 12 years. That's inflation, and it eats into savings that don't earn enough interest.

Franklin's 200-year experiment

Benjamin Franklin, the American inventor and statesman, wanted to show what patience could do. When he died in 1790, his will left £1,000 each to the cities of Boston and Philadelphia. The money was to be lent to young workers, and most of it left untouched to grow for 200 years.

Franklin worked out that it might grow to millions. Things didn't go perfectly. Philadelphia's fund was managed less carefully and grew more slowly. Still, by 1990 Boston's fund was worth about $4.5 million and Philadelphia's about $2 million. A technical college in Boston, now called the Benjamin Franklin Cummings Institute of Technology, was founded in 1908 partly with money from that gift.

The other side of the coin

Compound interest also works against you. If you borrow money, on a credit card for example, the interest can compound too. A debt left unpaid can double in a few years. That's why adults warn about borrowing more than you can pay back.

Some loans in history became famous traps. Moneylenders in villages charged such high compound interest that farmers could stay in debt for their whole lives. Many Indian reformers, and later India's banks and cooperatives, worked to give farmers fairer loans.

Time is the secret

The biggest ingredient in compound interest is not the amount you start with. It's time. Someone who saves a little from age 15 can end up with more than someone who saves twice as much from 35. The early money simply has longer to double and double again.

Doubling gets out of hand fast. An old Indian legend tells of a king asked to pay one grain of rice on the first square of a chessboard, then two, then four. By the 64th square, the rice would bury the whole kingdom, and the chessboard puzzle shows exactly how.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Einstein said compound interest is the most powerful force in the universe.

Truth: This quote is all over the internet, but there is no record that Einstein ever said it. It began appearing decades after his death. The maths is real, even if the quote isn't.

Try this

Make a doubling chart. Start with 1 rupee and double it every day for 30 days, writing each number on a calendar. Guess first how much you'll have on day 30, then check. The answer, more than 50 crore, surprises almost everyone.
