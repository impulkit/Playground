Who was Thiruvalluvar?

Thiruvalluvar wrote 1,330 couplets about how to live well, and a 133-foot statue of him stands on a rock off Kanyakumari, one foot for each chapter.

The short answer

Thiruvalluvar was a Tamil poet and thinker who wrote the Tirukkural, one of the most famous books in the Tamil language. It is a collection of 1,330 short couplets, two-line verses, giving advice on how to live a good life.

Nobody knows exactly when he lived. Guesses range from about 300 BCE to about 500 CE. Almost nothing reliable is known about his life either.

Yet his words are everywhere in Tamil Nadu. They are painted on buses, taught in schools, quoted in speeches and printed on calendars. People of different religions all find something in his book, because it talks about honesty, kindness and fairness without preaching a single faith.

Surprising but true

Every couplet in the Tirukkural is just seven words long in Tamil, four in the first line and three in the second.

A book of short advice

The Tirukkural is split into three parts. The first is about aram, or virtue: being truthful, patient and kind. The second, porul, is about wealth and public life: how kings should rule, how to choose friends and how to work hard. The third, inbam, is about love.

There are 133 chapters, each with exactly ten couplets. Every couplet is just seven Tamil words long. That makes them easy to remember, like a proverb.

One well-known kural says, roughly, that the wound from fire may heal, but the wound from a harsh tongue never does. Another says that learning is the only wealth that never decays. These ideas still sound fresh today. Teachers in Tamil Nadu often ask children to learn a kural by heart, the way American students might learn a famous line from a speech.

The man behind the book

Because so little is known, stories filled the gap. A popular tradition says Thiruvalluvar was a weaver who lived in Mylapore, in what is now Chennai. Some stories give him a wife named Vasuki, who was famous for being patient and kind.

Historians treat these as legends, not facts. Even his name is a puzzle. "Thiru" is an honour, a little like "Shri". "Valluvar" may come from a community name, or simply mean someone who teaches.

Some scholars link the Tirukkural to the age of the Tamil Sangam poets. Others think its language and ideas place it a few centuries later. They still debate this.

A book for the whole world

The Tirukkural has been translated into dozens of languages, from English and Hindi to Russian and Japanese. Christian missionaries in the 1700s and 1800s were among the first to translate it into European languages.

Leo Tolstoy, the Russian writer, read parts of it in translation. In 1908 he quoted its lines on returning good for harm in a long essay called "A Letter to a Hindu". A young lawyer in South Africa named Mohandas Gandhi read that letter, printed it for Indian readers and later praised the Tirukkural himself.

Remembering Valluvar

Tamil Nadu honours him in many ways. Thiruvalluvar Day falls in mid-January, during the Pongal festival. In Chennai, a memorial called Valluvar Kottam, opened in 1976, is built in the shape of a temple chariot, with all 1,330 couplets carved on granite panels.

The most dramatic memorial stands off Kanyakumari, at the southern tip of India. On a small rock island, a stone statue of Thiruvalluvar rises 133 feet, about 40 m. It was unveiled on 1 January 2000.

The height is a code. The statue is 133 feet tall for the 133 chapters. Its base is 38 feet high, for the 38 chapters on virtue. The figure above stands 95 feet, for the chapters on wealth and love together. Waves crash below, and the statue looks out over the meeting of three seas. Since 2024 a glass-floored footbridge links it to the neighbouring Vivekananda Rock Memorial.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Tirukkural is a holy book of one particular religion.

Truth: It names no god of any single religion and no particular sect. That is one reason people of many faiths, and none, have claimed it as their own.
