Who was Hypatia?

About 1,600 years ago in Alexandria, a woman named Hypatia taught maths and astronomy to crowds of students, at a time when few women were allowed to teach anything.

The short answer

Hypatia was a mathematician, astronomer and philosopher who lived in Alexandria, Egypt, around 1,600 years ago. Alexandria was then one of the great cities of the Roman Empire, a busy port where scholars wrote in Greek. Its famous lighthouse, one of the wonders of the ancient world, still stood in the harbour.

She was the most famous teacher in the city. Students travelled from far away to hear her lectures. She is one of the earliest women mathematicians whose life is reasonably well recorded, although much about her is still uncertain. She was killed by a mob in 415 CE, and over the centuries she has become a symbol of learning and of women in science.

Surprising but true

There is a crater on the Moon and an asteroid named after Hypatia.

A mathematician's daughter

Hypatia's father, Theon, was a mathematician and astronomer. He prepared a version of Euclid's great geometry book, the Elements, that was copied for hundreds of years. Hypatia learned from him and is thought to have worked with him on some of his books.

Nobody knows exactly when she was born. Historians suggest somewhere between 350 and 370 CE. By her thirties or forties she was teaching on her own, and people said she had gone beyond her father. In a city where most scholars were men, that made her stand out even more.

What she taught

Hypatia taught geometry, astronomy and philosophy, especially the ideas of Plato. She is said to have lectured in public, wearing a scholar's cloak, and city leaders came to her for advice.

Later writers say she wrote explanations of three hard mathematical books. One was the Arithmetica of Diophantus, a collection of puzzles about unknown numbers that is an early ancestor of algebra. Another was about conic sections, the curves you get by slicing a cone at different angles. The third helped readers with Ptolemy's tables of the movements of the stars and planets. Copies of these works may survive inside later books, but no one can be sure which parts are hers.

What happened to the Library of Alexandria?The ancient world's most famous library did not vanish in a single dramatic fire. It faded away over hundreds of years.

Letters from a student

One of her students, Synesius, later became a bishop in what is now Libya. He kept writing to Hypatia for years, calling her his teacher and "mother". In one letter he describes an astrolabe, a brass instrument for measuring the positions of stars, that he had made using what she taught. In another, he asks her to have a hydroscope made for him, a device for measuring how dense a liquid is.

These letters are precious, because almost nothing else written by people who knew her has survived. They show a real teacher whose students trusted her advice long after they left her classes.

A violent end

In 415 CE, Alexandria was split by a bitter quarrel between the Roman governor, Orestes, and the city's bishop, Cyril. Hypatia was a friend and adviser of the governor. Rumours spread that she was stopping the two men from making peace. A mob attacked her in the street and killed her.

Historians still argue about how much the quarrel was about religion and how much about power. Some later writers turned her into a simple symbol, while others tried to blame her. The few real sources tell a more tangled story. What is clear is that the city lost its most respected scholar.

Remembered today

For a long time, Hypatia was half-forgotten. From the 1700s onwards, writers and artists rediscovered her, sometimes adding details that had no evidence behind them. Today she appears in books, plays and films. A crater on the Moon and the asteroid 238 Hypatia carry her name, and she is often the first name on lists of great women in mathematics. In Alexandria, the city she taught in, a modern library stands near where the ancient one once did.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Hypatia died when the Great Library of Alexandria was burned.

Truth: The Great Library had faded away long before her time, after centuries of war and neglect. Her death in 415 CE was the result of a local political quarrel, not part of the library's destruction.

Make a simple sundial, the kind of tool ancient astronomers used. Push a pencil upright into a lump of clay on a sheet of paper in a sunny window or on a balcony with an adult. Mark the tip of the shadow every hour. Next day, check whether the shadow lands on the same marks at the same times.
