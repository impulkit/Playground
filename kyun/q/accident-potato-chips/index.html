How were potato chips invented?

The famous story says an angry cook in New York invented chips in 1853 to annoy a fussy customer. An English cookbook had the recipe 36 years earlier.

The short answer

Nobody knows who made the very first potato chip. Thin slices of potato fried in fat appear in an English cookbook from 1817, called The Cook's Oracle. An American cookbook printed a similar recipe in 1824.

But the story most people tell is more fun. It says that in 1853, at a lakeside hotel in Saratoga Springs, New York, a diner kept sending back his fried potatoes for being too thick. The cook, George Crum, lost his temper. He sliced them paper-thin, fried them hard and salted them heavily. The customer loved them. It's a good tale, but historians have found no proof it happened.

Surprising but true

In 2009, a British court had to decide whether Pringles count as potato crisps. The makers argued they didn't, and lost.

The legend of Saratoga

Saratoga Springs was a fashionable holiday town, full of rich visitors drinking its spring water. George Crum was a real chef there, of African American and Native American heritage, and later ran his own popular restaurant.

What we do know is that thin fried potatoes became a local speciality called "Saratoga chips". They were served in hotels and sold in paper cones. The legend about Crum and the fussy diner seems to have been written down much later, in the 20th century. Some versions even claimed the diner was a famous millionaire, which makes historians more suspicious, not less.

Why a chip goes crunch

A raw potato is about four-fifths water. When you drop a thin slice into hot oil, that water boils off fast as steam. You can hear it as a fierce sizzle.

Once most of the water is gone, the starch inside hardens into a light, brittle sheet. Sugars and proteins on the surface react with the heat and turn golden brown, which gives chips their toasty flavour. This browning shows up all over cooking science, from toast to roasted coffee.

Thickness is everything. A thick slice stays soft in the middle, like a French fry. A slice a millimetre or two thin dries out right through.

From hotel treat to every shop

For decades, chips were made by hand and went soft quickly. Two inventions changed that. Machines that peeled and sliced potatoes made chips cheap. And better bags kept the air out.

In the 1920s, a Californian businesswoman named Laura Scudder had workers iron sheets of waxed paper into sealed bags. Chips stayed crisp for longer. Today, chip bags are filled with nitrogen gas instead of air, which keeps the oil from going stale. So the "air" in your packet is there on purpose, though it also makes a small amount look like more.

A plant from the mountains

Chips depend on potatoes, which were first farmed thousands of years ago in the Andes mountains of Peru and Bolivia. People there still grow thousands of varieties, in purple, red and yellow.

Spanish sailors carried potatoes to Europe in the 1500s. Portuguese traders and later the British helped spread them in India. Today India is one of the world's biggest potato growers.

Chips around the world

In Britain, these are called crisps, and "chips" means thick fries. In the USA they are chips. In much of India people say wafers or chips, and shops sell flavours like masala, magic masala and tomato.

Kerala makes its own famous version from bananas, sliced thin and fried in coconut oil. And in 2009, a British court decided that Pringles, which are made from a dough of dried potato, still count as potato crisps for tax purposes. Their makers had argued they were too different to count.

Food inventions often seem to start with a legend and a happy mistake. The ice lolly has a story like that too, starring an 11-year-old boy and a freezing night.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Chef George Crum invented potato chips in 1853 to get back at a customer who kept sending his fries back.

Truth: There is no record of this from the time, and Crum never seems to have claimed it. Recipes for fried potato slices were printed in England in 1817 and in the USA in 1824.

Try this

With an adult, cut one potato into thick slices and one into slices as thin as you can, then ask the adult to bake them on a tray until golden. Compare how crunchy each is. Thinner slices lose their water faster, which is the whole secret of a crisp.
