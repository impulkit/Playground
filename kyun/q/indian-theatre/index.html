What is Indian theatre like?

India has a 2,000-year-old book about acting that tells performers how to move their eyebrows to show surprise.

The short answer

Indian theatre is a huge family of traditions, some ancient and some brand new. It ranges from Sanskrit plays more than 1,500 years old to all-night folk performances in village squares, and from modern plays in city auditoriums to street plays on a corner.

Much Indian theatre mixes acting with music, dance and song. Performers often tell stories from the Ramayana, the Mahabharata and the Puranas, as well as local legends. Actors use detailed hand gestures, facial expressions and painted make-up. The aim is often less about making things look realistic and more about making the audience feel deeply. The oldest guide to all this is the Natyashastra.

Surprising but true

Kutiyattam, a form of Sanskrit theatre from Kerala, is one of the oldest theatre traditions still performed anywhere. Some performances once lasted for weeks.

A handbook for actors

The Natyashastra is credited to a sage called Bharata Muni. Scholars date it to somewhere between about 200 BCE and 200 CE. It is an enormous book covering almost everything about putting on a play: acting, stage design, costumes, make-up, music and dance.

It describes how to show emotions with every part of the body, down to the eyebrows and eyelids. Its central idea is rasa, the "flavour" an audience tastes when a performance works. The Natyashastra describes eight rasas, including love, humour, sorrow and wonder. Later writers added a ninth, peace, making the navarasa, or nine rasas, still taught today.

Kalidasa and Sanskrit plays

Classical Sanskrit drama reached a high point with Kalidasa, who probably lived around the 4th or 5th century CE. His play Abhijnanashakuntalam tells of King Dushyanta, who falls in love with Shakuntala in a forest hermitage and then forgets her because of a curse. When it was translated into English and German around 1790, writers in Europe were deeply impressed.

Sanskrit plays usually ended happily. The Natyashastra advised against showing death on stage, so tragedy, in the Greek sense, was rare.

In Kerala, a form called Kutiyattam has kept Sanskrit theatre alive for centuries. Performers might spend a whole night acting out a single scene in great detail. UNESCO recognised it in 2001.

Theatre in every region

Almost every part of India has its own folk theatre. Yakshagana from coastal Karnataka has huge headdresses, drums and all-night shows. Kerala's Kathakali uses brightly painted faces and dance to tell epic stories. Bengal has jatra, travelling troupes who perform under the open sky. Maharashtra has tamasha, full of song and comedy. Uttar Pradesh has nautanki, and Gujarat has bhavai.

Ramlila, a retelling of the Ramayana staged before Dussehra, is performed in towns and villages across north India. In Ramnagar, near Varanasi, it lasts about a month and moves around the town, with different neighbourhoods acting as different places in the story.

Modern stages

In the 1800s, Parsi theatre companies in Bombay put on lively shows with songs, painted backdrops and stage tricks. Many early film actors and writers came from this world, and you can see its style in early Bollywood films.

After independence, the National School of Drama opened in Delhi in 1959. Playwrights such as Girish Karnad, Vijay Tendulkar and Badal Sircar wrote powerful plays in Kannada, Marathi and Bengali. Habib Tanvir worked with folk actors from Chhattisgarh, and his play Charandas Chor, about an honest thief, toured the world.

In Mumbai, the actor Shashi Kapoor and his wife Jennifer Kendal opened Prithvi Theatre in 1978, named after his father Prithviraj Kapoor, who had run a touring theatre company. It's a small theatre where audiences sit very close to the actors.

Where did it come from?

In the 1800s, some European scholars suggested Indian theatre began when Alexander's Greek armies arrived. Most historians today reject that idea. Indian drama seems to have grown from its own roots: temple rituals, storytellers, music and dance. Whatever its beginnings, it is still growing. Somewhere in India tonight, a curtain is opening, or a drum is calling a village to gather.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Theatre came to India from Greece with Alexander the Great.

Truth: Some European scholars in the 1800s suggested this, but there's little evidence for it. Most historians now see Indian theatre as growing out of India's own traditions of ritual, storytelling, music and dance.

Try this

Play the navarasa game. One person shows an emotion using only their face and hands and no words. Choose from love, laughter, sorrow, anger, courage, fear, disgust, wonder or peace. Everyone else guesses which one. Then try showing the same emotion with only your eyes.
