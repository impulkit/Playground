What is a free trade agreement?

A shirt made in Tiruppur can cost more in another country just because it crossed a border. A free trade deal is a promise to take that extra cost away.

The short answer

When goods cross a border, governments often charge a tax on them, called a tariff or customs duty. It makes foreign goods more expensive, which can protect local makers, but it also makes it harder for a country's own businesses to sell abroad.

A free trade agreement is a promise between countries to cut or remove these taxes on each other's goods. That makes it cheaper and easier for businesses on both sides to buy and sell.

Surprising but true

India and New Zealand first started talking about a trade deal in 2010. The talks stalled in 2015 and only restarted in 2025, so it took about 16 years from first meeting to final deal.

How it works

Imagine two neighbours who each charge an entry fee whenever the other's children come over to play. A free trade deal is like both families agreeing to drop the fee, so visits become easy.

Here is an example. Say a shirt from Tiruppur is worth ₹500, and another country charges a 10% tariff on it. A shop there must pay ₹550 before it even adds its own profit. If a deal removes the tariff, the shirt costs ₹500 at the border, so it can compete with shirts made locally or in other countries.

Deals are long, careful documents, often hundreds of pages. They usually cover four things.

Which goods. A list of thousands of products, each with its own tariff and a date for cutting it. Some drop to zero at once. Others fall slowly over five or ten years.

Protected goods. Things a country keeps out of the deal, or lets in only in small amounts, called a quota.

Rules of origin. A shirt only gets the deal if it was really made in the partner country. This stops a third country from sneaking its goods through.

More than goods. Many deals also cover services such as software and tourism, investment, and visas for workers and students.

Where it came from

Countries have traded for thousands of years, along routes like the Silk Road, and rulers have taxed that trade for just as long. In the 1800s and early 1900s, many countries kept tariffs high to protect their own factories.

After the Second World War, countries agreed to lower tariffs together. A worldwide agreement called GATT was signed in 1947, and in 1995 it grew into the World Trade Organization, which sets basic trade rules for most of the world. A free trade agreement goes further: two countries, or a small group, promise each other better terms than they give everyone else. It is a kind of treaty.

India's story

For decades after independence, India kept tariffs very high and bought few foreign goods. That changed with the 1991 reforms, when India began opening up.

India's first free trade agreement was with Sri Lanka, signed in 1998. Deals with South Asian neighbours, ASEAN countries, South Korea and Japan followed between 2004 and 2011. Then came a pause. In 2019, India chose to stay out of RCEP, a huge trade bloc of 15 Asian and Pacific countries including China. It worried that cheap imports, especially of dairy and factory goods, could hurt Indian farmers and makers.

From 2021, India began signing deals again, but with richer partners. It signed with Mauritius, the UAE and Australia, then four European countries called EFTA, the UK, Oman and New Zealand. In January 2026 it also agreed a deal with the European Union, which still needed formal signing.

In the New Zealand deal, India kept out milk, cheese and yoghurt, along with onions, sugar, spices and edible oils. India's dairy is made mostly by small farmers working together in groups like Amul. New Zealand apples can enter only in limited amounts, above a minimum price and in certain seasons, because apple growers in Himachal Pradesh worried about competition.

The numbers that matter

16 years. India and New Zealand first started talking in 2010. The deal came into force in 2026.

100% and 95%. New Zealand removed tariffs on every Indian product. India is cutting or removing tariffs on goods that make up about 95% of what New Zealand sends, often step by step over several years.

USD 2.4 billion. Total trade in goods and services between the two countries in 2024. That is tiny next to the size of India's economy, so the deal matters most for particular businesses.

5,000 and 1,000. New Zealand offered 5,000 temporary work visas for Indian professionals and 1,000 working holiday visas, along with easier work rights for Indian students after they finish studying there.

What people argue about

Supporters say deals help businesses export more, which creates jobs in places like Tiruppur's textile mills and Gujarat's medicine factories. Shoppers get more choice and sometimes lower prices. Investment from abroad can bring new factories and skills.

Critics worry that cheaper imports can hurt local farmers and small makers who cannot compete. Some point to India's 2010 deal with ASEAN countries, after which India's imports from them grew faster than its exports to them. Others say investment promises depend on what private companies actually choose to do.

Most experts agree the details matter more than the headline. Which goods are protected, how fast tariffs fall and how strict the rules of origin are can decide who gains and who loses.

Myths and mix-ups

"Free trade means everything becomes free." The goods still cost money. Only the border tax changes, and protected goods still pay it.

"A trade deal is the same as GST." GST is a tax inside India on things people buy. A tariff is charged only when goods cross the border. A trade deal changes tariffs, not GST.

"Only big companies gain." Big firms often gain first, but small exporters of tea, spices, leather and handicrafts can also find new buyers when tariffs fall.

What changed in India in 1991?In 1991, India had only enough foreign money to pay for about three weeks of imports, and it flew gold to London to borrow cash.

What it means for you

Trade deals touch your home in small ways. Imported kiwifruit, apples, chocolates or phone parts may cost a little less when tariffs fall. Clothes, medicines and engineering goods made in India may sell more abroad, which can mean more jobs in Indian towns.

For students, some deals make it easier to study abroad and work for a while afterwards. For farmers, the list of protected goods decides whether they face new competition. Next time you read "Made in New Zealand" or "Made in India" on a label, you are looking at the result of rules that countries have spent years arguing over.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A free trade deal means everything between two countries becomes completely free.

Truth: The goods still cost money. What changes is the tax charged at the border. And most deals keep a list of products that are protected, like India's dairy, which still pay the full tax.
