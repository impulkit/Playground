How does a microwave oven heat food?

The microwave oven was discovered by accident, when a radar engineer noticed something sticky melting in his pocket.

The short answer

A microwave oven fills its metal box with invisible waves called microwaves. They are part of the same family as radio waves and light, but far too long to see: each wave is about 12 centimetres from peak to peak.

Most food contains water, and each water molecule is slightly lopsided. One end carries a small positive electric charge and the other a small negative one. Microwaves make the electric field in the oven swing back and forth about 2.45 billion times a second. Every time it swings, the water molecules twist to line up with it. All that twisting and bumping into neighbouring molecules turns into heat, a bit like rubbing your hands together to warm them. The hot water then heats the rest of the food. Dry things, like a plain paper napkin or an empty glass bowl, warm up much less. Ice warms surprisingly slowly too, because the molecules in frozen water are locked in place and can't twist freely.

Surprising but true

The first microwave oven, sold in 1947, stood about as tall as a grown man and weighed around 340 kg, as much as a big motorbike with a rider on it.

The melting snack

In 1945, an American engineer named Percy Spencer was working at a company called Raytheon, which built radar sets. Radar uses a device called a magnetron to make powerful microwaves. As the story is usually told, Spencer was standing near a working magnetron when he noticed that a snack bar in his pocket had melted.

Curious, he tried pointing the microwaves at popcorn kernels, which popped all over the lab. Raytheon patented the idea and in 1947 sold the first microwave oven, called the Radarange. It was about 1.8 metres tall and very expensive, and was used mainly in restaurants, ships and railway dining cars. Small, cheap kitchen models came in the late 1960s and 1970s.

Why the food goes round

Inside the oven, microwaves bounce off the metal walls. Where waves overlap, some spots get strong waves and others get almost none. So a plate standing still would cook unevenly, with hot spots and cold spots a few centimetres apart.

That's why most microwaves have a turning plate, and why instructions say to stir halfway through. Without a turntable, the hot spots in a sheet of melted cheese or chocolate are spaced about 6 centimetres apart, half the length of one wave.

Keeping the waves in

The front door has a metal mesh with tiny holes, a millimetre or two wide. Light, with its very short waves, passes through, so you can watch your food. Microwaves are far too long to fit, so they bounce back inside. When the door opens, a safety switch shuts the magnetron off at once.

Metal objects inside can cause sparks, especially foil with crinkled edges or forks with points. The waves push electric charges in the metal, which bunch up at sharp tips and jump across the air. Ceramic, glass and many plastics let the waves pass straight through.

Safe and common

Microwaves don't make food radioactive. They stop the instant the oven switches off, and nothing lingers in the food. The heated food is just hot, the same as food warmed on a stove.

Microwaves in an oven also have a lot in common with Wi-Fi, which uses waves of a very similar length. A leaky old oven can sometimes make Wi-Fi stutter while it runs. Today microwave ovens sit in kitchens across the USA and in many Indian homes and offices, warming up rice, dal and leftover cooking in a couple of minutes.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Microwave ovens cook food from the inside out.

Truth: Microwaves only reach a few centimetres into food. They heat the outer layer first, and the heat then spreads slowly inward. That is why a thick piece of food can be hot outside and cold in the middle, and why it helps to stir.

Try this

Act out a microwave with friends. Each person is a water molecule holding a card with a + on one side and a − on the other. A leader stands at the front and keeps switching a sign between + and −. Everyone must spin to point their − side towards a + sign. Go faster and faster, and notice how warm all that bumping makes you.
