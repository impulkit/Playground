How fast can a goalkeeper react?

A hard penalty kick reaches the goal in under half a second. That is barely longer than it takes a human brain to see the ball and start moving.

The short answer

A goalkeeper's brain needs about a fifth of a second to see something and start moving. A powerful penalty, struck at over 100 km/h from 11 m away, reaches the goal line in about 0.4 to 0.5 seconds.

After seeing the ball, the keeper's muscles also need time to push the body sideways, more than a metre or two. Add that up and there just isn't enough time. So goalkeepers don't really react to penalties. They predict. They read the kicker's run-up and body, guess the direction and begin to dive as the ball is kicked, or a split second before.

Surprising but true

In a study of 286 penalty kicks in top football, goalkeepers dived left or right 94% of the time, even though staying in the middle would have saved more shots.

What happens in a reaction

When the ball moves, light from it reaches your eyes. Cells in the retina turn that light into electrical signals. These travel to the back of the brain, which works out what you are seeing. Then the brain decides what to do and sends commands along nerves to your muscles.

All of this takes around 0.2 to 0.25 seconds for most people when they see something. Sounds are slightly faster to react to, at about 0.15 seconds. Your reflexes, like pulling your hand from something hot, can be faster still, because the signal goes only to the spinal cord and back.

Reading the kicker

Since reacting to the ball is too slow, keepers watch the kicker instead. The angle of the run-up, the position of the hips and where the standing foot points all give clues. Many right-footed kickers find it more natural to shoot to their left.

Goalkeepers and their coaches now study video of every likely penalty taker. In big matches, some keepers carry notes on their water bottles about which way each opponent usually shoots.

Why keepers dive

In 2007, researchers studied 286 penalties in top leagues. Keepers dived left or right 94% of the time. Yet the kicks went to the centre nearly a third of the time, and keepers who stayed in the middle saved more of those.

So why dive? The researchers suggested a reason: a keeper who stands still and gets beaten looks lazy, while one who dives and misses at least looks as if he tried. Doing something feels better than doing nothing, even when the numbers say otherwise.

Cricket, hockey and baseball

Cricket batters face the same problem. A 150 km/h delivery takes about 0.45 seconds to travel down the 20 m pitch. Batters watch the bowler's hand and the ball's first flight, and decide their shot in a blink. They often guess where the ball will bounce before it lands.

Hockey goalkeepers face shots and deflections from much closer. P. R. Sreejesh, who helped India's hockey team win Olympic bronze medals in 2021 and 2024, became famous for diving saves at penalty corners, where the ball can fly at well over 100 km/h.

Baseball batters get about 0.4 seconds against a fast pitch. They must start swinging when the ball is only halfway to them.

Can you get faster?

Reaction time improves with practice up to a point, especially for a particular task. Athletes get faster mostly by learning what to watch for and what is likely to happen. Their brains spend less time deciding.

Being tired, distracted or sleepy slows everyone down. In tests, people who have missed a night's sleep can react as slowly as people who have drunk alcohol.

Sprinters have rules about this. World Athletics says that anyone who moves within 0.1 seconds of the starting gun has false-started, because nobody can genuinely react that fast. Goalkeepers have a harder job still when the ball swerves in mid-air, and the physics behind that swerve is the secret of the curving free kick.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Great goalkeepers simply have super-fast reflexes that let them react to any shot.

Truth: Fast reactions help, but no human can react in time to a well-struck penalty after it is kicked. The best keepers win by predicting, using clues from the kicker's body, studying past penalties and staying calm.

Try this

Test your reaction time with a ruler. Ask a friend to hold a 30 cm ruler hanging down, with your thumb and finger open around the zero mark. Without warning, they let go and you catch it. A catch at 20 cm means about 0.2 seconds. Try five times and see if you improve.
