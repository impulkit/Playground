How do Mumbai's local trains carry so many people?

At rush hour, a Mumbai local train built for about 2,000 passengers can carry more than 4,500, packed so tightly that railway engineers call it "super dense crush load".

The short answer

Mumbai's suburban railway carries around 60 to 75 lakh passenger trips on a typical weekday, more than the whole population of many countries. It does it with frequent trains, long trains, separate fast and slow tracks, and passengers who pack in far beyond normal limits.

At rush hour, trains on the busiest lines can arrive every three or four minutes. Each has 12 or 15 coaches. A 12-coach train is rated for about 2,000 passengers, but during the morning and evening peaks it often carries more than 4,500. People hang from doorways and squeeze into every gap. The system works, but at a huge cost in comfort and safety.

Surprising but true

In the busiest moments, Mumbai's local trains can have 14 to 16 standing passengers squeezed into every square metre of floor, a patch smaller than a single bed.

A city shaped like a finger

Mumbai is a long, narrow city, squeezed between the Arabian Sea and Thane Creek. Its old business centre is at the southern tip. Many people live in the northern suburbs and travel south every morning.

There is only so much land, so three main railway corridors carry most of these journeys: the Western line, the Central line and the Harbour line. Roads are crowded too, and a car journey can take two or three times as long at rush hour. For millions of people, the local train is the fastest and cheapest way to work. A monthly season ticket can cost less than a single taxi ride across the city.

Fast trains and slow trains

The busiest lines have separate tracks for slow trains, which stop at every station, and fast trains, which skip many. A board on each platform shows which is coming and where it will stop. Commuters learn to read these instantly.

Stops are short, often under 30 seconds. Passengers start getting off before the train has fully stopped, and others climb on before everyone is out. Regulars know which coach will stop near the exit at their station. There are coaches only for women, and some for people with disabilities and older passengers.

Crush load

Railway planners measure crowding in people per square metre. On a comfortable train, it might be four. In Mumbai at peak times, it can reach 14 to 16, called super dense crush load. That's like fitting a whole cricket team, plus a few substitutes, inside a square of tape one metre on each side.

The crowding is dangerous. People fall from open doorways or are hit while crossing tracks. In the 2000s, thousands of people died on Mumbai's tracks every year. Deaths have fallen since, thanks to new bridges, fences and safety campaigns, but the problem is not solved. New trains with automatic closing doors are being added.

More than a century of trains

India's first passenger train ran in Mumbai, then called Bombay, on 16 April 1853, from Bori Bunder to Thane, about 34 km. The first train was pulled by steam engines. On 3 February 1925, the first electric local train ran from Victoria Terminus to Kurla.

The network has grown ever since, with new lines, longer trains and air-conditioned locals. Central Railway alone ran about 1,800 suburban services a day in 2026.

Easing the squeeze

Mumbai is building new metro lines, more local trains and new railway tracks to spread the crowd. The Mumbai Trans Harbour Link and coastal roads add other routes. Planners hope these will make the morning trip less of a squeeze.

But the local train remains Mumbai's heartbeat. On a typical morning, the trains carry office workers, students, vendors, musicians and fishermen with baskets. People play cards, sing bhajans, chop vegetables for dinner and share news. And the dabbawalas ride alongside them, their lunchboxes stacked in crates, depending on the same trains to get every lunch there on time.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Mumbai's local trains run without any timetable, whenever they are full.

Truth: They follow a detailed timetable, with trains every few minutes at peak hours. Each train is marked "fast" or "slow" and shows its destination, and regular commuters know exactly which train they will catch.
