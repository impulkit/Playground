How does India keep its voter list correct?

Having a voter ID card is not enough to vote in India. Your name must also be written in one particular list for your own polling booth, and that list is checked again and again.

The short answer

India keeps a list of everyone who is allowed to vote, called the electoral roll. There is a separate list for each polling booth. The Election Commission updates these lists every year, adding new voters and removing names of people who have died, moved away or appear twice.

Sometimes it does a bigger, more careful check from scratch, called an intensive revision. Getting the list right matters, because a name wrongly left out means a person cannot vote, and a wrong name left in can be misused.

Surprising but true

In 2025, India's voter lists held nearly 100 crore names, more than the entire population of Europe. Every name is tied to one polling booth, and each booth has its own list.

How it works

India's Constitution promises one vote to every adult citizen, rich or poor. To be on the list, you must be an Indian citizen, at least 18 years old, and an ordinary resident of that area. Here is how the list stays up to date:

  • 1. Applying. New voters fill in Form 6, online or on paper. People who move apply in their new area.
  • 2. Checking. A Booth Level Officer, often a local teacher or government worker, visits homes to confirm who lives there.
  • 3. Draft list. A draft roll is published so everyone can look for their name.
  • 4. Claims and objections. Anyone can ask for a missing name to be added, or object to a wrong name using Form 7. Officers hear these cases.
  • 5. Final list. The corrected list is published and used on election day.

Think of a teacher's class register at the start of the school year. The teacher must add new students, remove those who left, and fix spelling mistakes. Crossing out a child who is still in class by mistake would be unfair, so the teacher checks carefully. India must do this for crores of people.

Where it came from

India's first general election, in 1951 and 1952, needed a voter list made from nothing. Workers went door to door and wrote down about 17 crore names by hand. Many women at first gave only names like "wife of" or "mother of", and officials had to ask for their real names.

Over time, the system grew. In 1989, the voting age was lowered from 21 to 18, adding millions of young voters. In the 1990s, photo identity cards for voters were introduced. Today, people can check their names online.

Lists can go wrong in many ways. People die and their names stay on. Families move to another city and get added there, while their old names remain. Spelling mistakes creep in when names are copied from one language to another. A woman who moves after marriage may be listed in two places, or in neither.

Ordinary revisions happen every year. Intensive revisions, where the list is checked far more deeply, happened in many states in the early 2000s, and then again in a big way starting in 2025.

India's story

In June 2025, the Election Commission began a Special Intensive Revision, or SIR, in Bihar. Every voter had to fill in a form, and some had to show documents. It later spread to many more states and Union Territories in phases.

The changes were huge. In the second phase, covering 12 states and Union Territories including Uttar Pradesh, West Bengal, Tamil Nadu and Gujarat, the number of voters fell from about 51 crore to about 46 crore, according to one count. That is more than 5 crore names, about one in every ten. Officials said millions of those removed had died, and others had moved or were listed twice. A third phase began in May 2026 in 16 more states and 3 Union Territories.

In May 2026, the Supreme Court said the SIR was lawful. But protests continued. Opposition parties and many citizen groups say some genuine voters, especially poor people and workers who move for jobs, were removed because they lacked papers. In October 2026, marches in Delhi demanded that the SIR be stopped.

The numbers that matter

  • - Nearly 100 crore: India's voters in 2025, before the SIR, the largest electorate in the world.
  • - About 17 crore: voters on India's first list, in 1951 to 1952.
  • - More than 5 crore: names removed in the SIR's second phase, about 10% of the list in those states.
  • - About 67 lakh: names removed in the SIR's second phase because the voter had died, according to reports.
  • - 18: the voting age since 1989. Since 2022, young people can apply when they turn 18 on any of four dates in the year, not just on 1 January.

What people argue about

The Election Commission and its supporters say clean lists protect every vote. If dead people or duplicate names stay on the list, someone could misuse them. They say officers give people chances to object and to be added back.

Critics say the checks put too much burden on ordinary people to prove they belong, and that the poor, migrants and women who changed homes after marriage are most likely to be left out. Some want the Chief Election Commissioner to resign. Others say the protests are political. Both sides agree on one thing: every eligible citizen should be able to vote. They disagree on whether the SIR helps or hurts that goal.

Myths and mix-ups

"A voter ID card means you are on the list." The card proves who you are. You can vote only if your name is on the roll for your booth.

"Once removed, you can never vote again." A removed person who is eligible can apply again using Form 6.

"The voter list and the Census are the same." The Census counts everyone who lives in India, including children. The voter list includes only adult citizens who have registered.

What it means for you

When you turn 18, applying to get your name on the voter list is one of your first big rights and duties as a citizen. Families should check that every adult's name is on the list before an election, especially after moving house.

You can search for your name on the Election Commission's website or ask your Booth Level Officer. Knowing how voting works and making sure your name is in the right place is how a democracy makes sure every voice counts.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If you have a voter ID card, you can always vote.

Truth: The card shows who you are, but what really counts is whether your name is on the electoral roll for your booth. If your name has been removed or never added, you need to apply to get it on the list.

Try this

Make a "class voter list" with your friends. Write each person's name, age and home street. Then pretend a month has passed. Who moved? Who joined? Who is listed twice by mistake? Fixing it fairly, without wrongly crossing anyone out, is harder than it looks.
