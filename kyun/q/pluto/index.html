Why isn't Pluto called a planet any more?

Discovered in 1930, Pluto was reclassified in 2006. Some scientists still argue about it.

The short answer

Pluto was discovered in 1930 by Clyde Tombaugh, a young astronomer at Lowell Observatory in Arizona, USA. For 76 years it was called the ninth planet.

Then astronomers found many more icy worlds out beyond Neptune, some almost as big as Pluto. In 2006, the International Astronomical Union, which names objects in space, made a rule. A planet must orbit the Sun, be round, and have cleared its path of other objects. Pluto shares its path with lots of icy bodies, so it became a dwarf planet.

Surprising but true

Pluto has a giant heart-shaped region, half of it a plain of nitrogen ice, spotted by the New Horizons spacecraft in 2015.

A farm boy finds a planet

Clyde Tombaugh grew up on a farm in Kansas and built his own telescopes. He sent drawings of Mars and Jupiter to Lowell Observatory, and in 1929 they hired him, aged 22, without a university degree.

His job was slow. He photographed the same patches of sky a few nights apart, then flicked back and forth between the photos with a special viewer. Stars stay put. Anything that moved might be a planet. On 18 February 1930, after months of searching, he spotted one faint dot jumping. Pluto has not even finished one trip around the Sun since then. It will complete its first in 2178.

Named by a schoolgirl

The new world needed a name. In Oxford, England, an 11-year-old girl named Venetia Burney suggested Pluto, the Roman god of the underworld, a fitting name for a dark, cold place. Her grandfather passed the idea to an astronomer, and the observatory chose it.

The first two letters, P and L, are also the initials of Percival Lowell, who had founded the observatory and spent years hunting for a ninth planet.

Why do names have meanings?Almost every name was once an ordinary word. Aditya means sun, Smith meant a metal worker, and Johnson meant "son of John".

Crowded neighbourhood

From the 1990s, astronomers began finding hundreds of icy objects in the same region as Pluto, now called the Kuiper Belt. In 2005, they found Eris, which is almost as wide as Pluto and has more mass.

That forced a choice. Either Eris and maybe dozens of others were planets too, or Pluto was something different. Astronomers voted in Prague in 2006 and chose the second answer. The decision was so famous that "plutoed", meaning demoted, was picked as the 2006 Word of the Year by the American Dialect Society.

Not everyone agrees

Some scientists think the rule is unfair. Alan Stern, who leads the New Horizons mission, argues that anything big enough to pull itself into a ball should count as a planet. By that rule, Pluto has mountains, weather, a hazy sky and five moons, and is clearly a planet. Others say "clearing the path" is a useful way to sort the solar system. The argument continues.

A heart in space

New Horizons launched in 2006, the same year Pluto was reclassified. It travelled for nine and a half years and flew past Pluto in July 2015. It found mountains of water ice as tall as the Rockies, blue haze and a huge heart-shaped region now called Tombaugh Regio. Its left half is a vast plain of frozen nitrogen named Sputnik Planitia. A little of Clyde Tombaugh's ashes travelled on the spacecraft.

Pluto's biggest moon, Charon, is about half as wide as Pluto. The two circle each other like dancers holding hands. Pluto is about 2,380 km wide, smaller than our own Moon. See how it compares with the planets, or meet its icy neighbours, the comets.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pluto stopped being a planet because it is too small.

Truth: Size was not the rule that ruled it out. The 2006 definition says a planet must have cleared its path around the Sun of other objects. Pluto shares its zone with many icy bodies, so it was called a dwarf planet instead.

Try this

Draw about 30 small dots on a sheet of paper for stars. Copy the page exactly, but move one dot a tiny bit. Now flip quickly between the two pages. The moving dot jumps out at you. Clyde Tombaugh found Pluto this way, flicking between photos of the sky taken a few nights apart.
