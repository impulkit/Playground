How big was the dinosaur asteroid?

Not as big as you might think. Its speed did the damage.

The short answer

The asteroid was about 10 km wide, tall as a mountain, but tiny compared with Earth.

It was moving about 20 km every second. At that speed you'd go from Mumbai to Pune in 6 seconds.

It made the Chicxulub crater (say: CHEEK-shoo-loob), about 180 km wide, now buried under Mexico. The impact, about 66 million years ago, set off a chain of disasters that wiped out roughly three-quarters of all kinds of plants and animals, including most dinosaurs.

Surprising but true

Compared with Earth, the asteroid was like a grain of sand hitting a football.

Small rock, enormous energy

Ten kilometres is about the height at which passenger jets cruise. Stood on end, the asteroid would have been a little taller than Mount Everest. Earth, by comparison, is almost 12,800 km wide.

What made the asteroid so destructive was its speed. Something moving at 20 km a second carries a staggering amount of energy. When it hit, it punched through the shallow sea and deep into the rock below in a fraction of a second. The rock itself flowed like liquid for a few minutes, then froze into a crater with a ring of peaks inside it, like the splash of a stone in mud.

Scientists think the asteroid was a carbon-rich type. A study published in 2024 suggested that it came from the outer solar system, beyond Jupiter, before something knocked it inwards.

A very bad day

Within minutes, a blast of heat and a shower of hot debris spread out from the crater. Giant waves, far bigger than any modern tsunami, raced across the young Gulf of Mexico. Rock vapour shot high above the atmosphere, then rained back down across the planet as tiny glassy beads.

The worst effects came later. The impact threw vast amounts of dust, soot and sulphur into the upper air. They spread around the world and blocked much of the sunlight, perhaps for years. Plants and tiny ocean organisms that need light died off, and the animals that fed on them followed.

Some animals survived, mostly small ones that could hide, burrow or eat seeds and scraps. Among them were mammals and a few groups of birds.

How we found the crater

In 1980, the father-and-son scientists Luis and Walter Alvarez studied a thin layer of clay in rocks near Gubbio, in Italy. The clay was full of iridium, a metal that is rare in Earth's crust but common in asteroids. They found the same layer around the world, and suggested that a huge asteroid had hit. You can follow that detective story in the iridium evidence.

But where was the crater? In 1978, two geophysicists working for Mexico's state oil company, Glen Penfield and Antonio Camargo, had noticed a giant buried ring under the Yucatán Peninsula while searching for oil. In 1991, Alan Hildebrand and his colleagues showed that this ring was an impact crater of exactly the right age.

Buried but not gone

Today you can't see Chicxulub from the ground. It lies under about a kilometre of younger rock, half beneath land and half beneath the sea, near the small town of Chicxulub Puerto. The only clue on the surface is a curved line of sinkholes, called cenotes, that roughly traces the crater's edge.

In 2016, scientists drilled into the crater's ring of peaks from a platform at sea. The rock cores they brought up showed how the crater formed, and how quickly life returned to the area afterwards: within a few years, tiny living things were back.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The asteroid wiped out all the dinosaurs.

Truth: It wiped out all the dinosaurs except one group, the birds. Birds are living dinosaurs, descended from small feathered dinosaurs that survived. So every pigeon and sparrow is a dinosaur that made it.

Try this

Fill a baking tray with a few centimetres of flour and sprinkle a thin layer of cocoa powder on top. Drop marbles or small pebbles from different heights, up to about shoulder height. Look at the craters and the splashes of white flour thrown out. Faster drops make bigger craters, even with the same marble.
