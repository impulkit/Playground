What machines did ancient people build?

An iron pillar in Delhi has stood out in the rain for about 1,600 years, and it has barely rusted.

The short answer

Ancient people built many clever machines, often using a few simple ideas: the lever, the wheel, the pulley, the wedge and the screw. With them they lifted water, moved giant stones, told the time and even predicted eclipses.

The ancient Greeks made gear-driven calculators and toy steam engines. Chinese inventors built an earthquake detector and a carriage with a pointer that always faced south. In the Indus Valley, people built covered drains and carefully planned cities over 4,000 years ago. And in India, metalworkers made iron so well that one pillar has hardly rusted in about 1,600 years.

Surprising but true

Nearly 2,000 years ago, Heron of Alexandria described a spinning ball powered by steam, and a temple machine that took coins and gave out holy water.

Lifting water and stone

Moving water was one of the biggest jobs in the ancient world. In Egypt and Mesopotamia, farmers used the shaduf, a long pole with a bucket on one end and a heavy weight on the other. A worker could swing water up from a river with little effort, using the pole as a lever.

The Archimedes' screw, named after the Greek scientist who lived in the 3rd century BCE, is a spiral inside a tube. Turn it, and water climbs up the spiral. Similar screws are still used today to lift water and even move grain. In Persia, engineers dug qanats, long underground tunnels that carried water from hills to villages without losing much to evaporation.

How did people build pyramids, temples and huge forts without engines? With levers, ramps, sledges, ropes and lots of organised workers. The Greeks and Romans added cranes powered by pulleys and giant treadwheels, which workers walked inside like hamsters on a wheel. A Roman treadwheel crane could lift several tonnes.

Who invented the wheel?The wheel came surprisingly late. People were farming, making pots and building towns for thousands of years before anyone rolled a cart.

Heron's wonders

Heron of Alexandria, an engineer in Roman Egypt in the 1st century CE, wrote about many inventions. His aeolipile was a hollow metal ball that spun when steam shot out of two bent pipes, an early steam engine, though it was treated as a curiosity. He also described a machine for temples that gave out a measure of holy water when someone dropped in a coin, often called the first vending machine.

Heron designed automatic theatre shows, with tiny figures moved by falling weights and strings, and temple doors that opened by themselves when a fire was lit on the altar.

Chinese inventions

In ancient China, Zhang Heng built a seismoscope in 132 CE: a bronze jar with eight dragons around it, each holding a ball. When an earthquake shook the ground, even far away, a ball dropped from one dragon's mouth into a bronze toad's mouth below. The south-pointing chariot used gears so that a figure on top kept pointing the same way, however the chariot turned.

India's rustless pillar

In the Qutb complex in Delhi stands an iron pillar more than 7 metres tall. It is about 1,600 years old, from the Gupta period, and carries an inscription in Sanskrit. Iron usually rusts quickly in rain, but this pillar has stayed almost free of rust.

Scientists, including teams from IIT Kanpur, found that the iron contains a lot of phosphorus. Over time this helped form a thin protective layer on the surface that keeps out water and air. Ancient Indian smiths couldn't have known the chemistry, but their way of making iron worked. Earlier still, the cities of the Indus Valley had brick drains running under their streets, something many cities in the world didn't have until the 1800s.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ancient people were too primitive to build the pyramids and other great monuments by themselves, so they must have had help from aliens.

Truth: Archaeologists have found the tools, ramps, quarries, workers' villages and even written records of the people who built the pyramids. Ancient builders were clever engineers using levers, ramps, sledges and huge teams of skilled workers.

Try this

Make a simple Archimedes' screw. Wrap a clear plastic tube in a spiral around a bottle, tape it in place, then put the bottom end in a bowl of water with the bottle tilted. Turn the bottle slowly and watch water climb up the spiral and pour out of the top end into a second bowl.
