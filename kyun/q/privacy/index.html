Why does privacy matter?

In 1890, two American lawyers worried that new pocket cameras and gossip newspapers would leave people no peace. They called for a "right to be let alone".

The short answer

Privacy is your right to keep parts of your life to yourself and to control who knows what about you. It covers your body, your home, your letters and messages, and, more and more, the data about you stored on computers.

Privacy matters because information is power. Someone who knows where you live, what you search for and who your friends are could sell that knowledge, use it to cheat you, or pressure you. Privacy also gives people room to think, make mistakes and grow without being watched. Many countries now treat it as a basic right.

Surprising but true

Many apps ask users to be at least 13 years old because of a 1998 US law that strictly limits collecting personal data from younger children.

Cameras and gossip

In 1890, two Boston lawyers, Samuel Warren and Louis Brandeis, wrote an essay called "The Right to Privacy". New, cheap cameras meant anyone could be photographed without permission, and newspapers were printing gossip about private lives. They argued that the law should protect "the right to be let alone".

Brandeis later became a judge on the US Supreme Court. The US Constitution doesn't use the word privacy, but its Fourth Amendment protects people against unreasonable searches of their homes and belongings, and courts built on that idea.

India's big judgment

For decades, it wasn't clear whether India's Constitution protected privacy. The question became urgent with Aadhaar, the national ID system that collects fingerprints and eye scans from over a billion people.

A retired judge, Justice K. S. Puttaswamy, challenged the system in court. The government pointed to two older judgments, from 1954 and 1962, which had suggested the Constitution gave no right to privacy. On 24 August 2017, a bench of nine Supreme Court judges ruled unanimously that privacy is a fundamental right, part of the right to life and liberty. They overruled the older judgments. Like other rights, it can be limited, but only by a fair law with a good reason. A separate judgment the next year allowed Aadhaar to continue with some limits.

Your data trail

Every time you use an app, search the web or play an online game, you leave a trail of data. Companies use it to recommend videos, show adverts and improve their products. Sometimes data is sold, leaked or stolen.

Laws try to put limits on this. Europe's General Data Protection Regulation, in force since 2018, gives people the right to see and delete data held about them. India passed its own Digital Personal Data Protection Act in 2023. In the USA, a 1998 law protects children under 13 online, which is why many apps ask for your age.

Protecting yourself

Some privacy depends on laws, and some depends on you. Strong passwords, not sharing your full name, school or address with strangers online, and asking before posting photos of friends all help.

Encryption scrambles messages so only the right person can read them, which is why many chat apps use it. Privacy also means respecting other people's. Anne Frank's diary, written while her family hid during the Second World War, began as a private notebook. She later rewrote parts of it, hoping to publish it one day, and her father did so after her death. Reading a sibling's diary or forwarding a friend's message without asking breaks the same rule that the courts protect.

Not every place agrees on the balance. Police and governments sometimes argue they need access to data to stop crimes, while privacy campaigners worry about too much watching. That tug-of-war is likely to continue as technology changes.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If you have nothing to hide, privacy doesn't matter.

Truth: Everyone has things they keep private, like health details, passwords, a diary or where they live. Privacy protects people from being tracked, cheated or embarrassed, and lets them think and speak freely.

Try this

With an adult, open the settings of one app on a phone or tablet and look at its permissions. Does it ask for your location, camera, microphone or contacts? For each one, ask whether the app really needs it to work. Switch off any that it doesn't, if the adult agrees.
