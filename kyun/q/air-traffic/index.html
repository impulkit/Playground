How do air traffic controllers keep planes apart?

Thousands of planes share the sky at once, and each one is kept inside an invisible bubble of empty air.

The short answer

Air traffic controllers keep planes apart by giving each one its own slice of sky. They watch every plane on radar screens and talk to pilots by radio, telling them which height to fly at, which way to turn and when to climb or descend.

The basic rule is simple. Two planes must never be closer than a set distance, either up and down or side to side. At cruising height, that is usually 1,000 feet (about 300 m) vertically or several kilometres horizontally. Controllers hand each flight from one team to the next, like a baton in a relay race, from the gate at one airport to the gate at another.

Surprising but true

The first air traffic controller, Archie League, worked in St. Louis in 1929 with a set of flags, a wheelbarrow and a beach umbrella.

Flags, then radios, then radar

In 1929, at Lambert Field in St. Louis, a young pilot named Archie League stood on the airfield with a red flag and a chequered one. Red meant "hold", chequered meant "go". He is often called the first air traffic controller.

Flags worked when only a few planes came in each hour. Soon, controllers used radio to talk to pilots instead. After the Second World War, radar arrived: a spinning antenna sends out pulses of radio waves, and echoes bouncing off planes show up as moving dots on a screen.

A tragedy pushed things further. In 1956, two airliners collided over the Grand Canyon in clear weather, outside any radar coverage. Two years later the United States set up a federal agency to control the country's skies, now called the FAA.

How does radio travel through the air?Radio waves don't need air at all. They are a kind of invisible light that can cross empty space.

A relay across the sky

A typical flight passes through several teams. "Ground" controllers guide planes along taxiways. The "tower" clears them for take-off from the runway. "Approach" controllers guide them as they climb away from the airport. Then "centre" controllers, often hundreds of kilometres away, look after them while they cruise.

Each team has its own patch of sky. As a plane reaches the edge, the controller tells the pilot to switch to a new radio frequency and say hello to the next team. In India, the Airports Authority of India runs this system, with large control centres in cities such as Delhi, Mumbai, Kolkata and Chennai.

The job is famously demanding. Controllers train for years, take regular breaks and work in shifts, because a tired mind makes mistakes. Computers help by warning them when two planes are heading for the same patch of sky. Airliners also carry their own collision warning systems, which talk to each other and tell pilots to climb or descend if they ever get too close.

Speaking the same language

Pilots and controllers use short, fixed phrases so nothing is misunderstood. English is the international language of flying, and letters are spelled with a special alphabet: Alpha, Bravo, Charlie, Delta. "Nine" is said "niner", so it is not confused with the German word "nein".

Every plane carries a transponder, a box that answers radar with a four-digit code. Controllers see that code next to the dot on their screens. Some codes have special meanings: 7700 means an emergency, and every controller who sees it pays attention at once.

Over the empty ocean

Radar can only see as far as the horizon, so over the middle of an ocean there is no radar at all. For decades, pilots there reported their position by radio every so often, and planes were kept much farther apart.

Today most airliners broadcast their own position, worked out by satellite navigation, several times a second. Satellites and ground stations pick up these signals, so controllers can now track planes in places radar never reached. That has allowed more flights to use the busiest ocean routes safely.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Once a plane is in the air, the pilot can fly wherever they like.

Truth: Airliners follow planned routes called airways, at heights given to them by controllers. A pilot who wants to climb, descend or turn off the route must ask permission first, except in a real emergency.

Try this

Play a controller game with friends. Mark "runways" on the floor with tape and give each "plane" a card with a number. Only the controller can say who moves, using short clear instructions like "Flight 7, turn left, stop". Try it with talking allowed and without, and notice how much safer short, fixed phrases make things.
