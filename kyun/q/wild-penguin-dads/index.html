Why do emperor penguin dads hold the eggs?

An emperor penguin dad balances an egg on his feet for about two months of Antarctic darkness, eating nothing at all.

The short answer

Emperor penguins breed in the middle of the Antarctic winter, on sea ice far from open water. The mother lays a single egg, then she is exhausted and hungry. So she passes the egg to the father and walks back to the sea to feed.

The father keeps the egg warm on top of his feet, under a fold of feathery skin called a brood pouch. For about 65 days, through months of darkness, he hardly moves and eats nothing. Only when the mother returns, around the time the chick hatches, can he finally head off to eat. By then he may have fasted for about four months.

Surprising but true

If the chick hatches before its mother returns, the starving father can still feed it a milky food made in his own throat.

Why breed in winter?

It sounds like a terrible plan. Temperatures can drop below -40°C and storms can blow at well over 100 km/h. But timing is everything. A chick needs months to grow its waterproof feathers. If eggs hatch in late winter, chicks are ready to swim by summer, when the sea ice breaks up and food is plentiful.

The mother has already used much of her energy to make the egg. Sending her back to the ocean first means someone is always building up strength for the next stage.

The tricky egg swap

Passing the egg is the most dangerous moment. The egg, about as heavy as three cricket balls, must roll from her feet to his without touching the ice for long. In such cold, an egg left uncovered for a minute or two can freeze, killing the chick inside.

Some couples fumble it, and the egg is lost. Once the egg is safe, the female leaves on a trek that can be tens of kilometres to the open sea.

The huddle

The fathers survive by huddling. Thousands of birds press together, often about 10 to every square metre. The middle of a huddle can rise above 30°C, so hot that birds sometimes have to step out to cool down.

Nobody gets stuck on the cold edge forever. Every so often the whole huddle shuffles a few centimetres. These small moves ripple through the crowd like a wave, slowly rotating birds between the centre and the outside, each still carrying an egg on his feet.

Milk from a dad

During the fast, a father can lose up to about 40% of his body weight. If the chick hatches before the mother is back, he does something strange. He makes a thick, milky, protein-rich food in his oesophagus, the tube to his stomach, and feeds it to the chick. It can keep the chick going for a few days.

When the mother returns, she finds her partner by his call, among thousands of other penguins. Each bird's voice is different. Then they swap, and the thin dad finally heads for the sea.

A terrible journey for three eggs

In July 1911, three men from Robert Falcon Scott's British expedition walked through the polar night to an emperor colony at Cape Crozier. They wanted eggs to study how birds evolved. Temperatures fell to about -60°C. One of them, Apsley Cherry-Garrard, later called his book about it The Worst Journey in the World. The tale is part of the bigger story of polar explorers.

Penguins from space

Today scientists find emperor colonies using satellites, by spotting the brown stains of penguin droppings on white ice. A count from 2009 found about 595,000 adults. In 2022, sea ice broke up early in one region and thousands of chicks were lost before they could swim. A 2025 study found that emperor numbers in one large region had fallen by about a fifth since 2009. The link between disappearing sea ice and a warming planet is explained in climate change.

In 2006 the film March of the Penguins, which follows this winter march, won an Oscar in the USA.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Penguins live at both the North Pole and the South Pole.

Truth: Wild penguins live almost entirely in the Southern Hemisphere. There are none in the Arctic. Polar bears and penguins only meet in cartoons and zoos.
