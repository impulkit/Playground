How do apps use your data?

Many free apps aren't really free. They earn money by learning what you like, where you go and what you tap, then selling ads aimed at you.

The short answer

Apps collect information about you, called data. Some of it you give on purpose, such as your name, age or photos. Much more is gathered quietly: where your phone is, what you tap, how long you watch a video and which other apps you use.

Apps use this data to work properly, to improve themselves and, very often, to show you adverts. Free apps usually make money from ads, and ads that match your interests earn more. That's why your data is valuable. Laws in India and the US try to protect children's data in particular.

Surprising but true

Many apps and websites say you must be 13 or older because of a US law from 1998, which makes them get a parent's permission before collecting data from younger children.

What apps can see

When you install an app, it may ask for permissions. These are switches that let it use parts of your phone, like the camera, microphone, contacts, photos or location. A map app needs your location. A photo editor needs your pictures. A game probably needs neither.

Location comes from your phone's GPS, Wi-Fi and mobile towers. Some apps check it constantly, even when you aren't using them. Over a week, that can show where you live, which school you go to and where you play on Sundays. Photos can carry location too, and contacts reveal who your friends and family are, even if those people never installed the app.

Data you don't notice

Apps also record behaviour. They note which posts you stop scrolling at, what you search for, what time you go to bed and which buttons you press. Each click seems tiny, but together they build a detailed picture of you.

Many apps share data with other companies, such as advertising networks and analytics firms. Some companies, called data brokers, buy information from many sources and combine it. This is how a shoe you looked at once can follow you around the internet for days. It isn't magic or spying by one app alone. It's many small pieces of data passed from company to company.

How do adverts try to persuade you?The first official TV advert in the USA, in 1941, showed a watch ticking over a map of the country for about ten seconds. It cost the company $9.

Laws that protect children

In 1998 the United States passed COPPA, the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act. It says apps and websites aimed at children under 13 must get a parent's permission before collecting their personal data. That's why many apps say you must be 13 to join.

India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act of 2023 goes further for young people. It says companies need a parent's consent to handle the data of anyone under 18. They are not supposed to track children's behaviour or aim ads at them. Europe has strict rules too. These laws take time to enforce, and not every company follows them well.

Reading the label

Since 2020, Apple's App Store has shown "privacy labels" that list what each app collects, a bit like the ingredients on a food packet. Google Play added a similar data safety section in 2022. They're worth a look before you download something new, though they rely on companies telling the truth.

You can also check permissions in your phone's settings and turn off anything an app doesn't need. Strong, different passwords and two-step login make it harder for anyone to break into your accounts.

Smart habits

Before you sign up for something, ask an adult. Use a nickname instead of your full name where you can, and don't share your address, school or phone number in games or chats. Turn location off for apps that don't need it.

Remember that everything you post or search adds to your digital footprint, which can last for years. Thinking before you tap is the simplest form of privacy. Most companies aren't evil, but they are businesses, and your attention and data are what many of them sell.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Apps secretly listen to everything you say through your phone's microphone to choose your ads.

Truth: Researchers who tested thousands of apps found no clear sign of this happening widely. Ads seem eerily accurate mostly because apps already know your searches, location, friends and clicks, which reveal a lot.

Try this

With a parent, open the settings on a family phone or tablet and look at the permissions for three apps. For each one, decide whether the app really needs its camera, microphone or location to do its job. Switch off any that don't make sense, with your parent's agreement.
