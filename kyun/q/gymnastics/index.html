How do gymnasts flip and land?

A gymnast can spin faster in mid-air without pushing off anything, just by pulling their arms and knees in tight.

The short answer

Gymnasts flip by pushing hard against the floor, a springboard or a bar to launch themselves upwards and set their bodies spinning. Once in the air, nothing can change how much spin they have. But they can change how fast they turn by changing their shape.

Pulling the knees and arms in tight, called a tuck, makes them spin faster. Stretching out slows the spin. To land, a gymnast opens out at the right moment, spots the floor and bends the knees to soak up the force of hitting the ground.

Surprising but true

When Nadia Comăneci scored the first perfect 10 in Olympic gymnastics in 1976, the scoreboard showed 1.00, because it had not been built to display 10.

The physics of a flip

Spinning objects carry something physicists call angular momentum. Once a gymnast leaves the ground, the total amount stays the same. If they pull their body in closer to the centre of the spin, they have to spin faster to keep the same amount.

This is why a gymnast tucks for a double somersault and opens out to land. Gravity gives them a limited time in the air, often less than a second, so every turn must be squeezed into it. Twists work in a similar way, using arm movements to tilt the body.

Sticking the landing

Landing is the hardest part. A gymnast falling from a high flip can hit the mat with a force several times their body weight. By bending the knees and hips, they spread that impact over a slightly longer time, which reduces the strain on bones and joints.

Judges reward a landing with no steps, called sticking it. Floors and mats help. The floor exercise area is 12 metres square and sits on springs or foam blocks, giving gymnasts extra bounce.

From the gymnasium to the Olympics

The word gymnastics comes from the ancient Greek "gymnos", meaning naked, because Greek athletes trained without clothes at the gymnasium. Modern gymnastics took shape in Germany in the early 1800s. A teacher named Friedrich Ludwig Jahn is credited with inventing the parallel bars and the horizontal bar.

Gymnastics was part of the first modern Olympics in 1896. Women's events joined in 1928. Today women compete on four pieces of apparatus and men on six, each testing different muscles. The balance beam is just 10 cm wide, narrower than a mobile phone is long, and more than a metre off the floor.

The perfect 10, and after

At the 1976 Montreal Olympics, 14-year-old Nadia Comăneci of Romania scored the first perfect 10. The scoreboard could only show three digits, so it read 1.00.

In 2006, gymnastics replaced the perfect 10 with an open-ended system. One panel scores difficulty, adding points for harder skills. Another scores execution out of 10, taking points off for mistakes. That rewarded gymnasts who tried harder skills. American Simone Biles has so many original moves that several are named after her.

India's gymnasts and the pole

At the 2016 Rio Olympics, Dipa Karmakar of Tripura became the first Indian woman to compete in Olympic gymnastics. She finished fourth in the vault final, performing the Produnova, one of the most dangerous vaults in the sport.

India also has its own gymnastic tradition, mallakhamb, where athletes perform poses and spins on a wooden pole or a hanging rope. It grew in Maharashtra and is now played at India's national games. Like modern skateboarding, it shows how far balance and practice can take a human body.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Gymnastics is still scored out of a perfect 10.

Truth: Since 2006, a score adds a difficulty mark to an execution mark out of 10. Top scores are now often in the 14s and 15s, so a perfect 10 no longer exists.

Try this

Sit on a swivel chair with your arms stretched out, and ask a friend to give you a gentle spin. Then pull your arms in to your chest. You should speed up. Stretch them out again and you slow down. Keep it gentle and stop if you feel dizzy.
