What is 5G, and is it really faster?

The "G" on your phone stands for generation, and each new one has changed what a phone can do, from voice calls to live video.

The short answer

5G is the fifth generation of mobile phone networks. Like 4G before it, it carries calls and internet data between your phone and nearby towers using radio waves. The difference is that 5G can carry much more data at once, and with a much shorter delay.

On a good 5G connection, a full film can download in under a minute. In everyday use, speeds are usually a few hundred megabits per second, several times faster than typical 4G, though it depends a lot on where you are. 5G networks launched in South Korea and the United States in April 2019, and in India on 1 October 2022.

Surprising but true

The fastest kind of 5G uses radio waves so short that a wall, a tree or even rain can weaken them.

One generation at a time

Each "G" brought a new trick. 1G, in the 1980s, carried voice calls as analogue signals, and anyone with the right radio could listen in. 2G, in the 1990s, turned calls digital and added text messages. 3G made the internet usable on a phone. 4G, around 2010, made it fast enough for video calls and streaming.

How does a mobile network connect your call?When you call someone across India, your voice leaps by radio to a tower, races through cables, and finds one phone among more than a billion.

5G is built for a world where phones are only part of the story. Sensors, cars, factory robots and smart meters all want to connect too, and 5G can handle far more devices in the same area.

Faster, and less waiting

Speed is how much data arrives each second. Latency is how long the first bit takes to arrive after you ask for it. On 4G that delay is often a few tens of thousandths of a second. On 5G it can drop to just a few.

That sounds tiny, but it matters for things that must react instantly. A doctor guiding a robot from far away, or a car swapping warnings with the car ahead, can't afford to wait. People hope low latency will help self-driving cars and remote-controlled machines, though many of these uses are still being tested.

Short waves, many antennas

Radio waves come in different frequencies. 5G uses three main bands. Low frequencies travel far and pass through walls, but carry less data. Middle frequencies, the main band used in India, give a good balance. The highest frequencies, called millimetre waves because their wavelength is only a few millimetres, carry huge amounts of data but fade within a few hundred metres.

Those short waves are stopped by walls, leaves and even heavy rain. So 5G networks use many small antennas, sometimes on lamp posts or bus stops. They also use antennas with dozens of tiny elements that aim a beam straight at your phone, a little like a torch instead of a bare bulb.

Is 5G safe?

During the COVID-19 pandemic, false rumours claimed 5G spread the virus. In Britain, some phone masts were even set on fire. Radio waves can't carry viruses. 5G uses non-ionising waves, which don't have enough energy to damage the DNA in cells, unlike X-rays.

The World Health Organization says that, after much research, no harm to health has been linked to exposure from wireless technologies within international safety limits. Scientists keep studying the higher frequencies as networks grow.

Not everywhere yet

In big Indian cities, 5G spread very quickly after 2022, as companies put up hundreds of thousands of new 5G antennas within two years. In rural areas of both India and the United States, many people still rely on 4G. Most phones switch back and forth without you noticing. The little "5G" at the top of the screen tells you which kind of tower your phone is talking to at that moment.

Myth or fact?

Myth: 5G radio waves spread viruses or damage your health.

Truth: Radio waves can't carry viruses, and 5G uses the same kind of non-ionising radio waves as earlier networks and Wi-Fi. The World Health Organization says no harm to health has been linked to wireless networks at the levels allowed by international guidelines.
