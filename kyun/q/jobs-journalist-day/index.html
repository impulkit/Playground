What is a day in a newsroom like?

A morning newspaper is planned at lunchtime, rewritten all evening and printed after midnight, and it lands on your doorstep before you wake up.

The short answer

A newsroom is the office where journalists gather, check and write the news. For a morning newspaper, the work starts around midday and runs late into the night. Reporters go out to find stories. Editors decide which ones matter most and fix the writing. Photographers, page designers and fact-checkers all play their part.

The day is shaped by one thing: the deadline. When the clock runs out, the pages must go to the printing presses, whether or not every story is perfect. Then trucks, vans and bicycles carry the paper to homes before breakfast. Online news never stops, so many newsrooms now run all day and night.

Surprising but true

In 1889 an American reporter called Nellie Bly raced around the world for her newspaper and made it in 72 days, beating the 80 days in a famous novel.

Morning: chasing the story

A reporter's day often starts on the phone. They check with police stations, hospitals, courts and government offices, and follow up on tips from readers. Some reporters have a "beat", a subject they cover every day, such as sport, schools, science or the city council.

Then they go out. A reporter covering a flood might talk to families, officials and engineers, and take careful notes of exactly what each person said. Most try to answer six questions: who, what, when, where, why and how. A good quote is gold, but it must be the real words the person used.

The afternoon meeting

Around lunchtime or early afternoon, editors meet to plan the next day's paper. Each section editor pitches the stories their team is working on. The group argues about which story deserves the front page and how much space each one gets.

This is where judgment counts. Is a cricket win bigger news than a new metro line? Does a story affect many readers, or only a few? A story that seemed huge at noon can shrink by evening if something bigger happens. Newsrooms always keep space they can change at the last minute.

How did the printing press change the world?Before the printing press, a single book could take a scribe months to copy by hand. Within fifty years of Gutenberg, Europe had printed millions.

Evening: writing against the clock

By late afternoon, reporters are back at their desks, writing. Most news reports follow the inverted pyramid: the key facts in the first sentence, details after, background at the end. That way an editor can trim from the bottom without losing what matters.

Sub-editors, called copy editors in the USA, then check every name, number and spelling. They also write headlines that fit a tight space. Checking matters more than ever, because fake news spreads fast and one wrong fact can damage trust in a whole paper. If a reporter can't confirm something with a second source, it often stays out.

Night: off to the presses

Late in the evening, the finished pages go to the press. The basic idea is the same as Gutenberg's printing press, though today's machines run on huge rolls of paper and print tens of thousands of copies an hour. Bundles are stacked, tied and driven across the city through the night.

Online, the deadline never really arrives. A story goes up on the website within minutes, then gets updated as new facts come in. Many newsrooms now have a night team whose whole job is to watch for breaking news while everyone else sleeps, and a morning team that takes over at dawn.

A reporter who raced the world

Some journalists make the news themselves. In 1889, Nellie Bly, a reporter for the New York World, set out to beat the record of Phileas Fogg, the hero of Jules Verne's novel Around the World in Eighty Days. Travelling mostly by steamship and train, she sent reports home along the way. She arrived back in 72 days, and crowds met her at the station.

Today, a reporter might send a story from a phone in a village with one bar of signal. The rule is still the one Bly followed: get there, see it yourself, and tell readers what you found.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Journalists put their own opinions into every news story.

Truth: Newspapers separate news reports from opinion. A news report tries to tell what happened, with facts and quotes. Opinion columns and editorials, usually on a separate page, are where writers argue a view.
