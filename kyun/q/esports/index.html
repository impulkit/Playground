What are esports?

In 2019 a 16-year-old won 3 million US dollars for coming first in a video game tournament, watched by a packed stadium in New York.

The short answer

Esports are video games played as competitive sports. Professional players and teams compete in organised leagues and tournaments, often for big prizes. Matches are streamed online and shown in arenas, with commentators, replays and cheering fans, much like a cricket or football match.

Popular esports games include League of Legends, Valorant, Counter-Strike, Dota 2 and Fortnite. In India, mobile games such as Battlegrounds Mobile India (BGMI) are hugely popular. Many are played by teams of four or five, though some, like fighting games, are one against one. Esports have become big enough to appear at the Asian Games.

Surprising but true

One of the first video game tournaments, held at Stanford University in 1972, offered as its prize a year's subscription to a music magazine.

How it began

In October 1972, students at Stanford University in California held an "Intergalactic Spacewar Olympics". They played Spacewar!, one of the earliest computer games, and the prize was a year's subscription to Rolling Stone magazine.

In 1980 the company Atari held a Space Invaders championship in the USA, and thousands of players took part. In the late 1990s and 2000s, faster internet let players compete online. South Korea built TV channels and arenas for games like StarCraft, and players there became celebrities.

Big stadiums, big prizes

Today the biggest tournaments fill huge arenas. The League of Legends World Championship has been held in stadiums in Seoul, Beijing and Paris, with millions more watching online.

Prize money can be enormous. At the 2019 Fortnite World Cup in New York, 16-year-old Kyle Giersdorf won 3 million US dollars. Dota 2's tournament, The International, has had prize pools of tens of millions of dollars, much of it raised by fans buying items in the game.

What makes it a sport?

People argue about whether esports are "real" sports. They do not need much physical strength. But they need very fast reflexes, quick decisions, teamwork and hours of practice. Top players can make hundreds of precise mouse clicks and key presses a minute.

Many teams train like athletes. They have coaches who study opponents, analysts who review recordings and fitness trainers. Players are encouraged to exercise, sleep well and take breaks, because tired players make mistakes. Careers are often short. Many professionals retire in their mid-twenties, when their reaction times begin to slow slightly, and move into coaching, commentating or streaming.

Esports in India

India's esports scene has grown fast, especially through smartphones and cheap mobile data. Many fans first played on their phones. In 2018, esports were a demonstration event at the Asian Games in Jakarta. At the Hangzhou Asian Games, held in 2023, they were a full medal event.

In December 2022 the Government of India said esports would be treated as part of multi-sport events, under the sports ministry. In 2025 India passed a law that bans online games played for real money, while supporting esports and other skill-based games.

Playing smart

Doctors and parents worry about too much screen time. Sitting for hours can strain eyes, wrists and backs, and late-night gaming can harm sleep. Most experts suggest regular breaks, good posture and a balance with outdoor play.

The International Olympic Committee has also shown interest in esports, and has talked about holding Olympic Esports Games, though plans have changed more than once. For now, most young players will enjoy games as a hobby. The few who turn pro usually say it takes far more discipline than people expect.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Esports players just sit and play games all day for fun.

Truth: Professionals train in a planned way, study recordings of matches and work closely with teammates. Many teams also limit practice hours, and doctors warn that too much gaming without breaks can harm sleep, eyes and posture.

Try this

Test your reaction time with a ruler. A friend holds a 30-centimetre ruler just above your open fingers and drops it without warning. Catch it as fast as you can and note the number where you caught it. Try five times. Lower numbers mean faster reactions, a skill esports players train.
