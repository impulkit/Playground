How did the most common bird in America vanish?

In 1866 a single flock of pigeons took about 14 hours to fly over one spot in Canada. Less than 50 years later, the species was gone.

The short answer

The passenger pigeon was once probably the most common bird in North America, with perhaps 3 to 5 billion of them in the early 1800s. Flocks were so huge they darkened the sky for hours. Yet by 1900 almost none were left in the wild, and on 1 September 1914 the very last one, named Martha, died in a zoo in Cincinnati, Ohio.

They were wiped out mainly by people. Hunters killed them in staggering numbers to sell as cheap meat, and the forests they needed were cut down for farms. Because passenger pigeons only bred well in giant groups, once their numbers fell, they couldn't recover.

Surprising but true

The last passenger pigeon, Martha, died in Cincinnati Zoo on 1 September 1914. Her body was frozen in a block of ice and sent by train to the Smithsonian in Washington.

A river of birds

People who saw the flocks struggled to describe them. In 1813 the bird artist John James Audubon watched pigeons pass over Kentucky for three days, so thick they blocked out the midday Sun.

In 1866 a flock flew over southern Ontario in Canada that witnesses said was about 1.5 kilometres wide and 500 kilometres long. It took around 14 hours to pass. Some estimates put it at over three billion birds, though nobody could really count.

The birds were sleek and long-tailed, bigger than the pigeons in city squares, with a rosy chest. Their name comes from the French word passager, meaning passing by.

Living in crowds

Passenger pigeons did everything together. They fed on acorns, beechnuts and chestnuts in the great eastern forests, sweeping across the land to wherever the trees had fruited that year.

Their nesting colonies were enormous. One in Wisconsin in 1871 is said to have covered about 2,200 square kilometres, bigger than the city of Delhi. Each pair laid just one egg a year. In such crowds, predators could only eat a tiny share of the chicks. That strategy worked for thousands of years, until people arrived with new tools.

Wires, rails and guns

In the 1800s, the telegraph let hunters learn within hours where a new colony had formed. Railways then carried them there, along with barrels and ice. Professional hunters netted and shot birds by the thousands and shipped them by train to cities like New York and Chicago, where pigeon pie was cheap food.

At the same time, settlers cleared the eastern forests for farmland. Fewer trees meant less food. Colonies became smaller, and small colonies failed. By the 1890s, flocks had nearly vanished. The last confirmed wild passenger pigeon was shot by a boy in Ohio in 1900.

Martha

A few birds lived on in zoos. The last of them was Martha, named after Martha Washington, wife of the first US president. She lived at the Cincinnati Zoo, where visitors came to see the final passenger pigeon. She died in 1914, aged about 29.

Her body was frozen in a block of ice and sent by train to the Smithsonian in Washington, where she's kept to this day. In 2014, a hundred years after her death, the museum put her on display again in an exhibition about lost birds.

The lessons left behind

The pigeon's fall shocked many Americans and helped change the law. In 1900 the USA passed the Lacey Act, one of its first national laws to protect wildlife. Later laws protected migrating birds.

Today, some scientists are studying the passenger pigeon's DNA, from museum specimens, and hope one day to recreate a similar bird using its living relative, the band-tailed pigeon. Whether that's possible, or wise, is one of the big debates around de-extinction. And the strangest part? Australia lost its striped Tasmanian tiger only 22 years after Martha died, for many of the same reasons.

Myth or fact?

Myth: An animal that exists in billions could never go extinct.

Truth: The passenger pigeon was probably the most common bird in North America, and it disappeared in about 50 years. Huge numbers don't protect a species if people kill it faster than it can breed.
