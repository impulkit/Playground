How is paper made?

A sheet of paper is a mat of tiny plant fibres, tangled so tightly they hold together without glue.

The short answer

Paper is made from cellulose, the tough substance in plant cell walls. Most paper today starts as wood from trees, but it can also come from cotton, bamboo, straw, sugarcane waste or old paper.

First, the plant material is broken down into separate fibres. Wood is chopped into chips and then either ground up or cooked with chemicals until the fibres come apart. The fibres are mixed with lots of water to make a runny pulp. This is sprayed onto a fast-moving mesh belt. Water drains away through the holes, leaving a thin wet mat of tangled fibres. Rollers squeeze out more water, and heated drums dry the sheet. Finally, it is pressed smooth and wound onto giant rolls.

A modern paper machine can be longer than a football field and roll out paper faster than a car drives in a city.

Surprising but true

Papyrus, the ancient Egyptian writing material, isn't really paper. It was made by pressing strips of a plant's stem together, not by breaking plants down into fibres.

Fibres that hold hands

Look at the torn edge of a piece of paper with a magnifying glass, and you'll see fuzzy fibres sticking out. Each one is a tiny tube from a plant. When the pulp dries, these fibres press close together and form countless small bonds where they touch. Nothing else is needed to hold them.

Papermakers often add other things, such as clay to make the paper whiter and smoother, or a coating called sizing that stops ink from spreading. Without sizing, writing in ink on paper would look like writing on tissue.

From China to the world

Chinese records tell a story from 105 CE. A court official named Cai Lun presented the emperor with paper made from tree bark, old rags, hemp and fishing nets. Archaeologists have found even older paper scraps in China, so he likely improved the method rather than inventing it.

For centuries, the art stayed mostly in East Asia. By the 700s, papermaking had spread to Samarkand in Central Asia, then to Baghdad, where paper mills helped scholars copy books. From the Islamic world it reached Spain and the rest of Europe. Later, the printing press made huge demands for cheap paper.

How did the printing press change the world?Before the printing press, a single book could take a scribe months to copy by hand. Within fifty years of Gutenberg, Europe had printed millions.

Paper in India

Before paper, people in India often wrote on palm leaves and birch bark. Paper became more common from around the 1200s onward, and many towns developed their own paper-making workshops.

At Sanganer, near Jaipur, families still make handmade paper using cotton rags, flowers and leaves, pressed and dried in the sun. The Kumarappa National Handmade Paper Institute in Jaipur teaches these skills. Handmade paper is often used for wedding cards, notebooks and art.

Bamboo is also used to make paper in India, because it grows very quickly and has long, strong fibres. Paper factories in places like Assam and Odisha have used it for decades.

Recycling paper

Paper is one of the easiest materials to recycle. Old paper is soaked in water, stirred into pulp, cleaned of ink and staples, then made into new sheets. Each time, though, the fibres get shorter and weaker. After about five to seven rounds, they are too short to bind and are often turned into egg cartons or newspapers.

Recycling paper saves trees, water and energy. A tonne of recycled paper can replace the wood from many trees, and the factories make less pollution. From a crumpled school worksheet to a cereal box, much of what you recycle can become paper again.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Papyrus was the first paper.

Truth: Papyrus was made by laying thin strips of the papyrus plant's stem in a criss-cross pattern and pressing them together. True paper is made by breaking plant material down into separate fibres and forming them into a new sheet, an invention from China.

Try this

Make your own paper. Tear old newspaper into small pieces and soak them in a bowl of water overnight. Squeeze and mash them with your hands into a soupy pulp. Dip an old net or a fine kitchen sieve into the pulp to collect a thin, even layer. Press it gently with a sponge to squeeze out water, then let it dry for a day or two and peel it off.
