How do people do sums in their heads?

In 1980, Shakuntala Devi multiplied two 13-digit numbers in her head in 28 seconds, in front of an audience in London.

The short answer

People do sums in their heads by turning hard problems into easier ones. Instead of lining numbers up in columns the way you might on paper, they round them, split them into pieces, or use number facts they already know by heart.

For example, 49 + 37 is awkward. But 50 + 37 is easy: 87. Then you take away the extra 1 to get 86. Your brain can only juggle a few things at once, so good mental maths keeps each step small. Shopkeepers, cricket scorers, cooks and carpenters do this kind of thing every day, often without thinking of it as maths at all.

Surprising but true

Some children trained on the abacus stop needing the real thing. They picture the beads in their minds and move them with tiny finger flicks in the air.

Rounding and fixing

Rounding is the most useful trick. To add ₹198 and ₹56, think ₹200 + ₹56 = ₹256, then take away 2 to get ₹254. To subtract 97 from 340, take away 100 to get 240, then add back 3 to get 243.

This works because round numbers, like 10, 50, 100 and 1,000, are easy to add and subtract. You change the problem into a friendly one, then fix the small difference. It is also a quick way to check whether an answer on a calculator or a bill looks about right, which is called estimation.

Splitting numbers apart

Another trick is to break a number into pieces. To work out 7 × 46, split 46 into 40 and 6. Then 7 × 40 is 280 and 7 × 6 is 42. Add them: 322.

Mental calculators often work from left to right, the opposite of school column methods. Starting with the biggest part gives you a good idea of the answer early on, and the small parts just tweak it. Knowing your times tables well makes every one of these steps faster.

Doubling and halving

If you double one number and halve the other, a multiplication answer stays the same. So 25 × 16 becomes 50 × 8, then 100 × 4, which is 400. To multiply by 5, multiply by 10 and halve. To find 15% of something, find 10%, then add half of that again.

These moves feel like magic at first. They work because of how multiplication is built, and they show why understanding numbers beats memorising steps.

How do we remember things?Every time you remember something, your brain rebuilds it from pieces, and sometimes it rebuilds it wrong.

The human computer

Shakuntala Devi, born in Bengaluru in 1929, learned card tricks with her father as a small child and soon showed an astonishing gift for numbers. She toured the world doing calculations on stage.

On 18 June 1980, at Imperial College London, she was given two 13-digit numbers chosen by a computer. She multiplied them in her head and gave the 26-digit answer in 28 seconds. The feat entered the Guinness Book of Records. She also wrote books to help children enjoy maths, and always said her methods could be learned.

The abacus in your head

In Japan, China, India and elsewhere, many children learn to calculate on an abacus, sliding beads on rods. After years of practice, some stop using the real one. They picture it in their minds and move the imaginary beads, often twitching their fingers in the air. In Japan, this is called anzan.

Expert users can add long lists of numbers flashed on a screen faster than most people can read them. Researchers think it works because the mind turns numbers into pictures, and pictures are easier to hold in memory than strings of digits.

Why bother, with calculators around?

Phones can do any sum in a flash, so why train your brain? One reason is speed in everyday life: working out a bill, the time left before a train, or runs needed per over in a cricket chase. Another is catching mistakes. If you know roughly what an answer should be, you'll notice when a machine or a person gets it wrong.

Mental maths also builds number sense, a feel for how numbers relate to each other. That feeling helps later with fractions, algebra and science, long after the sums themselves are forgotten.

Myth or fact?

Myth: People who are fast at mental maths have special brains and can't explain how they do it.

Truth: Most fast calculators use methods that can be learned, such as rounding, splitting numbers and remembering lots of number facts. Practice makes these steps quick, but they can describe them.

Try this

Play "shopkeeper" with a family member. Pick three items with prices, like ₹48, ₹27 and ₹19, and add them in your head using rounding (50 + 30 + 20, then take away the extra). Then work out the change from ₹100 by counting up. Swap roles and see who can explain their method best.
