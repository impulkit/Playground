What is Rani ki Vav?

For centuries, one of India's most beautiful buildings lay buried under river mud in Gujarat, with only its top showing.

The short answer

Rani ki Vav, or the Queen's Stepwell, is a giant well in Patan, Gujarat, with seven levels of stone steps leading down to water. It was built around 1063 CE. It is about 64 m long, longer than half a football field, and goes down about 27 m, as deep as a nine-storey building is tall.

Its walls and pillars are covered in more than 500 large carvings of gods, goddesses and dancers, plus over 1,000 smaller ones. It is often called the "queen of stepwells". In 2014 it became a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Surprising but true

Rani ki Vav appears on the back of India's lavender ₹100 note, issued from 2018.

Why build a palace for water?

In much of Gujarat and Rajasthan, almost all the year's rain falls in the monsoon. For the rest of the year, rivers dry up. But water still sits underground, soaking through sand and rock.

Stepwells let people reach that groundwater without ropes or buckets. You walked down the steps as far as the water level that season. When the water was high after the rains, the upper steps were enough. In the dry season, you went deeper. Stepwells stayed several degrees cooler than the hot streets above, so they were also places to rest and chat.

A queen's gift

Patan was the capital of the Chaulukya, or Solanki, kings. Historians believe Queen Udayamati built Rani ki Vav in memory of her husband, King Bhimadeva I.

It was designed like an upside-down temple. As you go down, you pass through pillared halls on each level. The walls get richer in carvings the deeper you go, as if you are walking towards something holy. Water itself was seen as sacred.

How did stepwells store water?Chand Baori in Rajasthan has about 3,500 narrow steps zigzagging down 13 levels, so that people could reach the water however low it fell.

A stone picture book

Many of the carvings show the ten avatars of Vishnu, including Rama, Krishna, Narasimha and Vamana. Vishnu also appears reclining on the serpent Shesha, floating on the cosmic ocean.

There are also apsaras, heavenly dancers, shown putting on make-up, fixing earrings or looking into mirrors. Some patterns on the walls look like the designs on Patola saris, the famous double-ikat silks still woven in Patan today.

Lost under the mud

Not long after it was built, the nearby Saraswati river flooded again and again. Mud and silt poured into the stepwell and slowly filled it up. For centuries, only the top of the well could be seen.

In the 1940s, digging in Baroda State began to show what was underneath. Excavation and restoration by the Archaeological Survey of India, mostly in the 1980s, cleared out the silt, level by level. Because the mud had covered the carvings for so long, many were protected from wind and rain. That is why so many look sharp after 900 years.

Secret tunnel stories

Local stories say a tunnel at the bottom of the well once led about 30 km to the town of Sidhpur, perhaps as an escape route for the royal family. The tunnel is said to be blocked with stones and mud. Nobody has proved it exists, so treat it as a legend.

A wonder in your wallet

In 2018, the Reserve Bank of India issued a new lavender ₹100 note with Rani ki Vav on the back. So many Indians carry a picture of it without knowing.

Rani ki Vav is among the finest of thousands of stepwells across western India. Several famous ones were paid for by queens and other women, as gifts to their towns. Some fell out of use when piped water and borewells arrived, and filled with rubbish. Others are now being cleaned and used again to store rainwater. The most dizzying of all may be Chand Baori in Rajasthan, where about 3,500 steps zigzag down into the earth.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Rani ki Vav was built by a king in honour of his queen.

Truth: It was the other way round. Historians believe Queen Udayamati built it in memory of her husband, King Bhimadeva I. That is why it is called the Queen's Stepwell.

Try this

Build a mini stepwell. In a sandpit or a big tub of damp sand, dig a deep hole and then cut steps down one side, pressing each one firm. Pour water in slowly from the top. Watch how the water level settles and which steps stay dry.
