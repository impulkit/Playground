Is Greenland really green?

About four-fifths of Greenland is buried under ice up to 3 km thick, yet a Viking explorer named it after the colour green.

The short answer

Mostly, no. About 80 percent of Greenland is covered by a vast ice sheet. In places it is more than 3 km thick, deep enough to bury ten Eiffel Towers stacked end to end. Only the rocky coasts are free of ice.

But along those coasts, especially in the south, summer brings green meadows, moss and wildflowers. The name probably comes from a Viking called Erik the Red, who settled there around AD 985. According to Icelandic stories, he called it Greenland because a nice name would attract settlers.

Surprising but true

If all of Greenland's ice melted, seas around the world would rise by about 7 m, enough to flood much of Mumbai, Kolkata and New York.

The biggest island on Earth

Greenland is the largest island in the world that isn't counted as a continent. It is about two-thirds the size of India. Yet only around 56,000 people lived there in 2025, fewer than fill a big cricket stadium. Most are Inuit, whose ancestors arrived from North America long before the Vikings.

Erik the Red's Norse settlers farmed in the south for about 400 years. They kept cows, sheep and goats, built stone churches and traded walrus ivory with Europe. Then, in the 1400s, the settlements fell silent. Nobody is completely sure why. Colder weather, poor harvests, falling ivory prices and the long sea journey to Norway probably all played a part. The Inuit, who hunted seals from kayaks and knew how to live on the ice, stayed.

Greenland is a self-governing part of the Kingdom of Denmark. Its capital, Nuuk, has colourful wooden houses scattered over bare rock. No roads link the towns, so people travel by boat, plane, helicopter, snowmobile and, in the north, dog sled.

A frozen history book

The ice sheet has built up over hundreds of thousands of years, one snowfall at a time. Each year's snow gets pressed into a thin layer of ice, with tiny air bubbles trapped inside. Scientists drill out long cylinders called ice cores and read them like a history book. The bubbles show what the air was like in the past.

And here is a surprise. In 2021, scientists studying old soil from under the ice found frozen plant bits, including twigs and moss. Tests published in 2023 showed those plants grew about 400,000 years ago, when parts of Greenland really were green.

Rivers of ice and giant bergs

The ice doesn't sit still. It flows slowly downhill towards the sea, like very thick honey, in rivers of ice called glaciers. When they reach the ocean, huge chunks crack off and float away as icebergs. The Ilulissat Icefjord glacier, on the west coast, is one of the fastest-moving in the world.

Many icebergs drift south into the North Atlantic. The berg that sank the Titanic in 1912 most likely came from a Greenland glacier.

A melting ice sheet

Greenland's ice is now melting faster than new snow can replace it. NASA satellites show it losing hundreds of billions of tonnes of ice each year in recent decades. If it all melted, which would take many centuries, the world's seas would rise by about 7 m.

That is why scientists in India and the USA watch Greenland closely. Its meltwater affects coastlines from Florida to Chennai, and it is a big part of sea-level rise.

Strange life in icy water

The waters around Greenland hide the Greenland shark, which swims very slowly in the cold, dark deep. It can reach about 5 m, longer than an autorickshaw and a car parked end to end, yet it adds only about 1 cm a year. Scientists who studied the eyes of these sharks estimated that one had lived for at least 272 years, possibly much longer. Some sharks swimming today may have been born before the USA existed. They are now among the strongest candidates in the list of the longest-living animals.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Greenland is always white and frozen, with nothing green at all.

Truth: The coasts, especially in the south, turn green in summer with grass, moss, wildflowers and small birch and willow shrubs. Farmers there keep sheep and grow potatoes.

Try this

Peel a mandarin orange and try to press the peel flat on a table. Notice how it tears and stretches, especially at the top and bottom. That is the problem mapmakers face when they flatten a round Earth, and why Greenland ends up looking too big.
