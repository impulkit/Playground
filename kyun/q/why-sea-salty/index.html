Why is the sea salty?

Rain, rocks and rivers have been slowly salting the sea for billions of years.

The short answer

The salt in the sea comes mostly from rocks on land. Rain is very slightly acidic, because it picks up a little carbon dioxide from the air. As it falls on rocks and soaks through soil, it slowly dissolves tiny amounts of minerals. Rivers carry these dissolved minerals down to the sea.

When seawater evaporates in the Sun, the water rises into the sky as vapour, but the salt stays behind. That water falls again as rain, and the cycle repeats, as you can read in the water cycle. Over hundreds of millions of years, the salt has built up. Today, every litre of seawater holds about 35 g of salt, roughly six teaspoons.

Surprising but true

If all the salt in the oceans were spread over the land, it would make a layer more than 160 m thick, about as tall as a 40-storey building.

Salt from under the sea

Rivers are not the only source. Along the ocean floor, there are cracks where seawater seeps deep into hot rock. There it picks up minerals and gushes back out of hot springs called hydrothermal vents. Undersea volcanoes add minerals too.

Most of the sea's salt is made of two parts: sodium and chloride. Joined together, they make sodium chloride, the same thing as the salt on your table. Seawater also holds magnesium, sulphate, calcium and potassium, plus tiny traces of almost everything, even gold. The gold is spread so thinly that nobody has found a way to collect it for a profit.

Why the sea doesn't keep getting saltier

If rivers keep adding salt, you might expect the sea to grow saltier every year. Scientists think its saltiness has stayed fairly steady for a very long time. That is because salt also leaves the sea. Some settles into mud on the sea floor and slowly turns into rock. Some is taken up by sea creatures that build shells. Over long stretches of time, what comes in and what goes out roughly balance.

Saltiness does change from place to place. The Red Sea is extra salty, because the hot Sun evaporates a lot of water and few rivers flow in. The Baltic Sea, fed by many rivers and melting snow, is much less salty. These differences matter. Cold, salty water is heavy and sinks, which helps drive slow currents through the deep sea, as you can read in ocean currents. From 2011 to 2015, a NASA satellite called Aquarius mapped the saltiness of the sea's surface from space, month by month.

Saltier than the sea

Some lakes are far saltier than any ocean. The Dead Sea, between Jordan and Israel, is almost ten times saltier than seawater. People float in it easily, like corks, because salty water is denser and pushes up harder. Utah's Great Salt Lake in the USA is also much saltier than the ocean. Water flows into these lakes but has no way out except by evaporating, so the salt keeps piling up.

What was the Salt March?In 1930, a 60-year-old man walked 385 km to the sea, bent down and picked up a lump of salty mud. It shook the British Empire.

Salt from the sea in India

India makes most of its salt by drying seawater or salty groundwater. In the Little Rann of Kutch, salt workers called Agariyas pump salty water into shallow pans and wait for the Sun to do its work. After weeks of raking, the pans turn white with crystals. It is hard work in fierce heat. Together, Gujarat's salt pans produce roughly three-quarters of all India's salt.

Salt has also shaped India's history. In 1930, Mahatma Gandhi walked about 390 km to the coastal village of Dandi to make salt from seawater, breaking a British law that taxed it. That protest, the Salt March, drew attention around the world to India's freedom struggle.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If you are stranded at sea, drinking seawater will keep you alive.

Truth: Seawater has more salt than your kidneys can handle. To get rid of it, your body has to use up even more water than you drank, so seawater makes thirst worse. Sailors and lifeboats carry fresh water for this reason.

Try this

Make sea salt crystals. Stir two big spoons of salt into half a cup of warm water until it dissolves. Pour a thin layer onto a dark plate and leave it on a sunny windowsill for a few days. As the water evaporates, salt crystals appear, just as they do in the salt pans of Gujarat.
