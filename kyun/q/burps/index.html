Why do we burp?

Astronauts in orbit can't burp properly. Without gravity to pull food down, a burp comes up mixed with whatever is in their stomach.

The short answer

A burp is gas escaping from your stomach up through your food pipe and out of your mouth. Most of that gas is air you swallowed without noticing.

Every time you eat, drink, chew gum or even talk, little gulps of air slip down with your food. Fizzy drinks add more gas, because they're full of dissolved carbon dioxide. That air and gas rise to the top of your stomach, like bubbles in a bottle.

At the top of the stomach is a ring of muscle that usually stays shut to keep food in. When enough gas builds up, it relaxes for a moment and lets the gas out. That rush of air vibrates the top of your throat, and you hear the "brrp".

Surprising but true

Almost all the methane a cow makes comes out of its mouth as burps, not out of its back end.

The gate at the top

Your food pipe, the oesophagus, joins your stomach at a ring of muscle called the lower oesophageal sphincter. Most of the time it stays tight, so stomach acid and food can't splash back up.

When the top of the stomach stretches with gas, nerves send a signal and the sphincter relaxes briefly. Gas, which is lighter than food, slips up first. Burping is your body's pressure-release valve.

Eating fast, gulping drinks, sucking through straws and chewing gum all mean more swallowed air, and more burps. Fizzy drinks are the biggest burp-makers of all.

Why babies need help

Babies swallow a lot of air while feeding, especially from a bottle. They can't always burp it out on their own, and trapped air can make them uncomfortable and fussy.

That's why parents hold a baby upright against their shoulder and gently pat or rub their back after a feed. Sitting up helps the air rise to the top of the stomach, where it can escape. Sometimes a little milk comes up with it, which is perfectly normal.

Burping in space

On Earth, gravity keeps food and drink at the bottom of your stomach and gas floats to the top, ready to leave. In orbit, there's no up or down. Food, liquid and gas float around together inside the stomach.

So when astronauts burp, the gas often brings food or liquid up too, a so-called wet burp. Some astronauts avoid fizzy drinks on the space station for exactly this reason. One trick they use is to push off a wall so their body moves forward, which nudges the gas to one end of the stomach.

What happens to the body in space?Astronauts come home a few centimetres taller, with weaker bones and puffy faces, and some have to relearn how to walk.

Cows and the climate

Cows, buffaloes, sheep and goats burp much more than people. Their first stomach chamber, the rumen, is like a big fermenting tank. Microbes there break down tough grass, and they release methane as they work.

The cow belches this methane out, many times an hour. Methane is a powerful greenhouse gas, and livestock are one of the largest human-related sources of it. That's why scientists are testing new feeds, including one made from a seaweed, that cut how much methane cows produce, as part of the fight against climate change.

When burps go wrong

Some people find they can't burp at all. They get gurgling noises in their chest and painful bloating instead. Doctors in the USA described this condition properly only in 2019, and it can often be treated.

Burping a lot, with pain, heartburn or trouble swallowing, is worth telling a doctor about. Most burps, though, are just air going back where it came from.

Rude or polite?

Whether burping at the table is rude depends on where you are and who you're with. In most Indian and American homes, people say "excuse me" after a loud burp. Babies get a free pass everywhere, and most grown-ups still cheer when a baby manages a big one.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Most of the methane from cows comes from their farts.

Truth: Most of it comes from burps. Microbes in a cow's first stomach chamber make methane while breaking down grass, and the cow belches it out through its mouth.

Try this

With an adult, pour a little fizzy water into a glass and stretch a balloon over the top of the bottle you poured from, then shake it gently. The balloon fills with carbon dioxide escaping from the drink. The same gas collects in your stomach after a fizzy drink and has to get out somehow.
