How does a baby grow before it is born?

You began as a single cell smaller than a full stop. Nine months later you were made of trillions of cells and could already recognise your mother's voice.

The short answer

Every baby starts as a single cell, made when an egg cell from the mother joins with a sperm cell from the father. That one cell holds a full set of genes, the instructions for building a whole person.

The cell divides into two, then four, then eight, and keeps going. In the first eight weeks, called the embryo stage, those cells organise themselves into a heart, brain, spine, arms and legs. After that the baby is called a fetus, and most of the remaining months are about growing bigger and getting each organ ready for life outside. A typical pregnancy lasts about 40 weeks, counted by doctors from a couple of weeks before the baby actually begins.

Surprising but true

Babies can hear sounds from outside the womb from around the middle of pregnancy. Newborns prefer their own mother's voice to a stranger's, which suggests they learned it before birth.

The first weeks

In the first days, the ball of cells travels into the womb, or uterus, and settles into its soft lining. Some of the cells start to build the placenta, a pad of tissue that connects the baby to the mother's blood supply.

By around the sixth week of pregnancy, a tiny heart is already beating, though the embryo is only a few millimetres long. Buds that will become arms and legs appear. By about 10 weeks of pregnancy, the embryo has fingers and toes, and from then on it's called a fetus.

Building and practising

At 12 weeks, the fetus is about as long as a lime, and all the major organs have formed. Over the next months it grows quickly and starts practising skills it will need later.

It moves, kicks and stretches. It swallows the fluid it floats in and even gets hiccups. It makes breathing movements, though its lungs are full of fluid, not air. Tiny ridges form on its fingertips, creating fingerprints that will stay the same for life. Even twins who share all their genes have different fingerprints.

Hearing from inside

From around the middle of pregnancy, the ears begin working. By the last few months, babies can hear their mother's heartbeat and voice, along with muffled sounds from outside, like music and other people talking.

In 1980, the American psychologists Anthony DeCasper and William Fifer tested newborns only a few days old. The babies sucked on a special dummy, and by sucking in a certain rhythm they could choose which recording to hear. They worked to hear their own mother's voice rather than a stranger's. They had learned it before they were born.

Seeing with sound

Doctors can check on a growing baby using ultrasound. A probe sends very high-pitched sound, far too high for humans to hear, into the body. The sound bounces back from the baby, a bit like an echo, and a computer turns the echoes into a picture.

The Scottish doctor Ian Donald pioneered using ultrasound in pregnancy in Glasgow in the 1950s. Today, ultrasound scans are a routine part of pregnancy care in India, the USA and around the world. Bats and dolphins use the same basic trick, sending out sounds and listening for the echoes to find their way.

Ready to be born

In the last weeks, the baby puts on fat, which keeps it warm after birth, and its lungs finish getting ready. Most babies turn head-down. When they are born, many are around 50 cm long and weigh roughly 3 kg, though healthy babies vary a lot.

With the first cry, the lungs fill with air for the first time. The heart and blood vessels quickly change their plumbing to send blood through the lungs instead of the cord. From that moment, the baby is breathing on its own, and its brain begins its fastest years of growth.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Babies in the womb live in silence and darkness and can't sense anything.

Truth: By the later months, babies hear voices, music and their mother's heartbeat, and they respond to light shone on the belly. They also taste the fluid around them, which changes with what their mother eats.

Try this

Ask a family member if they still have your baby records or photos, or a younger sibling's. Find out how long and heavy you were at birth. Then measure out that length with a tape and find something at home of similar weight, like a bag of rice or flour, to feel how small you were.
