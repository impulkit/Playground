How did TV come to India?

When TV started in India in 1959, there was one channel, a few hours a week, in black and white.

The short answer

TV pictures are made of tiny dots of red, green and blue light, changing many times a second.

For years, Doordarshan was the only channel. Families and neighbours often watched together. In many streets, the one house with a TV set filled up on Sunday mornings, with children sitting on the floor and adults standing at the door.

ISRO's 1975 SITE experiment used a satellite to beam educational TV to 2,400 villages.

Surprising but true

In 1975, India used a satellite to bring TV lessons to thousands of villages, one of the biggest education experiments of its time.

A small start in Delhi

India's TV began on 15 September 1959, as an experiment by All India Radio in Delhi. It broadcast for about an hour, a couple of days a week, mostly educational programmes for schools and community groups. Very few people owned a set, so many sets were placed in schools and community halls.

Daily broadcasts began in 1965. In 1967, Krishi Darshan started teaching farmers near Delhi about seeds, fertilisers and new methods, during the years of the Green Revolution. In 1976, TV was separated from radio and became Doordarshan, which means "seeing far" in Hindi.

TV from space

The scientist Vikram Sarabhai, founder of India's space programme, believed satellites could bring education to remote villages faster than building cables and towers. He died in 1971, but his idea went ahead.

In 1975 and 1976, ISRO borrowed a NASA satellite, ATS-6, for a year. Programmes were beamed to about 2,400 villages in six states, in several languages. Villagers gathered around community TV sets to watch lessons on farming, health, family planning and science. It showed the world that a developing country could use space for daily life. Later, India launched its own INSAT satellites, which carried TV to the whole country.

Colour and crowds

Colour TV arrived in 1982, just before the Asian Games in Delhi. A colour picture mixes red, green and blue light in different amounts, as explained in colour mixing. Sales of TV sets shot up.

The 1980s brought shows that the whole country watched together. Hum Log, India's first soap opera, began in 1984. When Ramayan was broadcast on Sunday mornings in 1987 and 1988, many streets emptied as families gathered around TVs. Some people garlanded their TV sets. The Ramayana had been told for thousands of years, and now crores watched it at the same moment.

Cartoons and channels

In the early 1990s, private satellite channels arrived, beamed from abroad. People put dish antennas on rooftops, and cable operators ran wires from building to building. Within a few years, viewers had dozens of channels, including news, music and cartoons all day.

Indian children's shows grew too. Malgudi Days, based on R.K. Narayan's stories, began in 1986, and the superhero show Shaktimaan started in 1997.

How the picture travels

A TV camera turns a scene into an electrical signal, line by line. A transmitter on a tall tower sends that signal out as radio waves, and an antenna on a roof catches it. A satellite works like a very tall tower, about 36,000 km up, so one satellite can reach a whole country. Old TV sets then fired beams at the screen to light up coloured dots, row after row, many times a second.

TV in America

In the USA, regular TV broadcasts began around 1939, two decades before India. Colour TV started there in the 1950s. In 1969, hundreds of millions of people around the world watched live pictures of astronauts walking on the Moon.

TV today

Today India has hundreds of TV channels, in dozens of languages. Many people now watch shows on phones, through the internet, whenever they like. But big cricket matches still bring families together around one screen, just as the first Doordarshan broadcasts did.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Sitting close to the TV will permanently damage your eyes.

Truth: Eye doctors say there is no good evidence that sitting close harms your eyes for good. Long hours of screen time can make eyes tired and dry, so it helps to take breaks and look into the distance. If your eyes hurt or you struggle to see, tell a parent or see an eye doctor.

Try this

Look very closely at a TV or computer screen with a magnifying glass, with an adult's permission. You may see tiny dots or bars of red, green and blue. Look at a white area, then a yellow one, and see which colours light up to make each.
