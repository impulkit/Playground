Why can airplanes fly?

A jet can weigh 400 tonnes. How does that stay up?

The short answer

A wing is shaped and tilted so that the air flowing past it gets pushed downward.

Push air down, and the air pushes the wing up. This upward push is called lift. The faster a plane goes, the more air its wings can turn, and the more lift they make. A big passenger jet needs to reach about 250 to 300 km/h on the runway before its wings can carry its weight.

More speed or more tilt makes more lift, but tilt the wing too much and the air stops flowing smoothly over it. The lift suddenly drops. Pilots call this a stall.

Surprising but true

The Wright brothers studied how birds twist their wings when they built the first airplane.

Turning the air

A wing in flight works a bit like a flat hand held up in a strong wind. Tilt your fingers up slightly and the rushing air lifts your hand. The air is being bent downwards, and by Newton's third law, the wing is pushed the other way.

At the same time, the air pressure above the curved wing drops, and the pressure below rises. That difference in pressure is another way of describing the same lift. Both explanations are true, and they fit together. Behind a big jet, you can sometimes see the downwash in clouds or mist, scooped down by the passing wings.

A myth in old books

Many books used to say that air over a wing goes faster because it has to "meet up" with the air underneath at the back edge. That's not true. Wind tunnel experiments show air over the top arrives well before the air below. NASA lists the idea as an incorrect theory. The air does speed up over the top, but because of how the wing bends the flow, not because of any meeting.

Controlling the plane

Moving flaps on the wings, a rudder on the tail and elevators let pilots climb, turn and slow down. At take-off and landing, extra flaps slide out of the wings to make them bigger and more curved. That gives more lift at low speed, so planes don't need impossibly long runways. On long flights an autopilot does much of the steady flying, while the pilots keep watch and handle take-off, landing and anything unexpected.

Jets cruise about 11 km up, higher than Mount Everest, because the air there is thin. Thin air means less drag, so the plane uses less fuel. Their jet engines still find enough air to burn.

First flights

In 1903, at Kitty Hawk in North Carolina, the Wright brothers made the first powered, controlled aeroplane flights. Orville was at the controls while Wilbur ran alongside, steadying the wing. The very first flight lasted 12 seconds and covered about 37 m, less than the wingspan of a modern jumbo jet. They had watched birds closely, especially how they twist their wings to turn, and copied the idea by twisting their own wings with wires.

India takes off

On 15 October 1932, J.R.D. Tata flew a small single-engine plane from Karachi to Bombay, now Mumbai, via Ahmedabad, carrying mail. It was the first flight of Tata's airmail service. His service grew into Tata Airlines, which later became Air India. Today India is one of the world's busiest flying nations, and every one of those flights stays up for the same reason as the Wrights' wooden flyer.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Air over the top of a wing goes faster because it has to "catch up" with the air underneath at the back.

Truth: Nothing makes the air meet up again. Air over the top actually arrives at the back well before the air below. Wings work by turning air downwards, which creates lower pressure above the wing and higher pressure below.

Try this

Stand about a metre in front of a pedestal fan and hold a stiff piece of card flat in the breeze, holding it loosely at one edge. Tilt the front edge up a little and feel the card try to rise. Tilt it more and more. At some point the lift fades and the card just gets shoved backwards. That is a stall.
