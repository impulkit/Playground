How can one banyan tree become a whole forest?

Some banyans look like a forest. It's actually one tree.

The short answer

The banyan is India's national tree.

Roots grow down from its branches. When they reach the ground, they become new trunks holding up the spreading tree. Year after year, the branches stretch outward, drop more roots, and the tree creeps across the ground like a slow green army.

The Great Banyan in Kolkata lost its main trunk in 1925, and it's still alive, held up by thousands of other roots. From a distance it looks like a small wood. Walk inside and you're standing under a single tree.

Surprising but true

The Great Banyan in Kolkata has no main trunk anymore, yet it's still one living tree.

A strangler start

Many banyans begin life high on another tree, where a bird left a seed. Their roots wrap around the host and reach the ground. Sometimes the host tree eventually dies inside.

The seedling has a clever head start. Up in the branches it gets plenty of sunlight, which a baby tree on the dark forest floor would struggle to find. Its roots slowly thicken and squeeze the host, while its leaves shade it from above. That's why banyans and their fig cousins are sometimes called strangler figs. In cities, banyan seeds often sprout in cracks in old walls, temples and bridges, and their roots can split stone apart.

The giant of the botanic garden

The Great Banyan grows in the Acharya Jagadish Chandra Bose Indian Botanic Garden in Howrah, across the river from Kolkata. It is thought to be at least 250 years old. Two great cyclones in the 1860s tore off some of its big branches. Later, fungus rotted the main trunk, and in 1925 gardeners removed it.

The tree hardly noticed. Over 3,000 of its hanging roots had already reached the soil and turned into trunks. Today its crown covers about 1.9 hectares, about two and a half football fields. A road runs around it, and gardeners guide new roots down into the ground with bamboo poles.

The biggest canopy of all

In Andhra Pradesh grows Thimmamma Marrimanu, a banyan whose branches cover about 4.7 acres. In 1989 Guinness World Records listed it as the tree with the largest canopy on Earth. Hundreds of people can shelter in its shade at once. A small shrine to Thimmamma, a local woman remembered in legend, stands beneath it.

That's a different record from the tallest tree, a redwood in California. Banyans spread sideways instead of up.

Shade and stories

Banyans have long been village meeting places. Elders hold meetings beneath them, travellers rest in their shade, and markets set up under their spreading arms. In Hindu tradition the banyan, or vat, stands for long life. On Vat Purnima, many married women in Maharashtra, Gujarat and other states tie threads around a banyan's trunk and pray for their husbands' long lives.

The word "banyan" itself comes from the banias, Indian traders. Early European travellers saw merchants gathering under these trees to do business and named the tree after them.

A tree that feeds many

Banyans are figs. Their small red figs feed bats, monkeys, squirrels and many birds, such as hornbills, barbets and mynas. Each fig needs tiny wasps to make seeds. The Buddha is said to have reached enlightenment under a peepal, another Indian fig tree, so the family is special in more than one way. Like all green plants, banyans build their wood from sunlight and air through photosynthesis.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A banyan with many trunks is really a group of trees growing close together.

Truth: All those trunks grew from roots that dropped down from the same tree's branches. They stay joined above ground, so even the biggest banyan "forest" is usually one living plant.

Try this

Look for a banyan or peepal near your home or school. Count how many hanging roots you can see, and how many have reached the ground. Feel the difference between a thin new root and an old one that has become a trunk. If you're in the USA, look for a banyan in a park in Florida or Hawaii, or in a botanical garden greenhouse.
