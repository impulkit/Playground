Why is the platypus so strange?

When the first platypus skin reached Britain in 1799, scientists checked it for stitches because they thought someone had sewn a duck's bill onto a beaver.

The short answer

The platypus looks like it was put together from spare parts. It has a rubbery bill like a duck, a flat tail like a beaver, webbed feet like an otter and thick fur. It lays eggs like a bird. It is also one of very few venomous mammals, and its family line is far older than the rise of most mammals after the dinosaurs.

None of this is a mix-up. The platypus belongs to the monotremes, the oldest surviving branch of mammals. Only the platypus and four kinds of echidna are left. Their ancestors split from other mammals well over 160 million years ago, while dinosaurs still ruled. They kept some ancient features, such as eggs, and evolved others, such as an electric bill.

Surprising but true

Under ultraviolet light, platypus fur glows a soft blue-green, something scientists only noticed in 2020.

The animal nobody believed

In 1799, a dried platypus skin arrived in London from Australia. The naturalist George Shaw described it, but he admitted he could hardly believe his eyes. Some people suspected a prank, since sailors sometimes sewed animals together to sell as curiosities. Shaw reportedly looked for stitches around the bill.

That very skin is still kept at the Natural History Museum in London. It took scientists until 1884 to confirm that platypuses really lay eggs.

A bill that feels electricity

A platypus dives for about 30 to 140 seconds at a time, hunting worms, shrimp and insect larvae on riverbeds. Underwater it shuts its eyes, ears and nostrils with folds of skin. It is basically diving blind. It also can't hold much food in its mouth, so it stuffs prey into cheek pouches and eats at the surface, floating on its belly like a fluffy cushion. A platypus can eat around a fifth of its own body weight in a single night.

Instead, it sweeps its bill from side to side. About 40,000 sensors in the soft skin pick up the weak electricity made by a moving animal's muscles. Tens of thousands more sense touch and ripples. An electric eel makes electricity to hunt. The platypus reads it.

Milk without nipples

A mother platypus lays one to three eggs, each about the size of a grape, in a burrow in the riverbank. She curls around them for about 10 days. When the babies hatch, she has no nipples. Instead, milk oozes from patches of skin on her belly, and the babies lap it up from her fur.

Babies are tiny, about the size of a jelly bean, and stay hidden in the burrow for three to four months before they first swim out into the river.

Adults have no teeth either. Young platypuses have a few, but they fall out. Adults grind food between hard pads in their mouths, often scooping up gravel to help. And a platypus has no stomach. Its food goes straight from the gullet into the gut.

A poisonous kick

Male platypuses have a sharp spur on each back ankle, connected to a venom gland. The venom is strongest in the breeding season, so scientists think males use it against each other. It can kill a dog. In people it causes pain so severe that ordinary painkillers barely help, sometimes for weeks.

That makes the platypus one of very few mammals with venom, alongside some shrews and the solenodon.

The glowing surprise

In 2020, scientists shone ultraviolet light on old museum specimens in the US and saw the brown fur glow blue-green. Nobody knows why. Platypuses are most active at dusk and night, so it might help them stay hidden, or it might mean nothing at all.

Platypuses are listed as near threatened, as droughts, dams and fires shrink their rivers. They live only in eastern Australia, and their ten sex chromosomes, compared with our two, still puzzle geneticists. Some animals make their own light. Others, like the platypus, simply glow when light hits them, and the line between the two is stranger than it sounds.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The platypus is a mix of a duck, a beaver and an otter, or a step between birds and mammals.

Truth: The platypus is a true mammal. It has fur and feeds its young on milk. It belongs to a very old branch of mammals that split off from the others more than 160 million years ago, long before ducks or beavers existed.
