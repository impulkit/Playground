What if Jupiter came closer to Earth?

If Jupiter sat where our Moon is, it would fill the sky about 40 times wider than the full Moon, and its pull could squeeze Earth enough to wake volcanoes.

The short answer

Jupiter is the biggest planet in our solar system, about 318 times heavier than Earth. It is normally more than 590 million km away, so it looks like a bright star. Bring it much closer and its gravity would start to take charge.

At the Moon's distance, Jupiter would look about 40 times wider than the full Moon. Its pull would raise ocean tides thousands of times stronger than today's. It would also stretch the solid ground itself, heating Earth's insides, setting off earthquakes and possibly waking volcanoes. Earth's orbit around the Sun would change too. In the end, Earth might become a moon of Jupiter.

Surprising but true

Jupiter's moon Io is about as far from Jupiter as our Moon is from us, and Jupiter's tides squeeze it so hard that it has more than 400 active volcanoes.

A giant in the sky

Jupiter is about 1,40,000 km wide, 11 times wider than Earth. You could line up 11 Earths across its face. Its famous Great Red Spot is a storm wider than our whole planet.

If it hung where our Moon is, it would stretch about a ninth of the way from one horizon to the other. Its colourful stripes of cloud and swirling storms would be clear to the naked eye. On "Jupiter-lit" nights, its reflected sunlight would be bright enough to read a book by.

Tides of terror

Our Moon pulls on Earth's oceans and raises the tides. The pull is stronger on the side facing the Moon and weaker on the far side, so the ocean bulges on both sides.

Jupiter is about 26,000 times heavier than the Moon. At the same distance, its tidal pull would be about 26,000 times stronger. Rather than gentle tides of a metre or two, the sea would surge kilometres inland, then pull far back, twice a day.

Could life exist on Earth without the Moon?About 400 million years ago, a year on Earth had around 400 days. Corals recorded it in their skeletons, and the Moon is the reason it changed.

Squeezing the planet

Tides don't only move water. They also stretch rock. Earth's solid ground already rises and falls by about 30 cm each day because of the Moon and Sun, though nobody notices.

With Jupiter nearby, the rock would flex much more. That bending produces heat, like a paperclip getting warm when you bend it back and forth. Over time, the extra heat and strain could trigger earthquakes and volcanic eruptions.

We know this happens, because it is happening right now to Io. Io orbits Jupiter at about the same distance as our Moon orbits us. Jupiter's tides, helped by tugs from its other moons, keep Io's insides molten. It has more than 400 active volcanoes, some shooting plumes hundreds of kilometres high.

Too close for comfort

There is a limit to how close a moon or planet can get to a giant before being pulled apart. Scientists call it the Roche limit. For Earth near Jupiter, it would be roughly 1,00,000 km from Jupiter's centre, a little over a quarter of the Moon's distance from us.

Inside that line, Jupiter's tides would pull harder on Earth's near side than gravity holds Earth together. Our planet would be stretched and broken into a ring of rubble, like a far bigger version of Saturn's rings.

Radiation and auroras

Jupiter is wrapped in a huge magnetic field that traps deadly radiation. At the Moon's distance, Earth would be inside it. Our own magnetic field would help, but astronauts and satellites would be in trouble. Earth's sky would also glow with powerful auroras, the northern and southern lights.

Jupiter, friend or bully?

In real life, Jupiter stays far away. Some scientists think its gravity helps Earth by catching or flinging away comets that might otherwise hit us. In 1994, the world watched comet Shoemaker-Levy 9 smash into Jupiter in more than 20 pieces.

Other scientists point out that Jupiter can also nudge asteroids towards the inner planets. Its real role is still debated. Our own little Moon matters too, steadying Earth's tilt and seasons, and life would look very different without the Moon.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Jupiter could come crashing towards Earth one day.

Truth: Planets' paths around the Sun are steady over billions of years. Computer models show Jupiter will stay roughly where it is for as long as the Sun shines normally. This "what if" is for exploring physics, not a real danger.

Try this

Feel how distance changes a pull. Hold a strong fridge magnet near a paperclip and slowly bring it closer, noting the distance where the clip jumps. Gravity works in a similar way: halve the distance and the pull gets four times stronger.
