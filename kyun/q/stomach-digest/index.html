What happens to food after you swallow it?

Your breakfast travels through a tube about 9 metres long, gets bathed in acid strong enough to kill most germs, and takes a day or more to finish the trip.

The short answer

Digestion starts before you swallow. Your teeth cut and grind the food, and saliva begins to break down starch. Then a muscular tube called the oesophagus squeezes each mouthful down to your stomach.

The stomach churns food with acid and enzymes into a thick soup. Bit by bit, it squirts this into the small intestine, where most of the useful parts are pulled out and carried away by the blood. What's left moves into the large intestine, which soaks up water. The rest leaves your body as poo, usually one to three days after you ate.

Surprising but true

Swallowing doesn't depend on gravity. Muscles squeeze food along, so astronauts can eat in space and you could swallow while standing on your head.

Squeezed all the way

Food doesn't fall down your gut. Rings of muscle along the tube tighten one after another, pushing food forward the way you push toothpaste up a tube. This wave is called peristalsis. It's why astronauts can swallow while floating, and why your gut works when you lie down.

At the top of your windpipe there is a small flap that closes when you swallow, so food goes down the right pipe. When it doesn't close in time, you cough hard to clear it.

A bag of acid

An adult stomach can hold about a litre of food comfortably and stretch to hold more. Its walls make hydrochloric acid, strong enough to kill many germs and to start breaking down proteins. So why doesn't the stomach digest itself? It coats its lining in thick mucus, and it replaces the cells of its inner surface every few days.

Much of what we know about the stomach comes from one strange accident. In 1822, a young fur trader in Michigan, Alexis St. Martin, was shot by accident. He survived, but the wound left a hole into his stomach. An army doctor, William Beaumont, spent years dropping food on strings through the hole and pulling it out to see how it had changed.

He also drew out some of the stomach's juice and kept it in a warm jar with pieces of meat. The meat still broke down, slowly, with no stomach around it. That helped show that digestion is mostly chemistry, with squashing and grinding as helpers. Beaumont published his results in 1833, and doctors still mention his notes today.

The long middle section

The small intestine is coiled up in your belly, but stretched out it would be about 6 metres long, longer than a car. Your liver sends bile into it to break fat into tiny droplets, and the pancreas adds more enzymes. The inside wall is covered in millions of tiny bumps called villi, like the threads on a towel. They give it a huge surface for soaking up sugars, fats, vitamins and building blocks of protein.

The last stop

The large intestine is wider but shorter, about 1.5 metres. Here the body takes back most of the water, so you don't dry out. This part is home to trillions of friendly microbes. They eat the fibre that you can't digest, such as the skins of fruit and the husks of grains, and make some useful vitamins in return.

Fibre also keeps everything moving. A meal of dal, rice and vegetables gives your gut microbes plenty to work on. When food passes through too quickly, the body can't take back enough water, and that is diarrhoea. When it moves too slowly, too much water is removed, and that is constipation.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Swallowed chewing gum stays in your stomach for seven years.

Truth: Your body can't digest gum, but it doesn't get stuck. It moves through your gut and comes out within a few days, like other things you can't digest. Swallowing a lot of gum at once is still not a good idea.

Try this

Put a piece of bread in a zip-lock bag with a few spoons of water and squeeze it with your hands for two minutes. That is roughly what your stomach muscles do. Now push the mush down through the leg of an old pair of tights, squeezing behind it, to see how the gut moves food along.
