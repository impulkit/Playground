How do you make the best paper plane?

In December 2025 a team of Chinese teenagers threw a folded sheet of A4 paper 98.43 m, almost the length of a football field.

The short answer

The best paper plane depends on what you want. For distance, fold a slim, pointy dart with small, stiff wings and throw it hard and slightly upwards. For time in the air, fold a glider with wide wings and launch it gently. In both cases, three things matter most: the weight should sit towards the nose, the two sides must match exactly, and every fold should be sharp.

Small tweaks make a big difference. Bending the back edges of the wings by a millimetre or two can turn a plane that dives into one that glides across the room.

Surprising but true

The best paper plane for flying far looks very different from the best paper plane for staying up long. One is a slim dart, the other a wide glider.

Four forces on a sheet of paper

A paper plane obeys the same rules as a jumbo jet. Your arm gives it thrust. Gravity pulls it down. Air rushing over the wings gives lift, and air pushing against it causes drag, which slows it down.

A real plane keeps its engines running. A paper plane gets only one push, so from the moment it leaves your hand it is gliding, trading height and speed for distance. The longer it can keep that trade going, the better it flies. That is also how engineless gliders soar.

Balance is everything

The most important secret is where the weight sits. The plane's balance point, called the centre of gravity, should be slightly in front of the point where the wings push up. Then the nose tends to tip down a little, keeping the plane in a steady, gentle dive.

If the balance point is too far back, the plane points up, slows and stalls. A folded-over nose, an extra layer of paper or a paper clip at the front fixes this. Most distance designs fold several layers of paper into the nose for exactly this reason.

The little flaps that steer

The back edges of the wings act like the moving flaps on a real plane. Bend both up slightly and the tail is pushed down, so the nose rises. Bend both down and the nose dips. Bend one up and one down and the plane rolls and turns.

Symmetry matters too. If one wing is even slightly bigger or more bent, the plane will curve to one side. Champion folders check their planes from the front, by eye, before every throw, and fold on a hard table with a thumbnail pressed along each crease.

Records in the sky

In December 2025, a six-person team of Chinese students, average age about 17, threw an A4 paper plane 98.43 m in a big exhibition hall in Shanghai. Guinness World Records confirmed it, beating a record of about 88 m set in 2022. Records like this are flown indoors, where no wind can help. How Guinness checks such claims is explained in world records.

The record for time in the air was long held by Takuo Toda of Japan, whose glider stayed up 29.2 seconds in 2010. In February 2026, the same Chinese team beat it with a flight of 31.2 seconds, which Guinness World Records later confirmed. For time records, the plane is thrown almost straight up, as high as possible, then left to drift down in slow circles.

Distance versus time

Distance planes are narrow and heavy for their size. They cut through the air with little drag and keep their speed. Throwers launch them hard, upwards at a steep angle, like a cricketer hurling the ball in from the boundary.

Time planes have big, broad wings and are light for their size, so they float like a falling leaf. Paper type matters too: thin, stiff paper folds crisply and holds its shape. And the science doesn't stop at the classroom window. The same balance and flap tricks keep real aeroplanes steady in the sky.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The harder you throw any paper plane, the further it flies.

Truth: Only planes built for speed, like slim darts, like a hard throw. A wide glider thrown hard usually loops up, stalls and crashes. Gliders fly best with a gentle, level push.

Try this

Fold two planes from the same kind of paper: a slim dart and a wide-winged glider. Throw each five times from the same spot, measuring the distance in footsteps and timing each flight. Then add a paper clip to the nose of each and test again.
