Who built the first car?

The first long drive in a petrol car was made in secret, in 1888, by a woman and her two teenage sons. They refuelled at a chemist's shop.

The short answer

The German engineer Karl Benz is usually credited with building the first true car. In 1886 he received a patent for his Motorwagen, a three-wheeled carriage with a small petrol engine behind the seat. It had less power than a single horse and a top speed of around 16 km/h, about as fast as a child on a bicycle.

But it depends on what you count. In 1769, a French army engineer, Nicolas-Joseph Cugnot, built a heavy steam-powered tractor to haul cannons. And in the same year as Benz, Gottlieb Daimler and Wilhelm Maybach, also in Germany, fitted a petrol engine to a four-wheeled carriage. Many people were racing toward the same idea.

Surprising but true

Around 1900, about a third of the cars on American roads were electric. Petrol cars took over later, partly because petrol became cheap and roads grew longer.

The Motorwagen

Benz's machine looked like a large tricycle with spoked wheels. Its single-cylinder engine burned fuel inside a cylinder to push a piston, the basic idea still used in petrol engines today. A belt and chains carried the power to the back wheels. There was no roof, no windscreen and only a simple lever to steer.

Few people were interested at first. Horses were familiar, and the noisy machine scared some onlookers. Benz needed someone to prove it could be useful.

Bertha's road trip

That person was his wife, Bertha Benz. She had helped pay for his work with money from her family. In August 1888, without telling Karl, she set off with their two sons, aged 15 and 13, to visit her mother in Pforzheim, about 106 km away.

On the way, she bought fuel from a chemist's shop, cleared a blocked fuel line with a hatpin and used a garter to insulate a wire, according to the family's story. When the wooden brakes wore down, she asked a shoemaker to fit leather pads. She arrived that evening and sent Karl a telegram. The trip showed that cars could travel real distances, and it gave Benz ideas for improvements, such as an extra low gear for hills.

Electric cars came early

Around 1900, petrol cars were not winning yet. In the United States, steam, electric and petrol cars all competed. Electric cars were quiet and clean, and they didn't need to be cranked by hand to start. The US Department of Energy says around a third of cars on American roads at that time were electric.

But batteries were heavy and needed long charging. Big oil finds in Texas made petrol cheap. And from 1912, petrol cars got electric starters, so nobody had to crank the engine. Electric cars faded, until they began to return in the 2000s.

Ford's assembly line

In 1908, Henry Ford launched the Model T. In 1913 his factory in Michigan began using a moving assembly line. Instead of workers walking around a car, the car moved past them on a belt, and each worker did one small job. Building a car went from about 12 hours to about an hour and a half. Prices fell, and about 15 million Model Ts were sold.

Cars in India

Cars reached Indian cities in the early 1900s, owned mostly by the very rich, including maharajas who collected them. After independence, the Hindustan Ambassador, made near Kolkata from 1958 to 2014, became the car of politicians and taxi drivers. In 1983 the small, affordable Maruti 800 arrived, and for millions of families it was their first car.

Today engineers are working on cars that drive themselves, a story told in self-driving cars.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Henry Ford invented the car.

Truth: Cars existed for more than 20 years before Ford's Model T. What Ford did was make cars cheaply, using a moving assembly line, so that ordinary families in the United States could afford one.
