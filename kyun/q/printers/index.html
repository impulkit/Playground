How does a printer work?

An inkjet printer boils tiny drops of ink thousands of times a second, firing each one onto the paper through a nozzle thinner than a hair.

The short answer

A printer turns a picture on a computer into marks on paper. Most home and office printers are one of two kinds.

An inkjet printer squirts tiny drops of liquid ink onto the paper through rows of very fine nozzles. A print head slides back and forth across the page as the paper moves forward, building the picture line by line.

A laser printer works more like a photocopier. A laser draws the page onto a drum using static electricity. A dry powder called toner sticks to the charged parts, rolls onto the paper and is melted in place by heat.

Both build each page out of tiny dots. Look closely at any printout and you can see them.

Surprising but true

The laser printer was invented by an engineer at Xerox, Gary Starkweather, who changed a photocopier so that a laser beam, instead of a photo of a page, drew the image.

Boiling ink

Many inkjet printers use heat. Behind each nozzle is a tiny heater. When an electric pulse heats it for a few millionths of a second, a little of the ink boils into a bubble. The bubble pushes a drop out of the nozzle and onto the paper. Then the bubble collapses, and fresh ink flows in.

This happens thousands of times a second in each of hundreds of nozzles. Each drop is tiny, a few trillionths of a litre, far smaller than a speck of dust you could see. Canon called its version Bubble Jet. HP's ThinkJet went on sale in 1984.

Other inkjet printers use a crystal that bends when electricity passes through it. The crystal squeezes the ink like your fingers squeezing a toothpaste tube.

Drawing with a laser

In 1969 an engineer at Xerox named Gary Starkweather had an idea. A photocopier already used light to make a pattern of static charge on a drum. What if a laser drew the pattern instead, straight from a computer?

He built one, and by 1971 it was working at Xerox's research lab in California. Laser printers became common in offices in the 1980s.

Inside, the laser beam bounces off a spinning mirror and sweeps across a charged drum, line by line. Where the light hits, the charge changes. Toner, which is fine plastic powder mixed with colour, sticks only to the right places. A hot roller then melts it into the paper. That is why pages come out of a laser printer warm.

How did the printing press change the world?Before the printing press, a single book could take a scribe months to copy by hand. Within fifty years of Gutenberg, Europe had printed millions.

Four colours make thousands

Colour printers usually have just four inks: cyan (a bright blue), magenta (a pinkish purple), yellow and black. Printers call this CMYK, with K standing for black.

By laying tiny dots of these colours side by side or on top of each other, the printer can fool your eye into seeing almost any colour. Cyan and yellow make green. Magenta and yellow make red. All three together make a muddy dark brown, which is why printers add real black for crisp text.

This is different from a screen, which mixes red, green and blue light. That is why a photo can look a little different on paper than it did on your phone.

Beyond paper

The idea of building a picture from dots, one line at a time, is used far beyond offices. Printers make T-shirt designs, labels, wallpaper and big posters for buses. Special printers press dots into paper so blind readers can feel Braille.

3D printers take the idea a step further. Instead of one layer of ink, they lay down layer after layer of melted plastic, building up a solid object from the bottom.

Some scientists have even used inkjet-style printers to place living cells in neat patterns. The printer on a home desk, built to print homework, shares its idea with some of the most advanced machines in medicine.

Myth or fact?

Myth: When a printer says its ink is empty, the cartridge has run completely dry.

Truth: Many printers warn early to protect the print head, so some ink is often left in the cartridge. Some keep printing for a while after the warning.

Try this

Look at a printed photo in a newspaper or magazine through a magnifying glass. You'll see that the picture is made of tiny dots of a few colours. Try making your own dot picture with felt-tip pens using only blue, pink and yellow dots, and step back to see what colours appear.
