How do street vendors run their business?

The pani puri seller on your corner runs a business with no office, no ads and no boss, and by 2026 more than 75 lakh Indian vendors had taken small government loans.

The short answer

A street vendor runs a tiny business on a cart, a basket, a cloth on the pavement or a bicycle. The plan is simple. Buy goods cheaply early in the morning, carry them to where people are, sell them at a small profit, and do it again tomorrow.

Simple does not mean easy. A vendor must choose the right spot, guess how much to buy, set prices that beat the shop down the road and keep regulars coming back. All of this happens with almost no savings to fall back on. Many families in India depend on it. Estimates of how many street vendors India has usually start at about 1 crore and go much higher.

Surprising but true

By 2026, street vendors in one Indian government loan scheme had made more than 800 crore digital payments between them, many through a QR code taped to a cart.

A day that starts in the dark

A vegetable seller's day often begins around 4 or 5 in the morning at a wholesale market, called a mandi. Prices are lowest there, but only if you buy in bulk and bargain hard. By 7 she is at her spot, arranging tomatoes in neat pyramids so they look fresh.

Food vendors work differently. A pani puri seller may spend the afternoon at home frying hundreds of puris and boiling potatoes, then sell from 5 to 10 at night. Every vendor plays the same guessing game. Buy too little and you run out by noon. Buy too much and fruit rots, which can wipe out a day's profit. Prices follow supply and demand: when everyone has mangoes, mangoes are cheap.

The spot is everything

A vendor's location is their shop window. A cart outside a school, a railway station or an office gate sees hundreds of hungry people a day. Moving even one street away can halve sales.

Vada pav, Mumbai's famous potato burger, is often said to have been made popular by a stall near Dadar station in the 1960s. It worked because it was fast, filling and cheap, right where crowds of mill workers passed.

Regular customers matter too. Many vendors remember who likes extra chutney or less chilli. Some even let trusted buyers pay later.

Money, loans and QR codes

Most vendors have little spare cash. In the past, many borrowed from moneylenders who charged huge interest, sometimes ₹100 a day on a ₹1,000 loan. Over a month, that is far more than the loan itself. You can see how quickly interest adds up.

In 2020, the Indian government started PM SVANidhi, a scheme offering small loans without asking for property as security. By 2026, more than 75 lakh vendors had taken over 1 crore loans, and nearly half the borrowers were women.

Phones changed things too. Thanks to UPI, a printed QR code costs almost nothing, so even a chaiwala can accept digital payments. Paying by phone also builds a record, which helps vendors show a bank they have a steady income.

Rules of the road

Vendors often clash with city officials over space on crowded pavements. India passed the Street Vendors Act in 2014. It says cities should count their vendors, issue them certificates and set up vending zones with local committees.

The fight over pavement space happens in the USA too. New York City capped the number of food cart permits at about 5,000 for nearly 40 years, so many vendors waited years or rented permits from others. New laws in 2021 and 2026 began adding thousands more. Some American cities now celebrate their carts with awards and festivals.

There is a quiet cleverness in all of it. A good vendor reads the weather, the season and the crowd better than any app. And the snack with the longest queue on many Indian streets is pani puri, a crisp ball of spicy water with a different name in almost every city.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Street vending is not a real business.

Truth: A vendor has to buy stock, set prices, manage cash, keep customers and deal with rules, which is everything a shop owner does. Many famous Indian snacks, from vada pav to momos, grew popular through street stalls.
