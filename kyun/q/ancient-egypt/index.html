How did ancient Egyptians live?

Cleopatra lived closer in time to the first Moon landing than to the building of the Great Pyramid.

The short answer

Ancient Egyptians lived along the River Nile, a green ribbon of farmland running through the desert. Most were farmers who grew wheat, barley, flax and vegetables. They paid taxes in grain, believed in many gods, and were ruled by a king called the pharaoh.

Their civilisation lasted about 3,000 years, longer than any modern country has existed. They built the pyramids, wrote in hieroglyphs, made paper from reeds, and prepared their dead carefully for a life after death.

Surprising but true

Workers who built royal tombs near Thebes once stopped work because their food rations were late. It is one of the earliest strikes in recorded history.

A gift of the river

Every year, from about June to September, the Nile flooded. When the water went down, it left a layer of black, fertile mud. Egyptians called their country Kemet, "the black land", after this soil. The desert beyond was Deshret, "the red land".

Life followed three seasons. Akhet was the flood, when farmers could not work their fields and some joined building projects. Peret was the growing season. Shemu was harvest time. Officials measured the height of the flood each year, because it told them how big the harvest, and the taxes, would be.

Why is the Nile so important to Egypt?Almost all of Egypt is desert. Yet about 95% of its people live on a thin green ribbon along one river, just as their ancestors did 5,000 years ago.

Homes, food and clothes

Most Egyptians lived in mud-brick houses with flat roofs, where families slept on hot nights. They ate bread, onions, lentils, fish and fruit such as dates and figs. They drank a thick, mild beer made from barley, which was also used to pay workers.

People wore light linen clothes made from flax. Men and women both wore eye makeup, called kohl, which may also have helped protect against the glare of the sun and eye infections. Children played with balls, spinning tops and wooden toys, and adults played board games like senet.

Animals were everywhere. Cats kept mice away from grain stores and were linked with the goddess Bastet. Some families kept dogs, monkeys and even geese as pets.

The village of tomb builders

One of the best views of everyday life comes from Deir el-Medina, a village near Thebes, now Luxor. The skilled workers who carved and painted royal tombs in the Valley of the Kings lived there with their families for hundreds of years.

They wrote notes on broken pottery and flakes of stone, and thousands survive. They record sick days, arguments, shopping, lessons and love poems. One worker missed work because a scorpion stung him. Another stayed home to brew beer. Around 1150 BCE, when their grain rations were late, the workers stopped work and sat down outside temples until they were paid.

Women in Egypt

Egyptian women had more rights than in many ancient societies. They could own land, run businesses, sign contracts and go to court. A few women ruled as pharaoh. Hatshepsut reigned for about 20 years and sent a trading expedition to a land called Punt, bringing back incense trees.

Gods, kings and the afterlife

Egyptians believed in hundreds of gods, such as Ra the sun god and Osiris, ruler of the dead. The pharaoh was seen as a link between the gods and the people. They believed that after death, a person's heart was weighed against a feather. A good life meant a happy afterlife in a land like Egypt, only better.

That belief is why they made mummies and filled tombs with food, furniture, games and little figures of servants. Much of what we know about daily life comes from these tombs.

Writing on reeds

Scribes wrote on papyrus, a paper-like sheet made from reeds that grew along the Nile. Being a scribe was a respected job, and boys trained for years. Papyrus was so useful that it was traded across the Mediterranean. Our word "paper" comes from it, even though true paper was invented later, in China.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The pyramids were built by armies of slaves.

Truth: Evidence from workers' villages and tombs near Giza suggests the builders were paid workers, given bread, beer and meat, with doctors to treat injuries. Some worked full time and others came for a few months when their farms were flooded.
