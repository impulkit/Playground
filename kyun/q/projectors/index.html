How does a projector work?

A projector throws a tiny, brilliant picture across a dark room and lets a lens blow it up to the size of a wall.

The short answer

A projector does three jobs. It makes a very bright light. It shapes that light into a small picture. Then a lens spreads the picture out so it lands, sharp and large, on a wall or screen far away.

The small picture might be a painted glass slide, a strip of film, a tiny screen or a chip full of moving mirrors. The idea has stayed the same for more than 350 years. Only the picture maker in the middle has changed.

Because the light spreads out as it travels, a projector placed farther back gives a bigger picture. A cinema projector at the back of a hall can fill a screen as wide as a cricket pitch is long.

Surprising but true

Many modern projectors make their picture with a chip covered in up to a few million microscopic mirrors, each one tilting back and forth thousands of times a second.

Painted glass and candlelight

The earliest projectors were called magic lanterns, and they were in use in Europe by the 1660s. Inside was a candle or oil lamp, a curved mirror behind it and a lens in front. Pictures were painted on glass slides and pushed through a slot.

Showmen travelled from town to town with them. They told ghost stories and fairy tales while images of skeletons, ships and kings glowed on a sheet. Some slides had moving parts, so a windmill could turn or a sailor could open his mouth.

Then came film. In 1895 the Lumière brothers showed short films in Paris with a machine that projected moving pictures. Only months later, in July 1896, the same films played at Watson's Hotel in Bombay. Within twenty years Dadasaheb Phalke was making Indian films for those projectors to show.

Why the slide goes in upside down

A projector lens works a bit like your eye, but in reverse. Light from the top of the slide crosses over inside the lens and lands at the bottom of the screen. Light from the left ends up on the right.

So the picture comes out flipped both ways. That is why old slide projectors were loaded with every slide upside down and back to front. Film projectors had the same trick built in.

Focusing means sliding the lens forward or back until the crossed beams meet exactly at the screen. Too close or too far and each point of light smears into a blob, which is the blur you see when a teacher is still fiddling with the knob.

Millions of tiny mirrors

Most projectors today make their picture electronically. One common kind uses a small see-through LCD screen with the light shining through it. Another kind, called DLP, uses a chip invented at Texas Instruments in 1987.

A DLP chip is covered in tiny mirrors, up to a few million of them, each smaller than the width of a hair. Every mirror is one pixel. When a mirror tilts one way, it bounces light out through the lens and that dot on the screen glows. When it tilts the other way, the light is thrown into a dark corner and the dot goes dark.

The mirrors flick thousands of times a second. Your eye can't follow them, so it simply sees shades of grey. A spinning colour wheel or coloured lights add red, green and blue.

The trouble with daylight

A projector can only add light, never take it away. On a bright afternoon the sun is already lighting the screen, so the projector's picture has to compete with it.

That is why cinemas are dark and why school projectors look faded with the curtains open. It is also why projectors have grown far brighter. Old ones used hot lamps that needed fans and burned out. Many newer ones use LEDs or lasers, which run cooler and last for years. Some are small enough to fit in a pocket and still cast a picture the size of a door.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A projector shines black onto the screen to make the dark parts of a picture.

Truth: A projector can only add light. The "black" you see is just the screen with less light falling on it, which is why projected pictures look washed out in a bright room.

Try this

In a dark room, shine a phone torch through a magnifying glass onto a wall. Hold a small drawing on clear plastic or tracing paper between the torch and the lens, and move the lens back and forth until the drawing comes into focus. Notice which way up it appears.
