What is Rabindra Sangeet?

One poet wrote the words and music of more than 2,000 songs, and two of them became the national anthems of India and Bangladesh.

The short answer

Rabindra Sangeet means "Rabindra's songs". It is the name for the songs written and composed by Rabindranath Tagore, the Bengali poet who won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1913. Tagore wrote more than 2,000 of them, both the words and the tunes.

In Bengal, on both sides of the border between India and Bangladesh, these songs are everywhere. Children learn them at school, families sing them at festivals, and they are a fixture on Bengali radio. India's national anthem, "Jana Gana Mana", and Bangladesh's, "Amar Shonar Bangla", are both Tagore songs.

Surprising but true

One of Tagore's best-loved songs, "Purano Sei Diner Kotha", is set to the tune of the Scottish song "Auld Lang Syne", which Americans sing on New Year's Eve.

A poet who sang

Tagore was born in 1861 into a large, artistic family in Calcutta. Music filled the house. He heard Hindustani classical singers, Bengali devotional songs and Western tunes played by his brothers. He started writing songs as a teenager and kept going until a few months before he died in 1941.

He often composed while walking or travelling, humming a tune and fitting words to it. Then he would sing it to a relative or student, who wrote down the notes, because he did not always write music himself. Many songs also found their way into his dance-dramas, such as Chitrangada, which are still staged today.

Many roots

Rabindra Sangeet borrows from many traditions. Some songs are built on classical ragas, though Tagore bent the rules freely. Some draw on the folk songs of the Bauls, wandering singers he deeply admired. Others use Kirtan rhythms or Western melodies.

On a visit to England as a young man, he heard Scottish and Irish songs and liked them. "Purano Sei Diner Kotha", a song about old friendship, uses the tune of "Auld Lang Syne". Despite these mixtures, his songs have a style of their own, and Bengali listeners recognise one within a few notes.

Songs for every season

Tagore sorted many of his songs by theme in a collection called Gitabitan. There are songs of worship, love, the homeland and nature. The nature songs follow the six seasons of the Bengali year, from summer heat to the drumming rain of the monsoon, autumn, late autumn, winter and spring.

At Santiniketan, the school and university Tagore founded near Kolkata, the spring festival of Basanta Utsav is celebrated with his songs and dances. Students dressed in yellow sing as they walk. Santiniketan was named a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2023.

Songs that became anthems

"Jana Gana Mana" was first sung in 1911 at a meeting of the Indian National Congress in Calcutta. In 1950 its first verse became India's national anthem. "Amar Shonar Bangla", written in 1905 when the British split Bengal in two, became the anthem of Bangladesh after its independence in 1971.

During the freedom struggle, another Tagore song, "Ekla Chalo Re" ("If no one answers your call, walk alone"), became a favourite of Mahatma Gandhi. It is still sung at protests and rallies today.

Singing it right

For decades after Tagore's death, the university he founded, Visva-Bharati, held the rights to his songs. Its music board checked recordings to make sure singers kept to the notes Tagore had set. Some musicians felt this protected the songs. Others felt it stopped them from experimenting. The rights ran out in 2001, and since then singers have tried jazz, rock and electronic versions alongside the traditional style.

Famous singers include Debabrata Biswas, Kanika Bandyopadhyay, Suchitra Mitra and Hemanta Mukherjee. Film directors such as Satyajit Ray used Tagore's songs in their films. More than 80 years after his death, young singers in Kolkata and Dhaka still record his songs. His birthday, the 25th of the Bengali month of Boishakh, is marked with singing in homes, schools and halls across Bengal.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Rabindra Sangeet is all slow, serious religious music.

Truth: Tagore wrote songs about rain, love, seasons, festivals, patriotism and childhood as well as worship. Some are playful and quick, and many are sung at weddings, school events and the spring festival of Basanta Utsav.

Try this

Pick a short poem you like, in any language. Try singing it to two different tunes you already know, one slow and one fast. Notice how the same words feel happier or sadder with each tune. Tagore often wrote the words and the tune at the same time, so they fit like a glove.
