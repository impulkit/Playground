What is a smart city?

In some cities, traffic lights count cars, bins report when they are full, and a control room watches it all on giant screens.

The short answer

A smart city uses sensors, internet connections and computers to run everyday services better. Traffic lights can change their timing when a road is busy. Water pipes can report a leak. Buses can send their location to an app, so you know when the next one is coming.

The idea is that data, collected all the time and from all over the city, helps officials spot problems early and fix them faster. A smart city should also be easier for people to live in, with cleaner streets, safer roads and less waiting.

There is no exact rule for what counts as "smart". Different cities use the word in quite different ways. For some it means free Wi-Fi in the park. For others it means a whole new city wired from the ground up.

Surprising but true

Most of India's 100 "smart cities" are old cities being upgraded, not new ones built from scratch.

Sensors everywhere

Much of a smart city is an example of the internet of things: ordinary objects fitted with small sensors that send information over the internet. A dustbin might report when it is full, so the collection truck only stops where it is needed. Streetlights might dim when nobody is around, saving power.

Cameras at junctions can count cars, and traffic lights can use those counts to decide how long to stay green. Electricity meters can report usage every few minutes instead of once a month.

All of this data flows into a control room, where staff watch maps and alerts on big screens, a bit like air traffic controllers for a whole city.

India's 100 cities

In June 2015, the Government of India launched the Smart Cities Mission. Cities competed to join, and 100 were chosen in rounds between 2016 and 2018. Bhubaneswar came first in the first list.

Each city chose an area to upgrade, such as an old market, a lakefront or a station area, and added projects like smart roads, solar panels and better drainage. Every city also set up an Integrated Command and Control Centre. During the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, several cities used these rooms to track cases and organise supplies.

The mission's deadline was pushed back more than once, finally to March 2025.

Built from scratch, or upgraded

A few places really were built new. Songdo, near Seoul in South Korea, was built on land reclaimed from the sea, with sensors and pipes planned in from the start. Its rubbish is sucked away through underground tubes instead of being collected by trucks.

Singapore took a different route. In 2014 it launched a plan it called Smart Nation, adding sensors, digital services and data across a city that already existed. In the USA, cities such as New York have put sensors on streets and buildings to study noise, air and traffic.

The questions people ask

Smart cities raise real worries. A city full of cameras and sensors knows a great deal about where people go. Some cities use face recognition on their cameras, which many people see as a threat to privacy. Who owns the data, and who can see it?

Another question is who benefits. Critics of India's mission point out that many projects improved only a small area, while poorer neighbourhoods saw little change. Supporters reply that the mission gave cities their first real tools for managing traffic, water and emergencies. Both sides agree on one thing: technology helps only if the basic pipes, drains and buses work too.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A smart city has to be a shiny new city built from nothing.

Truth: A few, like Songdo in South Korea, were built on empty land. Most smart city projects, in India and elsewhere, add sensors, apps and better services to cities people already live in.

Try this

Make a "smart classroom" survey. For one week, count how often the lights or fans are left on in an empty room. Then design a simple sign or rule that would fix it, and count again the next week to see if it worked.
