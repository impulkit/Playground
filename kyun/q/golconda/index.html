What made Golconda fort special?

Clap your hands under the main gate of Golconda fort in Hyderabad, and the sound can be heard at the hilltop pavilion about a kilometre away.

The short answer

Golconda is a huge granite fort on a hill at the edge of Hyderabad. For about 170 years it was the capital of the Qutb Shahi sultans, one of the richest kingdoms in the world. Its wealth came from diamonds dug from mines in the surrounding region.

The fort is famous for clever engineering. Its walls run for kilometres, with 87 bastions and eight gates. Water was lifted to the hilltop. And its builders designed the entrance so that a single handclap could warn the guards at the top.

In 1687 the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb captured it after a long siege. The diamonds' fame lives on in its name.

Surprising but true

Golconda was so famous for its diamond trade that "golconda" became an English word for a source of great wealth.

From a shepherd's hill to a sultan's fort

A local story says the name comes from Golla Konda, meaning "shepherd's hill" in Telugu. A shepherd boy is said to have found a sacred idol there. The first fort on the hill was a mud fort built under the Kakatiya kings of Warangal.

In 1518, a governor named Sultan Quli Qutb-ul-Mulk declared his own kingdom. His family, the Qutb Shahis, turned the mud fort into a stone city with palaces, mosques, armouries, gardens and homes. Thousands of people lived inside the outer walls.

The fort grew so crowded that in 1591 Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah founded a new city nearby. Its centrepiece was the Charminar. That city became Hyderabad.

The clapping gate

The main entrance is called the Fateh Darwaza, the Victory Gate. Its huge wooden doors are studded with iron spikes to stop war elephants from ramming them.

Stand under the dome near the gate and clap, and the sound travels up to the Bala Hissar pavilion at the very top of the hill, about a kilometre away. Guards could use it as an alarm. Hard stone surfaces bounce sound, the same way echoes do in a big empty hall.

Visitors still try it today. Guides clap, and someone at the top shouts back. It works best early in the morning, before the crowds and traffic noise of a big city drown out the clap.

The diamond capital

For centuries, mines near the Krishna river, such as Kollur, produced some of the most famous diamonds on Earth. Golconda was where they were traded and guarded. Merchants travelled from Persia and Europe to buy them.

The Koh-i-Noor is believed to have come from these mines. So is the deep-blue Hope Diamond, now in the Smithsonian museum in Washington, DC, and the pink Darya-i-Noor in Iran. Diamonds form deep underground under great heat and pressure, as you can see in how gems form.

Golconda's fame was so great that its name entered English. A "golconda" means a mine of riches, a place of endless wealth.

Water on a hilltop

Getting water to the top of a granite hill was a huge challenge. The builders made tanks to catch rainwater. They also used Persian wheels, chains of pots turned by animals, to lift water through clay pipes, stage by stage, up to the palaces.

The fort had its own reservoirs, so it could survive long sieges. That was tested in 1687. Aurangzeb's army surrounded Golconda for about eight months. The walls held. Then, according to the usual account, an officer named Abdullah Khan Pani was bribed to open a gate at night. The last Qutb Shahi sultan, Abul Hasan Tana Shah, was taken prisoner.

Near the fort stand the Qutb Shahi Tombs, a garden of domed tombs of the sultans. And the strangest part? The city born from Golconda's crowds grew into one of India's biggest, whose story continues in Hyderabad.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Koh-i-Noor and the Hope Diamond were found inside Golconda fort.

Truth: They came from mines in the wider Golconda region, especially along the Krishna river, such as the Kollur mine. Golconda was the market and fortress where diamonds were cut, sold and guarded.

Try this

Stand at one end of a long corridor or stairwell at school and clap once, sharply. Then have a friend clap while you stand at the far end. Notice how hard walls carry and bounce the sound. Golconda's builders used the same idea on purpose.
