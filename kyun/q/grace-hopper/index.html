Who was Grace Hopper?

A US Navy officer handed out pieces of wire about 30 cm long so people could hold a nanosecond in their hands.

The short answer

Grace Hopper was an American mathematician, computer scientist and US Navy officer. She was one of the first people to program a large computer, the Harvard Mark I, during the Second World War.

Her biggest idea was that people should be able to give computers instructions in words that look a little like English, instead of strings of numbers. She built one of the first compilers, programs that translate human-friendly code into the binary code a machine understands. That idea led to COBOL, a language still running in many banks and government offices today.

Taking clocks apart

Grace Brewster Murray was born in New York City in 1906. As a seven-year-old, she was so curious about how alarm clocks worked that she took apart several of them, one after another, until her mother limited her to one.

She studied maths and physics at Vassar College and earned a PhD in mathematics at Yale in 1934. That was rare for anyone at the time, and especially for a woman. She then taught maths at Vassar.

Joining the Navy

When the United States entered the Second World War, Hopper wanted to serve. At first the Navy said she was too old, too light and too valuable as a maths teacher. She kept trying, and in 1943 she joined the Navy's reserve force for women.

She was sent to Harvard University to work on the Mark I, one of the first computers in the United States. It was about 15 metres long, as long as a city bus, and weighed about 4.5 tonnes. It clattered as it worked. Hopper and her team wrote programs for it to solve maths problems for the war, including calculations for the atomic bomb project.

The moth in the machine

In 1947, the team working on the Mark II computer found it was making errors. Inside, they found a moth caught in one of its switches. They removed it and taped it into the logbook with a note that read "First actual case of bug being found".

The word "bug" was already in use for faults, but the story became famous. Hopper told it for the rest of her life. The logbook page, moth and all, is now kept at the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History in Washington, D.C.

Teaching computers English

In the early 1950s, most programming meant writing long lists of numbers. Hopper thought this was slow and full of mistakes. In 1952 her team built a compiler called A-0.

Many experts said computers could only do arithmetic, not understand words. Hopper proved them wrong with a language called FLOW-MATIC, which used English commands such as "compare" and "transfer". It helped inspire COBOL, created in 1959 by a group of companies and the US government. Hopper did not write COBOL alone, but she pushed hard for it and for computers that could share programs. She followed in a line that goes back to Ada Lovelace, who imagined computer programs a century earlier.

Admiral Grace

Hopper retired from the Navy several times, and was called back each time. When she finally left in 1986, aged 79, she was a rear admiral and the oldest serving officer in the US Navy.

She was famous for a clock in her office that ran backwards, to remind people that things do not have to be done the usual way. A Navy warship, USS Hopper, is named after her. In 2016 she was awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom, one of America's highest honours. Today one of the world's largest gatherings of women in computing is named after her.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Grace Hopper invented the word "bug" for a computer problem.

Truth: Engineers, including Thomas Edison, were calling faults "bugs" in the 1800s. Hopper's team found a real moth in a computer in 1947 and joked about it in their logbook. She loved telling the story, which helped make the word famous.

Try this

Write step-by-step instructions for a simple job, like putting on a sock or making a sandwich. Give them to a friend or parent and ask them to follow every word exactly, nothing more. You will soon find missing steps. Programmers face this all the time, because computers do only what they are told.
