What do children eat for breakfast around the world?

A child in Tokyo might start the day with rice, miso soup and grilled fish, while one in Indore digs into poha topped with crunchy sev.

The short answer

Breakfast is wildly different around the world. In much of the United States, children pour a bowl of cereal or eat pancakes. In Japan it might be rice and soup. In south India, it's idli with sambar, and in Punjab a buttery aloo paratha. In Mexico, it could be crisp tortilla pieces in salsa.

What people eat depends on what grows nearby, how much time families have and old habits passed down for generations. Many breakfasts that seem unusual to outsiders are as normal to the people who eat them as toast is to others.

Surprising but true

Corn flakes were created in the 1890s as a plain, bland health food for patients at a sanatorium in Michigan, not as a sweet treat.

India's many mornings

India alone has dozens of breakfasts. In Tamil Nadu and Karnataka, children eat steamed idli or crisp dosa with coconut chutney. In Maharashtra and Madhya Pradesh, poha, flattened rice cooked with onion, peanuts and turmeric, is everywhere. Indore is famous for poha topped with sev and a squeeze of lemon.

Punjabi families often eat parathas stuffed with potato, with curd and pickle. Kerala has puttu, steamed rice flour layered with coconut, and Bengal has fluffy fried luchi. In the north-east, many families eat rice for breakfast, while Gujarati homes might serve thepla, a soft spiced flatbread that also travels well on long train journeys. A full tour of Indian breakfasts would take weeks of eating, and every region argues that its own is best.

Rice and soup in Japan

A traditional Japanese breakfast looks like a small lunch: a bowl of rice, miso soup, a piece of grilled fish, pickled vegetables and sometimes natto, sticky fermented soybeans with a strong smell. Many busy families in Japan now eat toast too.

China has congee, a soft rice porridge, often eaten with youtiao, long fried dough sticks. In Vietnam, many people start the day with pho, a noodle soup. Soup for breakfast is common across much of Asia.

What is your body clock?Deep in your brain, a cluster of about 20,000 nerve cells keeps time like a clock, telling your body when to feel sleepy, hungry and alert.

Spreads, beans and bread

In Turkey, breakfast is called kahvaltı, meaning "before coffee". It's often a table full of small plates: cheese, olives, tomatoes, cucumbers, eggs, honey and bread. In Nigeria, children may eat akara, fried bean fritters, with a smooth corn porridge called pap.

In Mexico, chilaquiles are fried tortilla pieces simmered in red or green salsa, often with eggs. In Brazil, children love pão de queijo, small cheese breads. In France, many children dunk bread or croissants in a bowl of hot chocolate. In Britain, a "full English" with eggs, beans, toast and sausages is a weekend favourite.

How cereal took over

In the 1890s, John Harvey Kellogg ran a health resort in Battle Creek, Michigan. He and his brother Will Keith Kellogg made flakes of wheat and then corn as a plain food for patients. Will added sugar, started a company and sold cereal to the whole country.

Cereal grew popular because it's quick: no cooking, just pour milk. Bright boxes, cartoon mascots and free toys inside helped sell it to children from the 1950s on. Today it's eaten worldwide, though many brands are high in sugar.

Breakfast at school

Many children have no breakfast at home, often because families are busy or short of money. Some schools step in. The United States made its School Breakfast Program permanent in 1975, and millions of students eat a free or cheap breakfast at school each day.

In 2022 Tamil Nadu began serving a free hot breakfast, such as upma or khichdi, to young children in government schools. It adds to India's mid-day meal, the lunch served to crores of schoolchildren. Teachers say hungry children find it hard to concentrate, and a warm meal at the start of the day helps them settle into class.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Breakfast cereal was invented as a tasty treat for children.

Truth: The first flaked cereals were made as plain, unsweetened health food for adult patients. Sugar and cartoon mascots came later, when companies started selling cereal to families.
