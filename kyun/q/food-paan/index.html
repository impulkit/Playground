What is paan?

Paan is a betel leaf folded around fillings, and India has chewed it for more than 2,000 years. Spat paan even ate into the steel of Kolkata's Howrah Bridge.

The short answer

Paan is a fresh, heart-shaped betel leaf wrapped around a filling and chewed after a meal. The classic version holds slices of areca nut, called supari, a smear of wet lime paste and a dab of kattha, a reddish paste from tree bark. Sweet paan swaps in rose jam, fennel seeds, coconut and cherries.

People across South and Southeast Asia have chewed paan for at least 2,000 years. It is offered to guests, served at weddings and placed before gods. But its most common ingredients, areca nut and tobacco, are a major cause of mouth cancer, so doctors warn against them.

Surprising but true

The betel leaf used for paan comes from a climbing vine in the pepper family, a close cousin of the plant that gives us black pepper.

A leaf with a long history

Old Sanskrit writings call paan tambula, and describe it as a sign of hospitality and luxury. Kings handed paan to visitors as a mark of honour. Accepting a paan from someone could seal a promise.

In the 1300s, the Moroccan traveller Ibn Battuta described the betel leaf in India and how prized it was. Under the Mughals, paan became an art. Nobles carried silver boxes called paandaan with tiny bowls for each filling. Folding a perfect paan, tight enough to hold together and small enough to pop in whole, became a skill people boasted about.

Why your mouth turns red

When betel leaf, kattha and lime are chewed together, they react and turn the saliva bright red. That is why paan chewers have red lips and why the streets of some cities are dotted with red splashes.

Those splashes cause real damage. In 2013, engineers in Kolkata found that spat paan was eating away at the steel casings at the base of Howrah Bridge's pillars. The mix of saliva and lime is so corrosive that the bridge's protective covers had worn much thinner. The fix was to wrap them in fibreglass and ask people to stop spitting.

What is special about Howrah Bridge?Howrah Bridge carries a river of people across the Hooghly every day, and it is held together by rivets, without a single nut or bolt.

The leaf and its farms

The betel vine is a climber in the pepper family, related to the plant that gives us black pepper. Farmers grow it under shady sheds of bamboo and dried grass, called barejas, because the leaves scorch in strong sun. Some betel gardens look like dim green rooms, cool and damp inside.

Different regions grow leaves with different flavours. Banarasi paan from Varanasi received a GI tag in 2023. Mahoba in Uttar Pradesh is famous for its desawari leaf. The Magahi leaf of Bihar is prized for being soft enough to melt in the mouth. Leaves are sold in bundles and wrapped in damp cloth to stay fresh. A skilled paan seller can fold dozens an hour, often pushing a clove through the top like a pin to hold each one shut.

The serious side

The World Health Organization's cancer research agency lists areca nut as a substance that causes cancer in humans. Chewing it for years, especially with tobacco, raises the risk of mouth cancer sharply. India has one of the highest rates of mouth cancer in the world, and paan with tobacco is one big reason.

Areca nut also stains teeth, wears them down and stiffens the inside of the cheeks over time. Many families now serve only sweet paan without supari or tobacco, made with gulkand, fennel and coconut. A sweet paan with no supari and no tobacco keeps the fun of the tradition without its biggest dangers.

The old custom of offering paan to guests survives at many Indian weddings, where a paan stall is often the busiest corner after dinner. And the spices folded inside, from cardamom to cloves, carry their own story of sea voyages and fortunes, one that begins with the great spice trade.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Paan is a healthy mouth freshener, so chewing it often does no harm.

Truth: The leaf and sweet fillings are not the problem, but areca nut and tobacco, often found in paan, cause mouth cancer and stain teeth red-brown. Doctors advise avoiding them completely.

Try this

With an adult, buy a single fresh betel leaf at a market. Hold it up to a window and trace its veins with your finger. Compare it with a peepal or mango leaf and notice its glossy heart shape and spicy smell.
