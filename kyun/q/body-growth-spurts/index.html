What is a growth spurt?

At the peak of a growth spurt, some teenagers shoot up about 10 cm in a single year, enough to outgrow new shoes in a few months.

The short answer

A growth spurt is a stretch of time when your body grows much faster than usual. The biggest one happens at puberty, the years when a child's body changes into an adult's. During it, many teenagers grow 8 to 10 cm in a single year.

Girls usually start their spurt earlier, often around 10 or 11. Boys usually start a couple of years later, around 12 or 13, and keep going longer. That's why, in many classrooms, girls are taller than boys for a few years, before the boys catch up.

Smaller spurts happen in early childhood too. And the very fastest growth of your life was when you were a baby.

Surprising but true

During a growth spurt, your hands and feet usually grow first, then your arms and legs, and your body last. That is why many teenagers feel clumsy for a while.

The baby record

A newborn is usually about 50 cm long. By its first birthday, it is often about 75 cm. That is 25 cm in one year, half its own length again. If you kept growing at that rate, by age 13 you would be taller than an Indian elephant.

After that first year, growth slows down to about 5 to 7 cm a year through childhood. Then puberty arrives and the speed jumps again, for two or three years, before slowing to a stop.

What sets off the spurt

Puberty begins in the brain. A small gland under the brain, the pituitary, starts sending out signals called hormones. They tell the body to make more growth hormone and sex hormones such as oestrogen and testosterone.

Those hormones act on the growth plates, soft layers of cartilage near the ends of long bones like the thigh and shin. Cells there multiply and turn into new bone, making the bone longer. At the end of puberty, the hormones also tell the growth plates to harden and close. Once that happens, you cannot grow taller.

Much growth hormone is released in deep sleep at night. That is one more good reason to get enough sleep.

Big feet, long legs, awkward elbows

The body does not grow evenly. Hands and feet usually go first, then arms and legs, and the chest and back last. So a 12-year-old might suddenly need much bigger shoes long before the rest of the body catches up.

Because legs and arms lengthen fast, the brain has to keep updating its map of where they are. For a while, it can feel like you are always banging your knee or tripping on the stairs. It passes.

Many children also feel hungrier than ever during a spurt. Their bodies need extra energy and building blocks such as protein and calcium. Some feel tired, or get achy legs at night, often called growing pains, though doctors are not sure growth itself causes them.

Everyone has their own timetable

Two children of the same age can be at very different stages. One might finish growing at 13 while a friend has not started. Both can be perfectly normal. Your final height mostly depends on the genes from your parents, plus good food, sleep and health along the way.

Average heights have changed over time too. In many countries, people today are taller than their great-grandparents, mainly because of better food and less disease. Indian children have been getting taller on average over the past century, though more slowly than in some other countries.

Doctors track a child's growth by plotting height on a chart over the years, which turns a set of marks on a door frame into a graph with a story. If you ever worry that you are growing too fast or too slowly, a doctor can look at that chart and tell you what is normal for you.

Still, no human comes close to the champions of growth. Some kinds of bamboo can grow almost a metre in a single day, fast enough that you could nearly watch it happen.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Drinking tea or coffee will stunt your growth.

Truth: There is no good evidence that tea or coffee make children shorter. Height depends mostly on genes, good food and enough sleep. Doctors still suggest kids keep caffeine low, because it can disturb sleep.

Try this

Ask an adult to mark your height on a strip of paper taped to a door, with the date, once every three months. After a year, plot the marks on a simple line graph. You might spot that you grow faster in some seasons than others, which many children do.
