Where did gulab jamun come from?

Gulab jamun has no roses and no jamun fruit in it. Its name, and maybe the sweet itself, travelled to India from Persia.

The short answer

Gulab jamun is a soft ball of milk dough, fried until golden brown and soaked in sugar syrup. Its roots probably lie outside India. Food historians think it grew from fried dough balls soaked in honey or syrup that were eaten in Persia and the Arab world for centuries.

When such ideas reached India, cooks made them their own, using khoya, milk boiled down for hours until it turns thick and fudgy. The name says a lot. Gulab comes from Persian words meaning rosewater, often used to scent the syrup. Jamun is the dark purple Indian fruit that the finished sweets resemble.

Surprising but true

In parts of Rajasthan, people cook gulab jamun ki sabzi, a spicy curry made with gulab jamuns in a tomato and onion gravy.

A sweet with Persian cousins

In the Arab world, cooks have long made luqmat al-qadi, which means "the judge's morsels". These are small fried balls of yeast dough, drenched in honey or syrup. They appear in cookbooks from Baghdad written around 800 years ago. Persians made similar sweets.

Persian-speaking traders, soldiers and rulers brought many foods and words to India. Under the Delhi Sultanate and later the Mughals, Persian was the language of royal courts. It is likely that the idea of a fried, syrup-soaked ball met Indian milk sweets somewhere along the way. A popular story says a cook of Emperor Shah Jahan invented gulab jamun by accident. It is a fun tale, but there is no written proof.

Milk, patience and low heat

Making gulab jamun takes time. First comes khoya. Milk is stirred in a heavy pan for an hour or more until most of the water steams away. Khoya is kneaded with a little flour and a pinch of baking soda, then rolled into smooth balls with no cracks. Any crack can split open in the oil.

The balls are fried in ghee or oil over low heat. They sink at first, then slowly rise and puff up as they turn brown. Rushing this step leaves a raw centre. Finally they go into warm syrup flavoured with cardamom and rosewater. Over a few hours, they soak it up like sponges and swell noticeably. Some cooks hide a few strands of saffron, a pinch of cardamom or a crystal of sugar candy inside each ball, which melts into a sweet pocket.

Many cousins across India

India has a whole family of gulab jamun relatives. Kala jamun is fried longer until it is almost black. Bengal has pantua, made partly with chhena, a fresh cheese. One famous Bengal version is called ledikeni. The story goes that a Kolkata sweet maker created it in the 1850s to honour Lady Canning, the wife of the governor-general, and people mispronounced her name.

In Rajasthan, some cooks go further. Gulab jamun ki sabzi is a curry where the sweets go into a spicy tomato and onion gravy, so every bite is sweet, salty and hot together. It sounds strange, but plenty of Rajasthani families swear by it, and cooking videos of it get curious viewers from all over India.

A sweet for every celebration

Gulab jamun turns up at weddings, birthdays and festivals like Diwali and Eid. Shops in India sell it from huge steel pots, and tins of it are shipped to Indian grocery stores across the USA, the UK and the Gulf.

It is very sweet, and a single gulab jamun can carry about as much sugar as two or three teaspoons, so most families save it for special days. The same syrup trick appears in another favourite, the crunchy spiral jalebi, which also has cousins across the Middle East. And at the heart of both lies one of India's oldest skills, turning plain milk into dozens of sweets.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Gulab jamun is made with roses and jamun fruit.

Truth: Neither is in it. "Gulab" comes from the rosewater often added to the syrup, and "jamun" refers to the fruit it looks like, small, round and dark.

Try this

Mix a few spoons of sugar into a cup of room-temperature water until it dissolves. Drop in a cube of sponge cake or a piece of rusk, and watch it soak up the liquid and swell. That is what a fried gulab jamun does in syrup.
