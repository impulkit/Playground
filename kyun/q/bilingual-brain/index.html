What happens in the brain of someone who speaks two languages?

When a bilingual person hears a word, both their languages switch on at once. Their brain then has to quietly choose one and hold the other back.

The short answer

In a bilingual brain, both languages are always switched on. When you hear or think of a word, matching words in both languages wake up. Your brain then picks the one you need and pushes the other one down, many times a minute, without you noticing.

This constant choosing uses brain areas that also handle attention and self-control. Scientists are studying whether all that practice changes the brain in useful ways. Some think it does. Others say the effects are small or hard to prove.

Surprising but true

Babies can hear the difference between speech sounds from any language. By about their first birthday, they start losing the ability to hear sounds their family's languages don't use.

Two languages, one brain

You don't have a separate box for each language. Both share the same main language areas, mostly on the left side of the brain. Words from both languages are stored in an overlapping web, linked to the same ideas.

So if a girl in Chennai who speaks Tamil and English sees a dog, both "naai" and "dog" light up. If she's talking to her English teacher, her brain holds back the Tamil word. With her grandmother, it does the opposite. Most of the time she doesn't feel any of this. The choosing happens in a fraction of a second. Brain scans show extra activity in control areas at the front of the brain while this happens.

Switching gears

Many bilingual people mix languages in the same sentence, like "Mujhe homework finish karna hai". This is called code-switching. It isn't a mistake. It's often a skilled choice, picking the word that fits best or signalling which group you belong to.

In India, mixing is everywhere, from Hinglish adverts to Tanglish film songs. In the United States, many families blend English with Spanish, Chinese or Hindi. About one American in five speaks a language other than English at home.

Babies tune in early

Newborns can hear the difference between speech sounds from every language. A baby in Kolkata could tell apart sounds used only in Japanese. But by around their first birthday, babies get better at their home languages' sounds and worse at others.

Babies who hear two languages from birth keep both sets of sounds. Their brains grow networks for each, and they sort the languages out surprisingly early. Some studies suggest they can even tell two languages apart by watching a speaker's face.

The great debate

Since the 2000s, some studies have suggested bilingual people are better at ignoring distractions and switching between tasks. Others found no such advantage once they compared people of similar backgrounds. Scientists now argue over how big any benefit really is.

There's a similar debate about old age. Dementia is a disease that slowly damages memory. A study in Canada, and a larger one in Hyderabad in 2013, found that bilingual patients showed its first signs about four years later, on average, than people who spoke one language. Some later studies didn't find this. For now, the honest answer is "maybe".

Learning later

You can become bilingual at any age, though most people who learn after childhood keep an accent. Adults often learn grammar faster than children at first, while children are better at sounding like native speakers in the long run.

India is a good place to try. In the 2011 census about a quarter of Indians said they spoke at least two languages, and many speak three or more. People who use sign language alongside a spoken language are bilingual too. Most experts on learning languages give the same simple tip: use the new language with real people, often, even if you make mistakes. Songs, cartoons and comics help too, because your brain remembers words better when they come with a story or a tune.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Learning two languages at once confuses children and delays their speech.

Truth: Research shows bilingual children develop language normally. Mixing words from both languages is a normal stage, not a sign of confusion, and many adults do it on purpose too.

Try this

Ask someone who speaks two languages to name as many fruits as they can in one minute in one language, then in the other. Notice whether they mix words, pause or switch. Ask them which language they count in, dream in or use when they're angry.
