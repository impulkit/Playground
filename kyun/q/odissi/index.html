What is Odissi dance?

Dancers carved in stone more than 2,000 years ago in Odisha strike poses that Odissi dancers still use on stage today.

The short answer

Odissi is a classical dance from Odisha, on India's east coast. It is one of the country's officially recognised classical dances. Odissi dancers move with soft, flowing curves, as if the body were a moving sculpture. The two most important positions are chauka, a strong square stance with bent knees, and tribhangi, in which the body bends at the head, chest and hips into an S-shape.

A performance mixes pure dance, full of rhythm and footwork, with expressive storytelling through faces and hand gestures. Many stories come from the love of Krishna and Radha. The dancer wears a silk sari, silver jewellery and a headpiece shaped like a temple tower. Odissi has deep roots in temple worship.

Surprising but true

Odissi almost disappeared in the early 20th century. Its modern form was rebuilt in the 1950s by a small group of gurus working from temple traditions, old carvings and folk dance.

Dancers in stone

The oldest evidence of dance in Odisha is carved in rock. At the Udayagiri caves near Bhubaneswar, carvings from more than 2,000 years ago show women dancing to music. Centuries later, the walls of temples such as Konark were covered with sculptures of dancers and musicians.

Today's Odissi dancers study these carvings closely. Some poses on stage are almost exact copies of figures cut in stone hundreds of years earlier.

Maharis and gotipuas

For centuries, dance was part of temple worship in Odisha. Women called maharis danced for Lord Jagannath at the great temple in Puri. In time, boys called gotipuas also danced, dressed as girls, in village festivals. Their style was more acrobatic, with bends and balances.

By the early 1900s, both traditions were fading. The mahari custom had lost support, and few people outside Odisha had ever seen the dance.

Rebuilding a dance

In the late 1940s and 1950s, a group of gurus, scholars and dancers set out to revive the art. Kelucharan Mohapatra, who had danced as a gotipua as a boy, became the most famous of these gurus. Others included Pankaj Charan Das and Deba Prasad Das.

They studied what the maharis and gotipuas remembered, compared poses with temple sculptures, and read old texts on dance. From all this they built a clear structure for performances. Pupils such as Sanjukta Panigrahi carried Odissi to stages across India and abroad. Today it is taught in dance schools from Bhubaneswar to New Jersey and California.

A performance from start to finish

An Odissi recital often begins with mangalacharan, an offering to the gods and to the earth. Next comes a pure dance piece that builds patterns of rhythm and movement. Then come expressive pieces, where the dancer acts out a song through face, eyes and hands.

Many of these songs come from the Gita Govinda, a Sanskrit poem about Radha and Krishna written by Jayadeva, a poet linked with Puri, in the 12th century. The recital often ends with moksha, meaning liberation, a fast, joyful dance that finishes in stillness.

Costume and music

Odissi dancers wear handwoven silk saris from Odisha, often arranged in pleats that fan out when the knees bend. The silver jewellery is usually filigree from Cuttack, made from fine twisted wire. Red alta colours the hands and feet, so every gesture stands out. The headpiece, called a tahia, is made of light white pith and shaped like a temple tower.

The music is played on the mardala, a barrel-shaped drum, with a singer, flute, violin or sitar and small cymbals. Odisha's painted scrolls, called pattachitra, show the same stories of Krishna and Radha that the dancers perform.

Myth or fact?

Myth: All Indian classical dances look much the same.

Truth: Each has its own shapes and feel. Odissi is known for soft, flowing curves and the S-shaped tribhangi pose, while Bharatanatyam is known for straight lines, sharp angles and crisp footwork.

Try this

Stand with your feet apart and knees bent outwards, like a square. That is chauka. Now shift your weight to one leg, push that hip out, lean your chest the other way and tilt your head back towards the hip. That is tribhangi. Try moving smoothly between the two.
