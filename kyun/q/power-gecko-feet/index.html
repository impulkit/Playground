How do geckos walk on ceilings?

The house gecko on your wall has no glue and no suction cups, yet it can hang from the ceiling by a single toe.

The short answer

Look closely at a house gecko's toes and you'll see rows of soft ridges underneath, like the gills of a mushroom. Each ridge is covered in hundreds of thousands of tiny hairs called setae. And each of those hairs splits at its tip into hundreds of even smaller pads, each about 200 nanometres wide. You could line up hundreds of them across the width of one human hair.

These pads get so close to a wall that the molecules in the pads and the molecules in the wall pull on each other. This pull, called the van der Waals force, is extremely weak for one pad. But a gecko has millions of them, and together they are strong enough to hold it on a ceiling.

Surprising but true

Each gecko toe is covered in millions of hair-like tips so small that they grip surfaces using the faint pull between molecules.

A foot with no glue

For thousands of years people wondered about geckos. The Greek thinker Aristotle wrote about them climbing trees upside down more than 2,300 years ago. Later, people guessed suction cups, sticky liquid or tiny hooks.

In 2000, a team led by the American biologist Kellar Autumn finally measured the grip of a single gecko hair. It turned out that the hairs worked best dry, with no glue at all. The force between molecules was enough. Their calculations suggested that if every hair on a tokay gecko's feet gripped at once, the animal could in theory hold up around 130 kilograms, about as much as two grown men.

Letting go is the clever part

Sticking is only half the trick. A gecko that couldn't unstick would be trapped forever. So geckos peel each toe off from the tip, like you'd peel off a sticky note or a plaster. Changing the angle of the hairs makes them let go instantly.

A running gecko attaches and detaches its toes many times every second, taking just a few hundredths of a second to unstick each one. Their feet even clean themselves as they walk. Dirt sticks better to the wall than to the tiny hairs, so it gets left behind with each step.

The gecko on your wall

In India, the house gecko or chhipkali is a familiar evening visitor, hunting insects around tube lights. Like many geckos, it is nocturnal, active at night. The bigger tokay gecko of the Northeast and Southeast Asia is famous for its loud "to-kay" call.

There are more than 2,000 species of gecko, living on every continent except Antarctica. Not all can climb. About 60% have sticky toe pads, and some, like the leopard gecko kept as a pet, have none at all. Scientists think toe pads evolved several separate times in different gecko families.

Copying the gecko

Engineers love geckos. Stanford University built a climbing robot called Stickybot that used gecko-style rubber feet. NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory has tested gecko-inspired grippers to grab objects in space, where suction cups are useless because there is no air.

There is even gecko tape, which sticks without glue and can be peeled off and reused, a bit like Velcro but smooth. Geckos are not the only animals using tiny hairs. Some spiders and beetles grip walls in a similar way. So when a household lizard stalks a spider across your ceiling at night, both hunter and hunted may be hanging on by the same trick.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Geckos stick to walls with suction cups or sticky glue on their feet.

Truth: Gecko feet are dry and have no suction. They grip using millions of tiny hairs that get so close to a surface that weak forces between molecules hold them on.

Try this

Press the bristles of two clean toothbrushes or two hairbrushes into each other and try to pull them apart. The many tiny contacts make them hold on. A gecko's foot works on a similar idea, but with millions of contacts so small you'd need a powerful microscope to see them.
