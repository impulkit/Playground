How did kings send messages across India?

In the 1330s, a sultan in Delhi got news from Sindh in about five days, a trip that took travellers fifty, thanks to runners with bells.

The short answer

They used relays. Instead of one messenger travelling the whole way, kings set up a chain of stations along the main roads. A fresh runner or rider waited at each one. When a message arrived, it was handed on at once, and the next person set off at full speed.

This system, called dak, could carry news across the country many times faster than any traveller. It served the court, the army and tax officials, not ordinary families. Ordinary people sent letters with traders, pilgrims or friends heading the right way.

Surprising but true

The English word "dawk", once used for post in India, comes from the Hindi word dak, and "dak bungalows" were rest houses built along the old mail routes.

Runners with bells

The best description comes from Ibn Battuta, a traveller from Morocco who reached Delhi in 1334 and worked for Sultan Muhammad bin Tughlaq. He wrote that the sultan had two kinds of post.

The horse post used royal horses changed every few miles. The foot post used runners. There was a station every third of a mile, about the length of four cricket grounds laid end to end. Each runner held a rod with copper bells at one end. When the next runner heard the bells coming, he got ready, grabbed the letter and sprinted off.

According to Ibn Battuta, news from Sindh reached Delhi in about five days, though the same journey took travellers about fifty.

Fruit and holy water by post

The relay carried more than letters. Ibn Battuta says it brought the sultan fresh fruit from Khurasan, in what is now Iran and Afghanistan. When the court moved to Daulatabad in the Deccan, runners carried water from the Ganga for the sultan to drink, a trip of around forty days for ordinary travellers.

The system also worked as a spy network. Officials sent reports on everything happening in the provinces, so the Delhi Sultanate could act on trouble quickly.

The idea of a relay post is very old. The Persian Empire ran a royal road with horse stations more than 2,500 years ago, and the Greek writer Herodotus admired its riders. Rome had its own state post. In the USA, the Pony Express did the same thing across the Wild West in 1860 and 1861, with riders changing horses about every 15 to 25 km.

Sher Shah's roads

In the 1540s, Sher Shah Suri ruled north India for only about five years, but he built fast. He improved the great road that ran from Bengal towards the Khyber Pass, later called the Grand Trunk Road. Along it, by tradition, stood about 1,700 sarais, or rest houses, each with wells, food and horses waiting for messengers.

Messengers changing horses at these sarais could carry news from Bengal to the north-west far faster than before. The Mughals kept and expanded the system. Their messengers, called harkaras, also gathered news and gossip for the emperor.

The Mughals used pigeons too. Akbar is said to have kept thousands of them, and trained birds could carry short messages between forts and cities far faster than any runner. Pigeon post stayed in use in India for centuries, and police in Odisha were still using pigeons to send messages in remote areas until 2008.

From dak runner to postman

When the East India Company took over, it kept using runners, many carrying a spear and a string of bells to warn off wild animals at night. Each runner covered one stage of road, often at night, before passing his bag on.

In 1854, the British set up a single postal department for all of India, with stamps anyone could buy. Runners still carried mail to remote villages well into the 1900s. Today India Post has around 1.6 lakh post offices (2024 count), more than any other country. And by the 1850s, a new kind of messenger had begun to push the runners aside: electric signals racing down the telegraph wire.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Before the British came, India had no organised postal system.

Truth: Indian rulers ran messenger and relay systems for centuries. The Delhi Sultanate, Sher Shah Suri and the Mughals all used chains of runners and riders, though these served mainly the government, not ordinary people.
