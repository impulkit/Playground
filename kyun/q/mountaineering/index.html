Why do people climb mountains?

Near the top of Everest, each breath holds only about a third of the oxygen you breathe at sea level. People still queue to get there.

The short answer

People climb mountains for many reasons: adventure, the view, the challenge, to test themselves, or to stand somewhere few humans have stood. When the British climber George Mallory was asked in 1923 why he wanted to climb Everest, he reportedly answered, "Because it's there."

Mountaineering is the sport of climbing big mountains, often over many days. Climbers use ropes, ice axes and spiked crampons on their boots. On the highest peaks, the thin air is as big a challenge as the steep rock and ice.

Surprising but true

In 2013 Arunima Sinha, who had lost a leg after being pushed from a moving train, became the first woman amputee to climb Mount Everest.

How it began

People have crossed mountain passes for thousands of years, to trade, to pray or to find new land. Climbing for sport began in the Alps. In 1786 two Frenchmen, Jacques Balmat and Michel-Gabriel Paccard, reached the top of Mont Blanc, the highest mountain in western Europe. This is often called the start of modern mountaineering.

In the 1800s British climbers raced to climb Alpine peaks. Then attention turned to the Himalaya, where 14 mountains rise above 8,000 metres.

Everest got its English name in 1865, after George Everest, a former head of the Survey of India. In 1852 a Bengali mathematician working for the survey, Radhanath Sikdar, is said to have been the first to calculate that it was the highest peak in the world. In Nepal it is called Sagarmatha, and in Tibet Chomolungma.

Everest at last

After many attempts, Tenzing Norgay, a Sherpa climber living in Darjeeling, and Edmund Hillary from New Zealand reached the summit of Everest on 29 May 1953. They stayed about 15 minutes. Tenzing left small offerings of sweets in the snow, a gift to the mountain.

In 1984 Bachendri Pal became the first Indian woman to climb Everest. In 2013 Arunima Sinha, a former national volleyball player who had lost a leg in a train attack, became the first woman amputee to stand on top.

How do your lungs breathe?Your lungs have no muscles to pull air in. A dome of muscle underneath them does almost all the work, about 20,000 times a day.

The death zone

Above about 8,000 metres, climbers enter what is called the death zone. The air pressure is so low that each breath holds only about a third of the oxygen at sea level. The body cannot adapt fully, so it slowly weakens. Most climbers carry bottled oxygen.

Climbers first spend weeks acclimatising, climbing to higher camps and returning to sleep lower down. Their bodies make more red blood cells. Even so, altitude sickness, frostbite and avalanches make high mountains very dangerous.

Sherpas and records

Much of the work on Himalayan expeditions is done by Sherpa climbers from Nepal, who fix ropes, carry loads and guide clients. Kami Rita Sherpa had climbed Everest 32 times by 2026, more than anyone else.

In 1978 Reinhold Messner of Italy and Peter Habeler of Austria became the first to climb Everest without bottled oxygen. Messner went on to climb all 14 eight-thousanders by 1986.

Crowds and care

Today hundreds of people may try to climb Everest each spring, and there have been long queues near the summit. Rubbish, old tents and used oxygen bottles have piled up on the mountain. Nepal and expedition groups now organise clean-up campaigns and require climbers to bring waste down.

You do not need to climb Everest to be a mountaineer. India has many training schools, including the Himalayan Mountaineering Institute in Darjeeling, founded in 1954 with Tenzing as its first director of field training. Many people begin with short treks in the hills of Nepal, Himachal or Uttarakhand, always with trained guides.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The top of Everest is the furthest point from the centre of the Earth.

Truth: Because the Earth bulges at the equator, the summit of Chimborazo in Ecuador is further from Earth's centre, even though it is about 2,500 metres lower above sea level. Everest is the highest above sea level.

Try this

Breathe normally and count your breaths for one minute. Then do 30 star jumps and count again. Your body breathes faster when it needs more oxygen. High on a mountain, climbers breathe hard even while resting, because each breath carries less.
