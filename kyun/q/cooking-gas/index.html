Where does cooking gas come from?

The gas in your kitchen has no smell at all. The rotten-egg whiff is added on purpose, so you notice a leak.

The short answer

Most cooking gas comes from deep underground, from the same places that give us crude oil. In India, the red cylinder in most kitchens holds LPG, short for liquefied petroleum gas. It is a mix of two gases, propane and butane, separated out at oil refineries and gas plants.

Some homes, especially in big cities, get piped natural gas instead. That gas is mostly methane and flows through underground pipes, a bit like water. In the United States, millions of homes cook on piped natural gas, while farms and barbecue grills often use propane tanks.

Surprising but true

An Indian 14.2 kg cylinder holds liquid, not gas. Squeezed under pressure, the gas takes up about 250 times less space than it would in open air.

Born from ancient seas

Natural gas formed over millions of years from tiny sea creatures and plants. When they died, they sank and were buried under mud and sand. Heat and pressure deep underground slowly cooked them into oil and gas, which got trapped in rock layers, sometimes under the seabed.

Companies drill wells to reach these pockets. Off the coast of Mumbai, the Bombay High field has supplied oil and gas since the 1970s. When the gas comes up, it is a mix. Plants separate the methane, which goes into pipelines, from heavier gases like propane and butane, which become LPG.

Squeezing gas into a cylinder

Propane and butane have a handy trick. Push them under moderate pressure and they turn into a liquid, taking up about 250 times less space. That is why one cylinder can last a family a month or more.

When you turn the knob, pressure inside drops a little, and some of the liquid boils back into gas. It flows through the rubber pipe to the burner. If you shake a cylinder gently, you can hear the liquid slosh. A full Indian home cylinder holds 14.2 kg of LPG, and the steel cylinder itself weighs about as much again. An empty cylinder still holds a little gas, which is why it never feels completely light.

What is fire?Fire isn't a thing at all. It's something happening, a fast chemical reaction that gives off heat and light.

Why the smell is fake

Pure LPG and methane have almost no smell, which would make leaks hard to notice. So suppliers add a tiny amount of a chemical called a mercaptan. Your nose can pick it up even in very small amounts, which is why a leak smells like rotten eggs or cabbage.

If you ever notice that smell, don't touch a light switch, don't light anything and tell an adult straight away. Open windows and doors, and leave the room. A single spark can set off gas that has built up in the air.

From wood smoke to a blue flame

Not long ago, most Indian homes cooked on firewood, coal, kerosene or dried cow dung cakes. These fuels fill a kitchen with smoke. The World Health Organization says smoke from cooking fires harms the lungs and causes millions of deaths around the world every year. Most of them are women and children, who spend the most time near the stove.

In 2016 India started the Ujjwala scheme to give free LPG connections to poorer families. By late 2025 it had given out about 10.3 crore. India now uses about 31 million tonnes of LPG a year, and more than half of it arrives by ship from countries such as Saudi Arabia, Qatar and the United States.

What comes next

Cooking gas is a fossil fuel, so burning it adds carbon dioxide to the air. Some villages make biogas from cow dung and food waste in sealed tanks. Many city families now use induction stoves that heat pans with magnets and electricity. Solar cookers work well on sunny days. Still, for many cooks, nothing quite replaces the instant control of a blue flame under a hot tawa.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Cooking gas naturally smells of rotten eggs.

Truth: LPG and natural gas have almost no smell. Suppliers add a tiny amount of a strong-smelling chemical called a mercaptan, so that people notice a leak long before it becomes dangerous.

Try this

With an adult, look inside the metal collar at the top of your gas cylinder. You should find a code like A-27 or C-26. The letter is a quarter of the year (A is January to March) and the number is the year, showing when the cylinder is due for a safety test. Check whether yours is still in date.
