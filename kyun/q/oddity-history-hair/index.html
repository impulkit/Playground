Why did people once wear huge wigs?

In 1778, a French hairstyle carried a model warship on top, sails and all, to celebrate a sea battle.

The short answer

Huge wigs became fashion in Europe in the 1600s and 1700s for a mix of reasons. A French king went bald young and covered it up. A common disease of the time made many people's hair fall out. Lice were everywhere, and shaving your head and wearing a wig was easier than fighting them in your own hair. Then rich people copied the king, and wigs became a sign of power.

By the 1770s, some of the most fashionable women in Paris piled their hair and false hair into towers decorated with feathers, fruit, birds and even a model ship.

Surprising but true

In 1795, Britain made people pay a yearly tax of one guinea just to use powder on their hair or wigs.

A bald king starts a trend

King Louis XIII of France began losing his hair in his twenties and started wearing a wig in the 1620s. His son, Louis XIV, had thick hair as a young man, but wore grand curly wigs for most of his long reign. When the king of the most powerful court in Europe wore something, the nobles copied it.

In England, King Charles II returned from exile in France in 1660 and brought the fashion with him. Soon any man who wanted to look important wore a wig. The best ones were made of human hair. Cheaper ones used horsehair or goat hair. That is where the word "bigwig" comes from, one of many idioms that outlived the thing they described.

Itchy, smelly and expensive

Wigs were hot, heavy and expensive, so owners cared for them carefully. Men shaved their heads or kept their hair very short underneath. Wigs were dusted with powder made from starch and fixed with grease. Thieves sometimes snatched them off people's heads in the street. Wigs had to be sent back to the wigmaker now and then to be cleaned, recurled and re-powdered, a bit like taking a car for a service. A good wig could cost more than a servant earned in a year.

In 1795 Britain, short of money because of a war with France, taxed hair powder. Anyone who used it had to buy a certificate costing a guinea a year. Many people simply stopped, and the fashion died faster.

What was the French Revolution?When crowds stormed the Bastille prison in Paris on 14 July 1789, they found only seven prisoners inside. The attack still began a revolution.

Towers of hair

The most extreme styles appeared in Paris in the 1770s. Queen Marie Antoinette and her hairdresser helped make the pouf famous: hair stretched over a frame of wire and padding, then decorated. One style, made in 1778, carried a model of the French warship La Belle Poule.

How tall did they get? Cartoonists in London drew women whose hair touched the ceiling, but cartoons exaggerate. Real portraits suggest some reached around half a metre above the head. Women had to kneel in their carriages, and a style could stay in place for weeks.

After the French Revolution began in 1789, looking like a rich aristocrat became risky. Short, natural hair came in.

Wigs in ancient Egypt

Wigs are far older than Louis XIV. Thousands of years ago in ancient Egypt, many wealthy people shaved their heads to stay cool and avoid lice, then wore wigs of human hair and plant fibre for special occasions. Beeswax and resin held the curls in place. Some of these wigs survive in museums today, still with their braids.

Still in court

Judges and lawyers in Britain kept their wigs long after everyone else gave them up. Many still wear horsehair wigs in criminal courts. Lawyers in India also once wore them under British rule, but dropped them long ago. The rest of us use our own hair, which grows about 1 cm a month. Why it keeps growing on your head but stops short on your arm comes down to tiny roots with different timers, explained in how hair grows.

Myth or fact?

Myth: George Washington wore a white powdered wig.

Truth: Washington wore his own hair, grown long, tied back and dusted with white powder. Paintings make it look like a wig, but it wasn't.
