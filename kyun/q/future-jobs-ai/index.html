Will AI take people's jobs?

NASA once employed people whose job title was "computer". Machines took that job, but the people who did it helped send astronauts into space.

The short answer

AI will almost certainly change many jobs. Whether it will leave fewer jobs overall is something experts strongly disagree about.

In 2025, the World Economic Forum surveyed more than a thousand large companies around the world. Their forecast was that by 2030, about 170 million new jobs would be created and about 92 million would disappear. That would leave more jobs than before. But forecasts like this are guesses, and many economists say the true numbers could be very different.

What most experts do agree on is this: AI is better at some tasks than others. Jobs are made of many tasks. A machine may take over some of them, and change what people spend their days doing.

Surprising but true

In 2016, a leading AI scientist said hospitals should stop training radiologists because AI would soon read scans better. Ten years later, demand for radiologists has kept growing.

When "computer" was a job

Before electronic computers, a computer was a person. Rooms full of people, often women, worked out long calculations by hand. At NASA in the 1950s and 1960s, human computers calculated rocket paths. Some of them, like Katherine Johnson, became famous, as told in NASA's hidden figures.

When electronic computers arrived, the job of human computer vanished. But many of those workers learned to program the new machines. Their skills moved into a new kind of work. Dorothy Vaughan, who led a group of human computers at NASA, taught herself and her team the programming language FORTRAN.

The same pattern has repeated many times. Telephone operators once connected every call by hand. Lift operators drove elevators. Those jobs are almost gone, yet far more people now work in phones and buildings than ever did.

Machines that made more jobs

During the Industrial Revolution, machines wove cloth faster than any human. Some weavers smashed machines in protest, fearing for their jobs. In the short run, many did suffer. In the long run, cheaper cloth meant people bought much more of it, and factories employed huge numbers of workers.

In 1900, about 40% of American workers worked on farms. Today it is under 2%, thanks to tractors and other machines. Yet most of those workers' grandchildren have jobs that didn't exist in 1900.

When ATMs spread, people expected bank tellers to disappear. Instead, US teller numbers kept rising for years, because cheaper branches meant banks opened more of them.

What is artificial intelligence?In 1997, a computer beat the world chess champion. Today AI can chat, draw and translate, yet it can still make mistakes a child wouldn't.

Why AI might be different

Earlier machines mostly replaced muscle, or one narrow skill. AI systems, like large language models, can write, summarise, translate, draw and write computer code. They can help with many kinds of office work at once.

Some economists worry that this could change jobs faster than people can retrain. Others point out that AI still makes mistakes and needs people to check its work, set goals and take responsibility.

In 2016, the computer scientist Geoffrey Hinton suggested that hospitals stop training radiologists, doctors who read scans, because AI would soon do it better. AI tools are now used in radiology, but demand for radiologists has kept growing. Predictions can go badly wrong in both directions.

What might stay human

Many experts think jobs that need care, trust, physical skill in messy places, or judgement about people will be hardest to automate. Nurses, electricians, teachers and plumbers are often on these lists. Jobs that involve working with AI may grow too, from people who train and test it to people who fix it when it goes wrong.

In India, millions of people work in IT and call centres, jobs that AI could change sharply. Governments, companies and schools are debating how to help people learn new skills.

The jobs you might do one day may not have names yet. And the strangest part? Some of the people building AI today are the most unsure of all about where it will lead.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Every new machine means fewer jobs overall.

Truth: History shows that new machines often destroy some jobs while creating others, and total work has usually grown. But the change can be very hard for the people whose jobs disappear, and AI may not follow the old pattern.
