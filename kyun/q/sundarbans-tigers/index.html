How do tigers live in a forest that floods twice a day?

Salty water, mud, and trees that breathe through their roots.

The short answer

The Sundarbans, shared by India and Bangladesh, is the world's largest mangrove forest.

Twice a day, tides flood the forest with salty water.

Mangroves protect the coast by slowing down storms and big waves.

Tigers live here by doing things most tigers rarely do. They swim across wide rivers, walk through sticky mud, catch fish and crabs, and drink salty water. They are the same Bengal tigers found in the rest of India, but they have learned to live in one of the hardest homes any big cat has.

Surprising but true

Mangrove roots stick UP out of the mud, like snorkels.

A forest made by rivers

The Sundarbans grows at the mouth of three great rivers: the Ganga, the Brahmaputra and the Meghna. Carrying mud all the way from the Himalayas, they built the largest delta on Earth. Where that mud meets the sea, hundreds of low islands are cut through by rivers and creeks.

Twice a day, as the tides rise, sea water pushes up the channels and floods much of the forest. As it falls, it uncovers mudflats full of crabs and mudskippers, fish that can hop about out of water. Some think the name comes from the sundari, a common mangrove tree here. Others say it means "beautiful forest" in Bengali.

Trees that breathe through snorkels

Mud has very little air, and salt kills most plants. Mangroves have found ways around both. Many send up pencil-like roots that poke out of the mud like snorkels, letting them take in air. Some push extra salt out through their leaves. Others have arching stilt roots that hold them steady in the soft mud as the tides come and go.

The roots also trap mud and slow down water. That turns the forest into a shield. When strong cyclones such as Aila in 2009 and Amphan in 2020 hit the coast, the mangroves softened the force of the storm surges for villages behind them.

How do mangroves live in salt water?Most trees die if you water them with seawater. Mangroves stand in it twice a day.

Tigers in the tide country

There are about 100 tigers on the Indian side and more on the Bangladeshi side. Counting them is hard, because the tide washes away pawprints twice a day. Scientists use camera traps tied to trees and recognise each tiger by its stripes.

The tigers hunt chital deer and wild boar, but also fish, crabs and monitor lizards. They swim between islands, sometimes several kilometres. Because there is little fresh water, they drink the brackish creek water.

Living with tigers

Millions of people live around the Sundarbans. Many fish, catch crabs or collect wild honey in the forest, and tiger attacks have long been a danger. In the 1980s, forest workers began wearing face masks on the backs of their heads, because tigers usually attack from behind. For a while the trick seemed to help.

Many people of the area, both Hindu and Muslim, honour Bonbibi, a forest goddess believed to protect those who enter the forest from tigers. Before going in, fishers and honey collectors often pray at her shrines.

An uncertain future

Rising seas are a threat. Some low islands, such as Ghoramara, have been shrinking as the sea eats away their shores. Salt water is spreading further into fields and ponds. Scientists warn that sea-level rise could drown parts of the forest unless the mangroves can build up mud fast enough to keep pace.

India's Sundarbans National Park became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987, and Bangladesh's Sundarbans in 1997. Protecting it is a shared job for two countries, and the tigers swim across the border without needing a passport.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Sundarbans tigers are a different species that has learned to live in water.

Truth: They are Bengal tigers, the same as tigers in the rest of India. They have simply adapted their habits, swimming between islands, hunting crabs and fish, and drinking brackish water.

Try this

Make mangrove snorkel roots. Fill a tray with wet sand or mud and stand a few drinking straws upright in it, sticking out by a few centimetres. Slowly pour in water to "high tide", just below the tops of the straws. Blow gently into one, and you'll see how air can still get down into the mud through a tube.
