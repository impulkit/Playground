What is a farmer's year like?

In much of India, a farmer's calendar starts when the first monsoon clouds arrive, and every week after that is decided by the sky.

The short answer

A farmer's year is shaped by seasons. In India there are two big ones. The kharif season begins with the monsoon in June or July, when rice, cotton and soybeans go into wet fields. The rabi season begins after the rains, around October, when wheat, mustard and chickpeas are sown for a spring harvest.

Between those two, there is ploughing, weeding, watering, spraying, harvesting and selling. Farmers also look after animals, mend tools and worry about prices. Some fit in a third, short summer crop called zaid, such as watermelons and cucumbers.

Surprising but true

An average farm in the USA is more than 150 times bigger than an average farm in India.

June: waiting for clouds

For a rice farmer in Odisha or Bengal, the year starts with a nervous wait. Seedlings are grown in a small, crowded nursery bed. When enough rain has fallen, the fields are flooded and churned into mud. Then the seedlings are pulled out and replanted by hand, a few at a time, in neat rows.

It is back-bending work, often done by whole families and hired workers together. If the rain comes two weeks late, everything shifts. If it comes too hard, young plants can drown. About half of India's farmland still depends mainly on rain rather than canals or wells, so the weather report matters more here than almost anywhere.

October to March: the winter crop

After the rice is cut, threshed and sold, the same field may be ready for wheat. In Punjab and Haryana, farmers sow wheat in November and watch it turn from green to gold by April. The cool nights help the grain fill out. A sudden hot spell in March can shrink the harvest.

This is also when a farmer's mind turns to money. Seeds, fertiliser, diesel and labour are paid for months before any crop is sold. Many farmers borrow, and a bad season can leave them in debt. That is why the government buys some crops at a fixed minimum price, and why prices are argued about so fiercely.

Harvest means festival

Across India, the end of a harvest is a reason to celebrate. Baisakhi in Punjab marks the wheat harvest. Pongal in Tamil Nadu, Bihu in Assam and Onam in Kerala all have roots in the farming calendar. Cooking new rice with jaggery and milk at Pongal is, quite literally, tasting the year's work.

In the USA, Thanksgiving grew out of autumn harvest feasts too.

An Iowa farmer's year

In the American Midwest, the rhythm is different. A corn farmer in Iowa plants in April and May, once the frozen ground has thawed. The fields grow all summer and are harvested between September and November by combine harvesters whose cutting bars are nearly as wide as a school bus is long. Winter is for fixing machines, planning and selling grain.

American farms are huge. In 2022 the average farm covered about 187 hectares. In India, the average is about one hectare, roughly the size of a football field and a half. So one American farmer with machines might work land that, in India, would be split into about 170 family farms.

Fewer farmers, more food

In 2025, a little over 40% of India's workers still earned their living mainly from farming, though that share has been falling for decades. In the USA, fewer than 2 in every 100 workers farm. Yet both countries grow far more food than they did 60 years ago, thanks partly to the Green Revolution, better seeds and irrigation.

Farmers have also learned to work together. Dairy cooperatives in Gujarat turned millions of small herds into one of the world's biggest milk businesses. The wheat in a roti on a Delhi dinner table was probably sown in November by someone who spent the winter watching the sky.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Farming is simple work that needs no special knowledge.

Truth: Farmers judge soil, seeds, weather, pests, water, machines and market prices all at once. Many use soil tests, weather apps and new seed varieties, and a wrong guess about the rain can cost a whole season.

Try this

Make a farm calendar for one year on a big sheet of paper. Ask a grandparent, a neighbour or a shopkeeper which fruits and vegetables are cheapest in each month, and write them in. You will see the farming seasons hiding inside your family's shopping.
