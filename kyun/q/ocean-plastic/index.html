Where does plastic in the ocean go?

A plastic bag has been found at the bottom of the deepest trench on Earth, almost 11 km down.

The short answer

Plastic reaches the ocean from rivers, beaches, drains, ships and fishing boats. Once in the sea, it goes several places. Some floats and is carried by ocean currents into slow, swirling areas called gyres, where it collects in patches. Some washes back onto beaches. Much of it sinks to the sea floor, even into the deepest trenches.

Plastic does not rot like food or leaves. Instead, sunlight and waves slowly break it into smaller and smaller pieces. Bits smaller than 5 mm are called microplastics. They are now found almost everywhere scientists look, from Arctic ice to deep-sea mud, and inside fish, birds and other animals.

Surprising but true

Only a small part of the plastic thought to enter the sea is found floating at the surface. Scientists sometimes call the rest the "missing plastic".

The garbage patches

Ocean currents move in giant loops. In the middle of each loop, the water turns slowly and floating things gather there. The biggest of these areas is the Great Pacific Garbage Patch, between Hawaii and California.

A 2018 study estimated the patch covers about 1.6 million square km, more than twice the size of Texas, or roughly half the size of India. But it is not a floating island. Most of it is scattered bits of plastic, often tiny, mixed through the top layer of water. By weight, a large share is old fishing nets and ropes. Similar patches exist in the Atlantic and Indian Oceans.

The missing plastic

A famous 2015 study estimated that about 8 million tonnes of plastic reached the sea from land in a single year, in 2010. Other estimates are higher or lower, because it is very hard to measure. But when scientists add up all the plastic they can find floating, it is only a small part of what should be there.

So where is the rest? Some has probably sunk, carried down by its own weight or inside the droppings of animals that ate it. Some is buried in beaches and coastal mud. Some has broken into pieces too small to catch in nets. In 2018, Japanese scientists reported a plastic bag spotted at the bottom of the Mariana Trench, almost 11 km down, near the deepest point in the ocean.

What it does to animals

Sea animals often mistake plastic for food. Sea turtles swallow plastic bags that drift like jellyfish, one of their favourite meals. Seabirds such as albatrosses pick up bright bits of plastic floating on the water and feed them to their chicks. On Midway Atoll, in the middle of the Pacific, dead albatross chicks have been found with stomachs full of bottle caps and lighters.

Seals, dolphins and whales can get tangled in lost fishing nets, which keep drifting and trapping animals for years. Scientists call these "ghost nets". Scientists are also studying how microplastics affect smaller animals, and people who eat seafood, but those effects are still not well understood.

Cleaning up

The best way to keep plastic out of the sea is to stop it getting there. That means using less, collecting waste properly and recycling more. India banned many single-use plastic items, such as plastic cutlery, straws and some thin bags, from July 2022. Several US states, starting with California, have banned thin plastic shopping bags.

People clean up too. From 2015, a lawyer named Afroz Shah and volunteers began clearing tonnes of rubbish from Versova Beach in Mumbai, week after week. In 2018, olive ridley turtles were seen hatching on the cleaned beach. The United Nations gave him its Champions of the Earth award, and his story is one of many in environment heroes.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Great Pacific Garbage Patch is a floating island of rubbish you could walk on.

Truth: Most of it is small pieces of plastic spread through the water, like flakes of pepper in soup, along with drifting fishing nets. You could sail through it without seeing a solid island, though nets and pieces show up everywhere.

Try this

Do a one-week plastic count. Keep a list of every piece of plastic thrown away at home, and sort it into bottles, wrappers, bags and other. At the end of the week, pick the biggest group and think of one way your family could use less of it.
