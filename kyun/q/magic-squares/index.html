What are magic squares?

Carved into a Jain temple wall at Khajuraho over a thousand years ago is a square of numbers that adds up to 34 in more than a dozen different ways.

The short answer

A magic square is a grid of numbers where every row, every column and both long diagonals add up to the same total. That total is called the magic sum.

The smallest one uses the numbers 1 to 9 in a 3 by 3 grid. The top row is 8, 1, 6. The middle row is 3, 5, 7. The bottom row is 4, 9, 2.

Add any row, column or diagonal and you get 15. There is only one way to do this, if you count turned and flipped versions as the same.

Bigger magic squares exist for every size except 2 by 2. Mathematicians even have a formula for the magic sum. For a square n numbers wide, using 1 up to n × n, the sum is n × (n × n + 1) ÷ 2.

Surprising but true

Benjamin Franklin, one of the founders of the United States, made magic squares to pass the time when he was bored in meetings.

The turtle from the river

The oldest known magic square comes from China. It is called the Lo Shu, or "scroll of the river Lo". According to legend, long ago a great flood struck China. As people made offerings to the river, a turtle crawled out with a strange pattern of dots on its shell. The dots formed the 3 by 3 square above.

The Lo Shu became important in Chinese traditions about harmony and the arrangement of homes. Nobody knows exactly how old it is, but it appears in Chinese writings from about 2,000 years ago.

Squares in Indian temples and books

At Khajuraho in Madhya Pradesh, the Parshvanatha temple, a Jain temple built in the 10th century, has a 4 by 4 square carved into its stone. Its magic sum is 34.

It's special even among magic squares. Besides the rows, columns and diagonals, many other groups of four add up to 34. They include the four corners, each 2 by 2 corner block and the "broken" diagonals that wrap around the edges. Squares like this are sometimes called "most perfect".

In 1356, the Indian mathematician Narayana Pandita wrote a book called Ganita Kaumudi with a long section explaining how to build magic squares of many sizes. Magic squares also turn up in other Indian temples, on charms and in old manuscripts.

A square in a famous engraving

In 1514, the German artist Albrecht Dürer made an engraving called Melencolia I. It shows a gloomy winged figure surrounded by tools and puzzles. On the wall behind is a 4 by 4 magic square with a sum of 34.

Look at the bottom row and you'll find the numbers 15 and 14 side by side, making 1514, the year Dürer made the picture. Copies of the engraving are kept in museums around the world.

A founding father's hobby

The American printer, scientist and statesman Benjamin Franklin loved magic squares. In a letter, he admitted that as a young clerk of the Pennsylvania Assembly, he made them to pass the time when debates grew boring.

He built an 8 by 8 square with extra tricks. Half of each row adds up to half the magic sum, and bent rows shaped like a V also add up to the total. It is still known as Franklin's square.

Squares and Sudoku

Magic squares are cousins of other number grids. In a Latin square, each symbol appears once in every row and column, which is the idea behind Sudoku. Magic squares go further by making the numbers add up.

Today, mathematicians study them as puzzles about patterns and counting. Nobody yet knows exactly how many different 6 by 6 magic squares exist, though computers have estimated the number. For the 5 by 5 size, there are 275,305,224, found by computer in 1973.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Magic squares are a modern puzzle invented for puzzle books.

Truth: Magic squares are well over a thousand years old. They were known in ancient China, studied by Indian and Arab mathematicians, carved into temples and painted into European art long before puzzle books existed.

Try this

Draw a 3 by 3 grid. Put 5 in the centre. Try to place the other numbers from 1 to 9 so that every row, column and diagonal adds up to 15. Hint: the even numbers go in the corners. When you've cracked it, try turning or flipping your square to find other versions.
