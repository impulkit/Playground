Who is Garuda?

Garuda, the giant bird who carries the god Vishnu, is also the national symbol of Indonesia and Thailand, and the name of an Indian Air Force commando unit.

The short answer

Garuda is a mighty bird from Hindu, Buddhist and Jain stories. He's usually shown with a human body, an eagle's beak, wings and talons. In Hindu belief he is the king of birds and the vahana, or carrier, of the god Vishnu. Paintings show Vishnu and the goddess Lakshmi riding on his back.

Garuda is famous for his strength, speed and loyalty. He's also the great enemy of snakes. His story appears in the Mahabharata and the Puranas, and he's still honoured in temples across India and Southeast Asia.

Surprising but true

Indonesia, the country with the largest Muslim population in the world, has Garuda on its national emblem and named its national airline after him.

A bet, a mother and the drink of life

The story begins with two sisters, Vinata and Kadru, both wives of the sage Kashyapa. Kadru became the mother of a thousand snakes, the nagas. Vinata laid two eggs. In some tellings, impatient, she broke one open early, and out came Aruna, half formed, who became the charioteer of the Sun.

The sisters made a bet about the colour of a heavenly horse's tail. Kadru cheated, and Vinata lost. As punishment she had to serve Kadru and the snakes. When Garuda hatched, blazing so brightly that the gods were frightened, he wanted to free his mother. The snakes set a price: bring us amrit, the drink of immortality.

The great heist

Amrit was guarded by the gods in heaven. Garuda flew there, battled the guards, put out a ring of fire by carrying water in his beak, and slipped past a spinning wheel of blades. He seized the pot.

On the way back, Vishnu was so impressed that he offered Garuda a gift. Garuda agreed to become Vishnu's carrier, and Vishnu promised him immortality. Garuda gave the snakes the amrit and freed his mother. But before the snakes could drink, Indra snatched it back. The snakes licked the grass where it had rested, and the sharp grass split their tongues. That, the story says, is why snakes have forked tongues.

A bird across Asia

Garuda flew far beyond India. As traders and teachers travelled to Southeast Asia, his stories went too. Indonesia, the country with the most Muslims in the world, uses the Garuda Pancasila as its national emblem, and its national airline is Garuda Indonesia. The emblem stands for Indonesia's national ideals, not religion.

Thailand's royal emblem shows Garuda, called Khrut, and it appears on government papers. In Mongolia, the capital Ulaanbaatar uses a Garuda in its emblem. In Indonesia's Bali, the giant Garuda Wisnu Kencana statue towers about 120 m high including its base, taller than the Statue of Liberty.

Garuda in India today

In India, the Brahminy kite, a reddish-brown bird with a white head, is often linked with Garuda, and some people bow when they see one. The Indian Air Force named its special forces the Garud Commando Force. The Garuda Purana, one of the eighteen major Puranas, is often read after a death in Hindu families.

Temples like the Vishnu temples of South India often have a Garuda shrine facing the main deity. The 'Garuda Seva' festival at temples such as Tirumala draws huge crowds. In Buddhist stories, garudas are a whole race of giant birds who live on mountains. Real birds of prey can't carry gods, but some come close to Garuda's powers: a soaring eagle can spot a rabbit from more than a kilometre away.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Garuda and Jatayu, the bird in the Ramayana, are the same.

Truth: They are different. Jatayu is the vulture-king who fought Ravana to save Sita. In some tellings Jatayu is the son of Aruna, Garuda's brother, which makes Garuda his uncle.

Try this

Watch birds near your home or school for ten minutes and look for one soaring in circles without flapping, such as a black kite or an eagle. Sketch its wing shape. Then draw your own Garuda, mixing that bird's wings with a human body, as temple sculptors did.
