What is Peru known for?

The potato in your samosa first grew in the mountains of Peru, where farmers today still plant thousands of different kinds.

The short answer

Peru is a country on the Pacific coast of South America. Few countries pack in such different lands. A narrow desert runs along the coast, where it hardly ever rains. Behind it rise the Andes, with peaks over 6,000 metres. On the far side, the mountains drop into the Amazon rainforest, which covers more than half of Peru.

Peru was the heart of the Inca Empire, the largest empire in the Americas before Europeans arrived. It is known for Machu Picchu, the mysterious Nazca Lines, llamas and alpacas, and the potato. Many people still speak Quechua, the language of the Inca, alongside Spanish. In the misty cloud forests lives the spectacled bear, the only bear in South America. Paddington Bear, in the children's books, comes from "darkest Peru".

Surprising but true

The Inca ran a huge empire without any known writing. They kept records on quipus, bundles of knotted strings.

The city in the clouds

Machu Picchu sits on a ridge high above a river gorge, about 2,400 metres up. It was built around 1450, probably as an estate for the Inca emperor Pachacuti. Its stone walls fit together so tightly, with no mortar, that you cannot slide a knife blade between the blocks. That tight fit helps them survive earthquakes.

The Inca left it about a century later. Spanish soldiers never found it, so it was not destroyed. In 1911, the American explorer Hiram Bingham was led there by local people, and his photographs made it world famous. Today visitor numbers are limited to protect it.

An empire on strings

From their capital, Cusco, the Inca ruled lands stretching about 4,000 kilometres along the Andes. They built a road network of about 40,000 kilometres, with rope bridges over gorges and rest houses along the way. Relay runners carried messages and could cover hundreds of kilometres a day, a bit like a Roman road system run by sprinters.

The Inca had no known writing. Instead, officials kept records on quipus, cords with knots tied in them. The kind of knot and its position showed numbers, using a base-ten system, an important chapter in the history of counting. Quipus recorded taxes, harvests and people. Some may also have stored stories, but nobody has been able to read them.

Potatoes, thousands of them

Farmers in the Andes around Lake Titicaca tamed the wild potato roughly 8,000 years ago. Andean families still grow thousands of kinds, in purple, red, yellow and blue, some knobbly, some long and thin. The International Potato Center in Lima keeps a seed bank of thousands of varieties so they will never be lost.

Andean farmers also invented a way to freeze-dry potatoes. They leave them out on frosty nights, let them thaw in the sun, then squash out the water by treading on them. The dried result, chuño, can be stored for years. The Spanish took potatoes to Europe in the 1500s, and from there they spread to India and the rest of the world.

Lines in the desert

On the dry plains near Nazca, people living about 2,000 years ago scraped away dark stones to reveal pale ground beneath. They made straight lines kilometres long and huge pictures of a monkey, a spider and a hummingbird. Because it barely rains or blows hard there, the lines have survived for centuries.

Nobody knows for sure why they were made. They may have been paths walked in ceremonies, perhaps linked to water, the most precious thing in a desert.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The explorer Hiram Bingham discovered Machu Picchu in 1911.

Truth: Local farmers knew about Machu Picchu, and some were living and farming on its terraces. A local man guided Bingham there. Bingham's photographs made the site famous around the world.

Try this

Make a quipu. Tie several pieces of string to one longer cord. Use knots to record something, such as how many glasses of water each family member drinks in a day. Use one kind of knot for tens and another for ones, and see if a friend can read your record.
