How do baby turtles find the sea?

They hatch at night, alone, on a beach. Nobody shows them the way.

The short answer

Baby turtles head for the brightest direction. On a natural beach at night, that is almost always the sea, because the open water reflects moonlight and starlight, while the land behind is dark with dunes and trees.

That is why street lights and buildings can confuse them. Hatchlings may crawl towards a lit road instead of the water. So some beaches switch off or dim their lights in nesting season.

Once in the water, the babies swim against the waves to reach the open sea. After that, scientists think, they steer by Earth's magnetism, and remember the magnetic "signature" of their home beach for years.

Surprising but true

Rushikulya and Gahirmatha in Odisha host one of the biggest turtle nesting gatherings on Earth.

The great arrival

Each winter, on a few beaches in Odisha, something astonishing happens. Over a few nights, huge numbers of olive ridley turtles crawl ashore together to dig nests and lay eggs. In good years, lakhs of turtles arrive at Rushikulya and Gahirmatha. This mass nesting is called an arribada, Spanish for "arrival".

Each mother digs a hole with her back flippers, lays about 100 eggs the size of ping-pong balls, covers them with sand and returns to the sea. She never sees her babies. About 45 to 60 days later, the hatchlings dig their way out together, usually at night when the sand is cool and fewer hunters are about.

A dangerous dash

The race to the water is short but deadly. Crabs, birds, dogs and jackals wait on the beach, and fish wait in the shallows. Of every thousand hatchlings, perhaps only one survives to grow up.

The survivors vanish into the open ocean for years. Scientists call this time the "lost years", because the babies are so hard to follow. Many seem to drift with rafts of floating seaweed, where they find food and shelter.

In Chennai, volunteers have walked the beaches at night every nesting season since 1988, moving eggs from busy beaches to safe hatcheries. Thousands of students have joined in.

A built-in compass

How does a turtle find its way across an ocean, and back to the same coast years later? In laboratory tanks in the USA, scientists placed hatchlings inside coils that could change the magnetic field around them. The babies changed direction to match, as if they were reading a map.

The leading idea is that each stretch of coast has its own magnetic pattern, and a young turtle remembers the pattern of the beach where it hatched. This is strongly supported, but scientists are still studying it.

Ancient travellers in a modern sea

Sea turtles have swum the oceans since the age of the dinosaurs, alongside the sea reptiles of that time. Today, all seven kinds face trouble. Some drown in fishing nets, so many trawlers now fit trapdoors that let turtles escape. Others swallow plastic bags, which look a lot like jellyfish, a favourite food of some kinds, such as leatherbacks.

Warmer sand is a new worry, since it produces more females. Lights are another. Along the coast of Florida, many towns now use dim amber lamps near nesting beaches, a colour hatchlings notice less. On the darkest nights, around the new moon in the Moon's cycle, the glow from a single hotel can pull a whole nest of babies the wrong way. Turning it off costs very little and gives them a clear run to the sea.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A turtle can crawl out of its shell.

Truth: The shell is part of the turtle's skeleton. Its backbone and ribs are joined to the inside of it, so a turtle can no more leave its shell than you can leave your ribs.

Try this

Lay a compass flat on a table and watch where the needle settles. Now slowly bring a fridge magnet near it and see the needle swing. Earth itself acts like a giant, weak magnet, and scientists think young turtles can feel it, a bit like a built-in compass.
