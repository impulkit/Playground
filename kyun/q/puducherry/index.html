Why does Puducherry feel French?

In Puducherry, street signs still say "rue", police officers have worn red French-style caps, and some families still hold French passports.

The short answer

Puducherry feels French because France ruled it, on and off, for nearly 300 years. French traders arrived in 1674, and the town became the capital of French India. When India won independence from Britain in 1947, Puducherry stayed French. It only joined India in 1954.

The French left their mark in the town's layout, buildings, food and even its police uniforms. In the old French Quarter, streets run in a neat grid, lined with yellow and white colonial houses with tall shuttered windows and bougainvillea spilling over the walls. Signs carry French street names. Bakeries sell croissants beside idli and vada.

Surprising but true

Puducherry is a union territory made of four separate pieces, scattered across three different states, hundreds of kilometres apart.

A French town in Tamil country

In 1674, François Martin set up a French trading post on the Coromandel Coast, in what is now Tamil Nadu, about 150 km south of today's Chennai. The British were already based in Chennai, then called Madras, so the two rivals were close neighbours. The French East India Company traded cloth, spices and indigo, and Pondichéry grew into a fortified town. The British and the French fought over it several times, and the British captured and largely destroyed it in 1761. It was handed back and rebuilt.

French India eventually had five main settlements: Pondichéry, Karaikal, Mahe, Yanam and Chandernagore in Bengal. They were tiny compared with British India. Chandernagore joined India in 1950 and later became part of West Bengal.

Two towns, one canal

The old town was divided by a canal. On the seaward side was the "White Town", where French officials and families lived, with wide planned streets in a grid. On the inland side was the "Black Town" or Tamil Quarter, where Tamil merchants, weavers and workers lived, with houses built around courtyards and with pillared verandas.

Today, people of all backgrounds live on both sides, and the names have changed. But you can still see the difference in the buildings. A seafront promenade, the Goubert Avenue, runs along the Bay of Bengal, and on many evenings it's closed to traffic so people can walk.

Joining India

After 1947, India asked France to hand over its settlements. France held talks with local leaders, and on 1 November 1954, Puducherry, Karaikal, Mahe and Yanam were transferred to India in practice. The formal treaty was ratified in 1962. Unlike Goa, which Portugal refused to give up until 1961, the French handover happened peacefully.

The four parts stayed together as one union territory, even though they are far apart. Mahe is on the Kerala coast, Yanam is in Andhra Pradesh, and Karaikal and Puducherry are in Tamil Nadu. People there could choose French or Indian citizenship, and some families kept French passports. The town was renamed Puducherry, its Tamil name meaning "new town", in 2006.

Ashram and Auroville

In 1910, Sri Aurobindo, a revolutionary who had become a spiritual teacher, came to Pondicherry to escape arrest by the British in Bengal. He stayed and founded an ashram, joined by a French woman, Mirra Alfassa, known as "the Mother".

In 1968, she founded Auroville nearby, an experimental township meant for people from all countries to live together. At its centre stands the Matrimandir, a huge golden globe used for silent meditation. Around it, residents from dozens of countries have planted forests on what was once dry, bare land, turning the red earth green.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Puducherry was a British colony, like the rest of India.

Truth: Most of India was ruled by the British, but Puducherry and a few other small towns were ruled by France for nearly 300 years. They only joined India in 1954, seven years after independence.
