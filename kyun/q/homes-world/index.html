What kinds of homes do people live in?

In the Australian opal-mining town of Coober Pedy, many people live in homes dug into the hillsides to escape summer heat above 40 °C.

The short answer

People live in an enormous variety of homes, from apartments in skyscrapers to tents, houseboats, mud huts, snow houses and caves. A home's shape and materials usually depend on the local climate, the materials close at hand, how much money people have, and whether they stay in one place or move around.

In hot places, homes often have thick walls, small windows and shady courtyards. In cold places, they hold in warmth. In places that flood, they may stand on stilts. In crowded cities, people live stacked on top of each other in tall buildings.

Surprising but true

After the 2001 earthquake in Gujarat, many traditional round mud houses called bhungas in Kutch were left standing while concrete buildings nearby collapsed.

Homes made from the land

For most of history, people built with what they could find nearby: wood, stone, mud, bamboo, grass, animal skins or snow. In Kutch, Gujarat, people build round houses called bhungas, with thick mud walls and cone-shaped thatched roofs. They stay cool in desert heat, and their round shape helps them resist earthquakes. After the 2001 Gujarat earthquake, many bhungas survived when stronger-looking concrete buildings fell.

In Assam, the Mising people build houses on bamboo stilts called chang ghar to stay dry when the Brahmaputra floods. In Kerala, old homes have sloping tiled roofs to shed monsoon rain, and some families live on houseboats in the backwaters.

Homes for the cold

Inuit in the Arctic built igloos from blocks of packed snow, mainly as shelters during winter hunting trips. Snow is full of trapped air, so it keeps warmth in. With people and a small oil lamp inside, an igloo can be around freezing while the air outside is −40 °C.

In Mongolia, many herding families live in round felt tents called gers, also known as yurts. They are warm in fierce winters and can be taken apart and loaded onto animals or trucks when the family moves to new pasture.

Homes in cliffs and underground

About 800 years ago, the Ancestral Puebloan people built villages of stone rooms tucked into cliffs at Mesa Verde, in what is now Colorado, USA. The overhanging rock sheltered them from sun and snow.

In Coober Pedy, Australia, many people live in "dugouts" carved into hillsides. Underground, the temperature stays comfortable all year while summer air outside can top 40 °C. In Matmata, Tunisia, families dug homes around deep pits in the ground.

City homes

Today more than half the world's people live in cities, many in apartments. In Mumbai, some families live in chawls, older buildings with rows of single rooms opening onto a shared corridor. Others live in high-rise towers. In the United States, many families live in separate houses with their own gardens, especially in suburbs.

Millions of people around the world live in informal settlements, built by residents themselves without official permission. They are often crowded and lack clean water or drains, but they are also full of families and small businesses.

A roof and more

New homes keep appearing too, from houses made of recycled shipping containers to apartments built by 3D printers.

A home is more than walls and a roof. It is where families eat, sleep, celebrate and store their memories. Who lives inside also varies, from one person to large joint families, as you can read in families around the world. Architects today are borrowing ideas from traditional homes, like thick earth walls and shady courtyards, to build houses that stay cool without much electricity.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Inuit people lived in igloos all year round.

Truth: Igloos were mostly used as winter shelters, especially while travelling or hunting. Many Inuit lived in houses of stone, whalebone and turf in winter and in skin tents in summer. Today most live in modern houses.
