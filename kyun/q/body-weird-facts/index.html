What are the weirdest facts about the human body?

You are about a centimetre taller when you wake up than when you go to bed, and astronauts can grow several centimetres taller in space.

The short answer

Your body is full of strange true facts. You were born with about 300 bones but now have fewer. You shrink a little each day and grow back each night. There is a hole in your vision you never notice. Your body gives off about as much heat as an old 100-watt light bulb, and you share it with trillions of tiny passengers.

None of this is harmful. It is just how bodies work. Many of these oddities are leftovers from our animal ancestors, or clever tricks the body uses to keep running smoothly. Here are some of the weirdest.

Surprising but true

The DNA inside just one of your cells would be about 2 metres long if you could unwind it and stretch it out.

You shrink every day

Between the bones of your spine sit soft, spongy discs, a bit like jelly-filled cushions. During the day, standing and walking squash them slightly. At night, lying flat, they soak up fluid and puff up again. That's why most people are about a centimetre taller in the morning.

In space, there's no gravity pressing down at all. NASA has found that astronauts can grow up to about 3% taller, several centimetres, while they are in orbit. Spacesuits are made with extra room for this. When they return to Earth, they shrink back.

Bones that join up

A newborn has about 300 bones and bits of bone, many still partly soft cartilage. As children grow, some of them fuse. The skull starts as several plates with soft gaps, so a baby's head can squeeze safely through birth. By adulthood, most people have 206 bones.

The longest is the thighbone, about a quarter of your height. The smallest, the stirrup bone deep in your ear, is about 3 millimetres long, smaller than a grain of rice. Your fingerprints are unique, yet koalas have prints so like ours that experts have found them hard to tell apart under a microscope.

Why do koalas sleep so much?A koala can sleep or rest for up to 20 hours a day, and its fingerprints are so like ours that they could confuse a detective.

A blind spot in each eye

At the back of each eye, all the nerve fibres bundle together and leave for the brain. That spot has no light sensors at all, so you can't see anything that falls on it. Yet you never see a hole in the world. Your brain quietly fills the gap using what's around it and what the other eye sees.

You also blink around 15 to 20 times a minute. Your brain hides those blinks too, so the world doesn't keep going dark.

Two metres of DNA in every cell

Almost every cell in your body holds a full copy of your DNA, the chemical instructions for building you. Unwound and stretched out, the DNA in one cell would be about 2 metres long, taller than a door. It fits because it is wound tightly around tiny protein spools, like thread on bobbins.

You have trillions of cells, so all your DNA together would be unimaginably long. Your heart helps feed all those cells, beating about 100,000 times a day.

You are not alone

Scientists estimated in 2016 that an average adult carries about 38 trillion bacteria, most of them in the gut. That is roughly the same number as human cells in the body, about 30 trillion. Most of these bacteria help, by digesting food and keeping harmful germs out.

You glow, a tiny bit

In 2009, scientists in Japan used super-sensitive cameras to show that the human body gives off a very faint light. It is about a thousand times too weak for our eyes to see, and it comes from chemical reactions in skin cells. The face glowed most, and the glow changed through the day. And the strangest part? Deep in the ocean, many animals make light bright enough to hunt with, a trick called bioluminescence.

Myth or fact?

Myth: People use only 10% of their brains.

Truth: Brain scans show that we use virtually all of our brain, though not every part at the same moment. Even during sleep, many areas stay busy. The 10% idea has no scientific basis.

Try this

Find your blind spot. Draw a small cross and, about 8 cm to its right, a dot on a card. Close your left eye, stare at the cross with your right eye and slowly move the card toward your face. At one point the dot will vanish, because it lands on the part of your eye with no light sensors.
