Does the left brain do maths and the right brain art?

You may have heard that you're "left-brained" or "right-brained". When scientists scanned over a thousand brains, they found no such thing.

The short answer

No, not in the simple way the popular idea suggests. Your brain does have two halves, called hemispheres, and they are not identical. In most people, the left hemisphere does more of the work of language, and the right does more of the work of recognising faces and judging space.

But maths isn't a left-brain job, and art isn't a right-brain one. Both use many areas spread across both sides, connected by a thick bridge of nerve fibres. And no one is "left-brained" or "right-brained". Everyone uses their whole brain.

Surprising but true

Each side of your brain controls the opposite side of your body, so your right hand is steered mostly by your left hemisphere.

Two halves, crossed wires

Look at a model of the brain and you'll see a deep groove running front to back, dividing it into left and right. Each half controls movement and feeling on the opposite side of the body. If you wave your right hand, signals mostly come from your left hemisphere.

The two halves talk constantly through the corpus callosum, a band of roughly 200 million nerve fibres. Information flashes across it in a fraction of a second, so the two sides work as one team. Our article on how the brain works explains how neurons pass these messages.

A clue from 1861

In 1861, a French doctor named Paul Broca examined a patient who could understand speech but could say almost nothing except one syllable, "tan". After the man died, Broca found damage in a small area on the left side of his brain. Other patients showed the same pattern.

This was early evidence that, for most people, key language areas sit in the left hemisphere. Today we know this is true for about 95 percent of right-handed people and most left-handed people too.

The split-brain experiments

In the 1960s, some people with severe epilepsy had their corpus callosum cut to stop seizures spreading. The American scientist Roger Sperry and his team tested these "split-brain" patients.

If a word was shown only to a patient's left hemisphere, they could say it. If it was shown only to the right, they couldn't name it, but their left hand could pick out the matching object. Each half seemed to have its own skills. Sperry won a Nobel Prize in 1981 for this work.

How a myth was born

Newspapers, magazines and self-help books took these careful findings and stretched them. Soon people were told the left brain was logical and the right brain creative, and that each person had a "dominant" side that shaped their personality.

In 2013, scientists at the University of Utah looked at brain scans from over 1,000 people. They found that particular networks did lean to one side, but individuals didn't use one hemisphere more than the other overall. Nobody was left-brained or right-brained. It is a good example of how a real finding can be stretched into a false story.

Hands and halves

About one person in ten is left-handed. Leonardo da Vinci, for instance, wrote his notes from right to left in mirror writing. Left-handers are a little more likely than right-handers to have language spread over both hemispheres, or mainly on the right. But being left-handed doesn't make someone more artistic, and being right-handed doesn't make someone better at maths.

Brains are also flexible. When a young child's brain is injured on one side, the other side can often take over jobs like language. Your memory, your maths and your music all live in networks that stretch across both halves of your head.

Myth or fact?

Myth: People are either left-brained (logical, good at maths) or right-brained (creative, artistic).

Truth: Brain scans show that people use both halves of their brain about equally. Some tasks lean more on one side, but logical and creative thinking both use networks spread across the whole brain.

Try this

Find out your "sides". Kick a ball and see which foot you use. Make a small hole with your hands, look at a far object through it with both eyes, then close one eye at a time. The eye that still sees the object is your dominant eye. Compare with your writing hand. They don't always match.
