Why do Tanjore paintings shine with gold?

Tanjore painters press real gold onto their gods, and that gold can keep its shine for hundreds of years.

The short answer

Tanjore paintings shine because they use real gold. Artists press thin sheets of gold leaf onto raised parts of the picture: a god's crown, jewellery, the pillars of a temple arch. Then they add glittering glass beads and coloured stones.

The style comes from Thanjavur (once called Tanjore), a temple town in Tamil Nadu. These paintings were made to be worshipped as well as admired. In a dim prayer room, lit by an oil lamp, the gold glows and seems to flicker. That was the point. The god in the picture looks as if it is shining with its own light.

Surprising but true

The gold in a Tanjore painting is beaten so thin that a sheet of it is hundreds of times thinner than a sheet of printer paper.

A town of temples and kings

Thanjavur had been a great centre of art since the Cholas built the Brihadeeswarar temple there about a thousand years ago. The painting style we call Tanjore grew much later, from the 1600s to the 1800s. By then Thanjavur was ruled by a Maratha royal family, and kings like Serfoji II supported musicians, scholars and painters.

Many of the painters came from families of craftsmen who had worked on temples and palaces for generations. They painted Hindu gods and goddesses, saints and scenes from sacred stories, for temples and for the prayer rooms of wealthy homes.

Building a painting in layers

A Tanjore painting starts with a plank of wood. A piece of cloth is glued on top, then coated with a smooth paste of chalk and gum and rubbed until flat. The artist sketches the design, usually with a big central figure.

Next comes the clever part. Using a thicker paste, the painter builds up the crown, necklaces and borders so they stand out from the surface, a little like icing on a cake. Glass beads and stones are pressed in. Only then does the gold go on, followed by the paint.

Why gold, and not paint

Gold has a special property that paint does not. It barely reacts with air or water, so it does not rust or turn dark the way silver and copper do. That is why gold coins dug up after many centuries can still gleam.

Gold can also be hammered extraordinarily thin. Craftsmen beat it between layers of leather or paper until a lump the size of a pea spreads across a large sheet. A little gold covers a lot of painting, and it reflects lamplight beautifully.

Big eyes, bright colours

Tanjore figures are easy to recognise. Faces are round and gentle, with large, almond-shaped eyes. The main god sits in the centre, much larger than the people and animals around it, because size shows importance.

The most loved subject is Krishna as a chubby baby, often holding a ball of butter. Colours are flat and strong: deep red, green, blue and white, with dark outlines. Unlike the shading in a Raja Ravi Varma painting, there is little attempt to look like real life. The aim is to feel holy.

Still made today

Families in and around Thanjavur still make these paintings. "Thanjavur Paintings" is protected by a geographical indication, a legal label that says real ones must come from the region and follow the traditional method. Today many go into homes as gifts at weddings or housewarmings, often hung where a family prays, sometimes beside a small bronze image cast in the same part of Tamil Nadu.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The gold in Tanjore paintings is just yellow paint.

Truth: Traditional Tanjore paintings use real gold leaf, hammered into sheets thinner than tissue paper. Cheaper modern copies sometimes use imitation foil, which is why experts look closely at how the gold has aged.

Try this

Draw a simple crown or necklace on card. Trace the outline thickly with craft glue, let it dry until it is firm and raised, then cover it with kitchen foil, gently pressing the foil around the bumps. Shine a torch from the side and watch how the raised parts catch the light.
