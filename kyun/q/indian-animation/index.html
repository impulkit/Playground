How is animation made in India?

In 2025 an animated film about the god Narasimha earned around ₹300 crore in cinemas, far more than any Indian cartoon film before it.

The short answer

Animation in India is made the same way as anywhere else, with a mix of drawing, computer work and a great deal of patience. A team writes a story, sketches it as a comic-like storyboard and designs the characters. Then animators bring them to life frame by frame, either drawn by hand or built and moved inside a computer.

India has a large animation industry. Its studios make home-grown shows like Chhota Bheem and Motu Patlu, films based on myths and epics, and work for studios in the USA, Europe and Japan. Many of the artists working on cartoons watched by children in America sit in offices in Mumbai or Hyderabad.

Surprising but true

Many artists in Mumbai, Hyderabad, Pune and Chennai spend their days working on Hollywood films and shows, so Indian hands have shaped cartoons that children everywhere watch.

From flip book to film

Every animated film is an illusion. Your eye sees a quick series of still pictures and your brain blends them into smooth motion, the trick explained in how movies move. Films usually show 24 frames each second.

In traditional 2D animation, artists draw key poses first, such as the start and end of a jump. Then other artists draw the in-between pictures. In 3D animation, artists build a digital model of a character, give it a skeleton of joints, a step called rigging, and move it pose by pose on a computer. Lighting and shading are added, and powerful computers "render" each finished frame. A single frame of a detailed film can take hours to render.

Early days

India's animation history goes back to the early 1900s, but one of its best-loved early works is Ek Anek Aur Ekta, a short film from 1974 shown again and again on Doordarshan. It used a bundle of sticks to show that people are stronger together, and its song is still remembered by many parents.

In 1992, Indian and Japanese artists worked together on Ramayana: The Legend of Prince Rama, a full-length animated film. The Indian animator Ram Mohan, often called the father of Indian animation, was one of its leaders.

What is the Ramayana?A prince, a princess, a demon king and an army of monkeys. The Ramayana has been retold for more than 2,000 years, from Indian villages to Thai palaces.

Bheem and friends

The big change came in the 2000s, when cable channels needed lots of children's shows. In 2005, the animated film Hanuman became a surprise hit in cinemas. In 2008, a Hyderabad studio called Green Gold Animation launched Chhota Bheem, about a strong, kind boy in the village of Dholakpur. It became one of the most popular children's shows in India.

Other characters followed, like Motu Patlu, based on an old comic, and Little Singham. These shows were cheaper to make than many foreign cartoons and spoke directly to Indian children, with laddoos, festivals and village life.

Working for the world

Indian studios also do a lot of work for others. They might animate scenes, add visual effects, paint backgrounds or clean up 3D models for films made in Hollywood or Europe. Some Indian-owned effects companies have worked on films that won Oscars for visual effects.

The government calls this the AVGC sector, short for animation, visual effects, gaming and comics, and has set up training programmes because the industry needs more skilled artists.

The big screen breakthrough

For years, Indian animated films struggled in cinemas, where big Hollywood cartoons won most of the audience. Then, in July 2025, Mahavatar Narsimha, a 3D film about Vishnu's half-lion, half-man avatar, opened with little fanfare and kept growing by word of mouth. It earned around ₹300 crore or more, far more than any Indian animated film before.

Its success persuaded many producers that Indian audiences will pay for home-made animation. Studios are now working on more feature films, often drawing on the Ramayana, the Mahabharata and folk tales, and on new stories too.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Animated films are only for small children.

Truth: Many animated films are made for older children, teenagers and adults. In India, Mahavatar Narsimha drew families of all ages, and around the world animation is used for drama, history and even news.

Try this

Make a flip book. Take a small notepad or a stack of 20 sticky notes. In the bottom corner of each page, draw a ball in a slightly different place, so that it falls, bounces and rises again. Flip the pages fast with your thumb and watch the ball move.
