Who was Galileo?

In January 1610, an Italian professor pointed a home-made telescope at Jupiter and saw four tiny dots that changed how people saw the universe.

The short answer

Galileo Galilei was an Italian scientist who lived about 400 years ago. He was one of the first people to study the sky with a telescope. He found mountains on the Moon, four moons orbiting Jupiter, countless stars in the Milky Way and spots on the Sun.

He also did careful experiments with rolling balls and swinging weights, and showed that maths could describe how things move. His support for the idea that the Earth goes round the Sun brought him into conflict with the Catholic Church, and he spent his last years under house arrest.

Surprising but true

Galileo did not invent the telescope. He heard about a Dutch spyglass in 1609 and built a much better one himself within weeks.

A swinging lamp

Galileo was born in Pisa in 1564. His father, a musician, wanted him to become a doctor, but Galileo preferred mathematics. An old story says that as a student he watched a lamp swinging in Pisa Cathedral and timed it with his pulse. He noticed that big swings and small swings took about the same time. The story may be polished in the retelling, but he did study pendulums, and his idea was later used to make accurate clocks.

Another famous story says he dropped two balls of different weights from the Leaning Tower of Pisa to show they land together. He may never have done this exactly. He did show, by rolling balls down slopes, that heavy and light objects speed up at the same rate if air does not slow them down.

A new window on the sky

In 1609, Galileo heard that a Dutch spectacle-maker had made a tube that made distant things look closer. He worked out how it must be built and ground his own lenses. Soon he had a telescope that magnified about 20 times. To learn how telescopes work, he had to experiment for himself.

Pointing it upwards, he saw that the Moon was not smooth but covered in mountains and craters. In January 1610 he spotted four moons circling Jupiter. He later saw that Venus goes through phases like our Moon, which only makes sense if Venus goes round the Sun. He rushed his findings into a short book, The Starry Messenger.

Trouble with the Church

At the time, most people, including Church leaders, believed the Earth sat still at the centre of the universe. The Polish astronomer Copernicus had suggested in 1543 that the Earth moves round the Sun. Galileo's discoveries convinced him Copernicus was right.

In 1616, Church authorities told him not to teach the idea as fact. In 1632 he published a book that argued for it anyway. The next year he was put on trial by the Roman Inquisition, a Church court. He was made to say publicly that he rejected the idea. He spent the rest of his life under house arrest at his villa in Arcetri, near Florence, where he kept working and writing, even after he went blind.

A legend says that after the trial he muttered "And yet it moves", meaning the Earth. There is no record of this from the time.

Galileo's legacy

Galileo's habit of testing ideas with experiments and measurements is one reason he is often called a father of modern science. He lived during the late Renaissance, when Italian thinkers and artists were questioning old ideas.

In 1992, Pope John Paul II formally accepted that the Church had been wrong to condemn him. NASA named a spacecraft after him. Launched in 1989, it orbited Jupiter for nearly eight years and studied the four moons Galileo found. They are still called the Galilean moons: Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto. Two of his telescopes survive at the Museo Galileo in Florence.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Galileo invented the telescope.

Truth: The first telescopes were made by spectacle-makers in the Netherlands in 1608. Galileo built far better ones and was among the first to point them at the sky and carefully record what he saw.

Try this

Tie a small weight, like a metal washer or an eraser, to a piece of string about 50 cm long. Hang it from a table edge and time 10 swings. Now make the swings smaller, then try a heavier weight. The time hardly changes, but a shorter string swings faster. Galileo noticed this, and it later made accurate pendulum clocks possible.
