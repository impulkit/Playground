How were mummies made?

Egyptian embalmers pulled out the brain through the nose with a hook, but left the heart exactly where it was.

The short answer

A mummy is a body that has been dried and preserved so that it does not rot. Ancient Egyptians made mummies because they believed a person's spirit needed a body in the afterlife. The best-known method took about 70 days.

Embalmers removed the brain and most of the inner organs, then packed the body in natron, a natural salt, for about 40 days to dry it out. After that, they rubbed it with oils and resins and wrapped it in many layers of linen, with lucky charms called amulets tucked between them.

Surprising but true

The world's oldest known artificial mummies were made not in Egypt but by the Chinchorro people of the Atacama Desert in Chile and Peru, about 7,000 years ago.

Mummies made by sand

The first Egyptian mummies were made by accident. Over 5,000 years ago, people buried their dead in shallow pits in the hot, dry desert sand. The sand soaked up moisture so fast that the bodies dried before they could decay.

One such body, buried around 3500 BCE, is in the British Museum. Scientists scanned it and found it was a young man who had been stabbed in the back. When later Egyptians began burying important people in coffins and tombs, away from the sand, the bodies rotted. So they developed ways to dry them on purpose.

Seventy days in the workshop

The Greek historian Herodotus visited Egypt in the 400s BCE and described what he was told. First, priests drew out the brain through the nose with a metal hook. Then they cut open the left side of the body and removed the stomach, intestines, liver and lungs. These were dried and stored in four special containers called canopic jars. In later times, organs were wrapped and put back inside.

Next came the natron. This salt, collected from dry lake beds such as those at Wadi El Natrun, draws water out of the body, the same way salt dries fish or meat. Bacteria, which cause rotting, cannot live without water. It is the same science people use to preserve food.

Wrapping and amulets

After drying, the body was stuffed with linen or sawdust to give it shape. Then came wrapping, which could use hundreds of square metres of linen. Priests said prayers as they worked. Amulets, such as a scarab beetle placed over the heart, were meant to protect the dead person on their journey.

A mask was placed over the face. Tutankhamun's famous mask is made of about 10 kg of gold, roughly the weight of a big bucket of water. The mummy was then placed in one or more coffins, sometimes nested like Russian dolls.

Animal mummies

Egyptians mummified animals too, in enormous numbers. Cats, ibis birds, crocodiles, falcons and even baboons were preserved as offerings to the gods. Some animal cemeteries hold millions of mummies. Pets were sometimes mummified to join their owners.

Mummies around the world

Other peoples made mummies too. The Chinchorro people, who fished along the coast of what is now Chile and Peru, began preserving their dead about 7,000 years ago, two thousand years before the Egyptians. Their mummies, painted black or red, became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2021. In very cold or very dry places, such as mountains and deserts, nature has also made mummies on its own.

Reading mummies today

Scientists no longer unwrap mummies. Instead they use X-rays and CT scanners to look inside without damage. Scans have revealed broken bones, bad teeth, diseases and even what people ate. They showed that Tutankhamun had a damaged foot and probably walked with a cane, and many canes were found in his tomb.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Opening Tutankhamun's tomb unleashed a deadly curse.

Truth: Howard Carter, who led the dig, lived for another 17 years. Most people at the opening lived normal lives. Lord Carnarvon, who paid for the dig, died in 1923 from an infected mosquito bite, and newspapers turned it into a curse story.

Try this

See how salt dries things out. Slice an apple into two equal pieces. Cover one completely with salt in a small bowl, and leave the other on a plate. After a week, ask an adult to help you brush off the salt and compare the two. Then throw both away.
