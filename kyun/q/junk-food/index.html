What makes junk food junky?

Junk food is not poison. It is food built to be easy to love, and very easy to eat too much of.

The short answer

Junk food gives you lots of energy, usually from sugar, refined flour, oil and salt. But it has very little of what your body needs to grow and stay well, such as fibre, protein, vitamins and minerals.

Chips, sweet fizzy drinks, packaged cakes, instant noodles and many fried snacks fit this description. They are made to taste great, last a long time on a shelf and be quick to eat. None of that is wicked. The trouble is that it is very easy to eat a lot of them, and if they crowd out other foods, your body misses out.

Surprising but true

In a careful 2019 study, people offered ultra-processed meals ate about 500 more calories a day than when offered similar meals made from whole foods.

Built to be hard to stop

Food companies employ scientists to find the right mix of sweet, salty and fatty that makes people want more. The journalist Michael Moss wrote about this in his book Salt Sugar Fat. Food scientists sometimes talk about the "bliss point", the amount of sugar that makes a product most pleasing.

Many snacks also melt quickly or crunch loudly, which makes them feel light even when they carry lots of calories. That is part of why we like some foods so much more than others.

The ultra-processed idea

In about 2009, Brazilian scientist Carlos Monteiro and his team suggested sorting foods by how much they are processed. "Ultra-processed" foods are made mostly from factory ingredients, like syrups, starches and flavourings, rather than whole foods.

In 2019 a team at the US National Institutes of Health tested this. Twenty adults lived in a lab for four weeks. When given ultra-processed meals, they ate about 500 more calories a day than when given meals made from whole foods, even though they could eat as much as they liked of both.

Sugar, salt and your body

Too much sugar feeds the bacteria that cause tooth decay, and over many years a diet heavy in sugary drinks is linked to weight gain and type 2 diabetes. Too much salt is linked to high blood pressure later in life.

Why is sugar bad for teeth?Sugar doesn't rot your teeth by itself. Tiny bacteria living on your teeth eat it and spit out acid.

The World Health Organization suggests keeping added sugars below 10% of daily energy, and adults eating less than 5 g of salt a day. One packet of instant noodles with its seasoning can carry a large share of that salt.

Is a samosa junk food?

Not all fried or tasty food is junk. A samosa, a plate of pakoras or a slice of homemade cake is fine as an occasional treat. A samosa also has potatoes and peas inside it.

What matters most is the overall pattern. Dal, rice, vegetables, fruit, curd and nuts most days leave plenty of room for treats. The problem comes when packaged snacks and sweet drinks become the everyday food instead.

Warnings on the packet

Some countries now put bold warnings on unhealthy foods. In 2016 Chile began requiring black stop-sign labels on packets high in sugar, salt, fat or calories, and limited cartoon characters in adverts for them. Mexico and others followed. India and the United States have been debating front-of-pack labels for years.

Labels help, but so does reading the small print. Packets often list a "serving" that is much smaller than what people really eat. A bag of chips might say it holds three servings, even though most people finish it in one go. Numbers can be true and still give the wrong idea, a trick explained in how statistics can mislead.

Advertising matters too. Snacks are often sold with cartoon characters, free toys and famous cricketers or footballers. That is why some countries now limit junk food adverts during children's television shows.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Eating sugar makes children hyperactive.

Truth: Careful studies, in which neither parents nor children knew who had sugar, found no real effect on behaviour. Parents who believed their child had eaten sugar were more likely to see wild behaviour, even when the child had not.

Try this

Find the nutrition label on a packet of biscuits, a fruit drink or breakfast cereal. Look for "sugars" per serving. Using a teaspoon and a bowl, measure out that much sugar, counting about 4 g per level teaspoon. Line up the bowls and compare.
