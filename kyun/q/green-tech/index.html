How can technology help the planet?

Electricity from sunlight has become so cheap that, in many countries, it now costs less than electricity from burning coal.

The short answer

Most of the gases that are warming the Earth come from burning coal, oil and gas for electricity, transport and factories. Technology helps in three main ways. It can make energy without burning anything, as solar panels and wind turbines do. It can make machines need less energy in the first place. And it can help us reuse materials instead of digging up new ones.

None of this is magic. Every machine has a cost, from the metals in a battery to the land under a solar farm. The question engineers keep asking is which choice does the least harm for the most good.

Surprising but true

An old-style filament bulb turns about 90 per cent of its electricity into heat, not light.

Sunlight on the desert

In the dry northwest of Rajasthan, near the town of Phalodi, rows of solar panels stretch across land where little else grows. This is Bhadla Solar Park, one of the largest solar farms on Earth. It covers about 56 square kilometres, roughly the size of 3,000 cricket grounds. Rajasthan gets strong sunshine on most days of the year, which makes it a good place to catch it.

The first practical silicon solar cell was shown at Bell Labs in the United States in 1954. It was costly and was used mostly on satellites. Since then, factories have learned to make panels so cheaply that the cost of solar electricity fell by roughly 90 per cent between 2010 and 2022. In many countries it is now among the cheapest kinds of new power you can build.

Why is Earth getting warmer?In 1856, an American scientist showed that one gas in the air traps heat. That gas is now at its highest level for hundreds of thousands of years.

Doing more with less

The cleanest energy is the energy you never need. Take light bulbs. An old filament bulb glows because a thin wire gets white-hot, and most of the electricity is lost as heat. An LED makes light in a different way and uses at least 75 per cent less energy. India's UJALA programme handed out more than 36 crore LED bulbs at low prices from 2015, cutting electricity use in millions of homes.

The same thinking runs through better fridges, fans and air conditioners. In India, appliances carry a star label from the Bureau of Energy Efficiency, and in the United States many carry the Energy Star label. More stars mean less electricity for the same job.

Moving without smoke

Cars, buses and trucks burn a huge amount of oil. Electric cars have no exhaust pipe, which keeps city air cleaner. How clean they are overall depends on where their electricity comes from. Indian cities have also been adding electric buses, and electric autorickshaws are now a common sight in Delhi and Bengaluru.

The hard part is storing energy. The sun sets and the wind drops, so grids need giant batteries or other ways to save power for later. Batteries are getting better each year, but they need metals such as lithium, which has to be mined.

Rubbish as a resource

A tonne of old phones contains more gold than a tonne of gold ore from a mine, because so many tiny gold contacts are packed inside. Recycling aims to get such materials back. Some things, like aluminium cans, can be melted and reused almost endlessly. Many plastics are much harder.

Scientists are also testing machines that pull carbon dioxide straight out of the air. They work, but so far they capture only a tiny amount compared with what we release. Many experts think these machines will help a little, while cutting what we burn in the first place will do most of the work on climate change.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Electric cars cause no pollution at all.

Truth: They have no exhaust pipe, so the air on the street is cleaner. But the electricity may come from burning coal, and making batteries uses energy and mining. Studies usually find that over its whole life an electric car still pollutes less than a petrol car, and the gap grows as power grids get cleaner.

Try this

Fill two identical jars with the same amount of tap water. Wrap one in black paper and one in white paper, and leave both in the sun for an hour. Feel the water in each. The black jar soaks up more sunlight, the same idea that solar water heaters on rooftops use.
