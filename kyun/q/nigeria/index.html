What is Nigeria known for?

Roughly one in every seven Africans lives in Nigeria, a country of more than 500 languages and one of the world's busiest film industries.

The short answer

Nigeria is a large country in West Africa, on the Gulf of Guinea. It has more people than any other country in Africa, well over 20 crore by United Nations estimates. Its biggest city, Lagos, is one of the largest cities on the continent, though the capital is Abuja, a planned city in the middle of the country.

Nigeria is a land of many peoples. The three largest groups are the Hausa and Fulani in the north, the Yoruba in the south-west and the Igbo in the south-east, but there are hundreds more. Together they speak over 500 languages, so English, left over from British rule, is used as a shared official language. Nigeria is famous for its films, its music, its writers, its food and its ancient art.

Surprising but true

Nigeria's film industry, Nollywood, makes so many films that UNESCO once ranked it second in the world by number of films produced, behind India's.

Ancient sculptors

Some of Africa's oldest known sculptures come from Nigeria. The Nok culture, in the centre of the country, made striking clay heads with large eyes and carefully styled hair more than 2,000 years ago.

Later, the Kingdom of Benin, in what is now southern Nigeria, became famous for brass and bronze plaques and heads made for its royal palace. In 1897, British soldiers attacked Benin City and took thousands of these objects, now known as the Benin Bronzes. Many ended up in museums in Europe and the USA. In recent years, some museums have started returning them to Nigeria. Artists in Benin City still use the same lost-wax casting method today.

From colony to giant

Britain joined the northern and southern parts of the region into a single colony in 1914. Nigeria became independent on 1 October 1960, one of many African countries to win freedom in the wave of decolonisation that year.

The years that followed were hard. From 1967 to 1970, a civil war was fought after the south-east tried to break away as Biafra, and a very large number of people died, many from hunger. Since 1999, Nigeria has had elected governments. Oil from the Niger Delta has made the government much of its money, though many Nigerians remain poor.

Nollywood and Afrobeats

In the early 1990s, film-makers in Lagos began shooting films cheaply on video and selling them straight to viewers on cassettes. The 1992 film Living in Bondage is often named as the start of what became Nollywood. Within a few years it was producing hundreds of films a year. A 2009 UNESCO study placed it second in the world by number of films, behind India's film industries, including Bollywood.

Nigerian music travels too. In the 1970s Fela Kuti created Afrobeat, mixing jazz, funk and Yoruba rhythms. Today Nigerian Afrobeats stars fill stadiums in London and New York, and their songs climb charts around the world.

Writers and storytellers

In 1958, Chinua Achebe published Things Fall Apart, about an Igbo village leader whose world is shaken by the arrival of the British. It is one of the most widely read African novels and is taught in schools across the world. In 1986, the playwright Wole Soyinka became the first African to win the Nobel Prize in Literature.

Storytelling runs through daily life, in Yoruba proverbs, Hausa tales and Igbo folk stories. It reflects the country's mix of languages, each with its own songs and sayings.

Food carries stories too. Jollof rice, cooked in a spicy sauce of tomatoes, peppers and onions, turns up at almost every Nigerian party. Nigerians and Ghanaians tease each other endlessly about whose jollof is better.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Lagos is the capital of Nigeria.

Truth: Lagos was the capital until 1991, and it is still the biggest city. The capital moved to Abuja, a planned city built near the middle of the country.
