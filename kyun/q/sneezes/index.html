Why do we sneeze?

A sneeze is your nose's emergency eject button. Once it starts, almost nobody can stop it halfway.

The short answer

A sneeze is your body's way of blasting something unwanted out of your nose. The lining of your nose is full of nerve endings. When dust, pollen, a pinch of pepper or a cold virus irritates them, they send a message to your brainstem, the part of the brain that runs automatic jobs.

The brainstem takes over. You draw in a deep breath, your chest muscles squeeze hard, your tongue presses against the roof of your mouth, and the air rushes out through your nose with a bang. Your eyes snap shut too. The whole thing takes about a second, and it's a reflex, which means you don't decide to do it.

Surprising but true

Somewhere between one in six and one in three people sneeze when they step into bright sunlight, and scientists still aren't sure why.

The tickle before the blast

You often feel a sneeze coming. That itchy, tingly moment is the nerve signal building. Some people can stop a sneeze at that point by pressing under their nose or holding their breath, but once the big breath in starts, it nearly always finishes.

Holding a full sneeze in by pinching your nose and closing your mouth is a bad idea. Doctors have reported rare cases where the trapped pressure hurt someone's ears or throat. It's better to let it out, into a tissue or your elbow.

Spray

A sneeze carries thousands of tiny droplets of mucus and saliva. Scientists at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology have filmed sneezes with high-speed cameras. They found the droplets travel inside a warm, moist puff of air that can carry the smallest ones several metres across a room.

That's why a sneeze is one of the main ways colds and flu spread. Older books claimed sneezes shoot out faster than a speeding car. The real speed is hard to measure and varies a lot, but the spreading cloud is the bigger worry. Sneezing into your elbow catches most of it. A tissue is even better, as long as it goes in the bin straight after and you wash your hands.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, these films helped explain why masks and open windows mattered. A sneeze in a closed room leaves droplets hanging in the air for a while, while moving air carries them away.

Sun sneezes and other oddities

Pollen sets off sneezes in people with hay fever, which is a kind of allergy. Black pepper is a classic trigger, because tiny bits of it irritate the nose. That made pepper, the most traded of all spices, a favourite of cartoon jokes for a hundred years.

Some triggers are stranger. Bright sunlight makes some people sneeze, and some people sneeze after a very big meal. A few people always sneeze two or three times in a row. In 1981, a girl in England named Donna Griffiths began sneezing and didn't stop for 978 days. It's still listed as the longest sneezing fit ever recorded.

Bless you

In many places, people say something kind after a sneeze. English speakers say "bless you". In German it's "Gesundheit", which means health, and in Spanish it's "salud", which means the same. People have different stories about where these customs began, and nobody knows for sure.

Animals sneeze too. Cats and dogs sneeze to clear their noses, just like us. African wild dogs go further. A study in Botswana found that when a pack was deciding whether to go hunting, more sneezes made them more likely to set off, as if they were voting.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Your heart stops for a moment when you sneeze.

Truth: It doesn't. A sneeze changes the pressure in your chest, which can change the rhythm of your heartbeat for a beat or two. Your heart keeps beating the whole time.

Try this

Ask everyone in your family or class whether they sneeze when they walk out into bright sunshine. Make a tally. If a parent does, check whether their children do too, since this reflex often runs in families.
