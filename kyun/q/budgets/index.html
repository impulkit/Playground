How do you make a budget?

India's government spends money on a scale of lakhs of crores of rupees each year. It plans it the same basic way you might plan your pocket money.

The short answer

A budget is a plan for money. It has three parts: how much comes in, how much goes out and how much is left over.

To make one, first write down all your income, such as pocket money, gifts or money for jobs. Then list what you spend or need to spend. Take the second number away from the first. If there is money left, you can save it or plan something with it. If you are spending more than you get, something has to change.

Families, shops, schools, companies and whole countries all make budgets. The numbers get bigger, but the idea stays the same.

Surprising but true

India's first budget was presented in 1860 by James Wilson, a Scottish economist who also founded the magazine The Economist.

Needs and wants

The first step in budgeting is sorting your spending. Needs are things you must have, like food, rent, school fees and bus fares. Wants are things that are nice to have, like a new video game, cinema tickets or a fancy pencil case.

A popular rule of thumb is the 50/30/20 rule. It was made popular by the American writers Elizabeth Warren and her daughter Amelia Warren Tyagi in a 2005 book. It suggests spending about 50% of your income on needs, 30% on wants and 20% on savings. Real families change these numbers to suit their lives, but it is a handy starting point.

Ways to keep track

Some people keep a notebook. Others use a spreadsheet or an app on their phone. A very old method is the envelope system. You put cash for each kind of spending into its own envelope, labelled "food", "travel" or "fun". When an envelope is empty, that spending stops until next month.

Savings work best when they come first. Many people move money into savings as soon as they are paid, before they can spend it. Money kept in a bank can also earn interest, so savings grow slowly over time.

A country's budget

Every year, the Government of India presents a Union Budget to Parliament. It shows how much money the government expects to collect, mostly from taxes. It also shows how it plans to spend it on things like defence, roads, railways, schools, hospitals and paying interest on its loans.

The first budget in British India was presented on 7 April 1860 by James Wilson, a Scottish economist who had earlier founded The Economist magazine. After Independence, India's first finance minister, R.K. Shanmukham Chetty, presented a budget on 26 November 1947. Since 2017, the Union Budget has been presented on 1 February each year.

The United States government plans its spending in a similar way. Its year for budgeting, called the fiscal year, starts on 1 October.

When budgets don't balance

If a government spends more than it collects, the gap is called a deficit. It borrows money to fill the gap and pays interest on the loan. If it collects more than it spends, that is a surplus.

Households work the same way. Borrowing can make sense for something large, like a house or a college course. But borrowing to pay for everyday wants can lead to debts that grow faster than savings ever could.

Budgets as stories

A budget tells you what a family or a country cares about. A family saving for a trip might cut down on eating out. A country that spends more on schools one year is making a choice.

Newspapers often show the government's budget as a pie chart, with a slice for each kind of spending. Looking at the slices is one of the quickest ways to see where the money goes.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A budget means you can never spend money on fun things.

Truth: A good budget plans for fun. It sets aside money for things you enjoy, so you can spend it without worrying. The point is to choose where your money goes, instead of wondering where it went.

Try this

For one week, write down every rupee or dollar you receive and everything you spend, even small things like sweets or stickers. At the end of the week, add up each list. Sort your spending into "needs" and "wants" and see which was bigger.
