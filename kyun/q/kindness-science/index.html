Is kindness good for you?

In one experiment, people given money to spend on someone else ended the day happier than people told to spend it on themselves.

The short answer

Science suggests that being kind is often good for the person doing it, as well as for the person receiving it. Studies have found that people who help others, give gifts or volunteer tend to report feeling happier, more connected and less stressed.

The effects are usually modest, not magic, and researchers are still working out how much kindness itself causes these benefits. But the pattern turns up again and again, in different countries and age groups. Being kind also builds friendships, and strong relationships are one of the best-known links to health and happiness.

Surprising but true

Toddlers as young as 18 months will often help an adult pick up something they dropped, without being asked or rewarded.

Money spent on others

In a famous 2008 study in Canada, psychologists Elizabeth Dunn, Lara Aknin and Michael Norton gave people an envelope of money in the morning. Some were told to spend it on themselves, others on a gift for someone else or a donation. By evening, the people who had spent on others reported feeling happier.

The amounts were small, five or twenty dollars, and the amount did not seem to matter much. What mattered was who it was spent on. Later studies found similar patterns in countries as different as Uganda, India and Canada.

Born to help

Psychologists Felix Warneken and Michael Tomasello tested whether very young children help without being asked. An adult would "accidentally" drop a pen or struggle to open a cupboard with full hands. Many toddlers aged about 18 months toddled over and helped, even with no praise or reward. Young chimpanzees helped in some tests too.

This suggests the urge to help starts early in life. Families, friends and culture then shape it. Children learn when, how and whom to help by watching others.

What happens in the body

When people do something kind, parts of the brain linked with reward become more active, much as they do when we eat something tasty. Some researchers describe the warm feeling after helping as a "helper's high".

Kindness may also affect stress. Some studies suggest that people who regularly help others have lower blood pressure or recover better from difficult times. These studies are harder to be sure about, because people who are already healthy may simply have more energy to help.

Kindness in animals

Humans are not the only kind creatures. In a 2011 experiment in the United States, rats learned how to open a small cage to free a trapped companion. They did this even when they could have eaten a pile of chocolate chips alone instead. Many rats shared the chocolate afterwards. Elephants, chimpanzees and some birds also help each other in ways scientists are still studying.

Small acts count

Many cultures have long taught this. Ideas such as seva, selfless service, and daya, compassion, appear across Indian traditions, and similar teachings are found in religions and philosophies worldwide.

Kindness does not have to be big. Holding a door, saying thank you, including someone who is left out, or listening when a friend is upset all count. Researchers have found that people often underestimate how much a small kind act means to the person receiving it.

There is one caution. Helping others is good, but nobody should feel they must please everyone all the time or ignore their own feelings. Kindness works best when it includes being kind to yourself, and asking a trusted adult for help when you need it. Our emotions are part of what makes caring for others possible.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Humans are born selfish, and only learn to help others because adults teach them to.

Truth: Experiments show that toddlers often help others before they can say much, without any reward. Families and culture shape how kindness grows, but the urge to help seems to start early.

Try this

For one week, do one small kind thing each day, like helping with chores, writing a thank-you note or sharing a snack. Each evening, rate your mood from 1 to 5 in a notebook. Compare it with a week when you didn't keep track. Remember this is a fun test, not proof.
