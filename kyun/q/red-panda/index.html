Is the red panda a panda?

It was called a panda first. The famous black-and-white bear borrowed the name decades later.

The short answer

No, not in the way most people mean. The red panda is not a bear, and it is not closely related to the giant panda. It belongs to a family all of its own, and its nearest living relatives are raccoons, skunks and weasels.

But here is the twist. The red panda had the name first. A French scientist described it for Europeans in 1825 and called it a panda. The giant panda wasn't described by Western scientists until 1869, and only later came to share the red panda's name.

So if anyone has a right to the name, it's the little red one.

Surprising but true

Red pandas and giant pandas both have a "false thumb" for gripping bamboo, but they evolved it separately.

A cat-sized mountain climber

A red panda is about the size of a house cat, with a long, bushy tail ringed in red and cream. Its face is white and rusty red, with tear-like stripes running down from its eyes. Its fur is thick and reddish, which helps it blend in with the reddish moss and lichen on the trees where it lives.

Red pandas live in cool, misty mountain forests in the eastern Himalayas and southwest China. In India they are found in Sikkim, where they are the state animal, in the Darjeeling hills of West Bengal and in Arunachal Pradesh.

They spend much of their time in trees. They can even climb down head first, turning their ankles around to grip the trunk. In cold weather they curl up on a branch and wrap their tail around themselves like a blanket.

Life on bamboo

Most of a red panda's diet is bamboo leaves and young shoots, with some fruit, acorns, eggs and insects. Bamboo is not a great food. It is tough, and the red panda's stomach can only digest a small part of it.

So red pandas spend many hours a day eating, and save energy the rest of the time. They move slowly and sleep a lot. In very cold weather they can even lower how fast their body burns energy, a bit like turning down the heater in a house.

Two pandas, one trick

Both kinds of panda face the same problem: holding a stalk of bamboo. Both solved it the same way. Each has a bone in its wrist that has grown long and sticks out like an extra thumb. They press the bamboo against it to grip.

Yet the two animals are only distant relatives. They evolved their false thumbs separately, because they ate the same food. Scientists call this convergent evolution: different animals arriving at the same answer to the same problem.

Red panda relatives once lived far beyond Asia. Fossils of a red panda cousin about 5 million years old have been found in Tennessee, in the USA.

One species or two?

In 2020, a team of Chinese scientists compared the DNA of red pandas from different areas. They found two groups that had been separated for a very long time, divided roughly by a big river in Arunachal Pradesh. They suggested calling them the Himalayan red panda and the Chinese red panda.

Not every expert agrees that they are different species. The debate matters, because if there are two, each has fewer animals and may need its own protection plan.

Keeping red pandas safe

Red pandas are endangered. Their forests are being cut down for timber, farms and roads, and stray dogs can spread disease to them. Some are caught for the illegal pet trade.

The Padmaja Naidu Himalayan Zoological Park in Darjeeling breeds red pandas and has released some into the forests of Singalila National Park nearby. In Nepal, local villagers are trained as "forest guardians" who track red pandas and protect their trees. Good zoos around the world, including in the USA, share animals to keep a healthy backup population.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Red pandas are baby giant pandas, or a small kind of bear.

Truth: Red pandas are not bears at all, and they are fully grown at about the size of a cat. They belong to their own family, more closely related to raccoons and skunks than to giant pandas.

Try this

Hold a pencil with your thumb tucked into your palm and try to pick up a few straws or chopsticks without using it. It's hard. Now picture a small bump on your wrist that you could press things against. That is how a red panda's false thumb, an enlarged wrist bone, helps it grip bamboo.
