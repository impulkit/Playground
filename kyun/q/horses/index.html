How did horses change human history?

Horses first evolved in North America, vanished from it about 10,000 years ago, and came back only about 500 years ago on Spanish ships.

The short answer

Horses changed human history more than almost any other animal. Once people learned to ride them and harness them to wheels, they could travel, trade, farm and fight over much larger distances. News, goods, ideas and armies suddenly moved at the speed of a gallop.

Horses carried the Mongols across Asia, pulled chariots in ancient India and Egypt, hauled ploughs on European farms and carried the mail across the American West. For thousands of years, a strong horse was like a car, a tractor and a tank in one. Only in the last hundred years did engines take over most of their work.

Surprising but true

The earliest known ancestor of the horse, which lived about 55 million years ago, was only about the size of a small dog and had toes instead of hooves.

A North American start

The horse family began in North America about 55 million years ago. The first ancestor was about the size of a small dog, with several toes on each foot, living in forests and eating leaves. Over millions of years, as grasslands spread, horses grew bigger and faster, and their toes shrank to a single hoof. It is one of the best-known examples of evolution in fossils.

Horses crossed into Asia over a land bridge that once joined Alaska and Siberia. Then, about 10,000 years ago, they died out in the Americas, perhaps because of climate change and hunting. They survived only in Asia, Europe and Africa.

Taming the horse

People hunted wild horses for meat long before they rode them. Around 5,500 years ago, people at Botai in Kazakhstan were keeping horses and may have milked them. But DNA studies show those animals were not the ancestors of today's horses.

A big study published in 2021 traced modern domestic horses to the grasslands of southern Russia about 4,200 years ago. Within a few centuries, they spread across Asia and Europe. Light chariots with spoked wheels appeared around the same time, giving armies a terrifying new weapon.

Who invented the wheel?The wheel came surprisingly late. People were farming, making pots and building towns for thousands of years before anyone rolled a cart.

Horses in India

Horses play a big part in early Indian texts. The Rigveda praises them, and kings performed a ceremony called the Ashvamedha, involving a horse. In the epics, Krishna drives Arjuna's chariot pulled by white horses.

India's hot climate wasn't ideal for breeding big warhorses, so for centuries rulers imported fine horses from Central Asia and Arabia, often by sea through ports on the west coast. India also bred its own. The Marwari horse of Rajasthan and the Kathiawari of Gujarat have ears that curl inward until the tips nearly touch. Rajput warriors prized them, and the story of Maharana Pratap's horse Chetak is still told today.

Empires on horseback

In the 1200s, Mongol riders built the largest land empire in history. Each soldier often had several horses, switching when one tired, so armies could cover great distances in a day. They used a relay system of horse stations to carry messages across the empire.

Horses also changed farming. Heavy horses pulling iron ploughs could break tough soil, and in Europe they slowly replaced slower oxen in many areas. More food meant bigger towns.

Return to the Americas

When the Spanish arrived in the Americas from the 1490s, they brought horses back to the land where the family began. Some escaped. Over the next two centuries, nations of the Great Plains, such as the Comanche and Lakota, became superb riders. Horses let them hunt bison far more easily and travel widely.

In 1860 and 1861, riders of the Pony Express carried mail across the American West in about ten days, swapping horses at stations. The telegraph soon replaced them, and later cars and tractors replaced most working horses. Today, horses race, jump, pull tongas in some Indian towns and still help herders and farmers around the world.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The wild mustangs of the American West are really wild horses.

Truth: Mustangs are feral, descended from tame horses that escaped or were set free, many from Spanish stock. Przewalski's horse of Central Asia, which nearly died out and has been brought back to Mongolia, was long called the last wild horse never tamed by people. A 2018 DNA study suggests even its ancestors were once kept by people at Botai.
