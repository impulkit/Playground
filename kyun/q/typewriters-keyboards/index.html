Why are keyboard letters in a strange order?

The letters on your keyboard were arranged for a clunky metal typewriter in the 1870s. Nobody has managed to change them since.

The short answer

The letters on most keyboards follow a layout called QWERTY, named after the first six letters on the top row. It was designed in the 1870s for one of the first successful typewriters, made by the American inventor Christopher Latham Sholes and his partners. So many people learned to type on it that it became a habit no one could break. When computers arrived, they copied it.

Why Sholes put the letters in that order is still argued about. The most famous explanation is that it stopped the typewriter's metal arms from jamming. Another is that it suited telegraph operators, the first big group of professional typists. The honest answer is that we don't know for sure.

Surprising but true

You can type the word TYPEWRITER using only the top row of letters on a QWERTY keyboard.

The first typewriters

People had tried to build writing machines for over a century, but most were slow or awkward. In Milwaukee in the late 1860s, Sholes, a newspaper editor and printer, worked with partners on a new machine. Pressing a key swung a metal arm up to strike an inked ribbon against paper.

His early versions had keys in alphabetical order. Over several years he changed the arrangement many times. In 1873 the gun-making company E. Remington and Sons bought the rights, and in 1874 it began selling the Sholes and Glidden typewriter. It typed only capital letters.

The jamming story

On those early machines, the typebars sat in a circle beneath the paper. If you hit two neighbouring keys quickly, their arms could tangle and stick. The popular explanation says Sholes moved common letter pairs, like T and H, apart to reduce jams.

It's a neat story, but the evidence is mixed. In 2011, two researchers at Kyoto University, Koichi and Motoko Yasuoka, studied old patents and records. They argued the layout was shaped largely by telegraph operators, who used early typewriters to type out Morse code messages as they heard them. Some letters that are easy to confuse in Morse were placed close together. Other historians still favour the jamming idea, and both may be part of the story.

Why we never switched

Other layouts have come and gone. In 1936, an American professor, August Dvorak, patented a keyboard with the most common English letters on the middle row, where fingers rest. Some fans say it's faster and more comfortable.

But by then, schools taught QWERTY, offices were full of QWERTY machines and typists had spent years learning it. Changing would mean retraining everyone at once. Economists use QWERTY as a classic example of how an early choice can lock in, even if something better comes along later.

Typing in Indian scripts

Indian scripts posed a different puzzle. Devanagari, used for Hindi, and many other Indian scripts join letters and vowel signs together in hundreds of combinations. Fitting them onto a typewriter needed clever compromises.

In 1955, the Mumbai company Godrej built India's first home-made typewriter. For decades, typists outside courts and government offices tapped out letters and forms. Godrej stopped making typewriters around 2009. On computers today, the Government of India's InScript layout puts Indian letters onto a standard keyboard, and many people simply type Hindi words in English letters and let software convert them.

Phones still show QWERTY on their screens, even though their letters can never jam. A choice made for metal arms in the 1870s lives on under your thumbs.

Myth or fact?

Myth: QWERTY was designed to make people type slowly.

Truth: The usual story is that the layout reduced jams by separating letters often typed one after another, not that it slowed typists down on purpose. Even that explanation is debated. Some researchers think the layout grew out of feedback from telegraph operators who typed up Morse code messages.

Try this

Time yourself. Type or write out the sentence "The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog", which uses every letter of the English alphabet. Then look at which letters you typed with your left hand and which with your right. On QWERTY, many common English words lean on the left hand.
