How does an abacus work?

In 1946, a Japanese clerk with a wooden abacus raced an American soldier with an electric calculator. The abacus won.

The short answer

An abacus is a frame with rows of beads that slide along rods. Each rod stands for a place value: ones, tens, hundreds, thousands and so on, just like the columns in a number written on paper.

You show a number by sliding beads towards a central bar. To add, you slide more beads in. When a rod fills up past nine, you clear it and move one bead on the next rod to the left. That is exactly what "carrying" means in written addition. Subtraction, multiplication and division work in a similar way, with practice.

The abacus doesn't do the maths for you. It holds the numbers so your mind doesn't have to.

Surprising but true

Some expert abacus users can do sums without one. They picture the beads in their heads and move them in their imagination, faster than most people can type numbers into a calculator.

Counting with pebbles

The first counting tools were probably pebbles laid in lines drawn in sand or on a board. The word calculate comes from the Latin calculus, meaning a small pebble.

The oldest surviving counting board is the Salamis tablet, a slab of white marble found on the Greek island of Salamis in 1846. It is about 1.5 metres long and probably more than 2,000 years old. Romans used small bronze hand abacuses with beads in grooves, a bit like a pocket calculator.

Counting boards were common in medieval Europe too. They were used to count taxes and trade along busy trade routes, long before written sums with Indian-style numerals replaced them.

The Chinese and Japanese abacus

The best-known abacus today is the Chinese suanpan. It has a bar across the middle. Each rod has two beads above the bar, worth five each, and five below, worth one each. It was in wide use in China by about the 1500s, and may be older.

Japan adopted the abacus from China and slimmed it down. The modern Japanese soroban has one bead above the bar and four below. That is all you need to show every digit from 0 to 9, so there are fewer beads to move and fewer mistakes.

In both countries, shopkeepers, bankers and clerks used the abacus every day for centuries. You can still hear the click of beads in some shops in East Asia.

Abacus versus calculator

On 12 November 1946, just after the Second World War, an American army newspaper in Tokyo held a contest. Kiyoshi Matsuzaki, a clerk from the Japanese postal ministry, used a soroban. Private Thomas Nathan Wood of the US Army used an electric calculator, the best machine of the time.

They raced at adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing. Matsuzaki won four of the five rounds. Only in multiplication did the machine come out ahead. Electronic calculators soon became far faster, but for a while the beads were quicker.

The abacus in your head

In Japan, China, India and many other countries, children learn the abacus in classes after school. After a lot of practice, some learn to picture the abacus in their minds and move the beads in their imagination. In Japanese, this is called anzan.

Champions of mental abacus can add long lists of numbers flashed on a screen in a few seconds. Researchers have studied these children to learn how the brain handles pictures and numbers together.

Beads you can feel

The abacus has also been a tool for blind and partially sighted people. In the 1960s, an American named Tim Cranmer adapted the soroban so that its beads don't slide out of place when touched. The Cranmer abacus is still used to teach maths by touch in many schools for blind students around the world.

Before zero was written as a symbol, an empty rod on an abacus already did its job.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The abacus was invented in China.

Truth: People in Mesopotamia, Greece and Rome used counting boards and early abacuses long before the Chinese bead abacus, called the suanpan, became common. China developed its own famous version, but the idea of counting with stones or beads on lines appeared in many places.

Try this

Make a simple abacus with three pipe cleaners or straws pushed into a piece of clay, and threaded with beads, pasta or buttons. Use the right rod for ones, the middle for tens and the left for hundreds. Show your age, then add 15 by moving beads, carrying ten ones over as one ten.
