Which birds can sleep while flying?

Some common swifts stay in the air for about ten months without landing once, eating, drinking and probably sleeping on the wing.

The short answer

At least two kinds of birds are known to sleep while flying. The first is the frigatebird, a big seabird with long, thin wings. In 2016, scientists fitted frigatebirds with tiny brain recorders and proved they nap in mid-air, often with only one half of the brain asleep at a time.

The second is the swift. Swifts are small, sleek birds that live almost entirely in the air. Some common swifts stay aloft for about ten months without landing, so they must sleep while flying, though nobody has yet recorded their brains to prove it. Their look-alikes, the swallows, are less extreme. They spend hours flying but come down to perch and sleep.

Surprising but true

Flying frigatebirds can sleep with one half of their brain at a time, keeping one eye open to watch where they're going, much like dolphins do in the sea.

Ten months on the wing

Common swifts breed in Europe and parts of Asia and spend the northern winter over Africa. For years, people suspected they rarely landed. In 2016, a team at Lund University in Sweden fixed tiny data loggers, weighing about as much as a paperclip, to swifts' backs.

When the birds returned the next year, the loggers showed that some had flown non-stop for about ten months, all the way to Africa and back. They caught insects in the air and probably drank by skimming raindrops or water surfaces.

At dusk, swifts often climb high into the sky, up to a few kilometres. Scientists think they glide slowly down from there, snatching short naps on the way. A young swift that leaves its nest may not touch the ground again until it is ready to breed, perhaps two years later.

Half asleep over the ocean

Frigatebirds soar over tropical oceans for weeks, hunting fish and squid. They can't land on the water because their feathers aren't waterproof.

In 2016, scientists led by Niels Rattenborg recorded the brain waves of great frigatebirds flying over the Pacific near the Galápagos Islands. The birds slept for only seconds at a time, mostly while gliding upward on rising warm air. Often, one half of the brain slept while the other stayed awake, with one eye open, looking ahead. On land, they slept for around 12 hours a day. In the air, they managed under an hour.

Dolphins do something similar, resting half their brain at a time so they can keep swimming and breathing.

Why do we need sleep?You will spend about a third of your life asleep, and scientists still argue about exactly why.

Swift or swallow?

Swifts and swallows both have long, pointed wings, short beaks and wide mouths for scooping up insects. But they're not closely related. Swifts belong to the same group as hummingbirds. Swallows are songbirds, related to sparrows.

You can tell them apart by watching them land. Swifts have tiny legs and feet that are good for clinging to walls and cliffs but useless for walking or perching. Their family name means "without feet". Swallows have proper perching feet and often line up on electricity wires.

Barn swallows nest in both India and the USA, gluing cup-shaped nests of mud under bridges and roofs. Many migrate thousands of kilometres each year.

Spit, caves and soup

Swifts and their relatives make unusual homes. Many glue their nests together with sticky saliva. India's Asian palm swift sticks its nest, and even its eggs, to the underside of a palm leaf, so they stay put as the leaf swings in the wind.

Edible-nest swiftlets build nests almost entirely of hardened saliva, deep in dark caves. Some find their way in the dark using clicking sounds, a simple form of echolocation. Their nests are collected for bird's nest soup, an expensive dish in East Asia. In the Andaman Islands, over-collection badly hurt these birds, and conservationists have worked with local people to protect nests and let chicks fly.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Swifts and swallows are close relatives, since they look so alike.

Truth: They look alike because both hunt flying insects, so their bodies evolved similar shapes. Swifts are actually closest to hummingbirds, while swallows are songbirds related to sparrows and crows.

Try this

On a summer evening, watch the sky over your town or school for fast birds with long, narrow, curved wings. Notice whether they ever land on wires or roofs. Swifts almost never perch, while swallows often line up on wires, which is a good way to tell them apart.
