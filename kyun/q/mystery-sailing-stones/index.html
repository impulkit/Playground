How do rocks move by themselves in Death Valley?

On a dry lakebed in California, rocks heavier than you leave long trails across the mud, and for decades nobody saw one move.

The short answer

On Racetrack Playa, a flat, dry lakebed in Death Valley, California, rocks lie at the ends of long tracks carved into the cracked mud. Some trails run straight, some zigzag, and some rocks seem to have turned around. For about 70 years, people argued about how they moved.

In 2013 and 2014, a team of scientists finally watched it happen. After a rare rain, a shallow pond forms on the playa and freezes at night. On sunny mornings, the ice cracks into thin, floating panes, some tens of metres across. A light breeze pushes these panes, and they bulldoze the rocks slowly across the slippery mud.

Surprising but true

When scientists finally filmed the rocks moving in 2013, they crept along at a few metres a minute, slower than a person walking, pushed by ice only a few millimetres thick.

A puzzle in the desert

Death Valley is one of the hottest places on Earth. In 1913, a thermometer there recorded 56.7°C, the highest air temperature ever officially measured, although some experts doubt that reading. Not far away, Badwater Basin sits 86 metres below sea level, the lowest point in North America.

Racetrack Playa is higher and cooler, about 1,130 m up. It is roughly 4.5 km long and almost perfectly flat, so flat that from a distance it looks like a frozen lake. Rocks fall onto it from the surrounding hills, and then, somehow, they travel.

Guesses and more guesses

Over the years, people suggested almost everything. Hurricane-force winds. Slippery slime made by tiny algae. Magnetism. Pranksters dragging rocks at night. A few even blamed aliens.

In the 1970s, two geologists named dozens of rocks, gave them nicknames such as Karen, and mapped where they moved over seven years. Karen, a block weighing about 300 kg, did not budge during their study, while much smaller rocks wandered. The trouble was that nobody ever saw a rock in motion. The moves happened rarely, perhaps once every few years, and the playa is remote and empty.

Catching them in the act

In 2011, a team including the cousins Richard and James Norris got permission to place rocks fitted with GPS trackers on the playa. They also set up a weather station and time-lapse cameras. Then they waited.

In December 2013, they got lucky. A thin pond, just a few centimetres deep, had frozen. As the sun rose, the ice, only 3 to 6 mm thick, broke into large panes. A breeze of about 3 to 5 metres a second pushed them along, and rocks all across the playa began to move. One travelled about 224 metres.

The team had been ready to wait five or ten years for a single move. Instead, the big day came after about two, and dozens of rocks moved within minutes of each other.

Why ice makes such a good sail

A heavy rock sits firmly on dry mud. Friction, the gripping force between surfaces, holds it in place. But a wide sheet of ice catches a lot of wind, like a sail on a boat. Floating on water, it slides with almost no friction, and it pushes against the rock with the combined force of the wind on the whole sheet.

The soft wet mud underneath keeps a perfect record. The rock's bottom scratches a groove, and when the water dries away, the trail stays for years.

Protecting the trails

The National Park Service does not allow visitors to take rocks or drive on the playa. Car tyres leave scars that can last for decades, because the playa may only get a little rain each year to smooth them out.

Similar moving rocks have been found on a few other dry lakes in Nevada and elsewhere. And rocks are not the only things that seem to move without legs: some plants can track the sun or snap shut in a fraction of a second.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The rocks are pushed by super-strong desert winds.

Truth: The winds measured during real moves were gentle, about as strong as a breeze that rustles leaves. The trick is the ice, which acts like a sail and lets light winds push heavy rocks.

Try this

Put a coin on a smooth plate and blow on it through a straw. It barely moves. Now set the coin on a flat ice cube on the same plate and blow again. The ice slides easily on its own meltwater and carries the coin with it.
