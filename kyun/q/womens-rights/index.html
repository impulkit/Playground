How did women win equal rights?

In 1886, Anandibai Joshi, who had sailed from India to Philadelphia three years earlier, came home a qualified doctor, at a time when most women anywhere could not vote.

The short answer

For most of history, women in most countries could not vote, own much property in their own name or study at university. Laws and customs treated them as belonging to their fathers, then their husbands.

That changed bit by bit, over about two centuries, because women organised. They wrote petitions, marched, went on hunger strikes, went to court and stood for election. Some men supported them. The fight looked different in every country, and in many ways it is still going on. Today, most constitutions, including India's, promise equal rights for women and men.

Surprising but true

Indian women could vote in the very first general election of independent India, years before women in Switzerland won the right to vote in national elections in 1971.

The long march for the vote

In 1848, about 300 people met in Seneca Falls, New York, and 100 of them signed a declaration that "all men and women are created equal". It took 72 more years, and the 19th Amendment, before American women won the vote nationally in 1920.

New Zealand got there first among self-governing countries, in 1893, after women gathered a petition so long it was rolled out across the floor of Parliament. In Britain, campaigners called suffragettes chained themselves to railings and were sent to prison. Some countries were much slower. Swiss women could not vote in national elections until 1971, and one small canton, Appenzell Innerrhoden, kept women out of its local votes until a court forced it to change in 1990. The story of how women won the vote is full of people who never lived to see it.

India's different path

In India, reform began with campaigns against child marriage and for girls' education. Savitribai Phule opened a school for girls in Pune in 1848. In 1886, Anandibai Joshi graduated from the Woman's Medical College of Pennsylvania, becoming one of the first Indian women trained in Western medicine.

Women joined the freedom struggle in large numbers. Sarojini Naidu became the first Indian woman president of the Indian National Congress in 1925, and in 1930 she led protesters at Dharasana during the salt protests. When the Constitution came into force in 1950, every adult woman got the vote at the same moment as every man. Fifteen women were members of the Constituent Assembly that wrote it.

Beyond the ballot box

Voting was a start. Women also fought for equal pay, for the right to inherit and for protection from violence. In the USA, the Equal Pay Act of 1963 made it illegal to pay women less for the same work. A 1972 law called Title IX banned sex discrimination in schools that get federal money, and girls' sports teams grew fast.

In India, a 2005 change to the law gave Hindu daughters the same right as sons to inherit family property. Since 1993, at least a third of village council seats have been reserved for women.

Work still unfinished

Around the world, women still earn less than men on average, and hold fewer seats in parliaments and fewer top jobs. In some places, girls are kept out of school altogether, which is why Malala Yousafzai became famous for speaking up.

Progress has also come from people who simply did the work. Indian women scientists helped send probes to Mars and the Moon, and in the 1960s, Black American women did the maths for NASA's early crewed flights. Each first made the next one easier.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The 19th Amendment gave every American woman the vote in 1920.

Truth: It banned denying the vote because of sex, but many women were still kept out. Many Native American women could not vote until after 1924, and many Black women in the South were blocked by unfair rules until the Voting Rights Act of 1965.
