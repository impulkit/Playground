What is Pattachitra?

In a village near Puri, painters still grind seashells for white paint and boil tamarind seeds to make their canvas, much as their families did generations ago.

The short answer

Pattachitra is a style of painting from Odisha, on India's east coast. The name joins two Sanskrit words: patta, meaning cloth, and chitra, meaning picture. Painters work on specially prepared cotton cloth, using colours they often make by hand.

The pictures are bold and crowded. Gods and heroes have large eyes and sharp profiles, and every gap is filled with flowers, leaves or tiny patterns. A thick border runs around the edge like a frame. Most paintings tell stories about Lord Jagannath of Puri, Krishna, or scenes from the Ramayana and Mahabharata.

Surprising but true

Every year, while the gods of Puri's Jagannath temple are said to be resting and unwell, painted Pattachitra images take their place for worshippers.

A canvas made from tamarind

Making the canvas takes days. Painters soak tamarind seeds and grind them into a sticky gum. They spread it between two layers of old cotton cloth so the layers stick together. Once dry, the cloth is coated with a paste of chalk and gum, then rubbed smooth with a stone.

The result is stiff and slightly shiny, a little like thick card. It can hold fine lines without the paint soaking in or spreading.

Preparing canvas has traditionally been shared work in painter families. Some members prepare the cloth and grind colours, while the most skilled hands draw the fine lines and faces. A large painting can take weeks, and a very detailed one can take months.

Colours from shells and soot

Traditional painters make many of their own colours. White comes from powdered conch shells. Black comes from soot collected from an oil lamp. Red and yellow come from ground minerals, and other colours from plants. The powders are mixed with gum from the kaitha, or wood apple, tree so that they stick.

Brushes are homemade too. Fine ones were made from a few hairs of a mouse or squirrel tied to a stick, and thicker ones from buffalo hair. The painter starts with the border, then outlines the figures, fills them with flat colour and finally adds the delicate patterns.

Paintings for resting gods

Pattachitra grew up around the Jagannath temple in Puri. Every summer, after a grand bathing festival, the temple's three deities are said to fall ill. For about two weeks they are hidden from view while they recover. During this time, called anasara, worshippers pray before painted images of the gods instead.

Painters also sold small pictures to pilgrims as souvenirs to carry home, long before postcards existed. The temple and its yearly chariot festival, the Rath Yatra, are still favourite subjects. Jagannath, with his round eyes and broad smile, is easy to recognise even in a tiny painting, so he appears on everything from wall hangings to bookmarks.

The village of painters

Raghurajpur, a short drive from Puri, is a village where many families make Pattachitra. Houses have painted walls, and children often learn by helping their parents. Visitors can watch the whole process, from tamarind paste to finished painting. The Odisha government has developed it as a heritage crafts village.

The same artists often make tala pattachitra, where a design is scratched onto dried palm leaves with an iron stylus and then rubbed with soot so the lines show black. Some palm-leaf pieces fold out like a fan or a tiny book.

Neighbouring Bengal has its own scroll tradition, with a similar name. There, painters called patuas unroll long vertical scrolls one picture at a time while singing the story, a bit like a hand-drawn comic performed live.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pattachitra is just painting on paper with bright colours.

Truth: The traditional canvas is cloth, carefully layered with chalk and gum until it is smooth and stiff. Painters make most of their own colours, and the craft also includes pictures scratched onto palm leaves.

Try this

Fold a long strip of paper into four panels, like a mini scroll. Paint or draw a story you know, one moment per panel, and give every panel a thick decorated border. Then unroll it slowly for someone and tell the story aloud as each picture appears.
