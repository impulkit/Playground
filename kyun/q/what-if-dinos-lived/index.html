What if dinosaurs had never died out?

Sixty-six million years ago, a rock about 10 km wide hit Earth. If it had missed, you might never have been born.

The short answer

If the asteroid had missed, the world would almost certainly look very different. Big dinosaurs might still rule the land. Mammals, which were mostly small and hid in the shadows, might never have grown big. That probably means no elephants, no whales, no monkeys, and no people.

But nobody can be sure. Some scientists think dinosaurs were already declining, or that the giant volcanoes of the Deccan Traps in India were making life hard. Others think they were doing fine. It's one of the biggest "what if" questions in science.

Surprising but true

Dinosaurs never fully died out. Every pigeon, crow and peacock is a living dinosaur, and there are about 11,000 kinds of bird today.

The day the world changed

About 66 million years ago, an asteroid about 10 km wide, taller than Mount Everest, slammed into the sea near what is now Mexico. It left the Chicxulub crater, about 180 km across.

The blast threw dust and gas high into the sky, blocking sunlight for months or years. Plants struggled, food chains collapsed, and about three-quarters of all species died out. Every non-bird dinosaur disappeared.

At the same time, huge lava flows were pouring across western India. They built much of the Deccan plateau, the land under Mumbai, Pune and Nashik. Those eruptions added to the chaos.

The mammals' big chance

Mammals had already lived alongside dinosaurs for over 100 million years. Most were the size of mice, shrews or cats, many active at night.

Once the big dinosaurs were gone, mammals spread into empty spaces. Within about 15 million years there were early horses, bats, whales and primates. Over tens of millions more years, one primate line led to apes, and then to humans. Without the asteroid, those empty spaces would have stayed full of dinosaurs.

What would dinosaurs be like now?

They wouldn't look like the ones in museums. Evolution never stops, and 66 million years is a long time. That's more than 20 times longer than humans have existed.

Some groups might have kept getting bigger, like the long-necked sauropods. Others might have shrunk or gone extinct for other reasons. Grasses spread across the world after the dinosaurs died, and dinosaurs might have evolved new grazers to eat them.

Climate would matter too. Earth cooled a lot over the past 50 million years, with ice ages. Big dinosaurs might have had to adapt to cold, perhaps growing thicker coats of feathers.

The "dinosauroid"

In 1982, a Canadian palaeontologist named Dale Russell made a thought experiment. He picked a small, clever, big-eyed dinosaur called Troodon and asked what it might become if it kept getting brainier.

He ended up with a model called the "dinosauroid": upright, two-legged, with big eyes and three-fingered hands. It looked rather like a green person. Most scientists today think it looked too human. A smart dinosaur would more likely keep a long tail and walk bent forward, like a bird. Clever birds already exist, after all. Crows make tools and parrots can learn words.

Dinosaurs are still here

The asteroid didn't kill all dinosaurs. One group survived: small, feathered ones that became birds. Today there are about 11,000 kinds of bird, more species than mammals.

So in a way, dinosaurs did survive and kept on evolving. They gave us hummingbirds lighter than a coin and ostriches taller than a man. The peacock dancing in a field in Rajasthan is a dinosaur's great-great-grandchild, many millions of times over.

Could it happen again?

Big asteroid hits are rare, maybe once every 100 million years or so for one that size. Today, astronomers track thousands of asteroids near Earth. In 2022, NASA's DART spacecraft crashed into a small asteroid and nudged it off course, proving we might one day protect ourselves.

The dinosaurs had no telescopes. We do, thanks to a long chain of luck that began when mammals took over an empty world.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Dinosaurs and early humans lived at the same time.

Truth: The last non-bird dinosaurs died about 66 million years ago. The earliest humans appeared only a few million years ago. Less time separates T. rex from us than separated T. rex from Stegosaurus.

Try this

Go outside or look out of a window for ten minutes and count every bird you see or hear. Each one is a dinosaur descendant. Draw one and label the features it shares with its ancestors, such as scaly feet, a wishbone and feathers.
