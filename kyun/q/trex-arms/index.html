Why did T. rex have such tiny arms?

T. rex couldn't even scratch its own nose. So what were the arms for?

The short answer

T. rex's arms were only about 1 metre long, on a body about 12 metres long. That is roughly the length of a grown-up's arm, on an animal as long as a city bus.

Its giant head and jaws did the hunting. Over millions of years, as tyrannosaur heads got bigger, their arms got smaller. Nobody knows for certain what the arms were still used for. In science, it's fine to say "we don't know yet". That's where new discoveries start.

Surprising but true

Each tiny arm may have been strong enough to curl about 200 kg, more than two grown-ups.

Short but strong

The arms were stubby, but they were not weak. The bones were thick, and they show big rough patches where powerful muscles were attached. In 2001, Kenneth Carpenter and Matt Smith studied some of the best-preserved T. rex arm bones. They estimated that the main arm muscle could have curled about 200 kg. That is more than two adults.

Each hand had two working fingers with sharp claws, plus a tiny third bone that did nothing much. The arm could swing only a short way at the shoulder and elbow. It couldn't reach the mouth.

For years, nobody even knew what the hands looked like. The first skeletons were found without them, so early drawings guessed, and some gave T. rex three fingers. A nearly complete arm was not found until 1988, in Montana, by a rancher named Kathy Wankel.

Reading muscles from bones

Muscles rarely fossilise, but they leave clues. Where a muscle grips a bone, it leaves a scar or a ridge. Bigger muscles leave bigger marks. Scientists compare these with living animals, mostly birds and crocodiles, to work out the size and pull of muscles that vanished long ago. This is one of the tools in rebuilding a dinosaur from its bones.

The main ideas

Scientists have suggested several uses.

One idea is that the arms held on to struggling prey, pinning it close to the chest while the jaws did the work. Another is that they helped T. rex push itself up from lying down, giving a little lift before the legs took over. A third is that they were used for gripping a partner during mating.

In 2022, Kevin Padian suggested something different. Maybe several T. rex sometimes fed on one carcass together. Long arms would have been in the way of all those snapping jaws, and easily bitten. Shorter arms could have been safer. Not everyone agrees, because we don't know for sure that T. rex fed in groups.

The same trick, twice

T. rex wasn't alone. The abelisaurs, a separate family of big hunters that included India's Rajasaurus, ended up with even shorter arms. They weren't close relatives. Two different families arrived at a similar shape, probably because both relied on huge heads for hunting.

When the same feature evolves separately in different groups, scientists call it convergent evolution. Wings in bats and birds are another example. So are the streamlined bodies of dolphins and sharks, one a mammal and the other a fish.

Leftovers, or useful?

Some body parts shrink because they are no longer much use. Whales still carry tiny hip bones from their walking ancestors. Were T. rex's arms like that, slowly fading away? Their strong muscles suggest they still did something worth keeping. The answer may also be a mix: shrinking over time, yet still handy for a job or two.

Myth or fact?

Myth: T. rex's arms were weak and useless, like leftover parts.

Truth: The arm bones were thick and had large marks where strong muscles attached. They were short, but they were not feeble. What they were used for is the open question.

Try this

Tuck your elbows tight against your sides and try to touch your mouth, your nose and your knees using only your hands. Now try to pick up a small ball from a table without bending at the waist. You'll quickly see what T. rex could and couldn't reach.
