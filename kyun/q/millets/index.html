Why are millets making a comeback?

Ragi, jowar and bajra fed India for thousands of years, nearly vanished from city plates, and are now back in fashion.

The short answer

Millets are small, hardy grains such as ragi (finger millet), jowar (sorghum) and bajra (pearl millet). For thousands of years they were staple foods across dry parts of India and Africa, because they grow in heat and poor soil with little water.

After the 1960s, rice and wheat took over many farms and kitchens, and millets came to be seen as old-fashioned. Now they are returning. Farmers like that they survive droughts, doctors like their fibre and minerals, and the government has campaigned to bring them back to everyday meals.

Surprising but true

A bowl of ragi has many times more calcium than the same amount of rice or wheat.

Grains for tough places

Millets are tough. Pearl millet can grow in the sandy soils of Rajasthan where rice would die. Many millets need far less water than rice and ripen quickly, sometimes in under three months.

That matters more as weather becomes harder to predict. In a year when the monsoon arrives late, a field of bajra can still give a harvest while thirstier crops fail. Many millets also need little fertiliser, so they suit small farmers who cannot spend much.

What causes a drought?In 2016, a town in Maharashtra ran so dry that a train carried its drinking water in tank wagons.

An old Indian food

Millet grains have been found at Harappan sites, the cities of the Indus Valley. Ragi probably came to India from the highlands of East Africa around 4,000 years ago and became a favourite in the south.

Every region has its millet dish. Karnataka has ragi mudde, a soft ball dipped in sambar. Maharashtra has jowar bhakri, a thick flatbread. Rajasthan eats bajra rotis with garlic chutney, and the hills of Uttarakhand have mandua rotis made from ragi.

How millets lost their place

In the 1960s India faced food shortages. The Green Revolution brought new high-yielding varieties of wheat and rice, with fertilisers and irrigation. Harvests grew enormously and famines ended.

But government support, cheap ration grain and city tastes all favoured rice and wheat. Millets started to look like "poor people's food". Over the following decades the area of land growing millets in India shrank a great deal, and many families switched from bajra rotis to wheat chapatis.

The comeback

India proposed, and the United Nations declared, 2023 the International Year of Millets. The Indian government began calling them Shree Anna, and some states added ragi and jowar to school meals and ration shops.

Millets are rich in fibre and minerals such as iron and magnesium, and ragi is unusually high in calcium. They are also gluten-free, which helps people who cannot eat wheat. Still, scientists warn that millets are good food, not medicine. Some claims on packets go further than the evidence.

Cooking with millets

Millets can be tricky in the kitchen, which is one reason they fell out of favour in busy city homes. Millet flour has no gluten, so a bajra roti cannot stretch like a wheat chapati. Cooks pat it out by hand on a wet cloth or between their palms, and it cracks easily.

New recipes are helping. Shops now sell ragi dosa mix, jowar flakes, millet noodles and even millet biscuits. Some cooks simply swap half the rice in a pulao or khichdi for foxtail millet. Old grains, it turns out, can fit into new routines.

Millets in America

In the United States, millets are mostly a farm crop. Kansas is the biggest grower of sorghum, much of which feeds cattle or is turned into fuel. Proso millet fills bird feeders across the country. A few American bakers and chefs now use sorghum flour, often learning recipes from Indian and African cooks who never stopped.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Millets are just bird seed.

Truth: In the United States, millet is best known in bird feeders. But in India and much of Africa, millets are everyday food for hundreds of millions of people, eaten as rotis, porridge, dosas and more.

Try this

Soak a spoon of ragi or bajra in water overnight. Next morning, drain it and wrap it in a damp cloth. Keep the cloth moist and check every day. In two or three days you should see tiny white sprouts.
