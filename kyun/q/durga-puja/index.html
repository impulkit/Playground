Why is Durga Puja so grand in Kolkata?

For five days each autumn, Kolkata turns into an open-air art gallery, with thousands of pandals built like temples, castles, ships and even spacecraft.

The short answer

Durga Puja is a Hindu festival that honours the goddess Durga. In Hindu tradition, she defeated Mahishasura, a buffalo demon whom none of the gods could beat alone. It is celebrated across India, but nowhere quite as grandly as in Kolkata and the rest of West Bengal.

For five main days in September or October, Kolkata fills with pandals, temporary structures built to house clay images of Durga and her children. Some neighbourhoods spend months planning theirs. Millions of people walk from one pandal to the next late into the night, eating street food and admiring the art.

Surprising but true

In 2021, UNESCO added Durga Puja in Kolkata to its list of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, praising it as a festival of public art as well as worship.

The goddess and her family

Durga is shown with ten arms, each holding a weapon given by a different god, and riding a lion. At her feet lies Mahishasura. Beside her stand her four children: Lakshmi, goddess of wealth; Saraswati, goddess of learning; Ganesh, remover of obstacles; and Kartik, the warrior.

In Bengal, Durga's arrival is also seen as a daughter coming home. Many people believe she visits her parents' house each autumn with her children, then returns to her husband, Shiva, in the Himalayas. So the festival feels like a family reunion, happy and a little sad at the end.

Clay from the river

Many of Kolkata's Durga images are made in Kumartuli, a crowded neighbourhood of potters and sculptors in the north of the city. Work starts months before. Artists build frames of bamboo and straw, then cover them in layers of clay from the banks of the Hooghly river.

Once dry, the figures are painted, dressed in real cloth and decorated with jewellery. The eyes are painted last, in a ceremony called chokkhu daan, the "gift of eyes". Kumartuli images are sent across India and even abroad.

How is pottery made?A lump of mud becomes a pot as hard as stone in a fiery oven, and once that happens it can never turn back into mud.

From palaces to neighbourhoods

In the 1700s and 1800s, rich families in Calcutta held grand Durga Pujas in their mansions, inviting guests and British officials. One of the best known, at Shobhabazar Rajbari, dates back to 1757.

In the early 1900s, neighbourhood committees began holding sarbojanin pujas, meaning "for everyone". Anyone could visit, whatever their caste or income. These community pujas spread across the city and became the heart of the festival. Today Kolkata has thousands of them.

Theme pandals

Since the 1990s, many committees have competed to build theme pandals. One year a pandal might copy a famous temple, the next it might be made of clay cups, buttons or bicycle parts. Some show scenes from village life; others take on subjects like climate change or the Chandrayaan mission to the Moon.

Companies give prizes for the best pandals, images and lighting. Artists, many from small towns and villages, travel to the city for months of work. This is one reason UNESCO described the festival as a public art event as well as a religious one.

Sindoor and goodbye

On Dashami, the last day, married women smear red sindoor on the goddess and on each other, a custom called sindoor khela. Then the images are carried through the streets, with drums called dhak, to be immersed in the river.

Because paints and decorations can pollute water, Kolkata now cleans the river quickly after immersion, and many groups use natural colours. The festival ends with people visiting elders and saying "Shubho Bijoya". Many begin counting the days until next year's Puja almost at once, a feeling shared by fans of Ganesh Chaturthi in Mumbai.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Durga Puja and Navratri are completely different festivals.

Truth: Both honour the goddess at the same time of year. In Bengal and eastern India, the celebration centres on Durga Puja and her victory over Mahishasura; elsewhere it may be celebrated as Navratri and Dussehra, with different customs.

Try this

Design your own theme pandal on paper. Choose a theme, such as the ocean, a rainforest or a space station, and draw the outside, the entrance and where the goddess would stand inside. Use only everyday materials in your plan, such as bamboo, cloth, paper and clay, as real pandal makers often do.
