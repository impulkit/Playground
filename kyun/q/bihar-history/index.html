Why is Bihar so important in Indian history?

The Buddha found enlightenment here, Mahavira was born here, and the capital of India's first great empire stood on the banks of the Ganga at Patna.

The short answer

For more than a thousand years, the region that is now Bihar sat at the centre of Indian history. It was the heart of Magadha, the kingdom that grew into the Mauryan Empire, India's first empire to cover most of the subcontinent. Its capital, Pataliputra, is today's Patna.

Bihar is also where two great religious teachers lived and taught. The Buddha attained enlightenment at Bodh Gaya, and Mahavira, the great teacher of Jainism, was born and died in the region. Later came Nalanda, one of the world's great centres of learning. Even the state's name comes from vihara, the word for a Buddhist monastery.

Surprising but true

Pataliputra, today's Patna, was one of the largest cities in the world around 300 BCE, protected by a wooden wall with hundreds of towers.

The Buddha under the tree

In the 5th century BCE, or perhaps a little earlier, a prince named Siddhartha Gautama left his comfortable life to search for an answer to human suffering. According to Buddhist tradition, he sat in meditation under a pipal tree at Bodh Gaya and reached enlightenment. From then on he was called the Buddha, "the awakened one".

A descendant of that tree still grows beside the Mahabodhi Temple at Bodh Gaya, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2002. Buddhists from Sri Lanka, Thailand, Japan, Tibet and many other places travel there to pray. Monasteries built by many of those countries now stand around the town, each in its own national style.

At around the same time, Mahavira taught non-violence towards all living things. Jain tradition says he was born near Vaishali and reached his final liberation at Pavapuri, both in Bihar.

An empire from Pataliputra

Around 321 BCE, Chandragupta Maurya, helped by his clever adviser Chanakya, seized power in Magadha. He and his successors built an empire that stretched from Afghanistan to Karnataka. A Greek visitor called Megasthenes described Pataliputra as a huge city surrounded by a wooden wall with hundreds of towers and a deep moat.

Chandragupta's grandson Ashoka carved messages about kindness and fair rule on rocks and pillars across India. One of his pillar tops, the lion capital from Sarnath, is now India's national emblem.

Who was Aryabhata?Around 1,500 years ago, a 23-year-old astronomer in India wrote that the stars only seem to move because the Earth itself is spinning.

A university of the ancient world

From the 5th century CE, Nalanda grew into a vast monastery-university. Students came from China, Korea, Tibet and South-East Asia to study Buddhism, logic, grammar, medicine and astronomy. The Chinese traveller Xuanzang studied there in the 7th century and wrote about its towers, libraries and strict entrance tests.

Nalanda was attacked and badly damaged around the end of the 12th century, and slowly fell into ruin. Its red-brick remains were excavated much later and became a World Heritage Site in 2016. The great mathematician Aryabhata, born in 476 CE, is also linked to the region, and is often said to have worked near Pataliputra.

A republic, a Guru and a satyagraha

Vaishali was home to the Licchavis, who were governed by an assembly of leading families rather than a single king. That's why it is often called one of the world's earliest republics, although historians debate how far it was like a modern republic.

Bihar's story kept going. Guru Gobind Singh, the tenth Sikh Guru, was born in Patna in 1666, and a gurdwara marks the place. In 1917, Gandhi led his first major campaign in India at Champaran, in northern Bihar, supporting farmers forced to grow indigo for British planters. It became a model for the larger struggles that followed.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Nalanda was a single building, like a modern college.

Truth: Nalanda was a huge campus of monasteries, temples, lecture halls and libraries, built and rebuilt over several centuries. Its ruins today cover a large area, and only part of the site has been dug up.
