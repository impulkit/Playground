Is the giant squid real?

For centuries, sailors told tales of a sea monster with long arms. It turned out to be real, but nobody filmed one alive in the deep until 2012.

The short answer

Yes. The giant squid is a real animal that lives in the deep ocean all around the world. A large one can be about 12 or 13 m long from the tip of its body to the ends of its two long tentacles, roughly as long as a school bus. Females are bigger than males.

For a long time, people knew giant squid only from dead or dying bodies washed onto beaches or caught in fishing nets, and from tales of sea monsters. Nobody photographed a live one in its home until 2004, and the first video of one in the deep sea was made in 2012. Giant squid are hard to find because they live hundreds of metres down, in the dim twilight zone and below, where sunlight barely reaches.

Surprising but true

A giant squid's eye can be about 27 cm across, as big as a dinner plate, among the largest eyes of any animal.

Body of a giant

A giant squid has a long, torpedo-shaped body called a mantle, with a fin on each side. Around its head are eight arms lined with suckers and two much longer tentacles that shoot out to grab prey. Each sucker has a ring of tiny sharp teeth. Its mouth hides a hard, curved beak, like a parrot's.

Much of the animal's length comes from its tentacles. The body itself may be only about 2 m long. Like octopuses, squid have blue blood and three hearts, and can squirt dark ink, as you can read in octopus ink. They belong to the same group of animals as the octopus.

Eyes like dinner plates

Giant squid have some of the biggest eyes of any animal, up to about 27 cm across. That is roughly ten times wider than your own eyes. In the deep, there is very little light, so big eyes help collect what there is.

Scientists think their eyes may have a special job. Their main enemy, the sperm whale, is huge, and as it swims it disturbs tiny animals that give off flashes of light. A giant squid's big eyes may spot that glow from far away, giving it a chance to escape.

Battles in the dark

Sperm whales dive deep to hunt squid. Whalers found squid beaks, which can't be digested, inside the stomachs of sperm whales by the thousand. Some whales have round scars on their skin from squid suckers. Nobody has ever filmed a fight between the two, but the scars show that the squid fight back.

Giant squid eat fish and other squid. They probably drift and wait rather than chase, then strike with their tentacles. How long they live, and how they find mates in the vast dark ocean, are still mysteries.

Finally on camera

In 2004, two Japanese scientists, Tsunemi Kubodera and Kyoichi Mori, lowered a camera on a long line near the Ogasawara Islands south of Tokyo. It took hundreds of photos of a giant squid attacking bait about 900 m down. In 2012, a team using a submersible filmed one swimming in the deep for the first time. In 2019, an American team filmed a young giant squid in the Gulf of Mexico, using a camera with a fake glowing jellyfish as bait.

You can see real giant squid preserved in museums. At the Natural History Museum in London, a squid nicknamed Archie, about 8.6 m long, floats in a giant tank. The Smithsonian in Washington, DC, displays giant squid too. Its even heavier cousin, the colossal squid of the Southern Ocean, is just as mysterious. The deep sea still hides animals like these, which is why ocean explorers keep going down.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Giant squid attack and sink ships.

Truth: There is no reliable record of a giant squid sinking a ship. Those stories come from old legends like the Kraken. Giant squid live hundreds of metres down and rarely come near the surface while alive.

Try this

Measure a giant squid. Using a measuring tape, mark out 12 m in a school corridor or playground with chalk or string. That is about how long a large giant squid can be from fin tip to tentacle tip. Now draw a 27 cm circle at one end for its eye.
