What are America's midterm elections, and why do they matter?

Every four years, Americans choose a president. But two years into each term, they vote again, and that vote can decide whether the president gets anything done at all.

The short answer

The USA holds a big national election every two years. Every fourth year, Americans choose a president. In the years in between, halfway through the president's term, they choose members of Congress, the body that makes the country's federal laws, the ones for the whole nation. These in-between votes are called midterm elections.

The president is not on the ballot. But if voters hand Congress to the other party, the president suddenly needs their votes to pass laws or spend money. That is why midterms are often called a report card on the president.

Surprising but true

Since the Second World War, the president's party has lost seats in the House of Representatives in almost every midterm election. The only exceptions came in 1998 and 2002.

How it works

Congress has two parts. The House of Representatives has 435 members, and each serves just two years, so every single seat is up for election in a midterm. The Senate has 100 members, two from each state, and each serves six years. Their terms are staggered, so only about one-third face voters at a time.

Think of a school with a head teacher and a student council. The head teacher is chosen for four years. The council is chosen every two years. The head teacher can suggest new rules, but the council must agree before any rule is made.

So a midterm can change everything. If the president's party keeps both chambers, the president can usually pass big plans. If the other party wins one or both, the president must bargain, compromise or give up. Americans call this "divided government".

On the same day, many states also choose governors, state lawmakers, judges and local officials. Some states even ask voters to decide questions directly, a little like a referendum.

Where it came from

The rules come from the US Constitution, written in 1787. Its writers wanted the House to stay close to ordinary people, so they gave it short two-year terms. They made the Senate calmer and slower, with six-year terms. The president got four years.

Those three numbers, two, four and six, were never planned to create midterms. They simply did. The first Congress met in 1789, and Americans have voted every two years since, even during the Civil War and both World Wars.

The idea of "checks and balances" sits underneath it all. In a healthy democracy, no single person or group should hold all the power, so each part of government can slow down the others. The president can veto, or reject, a law. Congress can override that veto, but only with two-thirds of both chambers.

India's story

India has no exact midterm, because India runs a parliamentary system. Here the Prime Minister comes from whichever party or group controls the Lok Sabha. Lose that majority, and you lose the job. In the USA, the president and the lawmakers are chosen separately, so a president can stay in office while the other party runs Congress.

The closest thing India has is the steady stream of state elections. Because different states vote in different years, each round is often read as a mood check on the government in Delhi.

US midterms still matter to India. Congress votes on trade rules, defence deals and visa laws, such as the H-1B work visas used by many Indian engineers. It also watches how a president uses tariffs, the taxes put on goods arriving from abroad. In 2025, US tariffs on many Indian goods went up to 50%, before an early deal in 2026 brought them down to 18%. That is why Indian exporters follow these results closely, because trade between the two countries is worth billions of dollars.

The first person of Indian birth to sit in Congress was Dalip Singh Saund, a farmer from Punjab, elected in 1956.

The numbers that matter

  • - 435 and 35: in 2026, every House seat and 35 of the 100 Senate seats were up for election. That is why one day can flip a whole chamber.
  • - 2 of about 20: since 1946, the president's party has gained House seats in a midterm only twice, in 1998 and 2002. Losing seats is the normal pattern.
  • - About half: in 2018, roughly half of eligible Americans voted in the midterm, the highest share in over 100 years. In recent presidential years, about 6 in 10 or more have voted.
  • - Two-thirds: the share of both chambers needed to override a president's veto. A party with a small majority cannot do it alone.
  • - 43 days: the longest government shutdown, in 2025. It happened because Congress and the president could not agree on a spending law, so many offices closed and workers went unpaid.

What people argue about

Some Americans like divided government. They say it forces the two parties to talk, stops sudden swings and protects against any one leader growing too powerful. Others say it causes "gridlock", where nothing gets done, budgets are late and important problems are left waiting.

People also argue about how voting districts are drawn. In many states, politicians draw the map of House districts themselves. Critics say some maps are shaped to help one party win more seats than its votes deserve. Supporters of the current system say elected officials are accountable to voters, while courts can step in when maps break the law.

There is debate, too, over what a midterm really means. Some see it as a clear verdict on the president. Others say local issues, candidates and turnout matter just as much.

Myths and mix-ups

"The president is chosen in a midterm." No. The president's name is not on the ballot. The presidential election comes two years later.

"Only the House changes." The Senate changes too, but more slowly. Only about a third of senators face voters each time, so the Senate is harder to flip.

"If Congress is against the president, the president is powerless." Not quite. The president still runs the government, commands the armed forces, leads foreign policy and can veto laws. But passing new laws and setting budgets becomes much harder.

What it means for you

What happens in Washington can reach an Indian family's kitchen table. US decisions on tariffs affect which Indian goods sell well in America, from shrimp and jewellery to medicines and clothes. That touches jobs in places like Tiruppur, Surat and Visakhapatnam.

Visa rules affect Indian students and workers who hope to study or work in the USA. And when the world's biggest economy changes direction, markets, the rupee and even fuel prices can move.

Next time you hear the word "midterm", remember the school council. The head teacher has not changed, but who must agree to the rules just might have.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Midterm elections can remove the president from office.

Truth: The president's name is not on the ballot in a midterm. The president stays for the full four-year term. What changes is who controls Congress, which decides how easy or hard the president's job will be.
