Why is it easier to learn languages when you're young?

At six months old, a baby can hear the difference between speech sounds from almost any language. By their first birthday, much of that skill has faded.

The short answer

Young children are not simply smarter at languages. They learn differently. A baby's brain is busily tuning itself to the sounds of whatever language it hears most. Children soak up grammar through play and chatter, without worrying about mistakes.

Older learners have advantages too. They can use rules, reading and memory tricks, so teenagers and adults often make faster progress at first. But people who start a language young are far more likely to end up sounding like a native speaker, especially in their accent.

How strict the "best age" really is remains one of the liveliest arguments in the science of language.

Surprising but true

In some studies, older learners make faster progress than young children in the first months of a new language. Children usually overtake them in the long run.

Babies tune in

In the early 1980s, the Canadian psychologist Janet Werker tested babies from English-speaking families on sounds from Hindi and from a language of western Canada. Hindi has two different "t" sounds: one with the tongue curled back, as in tamatar, and one with the tongue against the teeth, as in taal. Most English-speaking adults hear them as the same.

At six to eight months old, the babies could hear the difference. By ten to twelve months, most had lost that ability. The American researcher Patricia Kuhl found something similar with Japanese babies and the English sounds "r" and "l". A baby's brain seems to become an expert in the sounds that matter around it, and stops paying attention to the rest.

Is there a deadline?

In 1967, the linguist Eric Lenneberg proposed a "critical period" for language, a window in childhood after which learning a first language becomes much harder. A few rare, sad cases of children who grew up without hearing any language seemed to support the idea, although those children faced many other problems too.

For second languages, the picture is fuzzier. In 2018, researchers from MIT and Harvard gave an online English grammar quiz to about 670,000 people. Their results suggested that the ability to learn grammar stays strong until about 17 or 18 and then slowly declines. To reach native-like grammar, though, it seemed to help to start before about age 10. Other scientists argue that how much time you spend, and how you learn, matter as much as age.

Many languages, one head

In much of India, children grow up with two or three languages: one at home, one at school and one in the street or on the cricket field. A child in Bengaluru might speak Kannada with friends, Hindi with neighbours, Tamil with grandparents and English in class. Across India's many languages, this is ordinary, not special.

Being bilingual does not confuse children, as some parents once feared. Children may mix two languages in one sentence, but that is a normal stage, and often a clever use of both. Deaf children who learn sign language from birth reach the same milestones as hearing children who learn to speak.

Never too late

Adults can and do learn new languages, sometimes brilliantly. Joseph Conrad grew up speaking Polish and also learned French. He only began learning English in his twenties, yet he became one of the great novelists in the English language. His strong Polish accent stayed with him all his life.

That is the usual pattern. Grammar and vocabulary can be mastered at almost any age, with enough practice, but an accent is hard to change after childhood. Conrad wrote all his famous novels in English, his third language, accent and all.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Learning two languages at once confuses young children.

Truth: Children can learn two or more languages at the same time without harm. They may mix languages in a sentence for a while, which is a normal stage, and millions of children around the world grow up speaking two or three languages.

Try this

Ask a family member who speaks a language you don't know to teach you and an adult in your family to count from one to ten. Practise for a week, then test each other. Who remembers more numbers? Who sounds more like the teacher? You may find the answers are different people.
