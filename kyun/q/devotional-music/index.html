What are bhajans and kirtans?

In 1966, a 70-year-old Indian monk sat under a tree in a New York park and began singing "Hare Krishna". Within a few years, the chant was on the radio.

The short answer

Bhajans and kirtans are kinds of devotional singing from India. A bhajan is a song of love and praise for God, usually short, tuneful and easy to join in. A kirtan is a gathering where people sing God's names or praises together, often in call and response: a leader sings a line and everyone sings it back.

Both grew out of the bhakti movement, a wave of devotion that spread across India from about the 7th century. Bhakti poet-saints taught that anyone, rich or poor, high-born or low, could reach God through love and song, without needing priests or Sanskrit. Their songs are still sung in homes, temples, gurdwaras and festivals, and now in yoga studios from London to Los Angeles.

Surprising but true

At the Golden Temple in Amritsar, sacred hymns called kirtan are sung by musicians almost all day, every day, and broadcast live so people around the world can listen.

Songs of the poet-saints

Many of the best-loved bhajans were written centuries ago by poets who sang in the languages of ordinary people. Mirabai, a Rajput princess in the 1500s, gave up palace life to sing for Krishna. Kabir, a weaver from Varanasi, mocked empty rituals and sang of a God beyond any single religion. Surdas sang of Krishna's childhood, and Tulsidas retold the Ramayana in Awadhi.

The songs of Mirabai and Kabir are still sung by folk singers, classical stars and film playback singers alike.

In Maharashtra, Tukaram and Dnyaneshwar wrote songs called abhangs. Every year, lakhs of pilgrims walk to Pandharpur singing them, in a journey called the wari.

Singing together

A kirtan has a pull that is hard to resist. It usually begins slowly, with the harmonium playing a simple tune and a drum like the dholak or tabla keeping time. Small cymbals called manjira chime on the beat. The leader sings, the group answers, and the pace builds.

In Bengal in the 1500s, Chaitanya Mahaprabhu led crowds through the streets singing the names of Krishna, a style called sankirtan. Scientists who study music have found that singing together can make people feel closer to each other, which may help explain why group singing appears in nearly every culture.

How did the harmonium become India's favourite instrument?It arrived from Europe in the 1800s, was banned from All India Radio for about 30 years, and still turned up at almost every bhajan, qawwali and music class.

Kirtan in the gurdwara

For Sikhs, kirtan means singing hymns from the Guru Granth Sahib, the holy book. The Sikh Gurus wrote these hymns to be sung in particular ragas, and they are performed by trained musicians called ragis, usually with harmonium and tabla.

At the Harmandir Sahib, the Golden Temple in Amritsar, kirtan continues from early morning until late at night. Loudspeakers carry the music around the sacred pool, and the hymns are broadcast live on television and online.

Bhajans for a nation

During the freedom struggle, Mahatma Gandhi held prayer meetings with bhajans. His favourites included "Vaishnava Jana To", written by the Gujarati poet Narsinh Mehta in the 1400s, which describes a good person as one who feels the pain of others. Another, "Raghupati Raghava Raja Ram", was sung on the Salt March in 1930. Its familiar tune is usually credited to the musician Vishnu Digambar Paluskar. Both are still sung at schools and public events on 2 October.

Hare Krishna in America

In 1965, a monk named A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada arrived in the USA by cargo ship. In 1966, he began chanting under a tree in Tompkins Square Park in New York and founded the International Society for Krishna Consciousness, known as the Hare Krishna movement. Young Americans joined him.

The Beatles' George Harrison became a follower and produced a recording of the chant in 1969 that entered the British charts. Today, kirtan singers perform at festivals across America and Europe. Some singers in India now mix bhajans with rock guitars or electronic beats, while others keep to the old village tunes.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Bhajans and kirtans belong only to one religion.

Truth: The words come mostly from Hindu traditions, but Sikh kirtan is central to worship in gurdwaras, Jains sing devotional songs called stavans, and Christian churches in India sing bhajans in Indian languages. Many styles overlap with Sufi music too.

Try this

Try call and response with your family or friends. One person sings or chants a short, simple line, and everyone else repeats it. Start slowly, then speed up a little each time. Notice how quickly the group falls into the same rhythm.
