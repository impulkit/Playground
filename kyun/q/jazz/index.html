Where did jazz come from?

Jazz was born in New Orleans more than a hundred years ago, and within a few decades it was playing in hotel ballrooms in Bombay.

The short answer

Jazz grew up in New Orleans, in the southern USA, around 1900. It was created mainly by African American musicians, who mixed several kinds of music: the blues, ragtime piano, brass band marches, church spirituals and the rhythms of the Caribbean.

The result had a bouncy, swinging feel, with notes landing off the beat. Above all, jazz musicians improvised. They made up new melodies on the spot, around a familiar tune, so no two performances were exactly the same. From New Orleans, jazz spread across America and then the world.

Surprising but true

In the 1930s, African American jazz musicians played at the Taj Mahal Hotel in Bombay, and Goan musicians later carried jazz into Hindi film songs.

Roots in a painful past

The roots of jazz reach back to slavery in America. Enslaved Africans brought rhythms, songs and ways of making music from many parts of West and Central Africa. They were often forbidden to keep their traditions, but music survived in work songs, church songs and gatherings.

In New Orleans, enslaved and free Black people gathered on Sundays at a place called Congo Square to drum and dance, in the late 1700s and early 1800s. Decades later, after slavery ended, the blues gave voice to hard lives, and ragtime filled dance halls with lively piano music.

How did slavery end in the USA?For nearly 250 years, millions of people in America were bought and sold. Ending it took escapes, books, protests and a civil war.

A city full of sound

New Orleans was a port city with French, Spanish, African and Caribbean influences. Brass bands played at parades, picnics and funerals, slow on the way to the cemetery and joyful on the way back.

In this mix, musicians began to play together in a new way. A trumpet led the tune, a clarinet wove around it, and a trombone added low slides beneath, all improvising at once over drums and bass. In 1917, a band made the first jazz record, and the new sound reached homes across the country.

Louis Armstrong and the solo

Louis Armstrong, born in New Orleans in 1901, changed jazz. His trumpet solos were bold, inventive and full of feeling. He showed that one player stepping forward to improvise could be the most exciting part of a song. He also sang in a gravelly voice and helped make "scat" singing popular, using made-up syllables instead of words.

Many Black musicians, like Armstrong, moved north to cities such as Chicago and New York during the Great Migration, looking for work and escaping unfair laws in the South. Jazz went with them.

Swing, bebop and beyond

In the 1930s, big bands led by musicians like Duke Ellington played swing music for dancing across America. In the 1940s, younger players invented bebop: fast, complex and made for listening. Later came cool jazz, Latin jazz and jazz mixed with rock.

All this happened while Black Americans faced segregation, and jazz musicians often could not stay in the hotels where they performed. Their fight for fairness was part of the wider civil rights story.

Jazz in Bombay

Jazz reached India in the 1920s and 1930s. African American musicians, including the pianist Teddy Weatherford, played at the Taj Mahal Hotel in Bombay, now Mumbai. Musicians from Goa, many trained in church music, picked up jazz quickly and played in dance halls.

Some of these Goan musicians later worked as arrangers for Hindi films. They brought jazz trumpets, saxophones and swing rhythms into many Bollywood songs of the 1950s and 1960s. In 1963, Duke Ellington's orchestra toured India, and audiences heard the music from its source.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Jazz musicians just play random notes when they improvise.

Truth: Improvising follows the chords and shape of the tune, and players spend years learning scales, patterns and the music of earlier players. It is more like a skilled conversation than random talk.

Try this

Play a call-and-response game with a friend. One person claps or sings a short pattern of four beats, and the other answers with a different pattern of the same length. Keep going without stopping to think. That quick back-and-forth is close to how jazz musicians improvise together.
