How do you keep your heart healthy?

Your heart beats about 1 lakh times a day without a single day off. The habits that keep it strong start in childhood.

The short answer

Your heart is a muscle about the size of your fist. It pumps blood around your body roughly 1 lakh times a day, carrying oxygen and food to every cell.

Keeping it healthy is mostly about everyday habits. Move your body every day, eat plenty of fruit, vegetables, pulses and whole grains, go easy on salty snacks and sugary drinks, get enough sleep and never start smoking. For most children, the heart is strong and healthy. The point of good habits is to keep it that way for the next 80 years.

Surprising but true

Doctors studying young people who died in accidents have found the first fatty streaks in artery walls in some children and teenagers, decades before any heart trouble shows.

A muscle that never rests

Each heartbeat squeezes blood out into a network of tubes. Arteries carry it away from the heart, veins bring it back, and tiny capillaries connect the two. Laid end to end, an adult's blood vessels would stretch for tens of thousands of kilometres.

Like any muscle, the heart gets stronger with use. When you run, cycle or play football, your heart beats faster to deliver more oxygen, as explained in heartbeat and exercise. Over weeks and months, regular exercise makes the heart pump more blood with each beat. That is why trained athletes often have a slower resting pulse than other people.

What goes wrong

The most common heart problems in adults start inside the arteries. Over many years, fatty material called plaque can build up in their walls, making them narrower and stiffer. If an artery feeding the heart muscle gets blocked, part of the heart is starved of oxygen. That is a heart attack. A blocked artery in the brain causes a stroke.

The World Health Organization says diseases of the heart and blood vessels kill more people than any other cause worldwide. Many of those deaths are linked to things people can change, such as smoking, inactivity, unhealthy food and high blood pressure.

Why do we need salt?Every message your brain sends to your muscles depends on salt, and in 1930 a handful of it helped shake the British Empire.

Food for a strong heart

No single food is magic, but some patterns help. Diets with plenty of vegetables, fruit, dal, beans, nuts and whole grains are linked to healthier hearts.

Too much salt can raise blood pressure over time, which strains the heart and blood vessels. Much of the salt people eat hides in packaged snacks, instant noodles, pickles and fast food, not just the salt shaker. Sugary drinks and lots of fried junk food can lead to weight gain and other changes that are hard on the heart.

Sleep, stress and screens

Sleep gives the heart a break, because your heart rate and blood pressure drop at night. Children aged 6 to 12 generally need 9 to 12 hours. Long hours sitting still in front of screens, without breaks, mean less exercise and often more snacking.

Feeling worried or stressed now and then is normal. Ongoing stress can affect sleep and eating, though, so talking to a parent, teacher or friend about worries is good for your whole body.

Heart health in India and the USA

Heart disease is a leading cause of death in both India and the United States. Studies suggest people of South Asian origin, in India and abroad, tend to develop heart disease at younger ages than many other groups. Doctors think genes, diet and lifestyle all play a part.

Both countries run campaigns to cut smoking and salt, and to get children moving. If you have questions about your own heart, such as a racing heartbeat when you are resting or chest pain, tell a parent and see a doctor. Most of the time it turns out to be nothing serious, but it's always worth checking.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Heart disease is only something old people need to worry about.

Truth: Heart attacks are much rarer in the young, but the narrowing of arteries can begin in childhood and builds up over decades. Habits formed early, like being active and not smoking, make a big difference later.

Try this

Find your pulse on the inside of your wrist with two fingers and count the beats for 15 seconds, then multiply by 4. Now do jumping jacks for one minute and measure again. Rest for two minutes and measure a third time. A fit heart slows back down more quickly.
