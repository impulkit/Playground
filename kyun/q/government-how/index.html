What does a government do?

A government decides which side of the road you drive on, what's in your textbooks, and who fixes the streetlight outside your home.

The short answer

A government is the group of people and organisations that run a country, state or town. It makes rules, called laws, that everyone must follow. It runs services people share, like roads, schools, hospitals and the army. It collects taxes to pay for them, and it keeps order through police and courts.

Different countries pick their governments in different ways. In a democracy such as India or the USA, citizens choose their leaders by voting, and leaders must follow a constitution. In other places, kings, the army or a single party hold power. Either way, someone has to decide how a country is run.

Surprising but true

Indian Railways, run by the Government of India, employs more than 12 lakh people, making it one of the largest employers in the world.

Three branches

Most democracies split government power into three parts, so no single person or group has too much.

The legislature makes laws. In India it is Parliament, with the Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha. In the USA it is Congress, with the House of Representatives and the Senate.

The executive carries laws out and runs the government day to day. In India, this is led by the Prime Minister and ministers. In the USA, it is led by the President.

The judiciary, the courts, settles disputes and decides whether laws and actions follow the constitution.

A French writer called Montesquieu argued for separating these powers in 1748, and the idea shaped both the US and Indian constitutions.

Many levels

A big country can't be run from one office. India's government has three levels. The Union government in New Delhi handles defence, money, railways and foreign affairs. Each state government runs its own police, hospitals and most schools. Local governments, panchayats in villages and municipalities in towns, look after water, drains, streetlights and local roads.

The USA works in a similar way: a federal government in Washington, D.C., 50 state governments, and counties and cities below them.

What governments actually do

Some government work is easy to spot: a policeman directing traffic, a government school, a passport office. Some is hidden. Governments test medicines, check that food is safe, forecast the weather and decide what time it is officially.

Governments also run huge services. Indian Railways carries crores of passengers every day and is owned by the government. The mid-day meal scheme serves a hot lunch to more than ten crore schoolchildren in government schools.

Paying the bills

Governments get most of their money from taxes. Workers pay income tax, shoppers pay GST or sales tax on things they buy, and companies pay tax on their profits. Each year India's Finance Minister presents a budget to Parliament, explaining how much the government plans to collect and spend.

When a government spends more than it collects, it borrows. How much to tax, spend and borrow is one of the biggest arguments in politics everywhere.

Keeping governments in check

Because governments are powerful, democracies build in ways to watch them. Voters can remove leaders at the next election. Courts can cancel laws that break the constitution. Newspapers and TV report what leaders do. Citizens can protest peacefully and ask questions. In India, the Right to Information Act of 2005 lets any citizen ask a government office for information, and it must reply within 30 days.

Even a small thing, like a broken streetlight, is the government's job. Many cities now let people report problems online, and a crew is sent to fix them.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Prime Minister or President makes all the laws.

Truth: In India and the USA, laws are made by elected lawmakers in Parliament or Congress. Leaders can propose laws and approve or veto them, and courts can cancel laws that break the constitution.
