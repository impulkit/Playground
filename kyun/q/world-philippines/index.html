What is special about the Philippines?

The Philippines is made of 7,641 islands, and a careful recount in 2016 found over 500 more than anyone had listed before.

The short answer

Pick almost any record for wildness and the Philippines turns up. It is a country of 7,641 islands in the western Pacific, strung out over about 1,850 km from north to south, roughly the distance from Delhi to Bengaluru. Only about 2,000 of those islands have people living on them.

It sits on the Pacific "Ring of Fire", so it has volcanoes, earthquakes and about 20 typhoons a year. It also has some of the richest seas on Earth and animals found nowhere else. Its 11.3 crore people (2024 census) are famous for loving basketball, karaoke and very long Christmas holidays.

Surprising but true

Each eye of the Philippine tarsier, a tiny primate, is about as big as its whole brain.

A country that keeps finding islands

For decades the official count was 7,107 islands. Then, in 2016, a government mapping agency used new satellite images and found 534 more. Most are tiny, some no bigger than a cricket field.

The islands are the tops of underwater mountains, many of them volcanoes. Mount Pinatubo erupted in 1991 and threw so much ash and gas into the sky that the whole planet cooled by about half a degree Celsius for a year or so. Mayon, another volcano, has one of the most perfect cone shapes in the world, like a child's drawing of a mountain.

Living on so many islands has made Filipinos great boat builders. For centuries, people moved between islands in wooden boats with bamboo outriggers, the same trick used across the Pacific by the Polynesians.

Tiny primates, giant sharks

The Philippine tarsier fits in your palm. Its eyes are so large it can't move them, so it swivels its head round to look straight behind it, like an owl. The Philippine eagle, one of the biggest eagles alive, has a wingspan of about 2 m and hunts flying lemurs and monkeys.

Under water it gets even busier. The Philippines sits in the Coral Triangle, which holds more kinds of reef fish than anywhere else on the planet. Tubbataha Reef, in the middle of the Sulu Sea, is so far from land that rangers live there on a platform for weeks at a time. Off Donsol, whale sharks as long as a school bus cruise past swimmers, eating plankton.

Stairs for rice

High in the mountains of northern Luzon, the Ifugao people carved rice fields into steep slopes. From above, the terraces look like giant green staircases climbing to the clouds. Water flows down from forests at the top, field by field, without a single pump.

How old are they? People long said 2,000 years. Some recent digs suggest many were built in the last 400 years or so. Either way, UNESCO lists them as a World Heritage Site.

Three empires in one history

Ferdinand Magellan's fleet arrived in 1521, and Magellan was killed in a battle on the island of Mactan, against the forces of a local chief called Lapulapu. Spain later took control and ruled for over 300 years. That is why most Filipinos are Christian, why towns hold huge fiestas for their saints, and why Christmas songs play in shops from September.

In 1898 the USA took over, and stayed until 1946. The Americans left behind English, a love of basketball, and thousands of army jeeps. Filipinos stretched those jeeps, painted them in wild colours and turned them into jeepneys, shared minibuses that still carry millions of people a day.

Food tells the same mixed-up story. Adobo, the unofficial national dish, is meat stewed in vinegar, soy sauce and garlic. Spanish settlers gave it its name, Chinese traders brought the soy sauce, and the vinegar trick for keeping meat fresh in the heat is older than both.

Living with storms

The country sits right in the path of Pacific typhoons, the same kind of storm Americans call a hurricane. People build on stilts, tie roofs down and move inland when warnings come. And the strangest part? The same warm water that feeds these storms also feeds those record-breaking reefs, which is why scientists studying hurricanes keep a close eye on the western Pacific.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Everyone in the Philippines speaks Spanish because Spain ruled it for centuries.

Truth: Very few Filipinos speak Spanish today. The official languages are Filipino and English, and more than 170 local languages are spoken, though thousands of Spanish words live on inside them.
