How do homing pigeons find their way home?

Take a homing pigeon hundreds of kilometres from home in a closed box, let it go, and it will usually fly straight back.

The short answer

Homing pigeons are a tame breed of the rock dove, the same species as the pigeons in city squares. People have bred them for thousands of years for one unusual skill: finding their way back to their home loft from places they have never been.

Scientists still don't fully understand how they do it. Experiments suggest pigeons use several senses together. They use the Sun as a compass, and they can sense Earth's magnetic field. Smells carried on the wind seem to help them work out where they are. Close to home, they recognise landmarks like rivers, roads and buildings.

A homing pigeon can fly hundreds of kilometres in a day and find its own roof at the end.

Surprising but true

When scientists in Oxford tracked homing pigeons with GPS, they found many birds followed roads and motorways home, even turning at junctions.

A compass in the sky

The Sun rises in the east and sets in the west, but its position changes all day. To use it as a compass, a pigeon must know the time. Pigeons have a good internal body clock.

In experiments, scientists shifted pigeons' clocks by keeping them under lights that switched on and off at the wrong times. On sunny days, these birds flew off in the wrong direction, just as you would expect if they were reading the Sun with a wrong clock.

On cloudy days, though, the same pigeons still got home. So they must have a backup.

Magnets, smells and roads

In the 1970s, the American scientist William Keeton glued small magnets to pigeons' heads. On sunny days, they homed normally. On cloudy days, many got lost. This suggested they sense Earth's magnetic field when the Sun is hidden. How they detect it is still debated. Iron-rich cells in pigeons' beaks were once thought to be the sensor, but later studies found they were mostly immune cells.

Italian scientists found that pigeons whose sense of smell was blocked often struggled to home from unfamiliar places. They suggested pigeons learn a "smell map" from the winds blowing over their loft. Not all scientists are convinced, and it may matter more in some places than others.

Close to home, pigeons use what they see. In the early 2000s, a team at the University of Oxford fitted pigeons with tiny GPS trackers. Many birds followed main roads and motorways, even turning at roundabouts, rather than flying in a straight line.

Pigeons at war

Before radio, pigeons carried urgent messages. In the First World War, armies kept thousands of them. In October 1918, an American battalion was trapped in a forest in France, and shells from its own side were falling on it. A pigeon named Cher Ami flew through gunfire with a note giving their position. The bird arrived badly wounded, but the shelling stopped and about 190 soldiers survived. Cher Ami was long thought to be female, until a DNA test in 2021 showed he was male. His preserved body is at the Smithsonian museum in Washington, DC.

In the Second World War, Britain gave a special medal for animal bravery, the Dickin Medal. Between 1943 and 1949, pigeons won 32 of them, more than any other kind of animal in those years.

Pigeon post in India and beyond

In 1850, before the telegraph linked the two cities, Paul Julius Reuter used pigeons to carry stock market news between Aachen in Germany and Brussels in Belgium. His news business later became the famous Reuters agency.

In India, the Odisha Police ran a pigeon service from about 1946. The birds carried messages from remote areas, and during floods when other links failed. It was one of the last police pigeon services in the world when it was closed around 2008, as mobile phones spread.

Today, pigeon racing is a popular sport from Kolkata to New York. Fanciers train their birds by releasing them further and further away. The best racers sell for huge sums: in 2020, a Belgian pigeon named New Kim sold at auction for €1.6 million.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Homing pigeons can be sent anywhere, like a postman.

Truth: A homing pigeon only flies home. To send a message, people carried pigeons away from their home loft and released them with a note. Two-way services needed two sets of birds.

Try this

Ask a family member to take you on a short walk by a route you don't know well, in a safe place. On the way back, try to lead without help, noticing which clues you use, like the Sun's direction, landmarks, sounds and smells. Pigeons seem to use a mix of clues too.
