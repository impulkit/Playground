How does GST work?

Every time someone in India buys a biscuit packet, a bus ticket or a phone, a small slice of the price goes to the government. That slice has a name.

The short answer

GST stands for Goods and Services Tax. It is a tax added to the price of most things we buy, from soap to cinema tickets to a haircut.

You pay it at the shop, as part of the price. The shop passes it on to the government, which uses it to pay for roads, schools, hospitals and much more. The money is shared between the central government and the states.

Surprising but true

India's monthly GST collection can top ₹2 lakh crore. In October 2026 it was about ₹2.31 lakh crore, more than ₹7,400 crore for every single day of the month.

How it works

Think of a relay race. Each runner carries the baton a little further, and at the end one runner crosses the line. GST is passed along a chain of businesses in the same way, until the last buyer, the shopper, carries it home.

Take a packet of biscuits. A farmer sells wheat to a mill, the mill sells flour to a factory, the factory sells biscuits to a shop, and the shop sells them to you. GST is charged at each step. But each business gets back the tax it already paid on what it bought. This is called input tax credit.

Here is a simple example with a pretend rate of 10%. The factory sells biscuits to the shop for ₹50 and charges ₹5 GST. The shop sells them to you for ₹80 and charges ₹8 GST. The shop pays the government only ₹3, because it already paid ₹5 to the factory. In the end the government gets ₹8 in total, which is exactly 10% of the final price, and that ₹8 came from you.

So the tax is only ever paid on the value added at each step. Rates are written as percentages of the price. Daily needs are taxed lightly, many fresh foods are not taxed at all, and luxury goods are taxed more.

On your bill, GST is split into two halves. CGST goes to the central government and SGST goes to your state. When goods travel from one state to another, the tax is called IGST, and it is shared out later.

Where it came from

This kind of tax was invented in France. A tax officer named Maurice Lauré designed it, and France made it law in 1954. It is often called VAT, or value added tax. More than 150 countries now use some form of it.

Before GST, India had many separate taxes on goods and services. The centre charged excise duty and service tax, and each state had its own sales tax or VAT with its own rules. A truck crossing from one state to another could wait for hours at a checkpost.

The idea of one national tax was discussed from 2000, under Prime Minister Atal Bihari Vajpayee. GST was first named in a Budget speech on 28 February 2006, by Finance Minister P. Chidambaram, with a target of 2010. It took ten more years of debate between the centre and the states.

India's story

Because GST takes away some of the states' power to set their own taxes, the Constitution had to be changed. Parliament passed the change, known as the 101st amendment, in 2016. You can read more about how such changes happen in constitution amendments.

GST began at midnight on 1 July 2017, at a special session in Parliament's Central Hall. It replaced more than a dozen older taxes. Checkposts at state borders were removed, and trucks started moving faster.

Decisions about GST rates are made by the GST Council. It is led by the Union Finance Minister and includes finance ministers from all the states. This is an example of India's federal system, where the centre and states share power.

For eight years, GST had four main rates: 5%, 12%, 18% and 28%. From 22 September 2025, the first day of Navratri, they were cut to two main rates, 5% and 18%. A special 40% rate covers luxury and harmful goods like pan masala, big motorbikes and yachts. Soaps and shampoos moved to 5%, and TVs and air conditioners dropped from 28% to 18%.

The numbers that matter

₹22.27 lakh crore. That was India's total GST in 2025-26, a record and 8.3% more than the year before. Written out, it is about ₹22,27,000 crore, a number so big it helps to know how a lakh crore works.

1.53 crore. The number of businesses registered and active under GST in June 2025. Most are small shops and traders, but a small share of big companies pays most of the tax.

₹2.31 lakh crore. GST collected in October 2026 alone, 22.9% more than in October 2025.

Two. The number of main GST rates since September 2025, down from four.

What people argue about

Supporters say GST joined India into one market. Goods move more freely, businesses file taxes online, and it is harder to hide sales. Collections have grown strongly over the years.

Critics raise other points. Many states say they gave up the power to set their own taxes and now depend on the centre's decisions. When GST began, the centre promised to make up for any loss in state income for five years, and some states wanted that help to continue. Small businesses have said filing GST returns takes time and money. Some economists also ask why petrol, diesel and alcohol are still kept outside GST.

Others welcome the 2025 rate cuts as simpler, while some states worry the lower rates could shrink their share of the money.

Myths and mix-ups

"GST is charged on everything." Many basic items, such as fresh vegetables, fruit and milk, have no GST at all.

"Petrol includes GST." It does not. Petrol and diesel are taxed separately, by the centre and the states, which is one reason fuel prices differ from state to state.

"GST and a trade tariff are the same." A tariff is charged only on goods crossing the border. GST is charged on goods and services bought inside India, including imported goods once they come in. Free trade agreements change tariffs, not GST.

What it means for you

Every time your family buys something, GST is part of the price. Look at a restaurant bill or a phone bill and you will see CGST and SGST lines. A food label shows the maximum retail price, and that price already includes GST.

When rates fall, as they did in 2025, some things can get cheaper, from shampoo to a new TV. The monthly GST total is also a clue about the economy. When it grows fast, it often means factories are busy and shoppers are spending, which is good news for jobs. And the money collected flows into the Budget that pays for the roads, schools and hospitals your family uses.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Only big companies pay GST.

Truth: Every shopper pays GST, because it is already included in the price. The shop or company collects it from us and passes it on to the government.
