Why is Ahmedabad a World Heritage City?

In 2017, Ahmedabad became the first city in India to be named a World Heritage City by UNESCO, thanks to its old walled neighbourhoods and carved wooden houses.

The short answer

Ahmedabad, in Gujarat, was named India's first World Heritage City by UNESCO in 2017. The honour is for its old walled city, on the east bank of the Sabarmati river. It still has narrow lanes, gated neighbourhoods called pols, carved wooden houses and centuries-old mosques and temples.

What makes it special is that the old city is not a museum. Families still live in the pols, shops still open in the morning, and temples and mosques are still in daily use. Ahmedabad is also where Gandhi lived and planned much of the freedom struggle, and it was one of India's great textile cities.

Surprising but true

Many lanes in old Ahmedabad have a tall, carved bird-feeder called a chabutro, where families leave grain for pigeons and sparrows every day.

A city of gates and lanes

Sultan Ahmad Shah founded the city in 1411 and named it after himself. It grew into a busy trading centre, famous for cloth, and was surrounded by walls with about a dozen gates. Many of the gates still stand, though the walls have mostly gone.

Inside the old city are hundreds of pols. Each is a cluster of houses along winding lanes, often entered through a single gateway. Houses have carved wooden balconies, brackets and doors, with courtyards open to the sky that let in light and catch rainwater. Many pols have a chabutro, a tall, decorated bird-feeding tower, where families leave grain for birds every day.

Stone that looks like lace

Ahmedabad's mosques mix Islamic, Hindu and Jain styles, because local craftsmen built them. The most famous carving is at the Sidi Saiyyed mosque, built in 1573. Its stone windows, called jali, show a tree with curling branches, cut from stone so finely that it looks like lace. The design has become a symbol of the city.

Near Ahmedabad is the Adalaj stepwell, built in 1499. Steps lead several storeys down to cool water, past carved pillars and walls. Stepwells kept people supplied with water through the hot, dry months, and gave them a shady place to rest. Down at the bottom, the air can feel several degrees cooler than at the top.

Gandhi's ashram

In 1917, Gandhi set up an ashram on the banks of the Sabarmati. He lived there with his wife Kasturba and his followers, spinning cotton, farming and teaching. He urged people to spin and wear khadi, handspun cloth, instead of buying factory cloth from Britain.

On 12 March 1930, Gandhi and 78 followers left the ashram on the Salt March to Dandi, about 385 km away on the coast, to make salt against British law. He vowed not to return until India was free, and he never lived there again. Today the ashram is a museum, with his simple room and spinning wheel.

Mills, kites and food

In the late 1800s and early 1900s, Ahmedabad built dozens of cotton mills and became known as the "Manchester of the East", after the English mill city. Its long tradition of textiles is shown in the Calico Museum, one of India's finest cloth collections.

Every January, during Uttarayan, the whole city climbs onto its rooftops to fly kites. The sky fills with thousands of them, and people try to cut each other's strings. At night, food streets serve dhokla, khandvi, fafda and other Gujarati snacks. Modern Ahmedabad has grown far beyond the old walls, with famous buildings by architects like Louis Kahn and Le Corbusier. Yet the city's identity still starts in those narrow lanes behind the old gates.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Gandhi lived in Ahmedabad's Sabarmati Ashram for most of his life.

Truth: He lived there for about 13 years, from 1917 to 1930. He left on the Salt March in 1930 and vowed not to return until India was free. He never lived there again.
