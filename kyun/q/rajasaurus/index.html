Meet Rajasaurus, India's royal dinosaur

It had a bump on its head like a crown. And it hunted in Gujarat.

The short answer

Rajasaurus narmadensis means "royal lizard of the Narmada". It lived in western India about 67 million years ago, near the very end of the age of dinosaurs.

It was a theropod, a two-legged meat-eater, about 8–9 metres long, as long as two cars parked end to end. On its head it had a rounded bump, like a small crown. Its closest relatives lived in Madagascar and South America. That's a clue that these lands were once joined.

Surprising but true

Rajasaurus's closest cousin, Majungasaurus, lived in Madagascar, India's next-door neighbour back then.

How it was found

In the early 1980s, a team from the Geological Survey of India, led by the geologist Suresh Srivastava, dug up bones near Rahioli village in Kheda district, Gujarat. The bones lay in rocks called the Lameta Formation, made of sand and mud laid down by rivers and lakes.

The bones waited in collections for years. Then Indian scientists, including Suresh Srivastava, Ashok Sahni and Ashu Khosla, studied them with the American scientists Jeffrey Wilson and Paul Sereno. They named the animal in 2003.

A royal name

Scientific names follow fixed rules, as you can read in how dinosaurs get their names. The first part, Rajasaurus, joins the Hindi and Sanskrit word raja, king, with the Greek sauros, lizard. The second part, narmadensis, points to the Narmada river region, where many of India's dinosaur fossils have been found.

Only part of the skeleton was recovered: the skull roof with its crest, the braincase, parts of the backbone, hips, legs and tail. Scientists filled in the rest by comparing it with close relatives. That is why its length is an estimate.

What kind of hunter

Rajasaurus belonged to the abelisaurs, a family of big hunters that lived mainly on the southern continents. Abelisaurs had short, deep skulls, strong necks and tiny arms, even smaller for their size than those of T. rex. Rajasaurus's arms have not been found, but its relatives suggest they were very short.

They probably hunted big plant-eaters like the long-necked titanosaurs, whose eggs are found nearby in Kheda and Balasinor. The head did the hunting, grabbing and biting rather than reaching with the arms.

A dinosaur neighbourhood

Kheda and the nearby Balasinor area are among the richest dinosaur sites in India. Thousands of fossil eggs have been found there, many still in nests. A fossil park at Raiyoli, near Balasinor, now protects part of the site.

The same rocks hold a surprise. In 2010, scientists described a fossil snake called Sanajeh, found in Gujarat coiled around a crushed dinosaur egg beside a hatchling. Rajasaurus shared its world with egg thieves.

A clue about moving continents

Abelisaurs lived in India, Madagascar, Africa and South America, lands that were once joined in Gondwana. Rajasaurus's closest known cousin, Majungasaurus, lived in Madagascar at about the same time.

India had split from Madagascar about 88 million years ago and was drifting north as an island. Its animals carried their family links with them. India's long journey explains why.

A world of fire

When Rajasaurus lived, the Deccan volcanoes were beginning some of the largest eruptions in Earth's history, spreading lava over western and central India. In many places, the Lameta rocks that hold its bones lie just beneath these old lava layers. Not long afterwards, in Earth's terms, the asteroid struck, and India's royal lizard vanished with the rest.

Myth or fact?

Myth: India has no real dinosaurs. They all lived in America and China.

Truth: India has a rich dinosaur record, including Rajasaurus, the long-necked Barapasaurus and thousands of fossil dinosaur eggs in Gujarat and Madhya Pradesh.
