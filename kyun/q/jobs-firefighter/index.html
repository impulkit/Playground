What do firefighters do?

Every year on 14 April, India's fire services remember 66 firefighters who died fighting a fire and explosion on a ship in Bombay's docks in 1944.

The short answer

Firefighters put out fires, but that is only the start. They rescue people trapped in buildings, cars and floodwater. They deal with gas leaks and chemical spills. In many places they are the first to arrive at medical emergencies. In the USA, in fact, medical calls make up most of the work, far more than fires. They also inspect buildings for fire safety and visit schools to teach children what to do if a fire breaks out.

They work in shifts so that a fire station is ready day and night. When an alarm comes in, a crew can be dressed, on the fire engine and on its way within a minute or two.

Surprising but true

In the USA, most calls that firefighters answer are not fires at all but medical emergencies, such as someone collapsing or being hurt in a crash.

The fire triangle

Every fire needs three things: heat, fuel and oxygen. Firefighters call this the fire triangle, and their job is to break it. Spraying water cools the fire down. Foam smothers the flames and cuts off oxygen. In a forest fire, crews may clear a strip of land so that the flames run out of fuel.

Different fires need different tools. Water is dangerous on burning oil or electrical equipment, so firefighters may use foam, powder or carbon dioxide instead. Understanding what fire is is the first thing trainees learn.

Heavy gear and hot work

A firefighter's gear protects against heat, smoke and falling objects. It includes a thick, fire-resistant suit, a helmet, boots, gloves and a breathing set with an air tank on the back. All together, the gear can weigh around 20 kg, about as much as a big bag of rice, and firefighters have to climb stairs and carry people while wearing it.

Smoke is often more dangerous than flames, because it is hot and poisonous and makes it impossible to see. Firefighters crawl low through smoky rooms, keeping one hand on the wall so they don't get lost, and use thermal cameras to find people.

What is fire?Fire isn't a thing at all. It's something happening, a fast chemical reaction that gives off heat and light.

Firefighting in India and the USA

In India, fire services are run by state governments and cities. You can reach them on 101 or on the general emergency number 112. Officers are trained at places like the National Fire Service College in Nagpur. In 2002, Harshini Kanhekar became the first woman to join that college's firefighting course, and many women now serve.

In the USA, there are more than a million firefighters, and most of them are volunteers, especially in small towns. One of the first volunteer fire companies in America was started in Philadelphia in 1736 by Benjamin Franklin. He had seen how quickly fires spread through wooden towns. Members kept leather buckets at home and promised to run to any fire, day or night.

The Bombay docks fire

On 14 April 1944, a cargo ship called the Fort Stikine caught fire in Bombay's docks. It was carrying cotton, gold and a large load of explosives. Firefighters were battling the blaze when two huge explosions tore through the docks. Hundreds of people died, including 66 firefighters.

The blasts were so strong that burning cotton and debris landed far across the city. Since then, India has marked 14 April as National Fire Service Day, followed by a Fire Service Week when fire stations hold drills and teach fire safety.

How you can help

Firefighters say the best fire is one that never starts. Simple habits help: never play with matches or lighters, keep things that burn away from stoves, and don't overload plug sockets. Working smoke alarms give families precious extra minutes to get out. If you ever smell smoke or see fire, get out first, stay out, and call for help from a safe place.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Firefighters spend nearly all their time putting out fires.

Truth: Fires are only part of the job. Firefighters also rescue people from crashes, floods and collapsed buildings, help with medical emergencies, check buildings for fire safety and teach people how to prevent fires.

Try this

With your family, plan a fire escape route from your home. Draw a simple map with two ways out of every room if possible, and choose a meeting spot outside, such as a gate or a tree. Practise walking the route once, and check where the nearest smoke alarm or fire extinguisher is.
