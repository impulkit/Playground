Why do we hiccup?

A hiccup is two things at once. Your breathing muscle jerks, and a split second later your throat slams shut on the air.

The short answer

Under your lungs lies the diaphragm, the dome-shaped muscle that does most of your breathing. Normally it tightens and relaxes in a smooth rhythm. During a hiccup, it suddenly jerks, as if it has been startled. That pulls in a quick gulp of air.

Less than a tenth of a second later, the vocal cords in your throat snap shut and stop the air. That sudden stop makes the "hic" sound. The whole thing is a reflex controlled by nerves running between your brainstem and the diaphragm, which is why you can't simply decide to stop.

Surprising but true

Babies hiccup inside the womb, weeks before they are born and before they have ever breathed air.

What sets them off

Hiccups often start after eating or drinking too fast, gulping fizzy drinks or swallowing air. A very full stomach sits right under the diaphragm and may irritate it or the nerves nearby. Spicy food, sudden excitement and laughing hard can trigger them too.

Most bouts last a few minutes and then fade. Hiccups that last for days are rare, and a doctor should check them, because they can be a sign of another problem.

Hiccups are also very regular. In most people they come every few seconds, at a steady beat, and the gap stays much the same throughout a bout. Depending on the person, the rate can be anywhere from about 4 to 60 hiccups a minute.

Leftovers from tadpoles?

Nobody knows for sure why we hiccup. One idea, put forward by French scientists in 2003, links it to our distant ancestors. Tadpoles and some fish breathe by gulping water over their gills while closing a flap to keep it out of their lungs. That gulp-and-close action looks a lot like a hiccup. The idea is that the brain circuit for it may have been passed down, long after our ancestors left the water.

Another idea is that hiccups help babies. Babies hiccup a lot, even before birth. Some scientists think hiccups may help them push swallowed air out of the stomach so there's more room for milk. Both ideas are still argued about, and neither has been proven.

Sixty-eight years of hics

The longest bout of hiccups on record belongs to Charles Osborne, a farmer from Iowa in the USA. His hiccups began in 1922, and they didn't stop until about 1990, roughly 68 years later. He was listed in Guinness World Records, and newspapers and radio shows came to talk to him.

Early on, he hiccupped dozens of times a minute, and over time it slowed down. Even so, he lived a full life, marrying twice and raising eight children. His case is very unusual, but it showed doctors how hard the reflex can be to switch off once something keeps setting it off.

Cures, real and imagined

Every family has a cure. Hold your breath. Sip water from the far side of a glass. Eat a spoon of sugar. Get a fright. Some of these may work by interrupting the nerve signals or by letting carbon dioxide build up in the blood, which calms the diaphragm. None has been proven in careful scientific tests.

The honest answer is that waiting usually works best. Eating slowly, not gulping fizzy drinks and taking calm breaths can make hiccups less likely in the first place. Animals hiccup too. Cats, dogs and rats have been seen doing it, a sign that this odd brain circuit is very old.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A sudden fright cures hiccups.

Truth: There's no good evidence for it. Hiccups usually stop within a few minutes anyway, so whatever someone tried last often gets the credit.

Try this

Next time someone in your family has hiccups, time how many hics come in one minute using a clock. Then let them try a favourite trick, such as sipping cold water slowly, and count again. Keep a family record of which tricks seem to work, and remember that hiccups often stop on their own.
