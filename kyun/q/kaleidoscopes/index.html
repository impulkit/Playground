How does a kaleidoscope make patterns?

A kaleidoscope turns a few loose beads into a perfect flower using nothing but three strips of mirror and a little geometry.

The short answer

A kaleidoscope is a tube with mirrors inside, usually two or three long strips joined into a narrow triangle. At one end is a small chamber of loose coloured beads, glass chips or bits of plastic. At the other end is a peephole.

When you look in, you see the beads directly in one small triangle. You also see their reflections in the mirrors, and reflections of those reflections, bouncing back and forth. Each copy is a flipped version of its neighbour, so together they make a pattern that is perfectly balanced around the centre, like a flower or a star.

Turn the tube and the beads tumble into new positions. Every reflection changes at the same moment, and a new pattern appears.

Surprising but true

The Scottish scientist who invented the kaleidoscope in 1816 hardly earned anything from it, because copies flooded the shops before his patent could protect him.

Mirrors making copies

Stand in front of one mirror and you see one reflection. Stand between two mirrors facing each other, as in some lifts or hairdressers' shops, and you see a long line of copies stretching away. Each mirror reflects what the other shows.

When two mirrors meet at an angle, the copies arrange themselves in a circle instead of a line. The angle decides how many. Divide 360 degrees by the angle between the mirrors. At 90 degrees you get four slices, at 60 degrees six, at 45 degrees eight. Kaleidoscope makers choose angles that divide 360 neatly, so the copies join up without a messy seam.

Built-in symmetry

Every pattern in a kaleidoscope has the same kind of balance as a snowflake or a flower. Mathematicians call it symmetry. Fold it along a line through the centre and the two halves match. Turn it by one slice and it looks the same.

With mirrors at 60 degrees, you get six-fold symmetry, the same as a snowflake. That's no accident. Water molecules join into six-sided crystals, so snowflakes grow six matching arms. Rangoli and kolam artists in India often build designs around a centre in a similar way, with the same pattern repeated in every direction.

The craze of 1817

The kaleidoscope was invented in 1816 by David Brewster, a Scottish scientist who studied how light reflects and bends. He was experimenting with mirrors and noticed the beautiful patterns. He named his invention from three Greek words: kalos, beautiful, eidos, shape, and skopeo, to look.

He patented it in 1817, but a mistake meant other makers could copy it, and they did. Kaleidoscopes became a craze in Britain and across Europe and America, with huge numbers sold in a few months. Brewster himself earned very little from them. He became famous anyway, for his scientific work on light.

Why the edges look darker

Look carefully at a kaleidoscope pattern. The copies near the centre are bright, but the ones near the outside are a little dimmer. That's because ordinary mirrors aren't perfect. Each time light bounces, a small amount is absorbed by the mirror.

The light that makes the outer copies has bounced many times, losing a little at each bounce. Expensive kaleidoscopes use specially coated mirrors that reflect almost all the light, so the pattern stays bright right to the edge.

Today artists build giant walk-in kaleidoscopes, and designers use the idea to make fabric prints and phone wallpaper. The toy itself has hardly changed in 200 years.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A mirror reflects all the light that hits it.

Truth: Every ordinary mirror absorbs a little light with each reflection. That's why, in a kaleidoscope, the copies furthest from the centre look slightly darker, because their light has bounced more times.

Try this

Tape two small mirrors together along one edge like a book. Stand them up and put a bead, a leaf or a scrap of coloured paper between them. Slowly open and close the "book" and count the copies. Find the angle that makes a pattern like a rangoli.
