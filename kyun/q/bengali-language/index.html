What makes Bengali special?

In 1952, students in Dhaka died fighting for the right to use Bengali. The whole world now marks that day as Mother Language Day.

The short answer

Bengali, or Bangla, is spoken as a first language by about 23 crore people, which places it among the ten most spoken languages on Earth. It is the national language of Bangladesh and an official language of West Bengal and Tripura in India. The 2011 Census found it was the second most spoken language in India after Hindi.

What makes it special is partly its size and partly its story. Bengali has a rich tradition of poems, songs and novels, a Nobel Prize-winning poet, and a day honoured by the whole world because people fought for the right to use it.

Surprising but true

One poet, Rabindranath Tagore, wrote the words of two countries' national anthems, India's and Bangladesh's, both in Bengali.

The day students stood up

When British India was divided in 1947, East Bengal became part of Pakistan, which also included lands more than 1,500 km away to the west. Most people in East Bengal spoke Bengali, but Pakistan's leaders announced that Urdu alone would be the national language.

Students in Dhaka protested. On 21 February 1952, police opened fire on a march, and several students were killed. The Language Movement grew, and Bengali was eventually accepted as one of Pakistan's national languages in 1956. The struggle fed into the later movement that created the independent country of Bangladesh in 1971.

In 1999, UNESCO declared 21 February International Mother Language Day. It has been marked around the world since 2000, to protect all languages, big and small.

What is Bangladesh like?Bangladesh fits about 17 crore people into a country smaller than the state of Odisha, most of it built by rivers.

A thousand years of writing

Bengali grew out of older spoken languages of eastern India, called Prakrits, which descend from the same ancient roots as Sanskrit. The oldest known Bengali writings are the Charyapada, Buddhist songs from roughly a thousand years ago. A scholar found them in the royal library of Nepal in 1907.

Bengali has its own script, with letters that hang from a line along the top. A very similar script is used for Assamese. Like most Indian scripts, it is an abugida, where each consonant carries a built-in vowel.

Tagore, Ray and a flood of new ideas

In the 1800s, Kolkata, then called Calcutta, was the capital of British India. It became a hotbed of printing presses, schools, debate and new writing, a time often called the Bengal Renaissance.

Its most famous writer is Rabindranath Tagore. In 1913 he became the first Asian to win the Nobel Prize, for Gitanjali, a collection of poems he translated into English himself. He wrote over 2,000 songs. Two of them became national anthems: Jana Gana Mana for India and Amar Shonar Bangla for Bangladesh.

Bengali children grow up with the nonsense rhymes of Sukumar Ray and the detective stories of his son, the film-maker Satyajit Ray. Bengali novelists such as Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay and Sarat Chandra Chattopadhyay were read across India in translation. Bankim wrote Vande Mataram, which became India's national song.

Sweet to the ear

Bengali speakers often describe their language as mishti, sweet, and it is common to hear people say it is one of the sweetest-sounding languages. That is a matter of taste, not science. But Bengali does have a gentle sound: its words often end in soft vowels.

In 2024, the Government of India added Bengali to its list of classical languages. On 21 February each year, people gather in Dhaka at the Shaheed Minar, the Martyrs' Monument, barefoot and carrying flowers, and sing a song written for the students of 1952.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Bengali is just a dialect of Hindi.

Truth: Bengali is a separate language with its own script, grammar and over a thousand years of writing. It is a cousin of Hindi, since both grew from older Indian languages, but speakers of one cannot easily understand the other.
