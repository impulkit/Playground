How deep is Chand Baori?

In a dusty Rajasthan village, about 3,500 stone steps zigzag down 13 storeys to a small square of green water.

The short answer

Chand Baori is about 30 metres deep, roughly as tall as a 10-storey building. It drops through 13 levels of stairs to a pool of water at the bottom. Its walls are lined with around 3,500 narrow steps, arranged in criss-crossing flights that make a pattern of sharp triangles.

It stands in Abhaneri, a village about 95 km east of Jaipur. It is one of India's oldest and deepest stepwells. Most historians date it to the 8th or 9th century, when a local ruler called Raja Chanda is said to have built it. People came here for water, for shade and to pray at the temple next door.

Surprising but true

The air at the bottom of Chand Baori can be about 5°C cooler than at the top, so villagers once came down here to escape the summer heat.

Following the water down

Rajasthan gets most of its rain in a few monsoon weeks. After that, the sky can stay dry for eight months. The groundwater that soaks into the soil then sinks lower and lower.

A normal well needs a long rope and a bucket. A stepwell lets people walk down to the water itself, wherever it happens to be. In August the water might be high up the steps. By May you might have to climb down 10 storeys to fill your pot. The criss-cross stairs let many people go up and down at once without bumping into each other.

The builders also had to stop the walls caving in. Loose soil pushes inwards with huge force at 30 metres down. So the sides of Chand Baori slope gently inwards as they go deeper, making the pit narrower at the bottom than at the top. The square shape and the stepped walls work like a giant set of braces, holding back the earth on every side. It has stood for well over a thousand years.

A cool room in the desert

Stand at the top in summer and the stone can be too hot to touch. Climb down and the air turns cool and damp. Visitors often measure the bottom at about 5°C cooler than the top.

That made the baori a gathering place. Families rested on the shady landings, and travellers stopped here on hot days. On the north side, a building with arched galleries and carved rooms looks down over the water. Historians think these rooms were added centuries after the well was dug.

What is groundwater?Beneath your feet, rainwater is soaking slowly through the rock. India pumps up more of it than any other country, about a quarter of all the groundwater used on Earth.

Patterns that trick the eye

From above, the steps look like a giant puzzle. Each flight forms a small triangle, and the triangles repeat across three walls like a tessellation. Your eyes keep trying to count them and losing track.

That look has made Chand Baori famous with filmmakers. It appeared in the fantasy film The Fall in 2006, and in the Batman film The Dark Knight Rises in 2012 as a prison in a deep pit.

The temple next door

Right beside the baori stands the Harshat Mata temple, dedicated to a goddess of joy. It was built around the same time but was badly damaged hundreds of years ago. Broken carvings from it now sit around the walls of the baori, including dancers and musicians.

Many people would wash at the stepwell before going into the temple. Water and worship were tied together in places like this.

Visiting today

The water at the bottom is now green and still, and visitors are no longer allowed down the steps. You watch from a railing at the top. Every year, the village holds the Abhaneri Festival, with folk dancers, puppet shows and camel rides.

Chand Baori is deep, but it is not the most decorated. About 600 km away in Gujarat, the Rani ki Vav is covered with hundreds of carved gods and goddesses, so beautiful that it appears on India's 100-rupee note.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Chand Baori was built by ghosts in a single night.

Truth: This is a popular local legend. In fact it was built by skilled masons over a long time, and later rulers added the arched pavilions on one side.

Try this

Fill a tall clear glass with water and mark the level. Leave it on a sunny windowsill and mark the level every day for a week. That slow drop is a tiny version of what happened to wells after the monsoon.
