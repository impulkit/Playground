How does an umbrella keep you dry?

Your umbrella cloth is full of tiny gaps between its threads, and raindrops could fit through them, yet the rain stays out.

The short answer

An umbrella keeps you dry in two ways. First, its dome shape sends rain sliding off the edge and away from your head. Second, its cloth is woven so tightly, and coated so well, that raindrops can't find a way through.

Most umbrellas today use polyester or nylon threads woven into a tight cloth. Even so, there are tiny gaps between the threads. Raindrops stay out because of surface tension: water clings to itself like a stretchy skin, so a drop would rather stay round than squeeze through a tiny hole.

Surprising but true

The first umbrellas were made to keep off the sun, not the rain. The word comes from the Latin "umbra", meaning shade.

Why the drops roll off

The cloth is also treated with a coating that makes water bead up instead of soaking in. You can see this on a new umbrella: drops sit in little balls and roll away when you shake it. Leaves of the lotus plant do the same trick naturally, which is why scientists study them to design better waterproof materials. The leaf is covered in tiny wax bumps, so a drop touches only their tips and can't spread out.

A coating like this is called water-repellent. Over years of use it wears off. That is why an old umbrella starts to let in a fine mist during heavy rain, even when there are no holes in it.

Shade for kings

The first umbrellas, thousands of years ago, kept off the sun. In ancient Egypt and Assyria, servants held them over kings. In India, a royal umbrella called a chhatra showed who was in charge, and you can see umbrellas carved over the heads of rulers and gods in old temple sculptures. Even today, decorated umbrellas are part of festivals like Thrissur Pooram in Kerala.

In China, people made umbrellas of oiled paper or silk stretched over bamboo. The oil made the paper waterproof, so these could be used in the rain as well.

The man who was laughed at

In Europe, umbrellas were long seen as something only women used against the sun. In the 1750s, a London traveller named Jonas Hanway began carrying one in the rain. People jeered at him in the street, and carriage drivers, who earned money from people escaping the rain, were especially rude. Within a few decades, umbrellas were common in London.

In the 1850s, a British maker named Samuel Fox introduced light steel ribs, which replaced heavy whalebone and cane. In the late 1920s, a German inventor patented a small umbrella that folded down short enough to carry in a bag.

How the frame works

An umbrella frame is a clever set of levers. The long ribs hold the cloth. Shorter stretchers connect the ribs to a sliding ring on the shaft. When you push the ring up, the stretchers push the ribs outward and the cloth goes tight. A small spring catch holds it there. Many umbrellas have eight ribs, a number that balances strength against weight. Automatic umbrellas add a spring inside the shaft that pushes the ring up when you press a button.

A strong gust of wind can get underneath and push the ribs the wrong way, flipping the umbrella inside out. Some modern designs have vents that let air escape, or bendy ribs made of fibreglass that spring back instead of snapping.

Monsoon umbrellas

In Kerala, where the monsoon arrives first each June, umbrella-making is a big local industry, and many families buy new ones before the rains begin. Across India you see huge black umbrellas held by farmers, colourful folding ones in school bags, and giant beach umbrellas over fruit sellers' carts. It is one of the few inventions that is used in exactly the same way, for both sun and rain, all over the world.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Umbrellas were invented to keep off the rain.

Truth: The oldest umbrellas we know of, in ancient Egypt, Assyria, China and India, were shades against the sun and symbols of royalty. Waterproof versions came later, starting with oiled paper and silk in China.

Try this

Stretch a clean handkerchief tightly over a glass and hold it in place with a rubber band. Fill the glass with water through the cloth, then quickly turn it upside down over a sink. Most of the water should stay inside, held back by surface tension. Now poke the cloth gently and watch what happens.
