Is graffiti art?

People have been scribbling on walls for thousands of years. In the ruins of Pompeii, you can still read jokes, election slogans and names scratched nearly 2,000 years ago.

The short answer

Graffiti means writing or drawings on walls and other public surfaces. Some of it, like hurried tags sprayed on a shop shutter, is generally seen as vandalism. Other work, especially large, skilful murals painted with permission, is widely seen as art. Many cities now invite artists to brighten up their walls.

So is graffiti art? It depends on whom you ask, and on the piece. Painting on someone else's property without permission is against the law almost everywhere, whatever it looks like. But some graffiti writers have become world-famous artists, and their work now hangs in museums.

Surprising but true

In 2018, a painting by the secret street artist Banksy partly shredded itself with a hidden blade in its frame, just moments after being sold at auction in London.

Writing on walls, forever

People have always marked walls. The first paintings we know of were made on cave walls tens of thousands of years ago. Ancient Egyptians and Greeks scratched their names on monuments.

In Pompeii, the Roman town buried by a volcano in 79 CE, archaeologists have found thousands of pieces of graffiti. There are election slogans, adverts, jokes, love messages, drawings of gladiators and even complaints about neighbours. Historians treasure them, because they show what ordinary people were thinking, not just emperors.

What happened at Pompeii?In 79 CE a volcano buried a Roman town so quickly that bread was left in its ovens, and it stayed hidden for about 1,700 years.

The subway kings of New York

Modern graffiti grew in Philadelphia and New York in the late 1960s and 1970s. Young people began writing their nicknames, called tags, on walls and subway trains. Soon tags grew into huge, colourful "pieces", with bubble letters, arrows and cartoon characters.

Graffiti became one of the four elements of hip-hop culture, along with DJing, rapping and breakdancing. But many New Yorkers saw it as a sign of a city out of control. In the 1980s the subway began removing graffiti from trains so that no painted train stayed in service.

From the street to the gallery

Some graffiti artists began showing work in galleries. In the 1980s Keith Haring drew chalk figures on empty advertising boards in New York subway stations, then became internationally famous for his dancing, glowing cartoon people.

In Britain, an artist known as Banksy began stencilling witty pictures on walls in Bristol in the 1990s. Nobody publicly knows who Banksy is. His works sell for millions, and people sometimes cut whole sections of wall away to sell them. In 2018 one of his pictures, Girl with Balloon, partly shredded itself right after an auction in London.

Walls that tell history

Murals can carry powerful messages. In Mexico in the 1920s, painters like Diego Rivera, husband of Frida Kahlo, covered public buildings with murals about Mexican history, so everyone could see art, not just the rich.

When the Berlin Wall still divided the city, artists painted the western side. After the wall fell in 1989, artists from many countries painted about 1.3 kilometres of the remaining wall. Called the East Side Gallery, it is now one of Berlin's most visited sights.

Street art in India

In India, painted walls have a long history, from village homes to film posters. Since the 2010s, festivals have invited artists to paint large murals in cities. In Delhi, the Lodhi Art District has dozens of giant murals on the walls of a housing colony. In Mumbai, artists have painted the warehouses at Sassoon Dock and the walls of Bandra.

Not everyone loves every mural, and some residents say they were not asked first. Even legal street art raises the question at the heart of the debate: who gets to decide what goes on the walls everyone shares?

Myth or fact?

Myth: All street art is illegal.

Truth: Much street art today is commissioned, meaning cities, building owners or festivals invite artists and pay them to paint walls. Graffiti on property without the owner's permission is against the law in most places.

Try this

Tape a large sheet of paper or old newspaper to a wall, with permission. Design your own "tag", a stylised version of your name or nickname, using thick markers or crayons. Try it in bubble letters, then in sharp, spiky letters, and see which style suits you best.
