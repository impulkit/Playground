Why are tongue twisters hard to say?

The trouble with "She sells seashells" isn't really your tongue. It's your brain, planning the next sounds before you've finished the last ones.

The short answer

Tongue twisters are hard because they pack similar sounds close together, in a confusing order. When you speak, your brain plans a few sounds ahead of your mouth. If two sounds are very alike, like "s" and "sh", the plans get mixed up and one sound slips into the wrong place.

Say "She sells seashells" slowly and it's easy. Speed up, and your brain has less time to keep the sounds apart. Out comes "she shells sea sells". The tongue just does what it's told.

Surprising but true

In 2013, scientists at MIT who study speech errors reported that "pad kid poured curd pulled cod" tripped people up so badly that some volunteers simply stopped speaking.

How speech works

Speaking is one of the most complicated things the brain does. To say a single word, it picks the word, lines up its sounds and sends signals to dozens of muscles in your lips, tongue, jaw and throat, and to your voice box. All of this happens in a fraction of a second.

Your brain doesn't plan one sound at a time. It prepares a little ahead, like a queue of sounds waiting their turn. Most of the time, they come out in the right order. In a tongue twister, the queue is full of near-twins, and they jump places.

Sounds that are almost twins

Linguists describe sounds by where and how they are made. "P" and "b" are both made with the lips. "S" and "sh" are both hissing sounds made with the tongue near the roof of the mouth, just a little apart. "T" and "k" are both quick stops, one at the front, one at the back.

Tongue twisters mix such pairs. "Peter Piper picked a peck of pickled peppers" swaps p, k and t sounds. "The sixth sick sheikh's sixth sheep's sick", once named by Guinness World Records as the hardest in English, packs s, sh, k and th into a tiny space.

In 2013, researchers at MIT tested volunteers with many twisters. "Pad kid poured curd pulled cod" was so hard that some people stopped altogether. It alternates p and k sounds at the start of words, and those two sounds are easy to confuse.

How does your voice work?Every word you speak starts as two small folds of tissue in your throat, slapping together hundreds of times a second.

Twisters in every language

Every language seems to have tongue twisters, and they use that language's tricky pairs. In Hindi, children try "kachcha papad, pakka papad" fast, and the k and p sounds soon swap places. Another favourite is "Chandu ke chacha ne Chandu ki chachi ko chandi ke chammach se chatni chatai", a crowd of ch sounds.

Some languages have sounds that English lacks, so their twisters use different traps. Twisters in tonal languages like Mandarin play with words that differ only by pitch.

Slips of the tongue

Tongue twisters are a kind of planned accident. Linguists study ordinary slips too, because mistakes show how speech is organised. When someone says "a blushing crow" instead of "a crushing blow", the swapped sounds reveal that the brain planned both words before speaking either.

These swaps are called spoonerisms, after William Archibald Spooner, an Oxford professor in the late 1800s who was said to make them often. Many of the famous ones were probably invented by his students.

Why people practise them

Actors, singers, radio presenters and news readers use tongue twisters as warm-ups. Saying them slowly and clearly trains the muscles and the brain to keep sounds crisp. Theatre schools have used them for generations.

Speech therapists sometimes use twisters to help people practise particular sounds. Writers love them too. Many of Dr. Seuss's books, such as Fox in Socks, are full of them. In the end, a tongue twister is a little game you play with your own brain, and the brain loses on purpose.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Tongue twisters are hard because your tongue gets tired or tangled.

Truth: The muscles are fine. The mistakes happen in the brain's planning, when similar sounds compete for the same slot. That's why slowing down helps so much.

Try this

Collect tongue twisters from family members in every language they speak. Have a contest to say each one three times fast. Then try saying them slowly, and notice that the mistakes almost vanish when your brain has time to plan.
