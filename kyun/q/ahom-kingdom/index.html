How did the Ahom kingdom rule Assam for 600 years?

In 1228 a prince crossed the Patkai hills from what is now Myanmar with a small band of followers. His family went on to rule Assam for about 600 years.

The short answer

The Ahom kingdom was founded in 1228 by Sukaphaa, a Tai prince from the hills of what is now northern Myanmar and southern China. He crossed the Patkai hills into the upper Brahmaputra valley and set up his rule there. His descendants governed much of today's Assam until 1826, roughly 600 years.

Several things kept them in power. They organised every adult man into a system of shared service, kept careful records, adopted local languages and customs, and used the mighty Brahmaputra river and its forests to defend themselves. They also beat back several large invasions by the Mughals.

Surprising but true

The Ahom kings kept detailed written chronicles called buranjis, which makes their history one of the best recorded of any Indian kingdom before modern times.

Newcomers who became Assamese

Sukaphaa arrived with a few thousand followers, along with horses and elephants. Rather than trying to wipe out the people already living in the valley, the Ahoms married into local families and brought them into their kingdom.

Over the centuries, the Ahoms came to speak Assamese and many adopted Hinduism, while keeping some of their own Tai customs and rituals. The word "Assam" itself is thought by many historians to come from the name Ahom. Rice farming in the flooded valley grew rich, and the kingdom grew with it.

Every man a worker and a soldier

The Ahoms ran their state through the paik system. Adult men, called paiks, were grouped into units called gots of three or four. Each paik was given land to farm. In return, one member of each got served the state at a time, as a soldier, a builder or a worker, while the others farmed for him.

This gave the kings a large army and workforce without paying wages. Paiks built raised roads and long embankments to protect fields from Brahmaputra floods. Some of these roads, called alis, are still in use.

Writing it all down

The Ahoms kept chronicles called buranjis. At first they were written in the Tai Ahom language and script, later in Assamese, on strips of bark from the agar tree. Court officials recorded battles, treaties, visits from envoys and decisions of the king.

Most Indian kingdoms of the time left inscriptions and poems, but few left detailed year-by-year histories. For historians, the buranjis are a treasure.

The battle on the river

The Mughals tried many times to conquer Assam. In 1662 the Mughal general Mir Jumla captured the Ahom capital, Garhgaon, but floods, disease and Ahom raids forced his army out.

In 1671, a huge Mughal force led by Raja Ram Singh of Amber came up the Brahmaputra. The Ahom commander Lachit Borphukan met it at Saraighat, near today's Guwahati, where the river narrows between hills. Though seriously ill, Lachit is said to have had himself carried into battle. Small, fast Ahom boats outmanoeuvred the bigger Mughal ships, and the Mughals withdrew. Assam celebrates Lachit Divas every 24 November in his memory.

Mounds, pavilions and the end

Ahom kings and queens were buried in moidams, large earth mounds over vaulted chambers, at Charaideo, their first capital. In 2024 the moidams became a World Heritage Site, the first cultural site in Northeast India on the list. At Sivasagar, the Rang Ghar, a two-storey pavilion built in the 1700s, let the royal family watch buffalo fights and games at festivals.

In the late 1700s the kingdom was weakened by civil war. Burmese armies invaded several times from 1817. The British drove them out, and under the Treaty of Yandabo in 1826, Assam came under British control. Soon after, they began planting the tea gardens that made Assam tea famous worldwide, on land the Ahom paiks once farmed.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Mughals ruled all of India.

Truth: Even at their height, the Mughals never conquered several regions, including most of Assam. The Ahoms fought off repeated Mughal campaigns, most famously at Saraighat in 1671.
