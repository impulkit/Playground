How do cuttlefish hypnotise their prey?

As a cuttlefish creeps up on a crab, dark stripes ripple across its body like clouds racing over a field, and the crab often just stays put.

The short answer

Cuttlefish are soft-bodied sea animals, cousins of the octopus and squid. They are among the best colour-changers on Earth. When hunting, some cuttlefish make bands of dark colour ripple across their bodies, a display scientists call "passing cloud". Prey such as crabs and shrimps often stay still while it happens, which is why people say cuttlefish "hypnotise" them.

Nobody is sure exactly how it works. The rippling may confuse the prey's eyes, make it hard to judge the cuttlefish's distance, or simply look like moving shadows on the seabed. Once close enough, the cuttlefish shoots out two long tentacles faster than you can blink and grabs its meal.

Surprising but true

Cuttlefish can match the colours around them almost perfectly, yet most scientists think they are colour-blind.

Living colour screens

A cuttlefish's skin is packed with millions of tiny colour sacs called chromatophores. Each one holds yellow, red, brown or black pigment, wrapped in a ring of muscle. When nerves tell the muscles to pull, the sac stretches open like a balloon and the colour spreads. When they relax, it shrinks to a dot.

Beneath those sacs are cells that reflect light, making shimmering blues and greens, and white patches. By controlling all these layers at once, a cuttlefish can change colour, pattern and even skin texture in under a second. It can raise little bumps to look like seaweed or coral. That is faster and more varied than a chameleon.

The passing cloud

The passing cloud display works like an animated sign. Dark bands sweep from the back of the cuttlefish's body towards its head, over and over. Scientists have filmed broadclub cuttlefish doing it as they creep up on prey.

One idea is that the moving stripes catch the prey's attention, so it watches the stripes rather than the approaching hunter. Another is that the motion makes the cuttlefish look like drifting shadows, not an animal. Some cuttlefish also use the display to startle prey into moving, which gives away its position.

Seeing without colour

Here is the puzzle. Cuttlefish eyes have W-shaped pupils and only one type of light sensor, which suggests they cannot see colour as we do. Yet they match coloured backgrounds very well.

Scientists are still working out how. Their eyes can sense polarised light, a pattern in light waves that we can't see. Their skin may even contain light-sensitive proteins. The W-shaped pupils may also help them pick up tiny differences in colour by blurring them slightly.

Three hearts and a hidden shell

Like an octopus, a cuttlefish has three hearts. Two pump blood through the gills and one pumps it around the body. Its blood is blue-green, because it uses copper to carry oxygen instead of iron.

Inside its back is a cuttlebone, a light, chalky shell full of tiny gas-filled chambers. By changing the amount of gas and liquid inside, the cuttlefish floats higher or sinks lower. Washed-up cuttlebones are sold in pet shops for birds to peck for calcium. Most cuttlefish live only a year or two. Common cuttlefish females lay clusters of eggs darkened with ink, which hang from seaweed like bunches of black grapes.

Clever and patient

In 2021, scientists in Cambridge, England, tested cuttlefish with a version of the marshmallow test. Many waited for their favourite food, live shrimp, instead of eating crab right away. Some waited more than two minutes. The ones that waited longest were also the fastest learners in other tests.

Cuttlefish also squirt ink to escape. And the strangest part? Their brown ink was once used by artists and writers, and gave its name to a colour, sepia, as the story of old art materials shows.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Cuttlefish are a type of fish.

Truth: They are molluscs, related to snails, octopuses and squid. They have no backbone. The "fish" in their name comes from old naming, much like starfish and jellyfish, which aren't fish either.
