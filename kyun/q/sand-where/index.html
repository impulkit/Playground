Where does sand come from?

Some of the white sand on tropical beaches has passed through the stomach of a fish.

The short answer

Sand is rock that has been broken into tiny pieces. Geologists call any grain between about 0.06 mm and 2 mm wide "sand", whatever it is made of.

Most sand starts in mountains. Rain, ice, frost and plants slowly crack rocks like granite apart, a process called weathering. Rivers carry the loose bits downstream, bumping and grinding them smaller along the way. Softer minerals wear away, but quartz is hard and doesn't dissolve easily, so it survives. That is why most beach and river sand is mainly quartz.

Some sand has a very different story. It can come from volcanoes, from broken shells and coral, or even from the guts of fish.

Surprising but true

The world uses so much sand for concrete and glass that, after water, it is one of the most used natural materials on Earth.

A very long trip downhill

A grain of sand on a beach in Odisha might have started as part of a rock high in the Himalayas. Rivers such as the Ganga and Brahmaputra carry enormous loads of sand and silt from the mountains, which is why they look brown during the monsoon. Those Himalayan rivers drop much of it where they slow down and meet the sea, building huge deltas.

Waves and currents then spread the sand along the coast. The trip can take thousands or even millions of years, as grains are buried, lifted and washed onwards again.

Black, white, pink and green

On young volcanic islands, there hasn't been time for quartz sand to arrive. Instead, waves smash cooled lava into black crumbs. Beaches on the Big Island of Hawaii and in Iceland are jet black for this reason.

On coral islands like the Maldives, the sand is mostly broken bits of coral and shells. Parrotfish help. They scrape at coral and algae with beak-like teeth, grind up the hard parts, and pass them out as fine white sand. By some estimates, a single large parrotfish can make hundreds of kilograms of sand a year.

Some beaches in Bermuda look pink, thanks to the shells of tiny red sea creatures. Papakōlea Beach in Hawaii is green, from crystals of a volcanic mineral called olivine. At White Sands in New Mexico, the dunes are made of gypsum, not quartz.

The sand we build with

After water, sand and gravel are among the most used natural materials on Earth. Mixed with cement, gravel and water, it becomes concrete. Every flyover, metro line and tall building in cities from Bengaluru to Chicago depends on it, including skyscrapers. Melted down, quartz sand becomes glass, and purified, it gives the silicon used to make computer chips.

A 2019 report by the United Nations Environment Programme warned that people are taking sand from rivers and beaches faster than nature replaces it. In India, as in many countries, illegal sand mining from riverbeds is a serious problem. It can deepen rivers, weaken bridges and wash away farmland.

Why desert sand doesn't help

You might think deserts could solve the shortage. But wind-blown desert grains are worn smooth and round, like tiny marbles. They roll past each other instead of locking together, so they make weak concrete. Builders prefer the rough, angular grains found in rivers and on some coasts.

So even countries surrounded by sand dunes sometimes buy sand from far away. Sand that formed over millions of years is now shipped around the world like any other cargo.

Myth or fact?

Myth: All sand is basically the same stuff.

Truth: Sand is defined by grain size, not by what it is made of. It can be quartz, volcanic rock, coral, shells, gypsum or even tiny green crystals, which is why beaches come in white, black, pink, gold and green.

Try this

Collect a spoonful of sand from a few different places, like a beach, a riverbank, a sandpit or a building site, with an adult. Spread each on dark paper and look at it under a magnifying glass. Compare colours, shapes and sizes, and look for bits of shell.
