What was the Indian National Army?

During the Second World War, tens of thousands of Indian soldiers changed sides and marched towards India behind the slogan "Dilli Chalo", on to Delhi.

The short answer

The Indian National Army, or INA, was an army of Indians formed during the Second World War to fight British rule. Most of its soldiers had been in the British Indian Army and were captured by Japan in Southeast Asia. Others were Indian workers and traders living in Malaya, Burma and Singapore.

From 1943 it was led by Subhas Chandra Bose, known as Netaji. Fighting beside Japanese forces, the INA tried to push into India through Manipur and Nagaland in 1944. It was defeated. Yet after the war, when the British put its officers on trial, the INA became a symbol of courage for millions of Indians.

Surprising but true

The INA had a regiment of women soldiers, the Rani of Jhansi Regiment, at a time when very few armies in the world let women fight.

Prisoners who changed sides

In February 1942, Japan captured Singapore, then a British base. Tens of thousands of Indian soldiers became prisoners. A captain named Mohan Singh persuaded many of them to form an army to free India, with Japanese support.

The first INA broke up within the year, because its leaders did not trust Japan's aims. It came back to life in 1943, when Subhas Bose arrived in Southeast Asia after a long, secret journey by submarine from Germany. He had left India in disguise in 1941, escaping house arrest.

Netaji's government

In October 1943, Bose announced a Provisional Government of Free India, the Azad Hind government. It had its own flag, stamps and money, and a few countries allied with Japan recognised it. At the end of that year he raised the Indian flag in Port Blair, in the Andaman Islands, which Japan had captured.

Bose gave the army stirring slogans. "Jai Hind" became its greeting and is still used today. The INA's marching song, "Kadam Kadam Badhaye Ja", is still played by Indian army bands. He also formed the Rani of Jhansi Regiment for women, led by a young doctor, Lakshmi Swaminathan, later known as Captain Lakshmi Sahgal.

The march on Imphal

In 1944, INA units joined a Japanese attack on Imphal and Kohima in northeast India. They raised their flag at Moirang in Manipur. But the attack failed. British and Indian troops held their ground, supplies ran out, the monsoon turned paths to mud, and many soldiers died of hunger and disease.

By 1945 the INA was retreating through Burma. Japan surrendered in August. Bose was reported to have died after a plane crash in Taiwan that month. Some people in India refused to believe it, and the question was debated for decades.

After independence, INA soldiers were not taken back into the Indian Army, but the government later recognised them as freedom fighters. Lakshmi Sahgal returned to medicine and ran a clinic in Kanpur for decades. In 2002, aged 87, she stood for election as President of India.

The trials that shook the Raj

In late 1945 the British put three INA officers on trial at Delhi's Red Fort: Shah Nawaz Khan, Prem Sahgal and Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon. One was Muslim, one Hindu and one Sikh, so people of every community felt the trial was about them. Lawyers including Jawaharlal Nehru and Bhulabhai Desai defended them.

The three were found guilty, but protests were so strong that their punishments were cancelled. In early 1946, sailors of the Royal Indian Navy also rose up. Some historians think these events showed Britain it could no longer rely on Indian soldiers to hold India. Others argue that the bigger reasons were the war's cost and the long freedom struggle. Many also criticise the INA's alliance with Japan, whose army treated prisoners and civilians very harshly. The debate about the INA's place in the story of 1947 is still alive.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The INA defeated the British army and won India's freedom on the battlefield.

Truth: The INA lost its main battles in 1944 and 1945. Its biggest effect came afterwards, when the trials of its officers stirred anger across India and shook British confidence in Indian troops.
