How did Amar Chitra Katha bring history to comics?

It began because a man watching a TV quiz was upset that Indian children knew Greek gods better than their own country's stories.

The short answer

Amar Chitra Katha is a series of Indian comic books started in 1967 by Anant Pai in Bombay. The name means "immortal picture stories". Each comic tells one story, from the Ramayana and the Mahabharata to the lives of emperors, freedom fighters and scientists, in about 30 colourful pages.

The comics brought history and mythology to children who might never read a thick history book. For decades, they were sold at railway stations, lent between friends and read on long train journeys. Many Indian adults say they first met Ashoka, Shivaji or the Rani of Jhansi in its pages.

Surprising but true

Amar Chitra Katha's very first titles weren't Indian stories at all. They were versions of Western fairy tales, and they didn't sell well.

A quiz that started it all

Anant Pai had worked on comics for a Bombay newspaper group. In 1967, as he often told the story, he watched a TV quiz from Delhi. The children could answer questions about Greek gods but not about Rama's mother. Pai felt Indian children were losing touch with their own stories.

He persuaded the publishers India Book House to try comics. The first titles were Indian versions of Western fairy tales like Jack and the Beanstalk. They didn't do well. The series took off once it turned to Indian stories, starting with Krishna.

How the comics were made

Pai and his team chose a subject, researched it in old texts and history books, and wrote a script panel by panel. Artists such as Ram Waeerkar, Pratap Mullick and Souren Roy then drew and inked the pages, and colourists finished them.

Each comic had to fit a lot of story into a small space. Pai insisted on research, sending writers to scholars and museums. He wanted clothes, weapons and buildings to look right for the period.

How are comics made?The most important part of a comic might be the part with nothing in it, the thin white gap between the panels.

From gods to scientists

Over the years, the series published hundreds of titles. Some retold the Ramayana and the Mahabharata in many parts. Others told history: Shivaji, the Rani of Jhansi, Ashoka, Akbar and Tipu Sultan. There were saints and teachers of many faiths, including Guru Nanak, Kabir, the Buddha and Mahavira.

Folk stories had their place too, with Akbar and Birbal, Tenali Raman and Jataka tales. Later comics covered scientists, sports heroes and modern leaders.

In 1980, Pai started Tinkle, a children's magazine. Its funny characters, like the foolish Suppandi and the hunter Shikari Shambu, became household names.

Praise and criticism

Amar Chitra Katha is often praised for getting millions of children interested in their country's past. It was published in English and in many Indian languages, and has sold tens of millions of copies.

Scholars have also raised fair criticisms. Some say the early comics tended to show heroes as fair-skinned and villains as dark. Others felt certain communities or rulers were shown one-sidedly, and that myth and history were sometimes mixed together. Readers today often enjoy the comics while also learning history from other sources.

Still turning pages

Anant Pai died in 2011, but the comics continue, now also as digital editions and apps. The look of the old comics, with their bright colours and bold outlines, is part of the wider story of comics in India.

Superhero comics from America, which took off in the 1930s and 1940s, mostly invented new heroes. Amar Chitra Katha did something different. It turned stories that were already thousands of years old into comics, and showed that real history could be as exciting as any invented superhero.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Amar Chitra Katha only tells myths about gods.

Truth: It has published hundreds of comics on rulers, freedom fighters, scientists, saints of many faiths, folk tales and Jataka stories, as well as myths.
