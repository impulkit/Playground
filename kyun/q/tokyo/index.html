How does Tokyo work with so many people?

Shinjuku Station in Tokyo handles about 3 million passengers a day, more than the whole population of many cities, and the trains still leave on time.

The short answer

More than 3 crore people live in Greater Tokyo, the area made up of the city and its neighbouring towns. That is more than the population of many countries. Yet streets are clean, crime is low and trains run almost to the second. The city works because of good planning, a huge rail network and people who follow shared habits, such as queuing neatly and keeping quiet on trains.

Tokyo also prepares carefully for disasters. It sits in a part of the world with frequent earthquakes, typhoons and floods, so much of its planning is about staying safe when things go wrong.

Surprising but true

Under the edge of Tokyo is a flood tunnel with a giant underground tank, so tall that people call it the "underground temple" because of its huge concrete pillars.

From Edo to Tokyo

Tokyo began as a fishing village called Edo. In 1603, the shogun Tokugawa Ieyasu, Japan's military ruler, made it his base. By the 1700s, Edo was one of the largest cities in the world, with perhaps a million people. Kyoto remained the emperor's home and Japan's official capital.

In 1868, the emperor moved to Edo, and it was renamed Tokyo, "eastern capital". The city was badly damaged twice in the 1900s, by the Great Kanto Earthquake of 1923 and by bombing in the Second World War. Each time it rebuilt.

Trains, trains, trains

Most people in Tokyo travel by train. Lines run above and below ground, operated by many different companies. Guinness World Records lists Shinjuku Station as the busiest station in the world, used by around 3 million passengers a day.

Trains are so frequent on some lines that they come every two or three minutes. At rush hour, they get extremely crowded, and station staff sometimes help push people in so the doors can close. In 1964, Japan opened the first bullet train, the Shinkansen, linking Tokyo and Osaka just before the Tokyo Olympics.

How do bullet trains go so fast?Japan's bullet train got its long pointed nose partly from a bird. An engineer who loved birdwatching copied the beak of the kingfisher.

Built to shake

Japan has many earthquakes, and Tokyo's buildings are designed for them. Skyscrapers have deep foundations and frames that bend instead of breaking. Some sit on giant rubber pads or shock absorbers that let the ground move beneath them. Tokyo Skytree, 634 m tall, has a central concrete column inspired by the design of old wooden pagodas, which have survived earthquakes for centuries.

Every 1 September, the anniversary of the 1923 earthquake, Japan holds Disaster Prevention Day. Schools and offices practise drills.

Keeping the water out

Typhoons can dump huge amounts of rain on Tokyo. To protect the city, engineers built one of the world's largest flood tunnels. Under Saitama, just north of Tokyo, giant shafts collect overflowing river water and send it to a huge tank, 177 m long and about 25 m high, held up by 59 concrete pillars. Pumps then push the water into a bigger river.

Small spaces, big ideas

Land in Tokyo is expensive, so homes are often small. People make clever use of space with folding furniture and tiny kitchens. The city is full of convenience stores open day and night, and vending machines selling drinks on almost every street.

Tokyo is also a capital of pop culture. Districts such as Akihabara are packed with shops selling electronics, games and manga and anime. Food is just as important. Tokyo has more restaurants with Michelin stars than any other city, but most people eat in small noodle shops and family restaurants.

A city that sorts its rubbish

Tokyo stays clean even though there are few public bins. People carry their rubbish home and sort it into types, such as burnable, non-burnable and recyclable, and put each out on its own collection day.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Tokyo has always been the capital of Japan.

Truth: Kyoto was the capital for over a thousand years, from 794. The emperor moved to Edo in 1868, and the city was renamed Tokyo, meaning "eastern capital".
