Is the blue whale bigger than any dinosaur?

Its heart weighs about as much as a big motorbike. Its tongue weighs as much as an elephant.

The short answer

Some long-necked dinosaurs, such as Argentinosaurus, may have been a little longer than a blue whale. But the whale is far heavier. A big blue whale can weigh 150 tonnes or more, about twice the best guesses for the largest dinosaurs.

So the blue whale is the heaviest animal known to have ever lived. Water holds up its enormous weight. On land, a body that heavy would struggle to support itself.

And this giant lives on some of the smallest food in the sea. Blue whales eat tiny shrimp-like krill, up to several tonnes a day when food is plentiful.

A heart as heavy as a motorbike

In 2014 a dead blue whale washed up in Newfoundland, Canada, and scientists at the Royal Ontario Museum preserved its heart. It weighed about 180 kg, roughly as much as a big motorbike. Visitors still line up to see it.

In 2019 a team in California stuck a sensor onto a wild blue whale with suction cups. On deep dives its heart slowed to just two beats a minute. At the surface it raced to about 37. Slowing down saves oxygen while the whale is underwater, a bit like your own heartbeat slowing when you rest.

How to eat a mountain of krill

A blue whale has no teeth. Instead it has rows of bristly plates called baleen hanging from its upper jaw. To feed, it charges at a cloud of krill and opens its mouth wide. Pleats in its throat stretch like a balloon, and it gulps a mouthful of water about as heavy as its own body.

Then it pushes the water out through the baleen, which works like a sieve. The krill stay inside. Scientists think whales can't grow much bigger because they would struggle to find enough krill to fuel the extra size.

A voice that crosses oceans

Blue whales make long, deep moans. They are among the loudest sounds made by any animal, and they can travel hundreds of kilometres through the water. Many of the notes are too low for human ears. Scientists record them with underwater microphones and speed them up to listen. You can hear more in whale songs.

Each population sings in its own style. Blue whales near Sri Lanka sound different from those off California.

The biggest baby

A newborn blue whale is already about 7 m long, longer than an autorickshaw and a car parked end to end. It weighs around as much as a full-grown rhino. Its mother feeds it rich, fatty milk, and the calf gains about 90 kg a day, the weight of a grown man.

Like all whales, a blue whale is a mammal. It breathes air through two blowholes on top of its head, and it must come up to breathe even while it sleeps. Its ancestors were small four-legged animals that lived on land about 50 million years ago, a story told in walking whales.

Back from the brink

In the 1900s, whaling ships with harpoon guns hunted blue whales for their oil. Hundreds of thousands were killed, most of them in the cold seas around Antarctica. By the 1960s very few were left, and in 1966 they were protected worldwide.

Their numbers are slowly rising, but they are still endangered. Today they face ship strikes and noisy oceans. The good news is that people now travel to watch them instead. Off Mirissa in Sri Lanka, boats head out every morning in season, hoping to see a tail rise above the waves.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Whales spray seawater out of their blowholes.

Truth: The tall spout is the whale's breath. It is warm and wet, so tiny droplets form a cloud when it hits the cooler air, like your breath on a cold morning.

Try this

Take a measuring tape outside and mark 30 m with chalk or two sticks. If you have no tape, count about 45 big steps. Walk from one end to the other. That is how long the biggest blue whales grow, about two and a half city buses.
