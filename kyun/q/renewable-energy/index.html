Which energy sources never run out?

The sunlight that reaches Earth in about an hour and a half carries roughly as much energy as all of humanity uses in a year.

The short answer

Renewable energy comes from sources that nature refills quickly, on a human timescale. Sunlight arrives every day. The wind keeps blowing because the Sun heats the Earth unevenly. Rain keeps refilling rivers. Heat keeps leaking out from deep inside the Earth. Plants regrow.

Coal, oil and natural gas are different. They formed from ancient plants and sea creatures over millions of years. Once we burn them, they are gone, and burning them releases carbon dioxide that warms the planet.

So no energy source lasts forever, since even the Sun will change in about 5 billion years. But for people, sunlight, wind, water and underground heat will never run out. In 2023, renewables made about 30% of the world's electricity.

Sunshine and wind

The Sun sends Earth far more energy than people could ever use. Roughly an hour and a half of sunlight carries as much energy as all of humanity uses in a year. Solar panels turn sunlight straight into electricity, and they have become much cheaper over the past 15 years.

India has some of the world's largest solar parks. Bhadla, in the Thar Desert of Rajasthan, spreads panels across an area larger than some cities. Rooftop panels now sit on homes, schools and railway stations. In 2015, the airport at Kochi in Kerala began running entirely on solar power.

Wind turbines work best on windy coasts, plains and hills. Tamil Nadu and Gujarat lead in India, while Texas produces more wind power than any other US state.

Falling water and hot rocks

Hydropower uses water falling from a dam or rushing down a river to spin turbines. It is the oldest big source of renewable electricity. The Hoover Dam on the Colorado River has been making power since the 1930s, and India's Himalayan rivers power dozens of projects.

Geothermal energy uses heat from underground. In Iceland, hot water from beneath the ground heats most homes, and steam spins turbines for electricity. It works best in volcanic places, though some heat is available almost anywhere if you dig deep enough.

The hard part: when the wind stops

The Sun sets and the wind drops, but people want power all the time. This is the biggest challenge for renewable energy. Engineers solve it in a few ways. They spread solar and wind farms across large areas, since it is rarely calm and cloudy everywhere at once. They build huge batteries, similar to the ones in phones but the size of shipping containers. And they use pumped hydro, which stores energy by pumping water uphill and releasing it later.

Some scientists are also working on hydrogen made with clean electricity, and on fusion, which tries to copy the way the Sun makes energy. These are still being developed.

Costs and choices

Renewables are not free of problems. Solar parks and wind farms need lots of land. Panels, turbines and batteries need metals that must be mined. Large dams can drown forests and force villages to move, which has caused protests in India and elsewhere, such as over the Sardar Sarovar dam on the Narmada.

Still, the sunlight, wind and water will keep arriving whatever we decide. Many countries, including India and the USA, are building renewable power quickly, partly to cut the pollution that causes climate change. India has set a goal of 500 gigawatts of power capacity from non-fossil sources by 2030.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Renewable energy has no downsides at all.

Truth: It produces far less pollution than coal or oil, but it still needs land, mining for metals like lithium and copper, and ways to store power. Big dams can flood forests and villages.

Place a small solar-powered calculator or garden light in sunlight, then in shade, then cover it with your hand. Notice how its output changes. Then think about how a city might keep its lights on when clouds roll in.
