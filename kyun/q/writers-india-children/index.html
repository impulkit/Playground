Who writes children's books in India?

A cartoonist started a children's publishing house in Delhi in 1957, and Indian children's books have been growing in dozens of languages ever since.

The short answer

Children's books in India are written by poets, teachers, journalists, scientists and even an engineer who became one of the country's best-loved storytellers. Some, like Ruskin Bond and Sudha Murty, are household names. Many more write in Bengali, Marathi, Tamil, Malayalam, Hindi and other languages for readers in their own corner of the country.

Behind them are publishers big and small, illustrators, translators and libraries. Together they have built a world of Indian stories, so that a child in Kochi or Kanpur can read about places that look like home.

Surprising but true

Pratham Books' free StoryWeaver website has turned the same picture books into hundreds of languages, including many that rarely appear in print.

Nonsense, ghosts and hills

One of the earliest stars was Sukumar Ray. His Bengali book of nonsense verse, Abol Tabol, came out in 1923 and is still recited by children in Kolkata. His son, the film-maker Satyajit Ray, later wrote the Feluda detective stories and edited Sandesh, the children's magazine his grandfather Upendrakishore Ray Chowdhury had started in 1913. Three generations of one family kept Bengali children reading for most of a century.

Ruskin Bond, born in 1934, has written for more than seventy years. His stories about the hills around Mussoorie and Dehradun, such as The Blue Umbrella, read like letters from a friend. He still lives in Landour, near Mussoorie, and children write to him from all over India. In Gujarat, a teacher named Gijubhai Badheka wrote simple stories for small children in the 1920s and changed how many schools thought about early learning.

A cartoonist starts a trust

In 1957, the cartoonist K. Shankar Pillai set up the Children's Book Trust in Delhi. He wanted good, cheap books written for Indian children, not just copies of British ones. The Trust also ran writing competitions that found new authors.

Ten years later, in 1967, Anant Pai began Amar Chitra Katha, comics retelling myths, legends and history. In 1980 he started Tinkle, whose characters like Suppandi made a whole generation laugh.

How did the printing press change the world?Before the printing press, a single book could take a scribe months to copy by hand. Within fifty years of Gutenberg, Europe had printed millions.

New voices

From the 1990s, new publishers changed what Indian picture books looked like. Tulika Books in Chennai and Tara Books, also in Chennai, worked with folk artists and printed stories in several languages at once. Pratham Books, started in 2004, set out to put "a book in every child's hand" at a low price.

Writers today cover everything from cricket to climate. Paro Anand writes about hard subjects for teenagers. Ranjit Lal writes about birds and animals, often with plenty of jokes. Subhadra Sen Gupta, who died in 2021, made Indian history lively for young readers. And Sudha Murty's grandmother tales sell in huge numbers in both English and Kannada.

One story, many languages

India has 22 languages listed in its Constitution and hundreds of others, which makes translation a big part of children's publishing. A good story written in Marathi may reach far more children once it is translated into Hindi or English.

Pratham Books' free website, StoryWeaver, launched in 2015. Anyone can read, print or translate its picture books, and volunteers have produced versions in hundreds of languages. A child in a village where nobody has ever printed a book in her mother tongue can now read one on a school tablet.

Prizes and fairs

The Sahitya Akademi, India's national academy of letters, gives the Bal Sahitya Puraskar to children's writers in 24 languages each year. Book fairs in Kolkata, Delhi and other cities draw huge crowds of families. The Kolkata fair, held every winter, is one of the busiest book fairs anywhere, and many children buy their first book of their own there. Smaller fairs and school libraries matter just as much. A writer can only reach a child if the book is actually on a shelf nearby.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Good children's books in India are only written in English.

Truth: Some of India's best-loved children's writing is in Bengali, Marathi, Malayalam, Hindi, Tamil and other languages. Sukumar Ray wrote in Bengali, and the Sahitya Akademi gives a children's literature prize in 24 languages every year.

Try this

Find a story you love in one language and tell it aloud to someone in another language you know, even if it is only a few words of Hindi, Tamil, English or Spanish. Notice which jokes and words don't translate. That puzzle is what translators solve every day.
