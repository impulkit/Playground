What is cyberbullying?

Cyberbullying can follow someone home, into their bedroom and onto their phone at midnight, which is part of why it hurts so much.

The short answer

Cyberbullying is bullying that happens through phones, computers or games. It can mean sending cruel messages, spreading rumours, posting embarrassing photos without permission, or deliberately leaving someone out of a group chat. Some bullies make fake accounts to pretend to be someone else.

It is common. In a 2022 survey by the Pew Research Center, 46% of American teenagers aged 13 to 17 said they had faced at least one kind of online bullying. Being called nasty names was the most common. Studies in India have found similar problems as more children get phones. The good news is that there are clear steps that help, and you never have to deal with it alone.

Surprising but true

In a 2022 survey by the Pew Research Center, almost half of American teenagers said they had been bullied or harassed online at least once.

Why it feels different

Bullying in a playground usually ends when the bell rings. Online, it can carry on at night, at weekends and on holidays. A cruel post can be seen by hundreds of people in minutes, and screenshots can be shared even after the original is deleted.

Distance also changes how people act. On a screen, you can't see someone's face fall or their eyes fill with tears. Psychologists call this the online disinhibition effect: people say things in messages they would never say out loud. Some bullies hide behind fake names, which makes them feel braver and the target feel more helpless.

What it can look like

Cyberbullying isn't always obvious. It can be a stream of mocking comments on a video, or a "joke" edited photo that everyone shares. It can be a group chat where one person's messages are always ignored. In online games, it can mean a team ganging up on one player, again and again.

The key word is "again". One rude comment is unpleasant. Bullying is when someone keeps targeting a person on purpose, and the target finds it hard to make it stop.

Sometimes it starts between friends. A falling-out at school carries on in a chat after dark, where other people pile in. That's one reason adults at school often want to know about online problems, even if they happened at home.

What to do if it happens to you

Don't reply. Bullies often want a reaction. Take screenshots of the messages, with dates, before anything gets deleted. Then block the person and use the app's report button. Most big apps and games have one.

Most important, tell a trusted adult: a parent, teacher or school counsellor. Many young people stay quiet because they fear losing their phone. A good adult will focus on keeping you safe. In India, serious cases can be reported at cybercrime.gov.in or on the helpline 1930. In the USA, StopBullying.gov explains what families and schools can do.

When you are the bystander

Most people in an online group are neither bullies nor targets. They're watching. What they do matters a lot. Liking or forwarding a cruel post spreads it further. Staying silent can make the target feel that everyone agrees.

Small actions help. Send the person a private message saying "that wasn't okay, are you alright?" Refuse to share the post. Help them tell an adult. Researchers who study empathy find that one supportive voice can make a big difference to how someone copes.

Thinking before you post

Everything you put online adds to your digital footprint, the trail of posts and messages you leave behind. A quick test helps: would you say this to the person's face, in front of their parents? If not, it probably shouldn't be sent. Many people who end up bullying online didn't plan to. They joined in, or made a joke that went too far, on social media where the audience was far larger than they realised.

Myth or fact?

Myth: It's just words on a screen, so it doesn't really hurt.

Truth: Online bullying can be as upsetting as bullying face to face, and sometimes more, because it can happen at any hour and in front of a big audience. It is real bullying, and it is worth taking seriously.

Try this

With a friend or family member, write two lists. On one, put things that make a group chat feel friendly. On the other, put things that make it feel unsafe. Then turn the first list into three simple rules you would like every group chat you join to follow.
