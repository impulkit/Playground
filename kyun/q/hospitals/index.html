What happens in a hospital?

A big hospital never sleeps. At 3 a.m., while you're in bed, someone is being X-rayed, someone is being born and someone is mopping the floors.

The short answer

A hospital is a place where sick and injured people are looked after by many different experts, all day and all night. Doctors and nurses are the best known, but they work alongside lab scientists, pharmacists, radiographers, cooks, cleaners, porters and engineers.

Some people come in for a few hours: to see a specialist, have a test or get a small operation. Others stay for days or weeks in a ward, a room with beds where nurses check on them around the clock. Babies are born there, broken bones are set, and very sick people get help breathing.

Surprising but true

Over 2,000 years ago, the Indian emperor Ashoka had it carved in stone that he had arranged medical care for both people and animals.

The front door: emergency

Many people first enter a hospital through the emergency department, called "casualty" in India and the "ER" in the USA. Here, a nurse does triage, a word from the French trier, meaning to sort. Each patient is quickly checked and sorted by how urgent their problem is.

Ambulances bring in the most serious cases. A team may already be waiting, warned by radio. Others may wait longer, which can feel unfair but means the system is working.

Behind the scenes

Much of a hospital's work happens where patients rarely go. In the lab, scientists test blood and other samples under microscopes and in machines, looking for infections or checking how organs are working. In the blood bank, donated blood is sorted by blood group and stored, ready for operations and accidents.

Radiology uses X-rays, ultrasound and scanners to look inside the body. The pharmacy prepares medicines. Kitchens cook special meals, and laundries wash huge piles of sheets every day.

The operating theatre

Surgery happens in an operating theatre, a room kept extremely clean. Surgeons scrub their hands and arms, then wear sterile gowns, gloves and masks. Instruments are cleaned with heat and steam so no germs survive.

This care is fairly new. In 1847, a doctor named Ignaz Semmelweis in Vienna found that far fewer mothers died after childbirth when doctors washed their hands in a chlorine solution. Many doctors ignored him at first. Today, handwashing with soap is one of the most important rules in any hospital.

Hospitals through history

Places for healing are very old. The emperor Ashoka, who ruled much of India in the 3rd century BCE, recorded on stone that he had provided medical care for people and animals. Large hospitals were built in cities like Baghdad, Damascus and Cairo more than 800 years ago, with separate wards and libraries.

In the 1850s, during the Crimean War, the British nurse Florence Nightingale cleaned up a filthy army hospital in Turkey and fought to keep wards airy and clean. She went on to found a school for nurses in London and made nursing a respected, trained profession.

In America, Benjamin Franklin and Dr Thomas Bond founded the Pennsylvania Hospital in Philadelphia in 1751, the first in the colonies. In 1956, India opened the All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS) in New Delhi, which still treats thousands of patients every day.

The intensive care unit

The sickest patients go to the intensive care unit, or ICU. Here each patient has many machines: monitors that beep with heart rate and oxygen levels, drips that deliver fluids, and ventilators that help them breathe. A nurse may look after just one or two patients.

It can look frightening, with all those wires and screens. But every beep is someone keeping watch.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In an emergency department, patients are seen in the order they arrive.

Truth: Patients are sorted by how serious their problem is, which is called triage. That is why a long wait usually means the doctors think your problem can safely wait a little.

Try this

Write five pretend patients on cards, such as a sprained ankle, a cough, a bad nosebleed, a broken arm and a child who can't breathe properly. With a friend, sort them in the order a triage nurse should see them, and explain your reasons. Then compare your order with a grown-up's.
