How does the US economy work?

About two-thirds of the American economy is ordinary people buying things, from groceries and haircuts to cars and video games.

The short answer

The US economy is the largest in the world, worth about 30 trillion dollars a year in 2025. It works mainly as a market economy. That means most things are made and sold by private businesses, and prices are set mostly by supply and demand rather than by the government.

Ordinary shoppers drive it. About two-thirds of the economy is households buying things: food, rent, phones, doctors' visits, streaming services, holidays. Businesses spend money building factories and offices. The government spends on schools, roads, the military and pensions. The rest comes from trade with other countries. When any of these slows down, Americans feel it in jobs and prices.

Surprising but true

If California were a separate country, its economy would rank among the five largest in the world.

Who does what

Most Americans work in services: shops, hospitals, banks, schools, restaurants, transport and technology. Factories still make planes, cars, medicines and computer chips, but they use more machines and fewer people than in the past.

Farms employ under 2% of workers, yet the USA is one of the world's biggest exporters of corn and soybeans, thanks to large farms and heavy machinery. Technology companies, many born in Silicon Valley in California, make some of the most valuable businesses on Earth.

Geography helps. The USA has huge areas of farmland, long rivers for shipping, oil and gas, and coasts facing both Europe and Asia. It also draws workers and students from around the world, including many Indian engineers and doctors.

Why is Silicon Valley famous?Fewer than a hundred years ago, the valley south of San Francisco was famous for apricot and plum orchards. Now it's home to Apple, Google and Nvidia.

Money and the Fed

The Federal Reserve, often called the Fed, is the USA's central bank. It was created in 1913 after a series of banking panics. Its main job is to keep prices fairly steady and jobs plentiful. It does this mostly by raising or lowering interest rates, the cost of borrowing.

The US dollar is the most widely used money in the world. Countries trade oil and many other goods in dollars, and central banks across the world keep dollars in reserve. That gives the USA unusual power, and makes the Fed's choices matter far beyond America.

The USA also buys more from other countries than it sells to them, which economists call a trade deficit. It imports phones, clothes, cars and oil, and exports planes, chips, farm crops, films and software. India is an important partner. Indian companies run software services for American firms, and Americans buy Indian medicines, jewellery and clothes. In 2025 the US government raised import taxes, called tariffs, on goods from many countries, including India, and that set off sharp arguments about trade.

Shares, savings and Wall Street

Many big American companies are owned by millions of people who hold small pieces, called shares. They are bought and sold on the stock market, centred on Wall Street in New York. Many families invest their retirement savings there.

When share prices rise, people feel richer and often spend more. When they crash, it can hurt everyone. In 1929 a stock market crash helped trigger the Great Depression, when about a quarter of American workers lost their jobs.

States, rules and gaps

The USA has 50 states, and each sets many of its own rules. The federal minimum wage has been 7.25 dollars an hour since 2009, but states such as California and Washington set it much higher. California's economy alone would rank among the world's five largest if it were a country.

The economy is rich but uneven. Average income per person is well over 80,000 dollars a year, but the richest families own a large share of the wealth, and many people struggle to afford housing and healthcare. Americans argue fiercely about taxes, trade and how much the government should do. Those debates shape elections every few years.

Myth or fact?

Myth: America doesn't make things in factories any more.

Truth: Factory jobs have fallen, but the USA is still the world's second-biggest manufacturer after China. Its factories make planes, cars, medicines, chips and machinery, often with more robots and fewer workers than before.

Try this

For one day, keep a list of every purchase your family makes, even small ones like a bus fare or a packet of biscuits. Next to each, write whether it was a good, like food, or a service, like a haircut. Count which list is longer. In both the USA and India, services are now the bigger part of the economy.
