What was the Partition of India?

In August 1947 a line drawn in five weeks split a land of 40 crore people, and families woke up on the wrong side of a border.

The short answer

When British rule over India ended in August 1947, the land was split into two countries: India and Pakistan. Pakistan itself came in two pieces, West Pakistan and East Pakistan, about 1,600 km apart with India in between.

The split, called Partition, divided the provinces of Punjab and Bengal roughly along religious lines. Areas with Muslim majorities went to Pakistan and the rest stayed with India. Millions of Hindus and Sikhs moved one way and millions of Muslims the other. It was one of the largest movements of people in history, and the violence that came with it killed hundreds of thousands, possibly far more.

Surprising but true

Cyril Radcliffe, the British lawyer who drew the new border, had never been to India before he arrived to do the job.

Why it happened

By the 1940s, Britain was weakened by the Second World War and ready to leave India. The question was what would replace British rule.

The Indian National Congress, led by people like Nehru and Gandhi, wanted one united India. The Muslim League, led by Muhammad Ali Jinnah, argued that Muslims would be outnumbered in a Hindu-majority country and needed a homeland of their own. In 1940 the League formally demanded what became Pakistan.

Talks failed, and trust between the groups broke down. In 1946 riots broke out in Calcutta and spread. Historians still argue over how much blame belongs to British policy, to the League, or to Congress. Most agree all three made choices that led there.

A line drawn in a hurry

In early 1947, Lord Mountbatten, the last Viceroy, brought the date of British withdrawal forward by almost a year, to August 1947. That left very little time.

Sir Cyril Radcliffe was given about five weeks to draw borders through Punjab and Bengal. He had never visited India, worked from old maps and census figures, and hardly left his office in Delhi. The line he drew cut through farms, rivers, railway lines and even villages. Lahore went to Pakistan, Amritsar to India.

The great migration

As the border became known, fear spread. People who suddenly found themselves a minority packed what they could carry and left homes their families had lived in for generations. They walked in columns many kilometres long, rode bullock carts, or crammed into and on top of trains.

Who are refugees?Albert Einstein was a refugee. So were millions of families who crossed new borders when India was partitioned in 1947.

There were terrible attacks on all sides, especially in Punjab. Many women and children suffered. At the same time, there are many stories of neighbours who hid and protected each other at great risk.

In Bengal, Gandhi went on a fast in Calcutta in September 1947, refusing food until the fighting stopped. Leaders from different communities promised peace, and the city calmed.

What it left behind

The new countries had to look after crores of refugees. Camps grew around Delhi and other cities. Whole new neighbourhoods and towns appeared, such as Faridabad near Delhi, built partly for families from the west.

Partition also left a dispute over Kashmir that India and Pakistan still argue about. In 1971, East Pakistan broke away to become Bangladesh.

Remembering

For many years families talked about 1947 only quietly. Writers such as Saadat Hasan Manto and Khushwant Singh wrote stories about it. In 2017 the Partition Museum opened in Amritsar's old Town Hall, collecting objects people carried with them: keys to houses they never saw again, letters, and a few cooking pots.

Today the border at Wagah, between Amritsar and Lahore, holds a flag ceremony every evening, with soldiers of both countries marching on either side of the line.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Partition happened only along India's western border with Pakistan.

Truth: Bengal in the east was split too. East Bengal became East Pakistan, and in 1971 it became the independent country of Bangladesh.

Try this

Many families in India, Pakistan and Bangladesh have stories from 1947. If someone in your family or neighbourhood is happy to talk about it, ask an older person what they remember hearing. Write down their story in their own words and keep it, because these memories are disappearing.
