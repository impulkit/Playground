How is kabaddi played?

In kabaddi one player walks into a crowd of seven opponents alone, tags as many as possible and tries to get home without being caught.

The short answer

Kabaddi is a contact sport for two teams of seven players. One team sends a single player, the raider, across the middle line into the other team's half. The raider tries to touch as many defenders as possible and get back to their own half. The defenders try to grab and hold the raider so that they cannot get back.

Every defender the raider touches is out, if the raider gets home. If the defenders stop the raider, the raider is out. Teams take turns raiding, and the team with more points at the end wins.

Surprising but true

Kabaddi was shown to the world at the 1936 Berlin Olympics by a sports club from Amravati, Maharashtra, as a demonstration sport.

One breath, one raid

In the traditional game, the raider had to chant "kabaddi, kabaddi" over and over on a single breath, called the cant. If the chanting stopped, the raider was out. It was a test of lungs as well as legs.

Modern professional kabaddi gives each raid a 30-second limit. Raiders still chant, but the clock decides. A raider must also cross the baulk line, a line in the opponents' half, for a raid to count, and can earn a bonus point by reaching a further bonus line.

Defenders, chains and the all-out

Defenders work together. They often hold hands in pairs or chains and wait for the raider to come close, then pounce by grabbing an ankle or wrapping the raider's waist. A single defender diving at a raider risks being touched and sent off.

Out players can come back. When a team gets one of the other side out, one of its own out players returns. If a team loses all seven players, the opponents score two bonus points for an "all-out", and the whole team comes back on. The game swings in an instant, because a single raid can win or lose four or five points. Good raiders have lightning reflexes and can twist out of a grip in a split second.

Played across South Asia

Kabaddi has been played for centuries in villages across India and its neighbours, under many names. It is hu-tu-tu in parts of Maharashtra, chedugudu in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, and hadudu in Bangladesh, where it is the national sport. Iran, Pakistan, Nepal and South Korea have strong teams too.

The game needs no equipment at all. A flat patch of earth and some lines scratched with a stick are enough.

On the world stage

In 1936 members of the Hanuman Vyayam Prasarak Mandal, a sports club from Amravati, travelled to the Berlin Olympics and demonstrated kabaddi and other Indian games. Kabaddi was standardised in the decades after independence.

It became a medal sport at the 1990 Asian Games in Beijing. India's men won every gold until 2018, when Iran beat them in the semi-final and went on to win. India took both the men's and women's golds back at the next Asian Games, held in 2023.

The Pro Kabaddi boom

In 2014 a professional league, Pro Kabaddi, began, borrowing ideas from cricket's IPL. It moved games indoors onto colourful mats, added the raid clock and put matches on prime-time television. Its first season drew huge audiences, and village players became household names.

Kabaddi is the closest cousin of kho-kho, India's other great chasing game. Both reward speed and teamwork. Kabaddi adds something extra: the courage to walk alone into a crowd of opponents and come back with points.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Kabaddi has been an official Olympic sport.

Truth: It has never been one. It was shown at Berlin in 1936 as a demonstration only. Its big international stage is the Asian Games, where it has been a medal sport since 1990.

Try this

In a safe open space, mark a line with chalk or a rope. Take turns crossing it while saying "kabaddi, kabaddi" on one breath, then return. Count how long you can chant comfortably, but stop and breathe as soon as you need to.
