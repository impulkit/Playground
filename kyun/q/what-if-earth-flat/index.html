What would happen if Earth were flat?

On a flat Earth, gravity would pull everything towards the middle, so walking to the edge would feel like climbing a hill that never ends.

The short answer

Earth is round. Photos from space, ships sailing around it, and the curved shadow it casts on the Moon all prove it. But scientists enjoy asking "what if", because it shows how things really work.

If Earth were a flat disc, gravity would not pull straight down everywhere. It would pull towards the centre of the disc. Near the middle, things would feel normal. Near the edge, "down" would point sideways and inwards, so the ground would feel like a steep hill. Oceans and air would slide towards the centre. The rim would be a dry, airless, freezing wasteland.

And the strangest part? A flat Earth could not stay flat. Its own gravity would crush it back into a ball.

Surprising but true

Around 240 BCE, the Greek scholar Eratosthenes worked out the size of the round Earth using shadows, a well and some geometry, and he came surprisingly close.

Gravity pulls to the middle

Gravity pulls everything towards the centre of a planet's mass. On a ball, that is the middle of the ball, so "down" points straight under your feet wherever you stand.

On a disc, the centre of mass is in the middle of the flat face. Stand near the centre and gravity pulls straight down. Walk outwards and it starts to tug you back towards the middle as well. By the time you neared the rim, walking would feel like climbing a mountain thousands of kilometres long, even though the ground looked flat.

Oceans in a heap

Water flows downhill. On a flat Earth, "downhill" means towards the middle. So the oceans would gather into a giant dome of water over the centre, deeper there than any real sea.

Air would do the same. The atmosphere would bunch up near the middle and thin out towards the edges. People living near the rim would struggle to breathe, like climbers on Mount Everest, and then not be able to breathe at all.

Day and night all at once

On our round Earth, half the planet is in sunlight at any moment. That is why it is morning in Mumbai when it is night in New York. It is all about how a spinning ball meets the Sun, as explained in day and night.

On a flat disc facing the Sun, the whole side would get daylight at once. Sunrise would happen everywhere at the same time. Time zones would be pointless. When the disc turned edge-on, sunlight would hit only the rim, and then the far side would be in daylight while ours went dark.

Why planets are round

Anything big enough gets squeezed into a ball by its own gravity. Every bit pulls on every other bit, so lumps get dragged down and dips filled in. The only shape where every part of the surface is equally close to the middle is a sphere.

For rocky worlds, this happens once they are very roughly 600 km across or bigger. Earth is about 12,700 km across, so it had no choice. Small asteroids can stay lumpy like potatoes, because they are too small to crush themselves.

How people knew long ago

Around 240 BCE, Eratosthenes in Egypt noticed that at noon on the longest day, the Sun shone straight down a well in the town of Syene. In Alexandria, further north, it cast a short shadow. Using the angle of that shadow and the distance between the towns, he worked out Earth's size. His answer was surprisingly close.

The Greek thinker Aristotle had pointed to the round shadow Earth throws on the Moon during a lunar eclipse. In 499 CE, the Indian astronomer Aryabhata wrote that Earth is round and spins, which is why the stars seem to move across the sky.

Sailors saw the proof too: a ship sailing away disappears hull first, then mast. On a flat sea it would simply shrink. And the most dramatic evidence turns up about once or twice a year, when Earth's curved shadow slides across the Moon during an eclipse.

Myth or fact?

Myth: People in the Middle Ages, including Columbus, thought the Earth was flat.

Truth: Educated people in Europe, India, the Middle East and elsewhere had known for about 2,000 years that Earth was round. The argument with Columbus was about how big Earth is, and he was the one who got it wrong.

Try this

See why big things become round. Squeeze a big handful of play dough into a flat pancake. Now press inwards from every side at once with your hands, as if gravity were pulling all of it towards the middle. However you start, the shape you end up with is a ball.
