What are the smallest countries in the world?

The smallest country on Earth is smaller than many city parks. You could walk across it in about 20 minutes, and it has its own post office.

The short answer

The smallest country in the world is Vatican City, inside the city of Rome in Italy. It covers about 0.44 square kilometres, less than many university campuses. Next comes Monaco, on the coast of France, at about 2 square km. Then come three island countries in the Pacific and Europe's hilltop republic of San Marino.

India is about 7 million times bigger than the Vatican. But tiny countries still have flags, laws, stamps and sometimes even their own football teams.

Surprising but true

Vatican City covers about 0.44 square km. Cubbon Park in Bengaluru is nearly three times bigger.

Vatican City: a country inside a city

Vatican City is the home of the Pope, the head of the Roman Catholic Church. It became an independent country in 1929 through a treaty with Italy. Its walls enclose St Peter's Basilica, the Vatican Museums and gardens.

Fewer than 1,000 people live there. Most are priests, nuns and the Swiss Guards, who have protected the Pope since 1506 in their striped uniforms. Almost nobody is born there. Citizenship usually comes with a job, and ends when the job does.

Yet it has a post office, a radio station, a railway station with very short track, its own stamps and coins, and a pharmacy. Millions of tourists visit every year, far outnumbering the residents.

Monaco: tiny but packed

Monaco sits on a rocky strip of the Mediterranean coast. It is about 2 square km, about the size of a big city neighbourhood in Mumbai. About 38,000 people live there, which makes it one of the most crowded countries anywhere.

Monaco is famous for its harbour full of yachts and its Formula One race through narrow city streets. It has added land by building out into the sea. Its army is tiny, and France has agreed to help defend it.

Nauru: an island of rock

Nauru is a single oval island in the Pacific, about 21 square km. You could drive right around it in about half an hour. It has no official capital city.

For much of the 20th century, Nauru was rich from mining phosphate, a mineral made from ancient seabird droppings that farmers use as fertiliser. Mining stripped much of the island's centre, leaving jagged limestone pinnacles. Most of its roughly 12,000 people live along the coast.

Tuvalu: a country barely above water

Tuvalu is made of nine small islands and atolls, adding up to about 26 square km. Its highest point is only about 4.6 metres above sea level.

That makes it one of the countries most at risk from rising seas. In 2023, Tuvalu signed a treaty with Australia allowing some of its citizens to move there each year. Tuvalu has also started making a digital copy of the country online, so its history and culture are saved even if land is lost.

San Marino and the rest

San Marino, on a mountain inside Italy, covers about 61 square km. It claims to be the oldest surviving republic in the world, founded, by tradition, in the year 301.

Next come Liechtenstein, between Switzerland and Austria, the Marshall Islands and Saint Kitts and Nevis. In Asia, the smallest country is the Maldives, with about 1,200 coral islands adding up to roughly 300 square km.

Small but not alone

Being small has advantages. Tiny countries can be fast at making decisions, and some, like Monaco and Liechtenstein, are wealthy. But they can't easily grow food or defend themselves, so they rely on friendly neighbours.

And there are bigger surprises in the opposite direction. Some cities, like Tokyo or Delhi, hold more people than whole countries, as you'll find among the world's biggest cities. Delhi alone has hundreds of times more people than Monaco, Nauru, Tuvalu and San Marino put together.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A tiny place can't really be a full country.

Truth: Size doesn't decide it. Vatican City, Monaco, Nauru and Tuvalu all run their own affairs and are recognised by other countries. All except the Vatican are full members of the United Nations, and the Vatican takes part as an observer.
