What street foods do people love around the world?

In 2020, UNESCO added Singapore's hawker centres to its list of world heritage traditions, the same kind of list that includes yoga and Kumbh Mela.

The short answer

Almost every city on Earth has its own street food, cheap snacks and meals cooked and sold on pavements, carts and stalls. India has pani puri, vada pav and chaat. Mexico has tacos. Vietnam has bánh mì sandwiches, Japan has takoyaki, Turkey has sesame-crusted simit, and New York has hot dogs and pretzels.

Many street foods tell stories of travel. Recipes moved with traders, workers and rulers, and street cooks mixed them with local tastes. People love them because they are fast, filling and full of flavour, and because eating standing up beside strangers is part of the fun.

Surprising but true

Mexico's famous tacos al pastor were inspired by Lebanese immigrants, who brought the idea of meat roasted on a turning vertical spit.

India's snack streets

India has an enormous range of street snacks, changing every few hundred kilometres. In Mumbai, the vada pav, a spicy potato fritter in a soft bun, is often traced to the 1960s, when stalls near train stations sold it to mill workers. Kolkata's kathi roll started as kebabs wrapped in paratha so office workers could eat without greasy fingers.

The same crispy shell filled with spicy water is called pani puri in Mumbai, golgappa in Delhi and puchka in Kolkata. In Indore, Sarafa Bazaar is a jewellery market by day and turns into a busy food street at night.

Mexico's spinning meat

In the early 20th century, immigrants from Lebanon settled in Mexico. They brought shawarma, meat stacked on a turning vertical spit. Mexican cooks used pork instead of lamb, marinated it with dried chillies and pineapple, and served it in small corn tortillas. The result was tacos al pastor.

Today, tacos are sold from carts on almost every corner of Mexico City. A cook slices meat straight off the spit and flicks a piece of pineapple onto each taco.

How do street vendors run their business?The pani puri seller on your corner runs a business with no office, no ads and no boss, and by 2026 more than 75 lakh Indian vendors had taken small government loans.

Asia's night markets

Singapore moved its street sellers into hawker centres from the 1970s, open-air food courts with dozens of stalls. In 2020, UNESCO added Singapore's hawker culture to its list of world cultural traditions. Some hawker stalls have even won Michelin stars.

In Japan, takoyaki is a ball of batter with a piece of octopus inside, cooked in a special pan with round dips. It was first sold in Osaka in the 1930s. In Taiwan, night markets sell bubble tea, which was invented there in the 1980s. In Bangkok, stalls sell noodle soups, grilled meat on sticks and mango sticky rice late into the night.

Bread, sandwiches and buns

Vietnam's bánh mì shows how history ends up in food. French rulers brought the baguette in the 1800s. Vietnamese cooks made it lighter and filled it with pickled carrots, coriander, chillies and pâté or tofu.

In Turkey, sellers carry stacks of simit, sesame bread rings, on trays balanced on their heads. In China, steamed baozi buns are a common breakfast eaten on the go.

Street food in the USA

In New York City, carts sell hot dogs, pretzels and halal chicken and rice. Food trucks became a big trend in the late 2000s, when a truck in Los Angeles started selling Korean barbecue tacos and announced its location online. Hot dog carts spread in New York in the late 1800s, many run by German immigrants. Soft pretzels arrived with German settlers too, and they are still a famous snack in Philadelphia.

The people behind the stalls

Behind every stall is someone who wakes before dawn to chop, fry and haul their cart into place. Millions of street vendors in India earn their living this way. And the strangest part? Many dishes now served in fancy restaurants, from pani puri shots to gourmet tacos, began as cheap snacks on a pavement corner.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Street food is always unsafe to eat.

Truth: Plenty of street food is cooked fresh in front of you at high heat, which kills most germs. Busy stalls with quick turnover, clean water and hot food are usually a good sign. As anywhere, care with uncooked food and water makes sense.

Try this

Plan a "street food world tour" at home with an adult. Pick two easy street snacks from different countries, like a simple chaat and a cucumber bánh mì-style sandwich. Make them together and score each for sweet, sour, salty, spicy and crunchy.
