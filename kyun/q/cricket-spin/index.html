How does a spinning ball change direction?

A spinner can make the ball jump sideways after it bounces. How?

The short answer

Spin creates a sideways push in the air called the Magnus effect. Footballers use it to bend free kicks. In cricket, it can make a ball drift a little in the air and dip sooner than expected.

When the ball bounces, friction between the spinning ball and the pitch turns it sideways. The spin acts like a car's wheels gripping a road: as the ball lands, the spinning surface "bites" the ground and kicks off in a new direction.

Finger spinners and wrist spinners make the ball turn in different directions. Batters must read the bowler's hand, the ball in the air and the bounce, all in less than a second.

Surprising but true

The same spin science lets a footballer curl a ball around a wall of defenders.

Fingers or wrist

Finger spinners, also called off-spinners when they bowl right-arm, twist the ball with their fingers as they let go. For a right-arm off-spinner, the ball usually turns into a right-handed batter from outside the off stump.

Wrist spinners flick the ball out of the back of the hand with a twist of the wrist. A right-arm leg-spinner's normal ball, the leg-break, turns the other way, away from a right-handed batter. Wrist spin is harder to control but can produce more turn and some sneaky variations, such as the googly, which turns the opposite way with almost the same action.

The grip on the pitch

Friction is what makes the ball turn after bouncing. On a dry, dusty pitch, loose soil and cracks give the ball more to grip, so it can turn a long way. On a hard, green, grassy pitch, it skids on.

That is why spinners love Indian pitches, which often get drier and dustier as a five-day Test goes on. In the 1960s and 1970s, India's famous spin quartet, Bishan Singh Bedi, Erapalli Prasanna, B.S. Chandrasekhar and S. Venkataraghavan, won many matches this way. More recently, R. Ashwin took more than 500 Test wickets before retiring in 2024.

Spin in the air

A spinning ball drags a thin layer of air around with it. On one side, the ball's surface moves with the passing air, and on the other, against it. The air leaves the ball unevenly, and the ball is pushed the other way, just like a baseball curveball.

With overspin, the push is downward, so the ball dips and lands shorter than the batter expected. With sidespin, it can drift sideways. Bowlers also toss the ball up slower, giving it "flight", so it hangs in the air and tempts the batter forward.

Famous spells

In 1993, the Australian leg-spinner Shane Warne bowled his first ball in an Ashes Test in England. It drifted, pitched outside Mike Gatting's leg stump and spun back to hit the off stump. It is often called the "Ball of the Century".

In 1999, at Delhi's Feroz Shah Kotla ground, India's Anil Kumble took all ten Pakistan wickets in one innings. Only two other bowlers have done this in Test cricket. Jim Laker of England did it in 1956. Ajaz Patel of New Zealand, who was born in Mumbai, did it in 2021, against India in Mumbai.

Spin all around us

Spin science turns up in many sports. Tennis players use topspin to make shots dip into the court. Footballers bend free kicks around walls of defenders. Table tennis players add so much spin that a ball can jump sideways off the table. And a spinning top or bicycle wheel shows another side of spin: things that spin fast are hard to tip over.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Spin only matters after the ball bounces. In the air, a spinning ball flies straight.

Truth: Spin also affects the ball in the air. Through the Magnus effect, a spinning ball can drift sideways and dip sooner than a batter expects, before it even lands.

Try this

Roll a rubber or tennis ball along a smooth floor towards a wall while twisting it with your fingers as you let go, first one way, then the other. Watch which way it moves after it hits the wall or bounces. Then try on a rug, which grips more, and compare.
