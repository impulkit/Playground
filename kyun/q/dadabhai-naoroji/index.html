Who was Dadabhai Naoroji?

In 1892 an Indian from Bombay won a seat in Britain's Parliament by just five votes, and used it to tell Britain that it was making India poor.

The short answer

Dadabhai Naoroji was a teacher, businessman, writer and politician from Bombay. He spent more than 60 years arguing that British rule was draining India's wealth, and he backed his case with numbers rather than slogans. He helped found the Indian National Congress in 1885 and led it three times.

In 1892 he became a member of the British House of Commons, the heart of the government that ruled India. He is usually remembered as the first Indian elected to it. That gave Indians a voice inside Parliament for the first time, and it is why people still call him the Grand Old Man of India.

Surprising but true

Dadabhai Naoroji's victory in London was so narrow that people nicknamed him "Mr Narrow Majority".

A teacher from Bombay

Naoroji was born in 1825 into a poor Parsi family. His father died when he was a small child, and his mother worked hard to keep him in school. He was a brilliant student at Elphinstone College in Mumbai, then called Bombay, and later became a professor of mathematics there. He was one of the first Indians to hold such a post.

He also cared about girls' education at a time when few girls went to school. With friends, he helped open schools for girls in Bombay in the 1840s, and he started a Gujarati newspaper, Rast Goftar, which argued for social reform. Later, in 1874, he served for a short time as diwan, or chief minister, of Baroda, one of the princely states ruled by Indian kings under British control. He tried to clean up its government but left after disagreements within a year.

The drain of wealth

In 1855, Naoroji moved to England to work for an Indian trading firm. Living there, he started asking a hard question. Why was India so poor when it produced so much?

His answer became known as the drain theory. India paid for British officials' salaries and pensions, for an army it did not control and for goods it was forced to buy. Much of that money left the country for good. He calculated India's income per person and showed it was tiny. These ideas filled his 1901 book, Poverty and Un-British Rule in India. The East India Company had once run India for profit. Naoroji argued that the British government still did.

Five votes in London

Naoroji decided that the best place to argue was inside Parliament itself. In 1886 he lost his first election. Two years later, Britain's prime minister, Lord Salisbury, said in a speech that British voters would not elect a "black man". The insult backfired. Many British people were shocked, and Naoroji became famous across London.

In 1892 he stood in Finsbury Central, a working-class area of London, and won by just five votes. He took his oath on the Khordeh Avesta, his Zoroastrian holy book. In Parliament he spoke about Indian poverty, unfair taxes and the need for Indians to hold top government jobs. He lost his seat in 1895, but he had made his point.

The word swaraj

By the time he presided over the Congress session in Calcutta in 1906, Naoroji was over 80. His message had sharpened. India, he said, needed swaraj, or self-government. It was one of the first times the demand was made so plainly from that platform.

He also inspired a younger generation. A young lawyer named Gandhi wrote to him for advice from South Africa, where Gandhi was fighting unfair laws. Later leaders built the swadeshi movement partly on his idea that India's wealth should stay in India.

Naoroji died in Bombay in 1917, thirty years before India became independent. A street in London's Finsbury is named after him.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The freedom movement only began in the twentieth century with Gandhi.

Truth: Leaders such as Naoroji were arguing for Indian rights from the 1850s onwards. He was calling for swaraj, or self-rule, in 1906, before Gandhi returned to India.
