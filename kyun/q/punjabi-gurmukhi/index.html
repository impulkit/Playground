What is the Gurmukhi script?

The same Punjabi words can be written in two completely different scripts, depending on which side of the India-Pakistan border you live on.

The short answer

Gurmukhi is the script used to write Punjabi in India. Its name means "from the mouth of the Guru". It was shaped and spread in the 1500s by Guru Angad Dev, the second of the ten Sikh Gurus. He wanted ordinary people to be able to read and write the hymns of Guru Nanak.

Gurmukhi has 35 letters in its traditional order, written left to right, hanging from a line along the top. It is used for everyday Punjabi in newspapers, shop signs and schoolbooks, and for the Guru Granth Sahib, the holy book of Sikhism.

Surprising but true

Punjabi is a tonal language. The pitch of your voice can change a word's meaning, which is rare among the languages of North India.

A script for everyone

Before Gurmukhi became widespread, Punjabi had no single standard script. Merchants used quick shorthand scripts for their accounts, and learned people often wrote in Sanskrit or Persian.

Guru Angad Dev took letters already in use in the region, arranged them clearly and taught them widely. Learning to read Gurmukhi became part of growing up Sikh. It is often said that the Guru taught children their letters himself, though stories like this are hard to check. When Guru Arjan Dev, the fifth Guru, compiled the hymns of the Gurus and other saints into the Adi Granth in 1604, he wrote it in Gurmukhi. That holy book, later called the Guru Granth Sahib, is still kept in every gurdwara, including the Golden Temple in Amritsar.

Like most Indian scripts, Gurmukhi is an abugida. Each consonant has a built-in "a" sound, and small marks above, below or beside a letter change the vowel.

One language, two scripts

When India was divided in 1947, the Punjab region was cut in two. Partition left the western half in Pakistan and the eastern half in India. Millions of people were forced to move across the new border.

Punjabi did not split, but its writing did. In Pakistan, Punjabi is usually written in Shahmukhi, a script based on Perso-Arabic letters that runs right to left. India's 2011 Census counted about 3.3 crore Punjabi speakers, but Pakistan has far more, so most of the world's Punjabi speakers do not write in Gurmukhi at all.

A poem by the 18th-century Sufi poet Bulleh Shah or by Waris Shah, who wrote the love story Heer Ranjha, is loved on both sides of the border. It just looks different on the page.

A language with tones

Punjabi has a rare feature for a North Indian language: tones. Like Chinese, it can change a word's meaning through the rise or fall of your voice. Words that look almost the same written down may sound different when spoken.

This happened as certain sounds that older forms of the language once had, like a breathy "gh" or "bh", slowly faded and left a change of pitch in their place. Gurmukhi still writes those old letters, so a reader has to know that a letter like ਘ now often signals a tone, not a breathy sound.

Punjabi around the world

Punjabi speakers have spread far. Many Punjabi families settled in Canada, the UK and the USA from the 20th century onwards. In Canada, Punjabi is one of the most spoken languages after English and French, and there are Punjabi street signs in parts of Vancouver and Toronto.

The beats of bhangra, once a harvest dance in Punjab's villages, now fill dance floors in London and New York. Many young Punjabis abroad learn Gurmukhi on weekends at their local gurdwara, so they can read the same hymns their great-grandparents read.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Punjabi can only be written in Gurmukhi.

Truth: Gurmukhi is the main script in India, but most Punjabi speakers in Pakistan write it in Shahmukhi, a Perso-Arabic script. The same language lives in two scripts.

Try this

Ask a Punjabi speaker to say ghoRa, the word for horse, slowly, and listen closely. Many speakers do not make a breathy "gh" at all. The word starts more like a "k", and the voice dips. Try copying them, then ask them to say a few other words where the tone changes.
