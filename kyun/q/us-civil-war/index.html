What was the American Civil War?

From 1861 to 1865, Americans fought Americans over whether the country would stay together and whether slavery would survive. More soldiers died than in any other American war.

The short answer

The American Civil War was fought between the northern and southern states of the USA from 1861 to 1865. Eleven Southern states, where slavery was legal and cotton plantations depended on enslaved workers, broke away and formed the Confederate States of America. The Northern states, called the Union, fought to keep the country together.

After four years and hundreds of thousands of deaths, the Union won. Slavery was abolished across the United States, and about four million enslaved people became free. The war shaped the country so deeply that Americans still argue over how to remember it.

Surprising but true

The war made Bombay rich for a few years. With American cotton cut off, British mills bought Indian cotton instead, and prices in Bombay soared until the war ended.

A country split in two

By 1860 the United States was growing west, and the big question was whether new states would allow slavery. The North had banned it. The South's economy relied on it. When Abraham Lincoln, who opposed spreading slavery, was elected president in 1860, Southern leaders feared slavery's end.

South Carolina left the Union in December 1860, and ten more states followed. In April 1861 Confederate guns fired on Fort Sumter, a Union fort in Charleston harbour. The war had begun.

A long, deadly war

Both sides expected a short war. It lasted four years. The North had more people, factories and railways. The South had skilful generals, such as Robert E. Lee, and was fighting mostly on its own ground.

New rifles could kill at long range, but generals often still ordered soldiers to charge in lines. Disease killed even more soldiers than bullets. Historians long estimated about 620,000 deaths. A newer study using census records suggests it may have been closer to 750,000.

The battle of Gettysburg in Pennsylvania, in July 1863, was the largest ever fought in North America. That November, Lincoln gave a short speech there that became one of the most quoted in history.

Freedom

On 1 January 1863 Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation declared enslaved people in the rebelling states free. It could only take effect where Union armies reached, but it turned the war into a fight against slavery. Thousands of people escaped to Union lines. Harriet Tubman guided a Union raid in South Carolina that freed more than 700 people.

About 180,000 Black men served in the Union army, and many more in the navy. Their courage helped persuade many Northerners that Black Americans deserved full citizenship.

The cotton connection

Before the war, Britain's huge textile mills got most of their cotton from the American South. When Union ships blockaded Southern ports, that supply dried up. Mill workers in northern England lost their jobs.

British buyers turned to India. Cotton prices in Bombay shot up, and traders there made fortunes almost overnight. Money poured into new buildings and land reclamation in the city. When the war ended in 1865, prices crashed and many of those fortunes vanished, but the boom left a lasting mark on Mumbai.

The end, and after

General Lee surrendered to General Ulysses S. Grant at Appomattox Court House, Virginia, on 9 April 1865. Five days later, Lincoln was shot at a theatre in Washington, D.C., and died the next morning.

The 13th Amendment to the Constitution abolished slavery in December 1865. News of freedom reached enslaved people in Galveston, Texas, only on 19 June 1865. That day, called Juneteenth, became a US national holiday in 2021. Yet for another century, unfair laws in the South kept Black Americans from voting and from equal treatment, until the civil rights movement fought to end them.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Civil War was not really about slavery.

Truth: Several Southern states explained why they were leaving the Union in written declarations, and they named protecting slavery as the main reason. Historians agree that slavery was at the heart of the conflict.
