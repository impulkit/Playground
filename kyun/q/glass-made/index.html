How is glass made from sand?

Glass is sand that has been melted and then cooled so quickly that its atoms never get the chance to line up neatly again.

The short answer

Most glass begins as ordinary-looking sand made of silica, also called silicon dioxide. Glassmakers mix it with soda ash, which lowers the melting point, and limestone, which makes the glass tough and stops it dissolving in water. Often they add broken recycled glass too, which melts more easily.

The mixture goes into a huge furnace and is heated to about 1,500°C or more, until it becomes a glowing, syrupy liquid. Then it is shaped, by blowing, pressing, pouring or floating, and cooled.

In sand, the atoms are lined up in neat repeating patterns called crystals. When melted glass cools fairly quickly, its atoms freeze in place in a jumble before they can line up. That jumble is what makes glass clear, smooth and hard.

Surprising but true

A boiled sweet is a kind of glass. It is sugar cooled so fast that it can't form crystals, the same trick that turns sand into a window.

Natural glass

Nature made glass long before people did. When some volcanoes erupt, their lava cools so fast that it forms a shiny black glass called obsidian. Stone Age people chipped it into blades sharper than steel scalpels.

When lightning strikes sandy ground, it can melt the sand into hollow, branching tubes of glass called fulgurites. In the Sahara, there is a pale yellow glass scattered across the desert. It is about 29 million years old, and many scientists think it formed when a space rock struck or exploded above the desert.

Beads, bottles and blowing

The first glass objects were small beads, made in Mesopotamia about 4,500 years ago. For a long time, glass was rare and precious, worn as jewellery or used for small bottles.

About 2,000 years ago, craftspeople around the eastern Mediterranean discovered glassblowing. A glassblower gathers a blob of molten glass on the end of a long metal pipe, then blows into it, like blowing up a balloon. With turning, swinging and shaping tools, the blob becomes a bottle, a bowl or a vase. Glassblowing made glass cheaper and spread it across the Roman Empire.

Glass beads were also made in ancient India, and archaeologists have found them at sites from more than 2,000 years ago, including in the south.

Windows that float

Making large, flat, perfectly clear window glass was hard for centuries. Panes were blown and spun into discs, or rolled and polished, which took time and money.

In the 1950s, the British engineer Alastair Pilkington had a better idea. His team poured molten glass onto a bath of melted tin. The glass floated on top like oil on water and spread into a perfectly flat, smooth sheet. This float glass process was introduced in 1959. Almost all window glass in the world is made this way today, from homes to the tall glass towers of Mumbai and New York.

Firozabad, city of bangles

In Uttar Pradesh, the city of Firozabad has been a centre of glassmaking for generations. It is famous for colourful glass bangles, which are worn across India, especially at weddings and festivals. Many families work in small workshops, shaping, joining and decorating bangles by hand after they come from the furnaces.

Glass can be coloured by adding small amounts of metals. Cobalt makes deep blue, chromium makes green, and gold can make a rich ruby red. The great stained-glass windows of Gothic cathedrals in Europe got their glowing colours this way. Unlike paper or plastic, glass can be recycled again and again without losing quality.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Glass is a very slow liquid, so old windows have flowed and become thicker at the bottom.

Truth: Glass is an amorphous solid, meaning its atoms are jumbled like a liquid's but locked in place. Old panes were simply made unevenly. At room temperature, glass would take vastly longer than the age of the universe to flow visibly.

Try this

Look closely at some sugar crystals with a magnifying glass. You'll see tiny cubes with flat sides. Then look at a clear boiled sweet. It's made of the same sugar, but it has no flat crystal faces, because it cooled too fast for the sugar to line up. That's exactly the difference between sand and glass.
