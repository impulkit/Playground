Who was Dadasaheb Phalke?

India's first full-length feature film was made in 1913 by a printer and part-time magician, and a man had to play the queen.

The short answer

Dhundiraj Govind Phalke, known as Dadasaheb Phalke, is called the father of Indian cinema. In 1913 he made Raja Harishchandra, the first full-length Indian feature film. It told the story of a truthful king from Indian legend, and it was silent, with title cards in Marathi and Hindi.

Phalke went on to make about 95 feature films and 26 short ones, mostly stories of gods and heroes. He built a studio, trained actors and camera operators, and invented his own tricks for special effects. India's highest award for cinema is named after him.

Surprising but true

To convince people to lend him money, Phalke filmed a pea plant growing one frame a day, so that weeks of growth flashed past in a minute.

A man of many trades

Phalke was born in 1870 in Trimbak, a temple town near Nashik, where his father was a Sanskrit scholar. He studied art in Bombay and Baroda, then tried a string of jobs. He was a photographer, a stage-set painter, a magician and a printer who made coloured pictures.

On stage he called himself Professor Kelpha, a jumbled-up version of his own surname, and he had learned the magic from a German conjuror touring India.

That mix turned out to be the perfect training for a filmmaker. He understood light, chemicals, painting, printing and illusion. He just had not yet seen what to do with them.

The film that changed his life

Around 1910, Phalke watched a foreign film called The Life of Christ in a Bombay theatre. He was gripped. If Christ's story could be told on a screen, he thought, so could the stories of Krishna and Rama.

He needed money and skills. To persuade backers, he planted peas in a pot and filmed the plant one frame at a time over weeks. Shown at normal speed, the seedling seemed to burst out of the soil. In 1912 he travelled to London, bought a camera and studied how films were made. The pictures that move on a screen are really still frames shown fast, and Phalke now knew how movies move.

A family business

Back in Bombay, his home became a studio. His wife, Saraswatibai, helped develop film, fed the cast and crew, and sold her jewellery when money ran short. Their children helped too. In a later film, Kaliya Mardan (1919), their daughter Mandakini played the young Krishna.

Finding actors was hard. No respectable woman would act in a film in 1913, so the queen in Raja Harishchandra was played by a young man, Anna Salunke. The film, about 40 minutes long, opened to the public on 3 May 1913 at the Coronation Cinema in Bombay. Audiences came back again and again.

Tricks with a camera

Phalke's mythological films were full of magic. Gods appeared out of smoke, demons vanished and heroes flew. He made them with double exposures, models and careful editing, years before most filmmakers in India had heard of such things. His later films, such as Lanka Dahan (1917), drew some of the biggest crowds Indian cinemas had yet seen. Today's special effects use computers, but the aim is the same.

A quiet ending

When sound films arrived in the 1930s, Phalke's style fell out of fashion. He made only one talkie, Gangavataran, in 1937. He died in Nashik in 1944, without much money and largely forgotten by the industry he had started.

In 1969, the Government of India created the Dadasaheb Phalke Award for lifetime achievement in cinema. The first winner was the actress Devika Rani. Later winners include Satyajit Ray, Lata Mangeshkar and Raj Kapoor. Every Indian film, from a Tamil blockbuster to a Bengali art film, traces its family tree back to a printer with a camera and a pot of peas.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Raja Harishchandra was the first film ever shown in India.

Truth: Films had been shown in Bombay since 1896, and short Indian films were made before 1913. Raja Harishchandra is remembered as the first full-length Indian feature film.
