What happens at a border crossing?

In one small library on the US–Canada border, a black line runs across the floor, and readers sit in one country while the books sit in the other.

The short answer

A border crossing is an official place where people and goods move from one country to another. Usually there are two kinds of check. Immigration officers check who you are and whether you are allowed in. They look at your passport, and sometimes a visa, which is permission to enter. Customs officers check what you are carrying, to stop banned goods and to collect tax on certain items.

Crossings can be busy road posts with long queues of lorries, quiet country lanes, airports, seaports or railway stations. Some borders are so open that you barely notice them. Others are tightly guarded. What happens at a border depends on how the two countries get along and what agreements they have made.

Surprising but true

Indian citizens can travel to Nepal and Bhutan without a passport, using other official ID such as a voter ID card.

Who are you, and what are you carrying?

At immigration, an officer checks your passport and may scan it, take your photo or fingerprints, and stamp it. They may ask why you are visiting and how long you will stay.

At customs, bags may pass through X-ray machines. Trained dogs can sniff out drugs or hidden food. Many countries are strict about plants, fruit and meat, because they can carry pests and diseases. Australia, for example, can fine travellers for not declaring an apple, to protect its farms and wildlife.

Open doors next door

India shares land borders with six countries: Pakistan, China, Nepal, Bhutan, Bangladesh and Myanmar. With two of them, Nepal and Bhutan, the border is open for each other's citizens.

At the town of Jaigaon in West Bengal, a decorated gate leads straight into Phuentsholing in Bhutan. Indians can walk through with simple ID. On the India–Nepal border, buses and bicycles cross each day, carrying workers, students, shoppers and families visiting relatives.

Ceremonies and lines on the floor

At Attari–Wagah, between Amritsar in India and Lahore in Pakistan, soldiers from both sides hold a flag-lowering ceremony at sunset. It features high kicks, stamping and loud cheers from crowds on each side, and it is a big tourist attraction.

The US and Canada share the longest land border between two countries, about 8,900 km. In the towns of Derby Line, in Vermont, and Stanstead, in Quebec, the Haskell Free Library and Opera House was deliberately built across the border in the early 1900s. A black line on the floor marks it, and the stage of its opera house is in Canada while most of the seats are in the US.

Strange borders

Some borders twist in odd ways. In Baarle, a town on the Belgian–Dutch border, little pieces of Belgium sit inside the Netherlands, and pieces of the Netherlands inside those. The line runs through houses, cafés and shops, marked by crosses on the pavement.

India and Bangladesh once had over 160 enclaves, small pockets of one country surrounded by the other. People living in them often struggled to get schools, hospitals or ID cards. In 2015 the two countries swapped the enclaves, and the residents could choose which country to belong to.

Borders without checks

In much of Europe, 29 countries form the Schengen Area. People usually drive or take a train from, say, France to Germany without stopping at all, though checks can be brought back for a time.

Not everyone crosses borders by choice. Refugees flee war or danger and may arrive with few papers. International laws say they should be allowed to ask for protection.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You always need a passport to visit another country.

Truth: Not always. Indian citizens can visit Nepal and Bhutan with other official ID. Within much of Europe's Schengen Area, people usually cross between countries without any border check at all.
