How did cricket commentary begin?

In 1934, Australian radio listeners heard "live" cricket from England that was really being made up in a Sydney studio, with a pencil tapped on wood for the sound of bat hitting ball.

The short answer

Cricket commentary began on radio in the 1920s and 1930s. In England, the BBC broadcast its first live cricket descriptions in 1927, only a few decades after radio was invented. In India, a Bombay journalist named A. F. S. "Bobby" Talyarkhan gave the first radio commentary in November 1934, from a match at the Bombay Gymkhana. In Australia, broadcasters even faked live coverage of Tests in England, building it in a studio from telegraph messages.

Commentary turned cricket into a shared experience. Before radio, fans had to wait for the next day's newspaper. After it, they could gather around a radio in a shop, a home or a school and follow every ball. They heard about the weather, the crowd and the bowler's run-up as if they were there.

Surprising but true

India's first radio cricket commentator, Bobby Talyarkhan, often refused to share the microphone and talked alone through an entire day's play.

Talking from Bombay

Bobby Talyarkhan was a sports journalist with a rich voice and a talent for stories. His first broadcast in 1934 was of a match between the Parsees and the Muslims, part of a famous Bombay tournament.

He didn't like sharing the microphone. He often spoke alone for a whole day's play, stopping only at lunch and tea. The historian Ramachandra Guha wrote about his flowing anecdotes. Listeners across India learned players' names through his voice. When All India Radio asked him to work in a team of three commentators in the late 1940s, he refused and left.

How did All India Radio begin?In 1942 a 22-year-old student named Usha Mehta ran a secret radio station for the freedom movement, moving it from place to place to dodge the police.

The fake Tests from Sydney

In 1934, Australian fans wanted to follow the Ashes series being played in England. Live radio links across the world were expensive and unreliable. So the Australian Broadcasting Commission tried something clever.

A short telegraph message, or cable, arrived in Sydney after each over, with the bare facts. Commentators in a studio, who had never seen the play, turned these into lively descriptions. They tapped a pencil on wood for the sound of bat on ball and played recorded crowd noise. Listeners heard what sounded like a live game from the other side of the world. The trick was used again in 1938.

Test Match Special

In 1957, the BBC began Test Match Special, broadcasting every ball of home Tests, all five days. Over the years, its commentators became famous for chatting about cakes sent by listeners, pigeons on the pitch and buses passing the ground, filling quiet moments with humour.

One of its best-loved voices, John Arlott, had a slow, rich accent that many fans loved. Commentators learned to paint pictures with words, describing the colour of the sky or the shape of a fielder's shadow, because listeners couldn't see.

Hindi, TV and beyond

In India, radio commentary spread in English, Hindi and other languages. Families crowded around transistor radios during big matches, and shopkeepers turned up the volume for passers-by. In the 1980s, colour television and India's 1983 World Cup win made TV commentary hugely popular. Doordarshan broadcast matches to millions of homes across the country.

Today, a big match can have commentary in English, Hindi, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Bengali, Marathi and more, broadcast on TV, radio and phones. Some streaming services let viewers pick their language and even their commentator.

The art of filling silence

A Test match has long gaps: bowlers walking back, drinks breaks and rain. Good commentators fill them with stats, stories and jokes. They need memory, quick thinking and a sense of rhythm. And the best still know when to stay silent and let the roar of the crowd say it all. Some of the stats they quote come from the long and strange story of cricket records, which commentators know by heart.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Radio commentators have always watched the game they describe.

Truth: Not in the early days. Australia's synthetic broadcasts in the 1930s were made from short telegraph messages, with commentators far away inventing the details, and sound effects, to make it feel live.

Try this

Be a commentator. Watch a few overs of a cricket match, or any sport, with the sound off, and describe it out loud as if you were on the radio. Try to tell your listener the score, who is batting and what the weather is like, without them seeing anything. Then switch roles with a friend.
