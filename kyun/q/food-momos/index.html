How did momos become India's favourite snack?

Fifty years ago, most Indians outside the Himalaya had never tasted a momo. Now you can buy tandoori, chocolate and even pizza momos in almost every Indian city.

The short answer

Momos are small dumplings, a wrapper of thin dough folded around a filling, usually steamed. They come from Tibet and Nepal, where they were warm, filling food for cold mountain days. Families in Sikkim, Darjeeling, Ladakh and the Northeast have eaten them for generations.

From the 1960s onwards, Tibetan refugees and Nepali and Northeastern families settled in cities across India. Many opened small eateries and street stalls. Momos were cheap, quick and tasty, and they spread like wildfire, especially in Delhi.

Today they are a street-food staple from Kolkata to Bengaluru, and among the most-ordered snacks on food delivery apps.

Surprising but true

The word "momo" is thought to come from a Chinese word for a steamed bun, travelling through Tibet before reaching India.

From the high plateau

In Tibet, where few vegetables grow, momos were traditionally filled with yak meat or mutton, seasoned with onion, garlic and ginger. They were steamed in stacked metal trays over a pot of boiling water. In the thin air of the plateau, at over 4,000 metres, water boils at only about 86°C, so steaming was a gentle, fuel-saving way to cook. Fuel was precious, often dried yak dung.

Newar traders from the Kathmandu valley, who travelled to Lhasa for centuries, are often credited with bringing momos to Nepal. There, people adapted them with buffalo meat, local spices and a fiery tomato pickle called achar.

The name itself has travelled too. Many experts think "momo" comes from a Chinese word for a steamed bun, which passed into Tibetan before reaching the rest of South Asia.

How momos came down the mountains

In 1959, the Dalai Lama left Tibet for India, and tens of thousands of Tibetans followed as refugees. They settled in Dharamshala, in Karnataka, in Delhi's Majnu ka Tilla and in many other places. To earn a living, many cooked the food they knew best.

At around the same time, families from Nepal, Sikkim and the Northeast were moving to big cities for work and study. Students from Delhi University and office workers in Kolkata discovered momos at small stalls. By the 1990s, momo carts were common on Delhi's streets. A plate was cheap, filling and ready in minutes, and for students living on pocket money, that was hard to beat.

Who are refugees?Albert Einstein was a refugee. So were millions of families who crossed new borders when India was partitioned in 1947.

The Indian makeover

Once momos reached the plains, cooks began to play. Vegetarian momos filled with cabbage, carrot and paneer appeared for families who did not eat meat. Then came fried momos, crunchy kurkure momos coated in cornflakes, and tandoori momos marinated in spiced yoghurt and grilled in a clay oven.

The chutney changed too. The bright red, garlicky chilli dip served with Delhi momos is hotter than many traditional Tibetan sauces. In Nepal, people love jhol momo, served floating in a tangy, soupy sauce.

Chefs have even made chocolate momos and cheesy pizza momos. In Ladakh and Sikkim, many people still prefer them the old way, plain and juicy, with a bowl of thukpa noodle soup on the side. Not everyone approves, but it shows how a dish becomes local: people cook it their own way.

Cousins around the world

Wrapping a filling in dough and cooking it is one of humanity's favourite food ideas. China has jiaozi and dim sum. Japan has gyoza. Turkey and Central Asia have tiny manti. Poland has pierogi. Italy has ravioli. India has its own too, like sweet modaks and Gujarati ghughra.

That savoury, mouth-watering taste in a good meaty momo has a name: umami. It is the same taste found in tomatoes, mushrooms and cheese, and scientists only agreed it was a real basic taste in the late twentieth century. Its story begins with a Japanese chemist and a bowl of seaweed soup.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The MSG (ajinomoto) used in some momos and chutneys is harmful to eat.

Truth: Food safety agencies such as the US FDA say MSG is safe at normal amounts. It is made from glutamate, the same substance that gives tomatoes, cheese and mushrooms their savoury taste.

Try this

Fold paper momos. Cut circles from paper about the size of your palm, put a cotton ball in the middle as "filling", and try pleating the edges together into a pouch or a half-moon. Count how many pleats you can make. Skilled momo makers often do a dozen or more in seconds.
