What is the Kremlin?

The Kremlin's famous red walls were built in the 1480s and 1490s by architects from Italy, which is why they look a lot like a castle in Milan.

The short answer

The Kremlin is a walled fortress in the centre of Moscow, the capital of Russia. "Kremlin" is an old Russian word for a citadel, the fortified heart of a town. Inside its red-brick walls are palaces, cathedrals, government buildings, museums and gardens.

For centuries it was the home of the tsars, Russia's emperors. Later it became the seat of the Soviet government. Today it is the official residence of the president of Russia, and part of it is a museum open to visitors. When news reports say "the Kremlin said", they mean the Russian government, much as "the White House" stands for the US president's office.

Surprising but true

Inside the Kremlin sits the Tsar Bell, the largest bell in the world, weighing about 200 tonnes. It has never been rung, because a huge piece cracked off in a fire in 1737.

From wooden fort to red walls

A wooden fort stood on this hill above the Moscow River by the 1150s. Over time it was rebuilt with oak walls, then white limestone. Moscow's rulers, the grand princes, grew more powerful and wanted something grander.

In the 1480s and 1490s, Grand Prince Ivan III hired architects from Italy. They built the red-brick walls and towers that stand today. The walls run for about 2.2 km, are up to 19 metres high in places and have 20 towers. The notched tops of the walls, shaped like swallowtails, look like those on castles in northern Italy.

Cathedrals with golden domes

In the middle of the Kremlin is Cathedral Square. Here stand three great cathedrals with golden domes. In the Cathedral of the Dormition, Russian tsars were crowned for centuries. In the Cathedral of the Archangel, many of them are buried.

Nearby is the Ivan the Great Bell Tower, about 81 metres tall, which was for a long time the tallest building in Moscow. At its foot sits the cracked Tsar Bell, about 200 tonnes, and the Tsar Cannon, a huge bronze cannon from 1586 that was never fired in battle.

Fire, war and revolution

In 1712, the tsar Peter the Great moved Russia's capital to his new city of St Petersburg. The Kremlin stayed important, and new tsars still came to Moscow to be crowned there.

In 1812, the French emperor Napoleon captured Moscow. Much of the city burned, and when his army retreated, it tried to blow up parts of the Kremlin. Several towers and walls were damaged but later rebuilt.

After the Russian Revolution of 1917, the new communist government moved the capital back to Moscow and set up in the Kremlin. Some churches inside were knocked down in the 1920s and 1930s. Red stars, lit from inside, were put on top of five towers in the 1930s, replacing the double-headed eagles of the tsars. They still shine at night today.

The Kremlin and Red Square

Just outside the eastern wall is Red Square, a huge open space where parades are held. "Red" here comes from an old Russian word that also meant "beautiful". At one end stands St Basil's Cathedral, with its swirling, colourful onion domes, built in the 1550s.

During the Cold War, the Kremlin was the centre of the Soviet Union, the rival of the United States. People in the West used "the Kremlin" as shorthand for Soviet leaders. Its parades of soldiers and missiles across Red Square were watched closely around the world.

A palace and a museum

Today, visitors can walk through parts of the Kremlin and visit its cathedrals and museums. The Armoury Chamber holds crowns, thrones, carriages and jewelled Easter eggs made by the Fabergé company for the tsars.

Like the Red Fort in Delhi or the Forbidden City in Beijing, the Kremlin is a fortress-palace that has seen several kinds of rulers come and go.

Myth or fact?

Myth: There is only one kremlin, in Moscow.

Truth: The word kremlin means a fortress in the middle of a town. Many old Russian cities have one, including Novgorod, Kazan and Nizhny Novgorod. Moscow's is simply the most famous.
