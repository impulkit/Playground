How is pottery made?

A lump of mud becomes a pot as hard as stone in a fiery oven, and once that happens it can never turn back into mud.

The short answer

Pottery is made from clay, a sticky earth made of very tiny particles of weathered rock. When clay is wet, it is soft and can be shaped. When it dries, it holds that shape. When it is heated to very high temperatures in a kiln, it turns hard, permanent and waterproof enough to hold food and water.

Potters shape clay in three main ways: pinching it with their fingers, building it up from coils or slabs, or spinning it on a potter's wheel. After shaping, the pot is dried, fired, and often coated with a glassy layer called a glaze.

Surprising but true

People were making clay pots long before they invented farming. Pieces found in a cave in China are about 20,000 years old.

Why clay is special

Clay forms when rocks are slowly broken down by water and weather over thousands of years, the same process that helps make soil. Clay particles are so small and flat that hundreds would fit side by side across a single millimetre.

When water gets between these flat particles, they slide over each other like a stack of wet playing cards. That makes clay soft and stretchy. As it dries, the water leaves, the particles lock together, and the clay stiffens.

The fire changes everything

Dried clay is still fragile. Drop it in water and it will slowly fall apart. The big change happens in the kiln.

Above about 600 °C, water locked inside the clay's crystals is driven out. At higher temperatures, parts of the clay begin to melt and fuse. Everyday earthenware, like a red terracotta pot, is fired at about 1,000 °C. Stoneware and porcelain are fired hotter, around 1,200 to 1,400 °C, which makes them denser and stronger. A glaze is a coating of fine powder that melts in the kiln into a thin layer of glass.

Who invented the wheel?The wheel came surprisingly late. People were farming, making pots and building towns for thousands of years before anyone rolled a cart.

The spinning wheel

The potter's wheel was invented in Mesopotamia about 5,500 years ago. Some historians think it came before wheels were put on carts. A spinning wheel lets a potter make round, even pots quickly. The potter centres the clay, pushes a hole in the middle, then lifts the walls up between the fingers as it turns.

In many Indian villages, potters traditionally used a heavy stone or wooden wheel spun with a stick, which kept turning for a long time once it got going. Electric wheels are common today, but the movements of the hands are much the same.

Pots that tell history

Pottery breaks easily, but the broken pieces, called sherds, last for thousands of years. That makes pottery one of the most useful finds in archaeology. Styles changed over time, so a sherd can help date a layer of earth.

The Indus Valley cities, over 4,000 years ago, made fine red pots painted with black patterns of fish scales, peepal leaves and circles. In China, potters developed porcelain, a white, thin, almost see-through ceramic. It was so prized in Europe that people still call fine plates "china".

Pots in daily life

In India, the kulhad, a small unglazed cup, is still used for chai in many places. Clay diyas are made by the million for Diwali, and the matka keeps water cool in summer. In Khurja in Uttar Pradesh and Blue Pottery workshops in Jaipur, potters make glazed plates and vases sold across the country.

In the United States, potters of the Pueblo peoples of New Mexico, such as the famous Maria Martinez of San Ildefonso Pueblo, revived a style of shiny black pottery in the 1900s. They shaped their pots by hand from coils, without a wheel, as their ancestors had done.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You can soak an old broken pot in water and turn it back into soft clay.

Truth: Firing changes clay chemically. Heat drives out water locked inside the clay's crystals and fuses the particles together. Fired pottery can break, but it will never soften back into clay.

Try this

Make a pinch pot. Roll a ball of air-dry clay or playdough the size of an orange, push your thumb into the middle, then pinch and turn it gently to thin the walls. Make a second pot with thick walls and compare them when dry. Which is lighter? Which feels stronger?
