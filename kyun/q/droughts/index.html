What causes a drought?

In 2016, a town in Maharashtra ran so dry that a train carried its drinking water in tank wagons.

The short answer

A drought is a long spell with much less rain or snow than a place normally gets. It creeps up slowly. First the soil dries out. Then crops wilt, rivers shrink, lakes drop and wells run low. Unlike a flood or a storm, nobody can say exactly which day a drought began.

The main cause is the weather pattern shifting. Rain clouds go somewhere else, or a rainy season arrives late and leaves early. Hot weather makes it worse, because warm air pulls even more water out of soil and plants.

Surprising but true

India pumps more groundwater than any other country, more than the USA and China put together.

When the monsoon fails

India gets most of its yearly rain in four months of monsoon. If those rains are weak, there is no second chance until next year. That is why a poor monsoon worries farmers, cities and governments all at once.

In 1965 and 1966, two weak monsoons in a row left India badly short of food. The shock pushed the country towards new high-yield seeds, more irrigation and fertiliser, a change later called the Green Revolution.

Far away in the Pacific Ocean, a warm patch of water called El Niño often weakens the Indian monsoon. It doesn't always, which is one reason forecasting is hard.

Why are millets making a comeback?Ragi, jowar and bajra fed India for thousands of years, nearly vanished from city plates, and are now back in fashion.

The water we can't see

Under our feet, rainwater soaks down into gaps in rock and soil. This stored water is called groundwater, and wells reach down to it. India uses more groundwater than any other country. In dry years, farmers dig deeper and pump harder.

In 2016, after years of poor rain, the town of Latur in Maharashtra had almost no water left. A special train with tank wagons carried millions of litres of water to the town from hundreds of kilometres away. Groundwater refills only when rain soaks back in, and that can take years.

Big cities aren't safe either. In 2018, Cape Town in South Africa came close to what officials called "Day Zero", the day the taps would be switched off. People cut their water use to about 50 litres each a day, roughly what one short shower uses. The rains returned in time, and the taps never ran dry.

Diaries written in wood

How do we know about droughts from before anyone kept records? Trees remember them. Each year a tree adds a ring of new wood, wide in wet years and thin in dry ones. By matching the rings of very old trees and ancient beams, scientists have traced droughts back hundreds and even thousands of years.

Dust in the wind

In the 1930s, the Great Plains of the USA had years of drought. Farmers had ploughed up the deep-rooted prairie grass that once held the soil. When the rain stopped, the dry topsoil simply blew away. On 14 April 1935, known as Black Sunday, a dust storm turned day into night across several states.

This period became known as the Dust Bowl. Hundreds of thousands of people left their farms. Afterwards, the US government paid farmers to plant trees as windbreaks and to plough in ways that kept soil in place.

Living with less water

People have always found clever ways to store rain for dry times. Stepwells in Gujarat and Rajasthan let people walk down many storeys to reach water. Today, many Indian cities ask new buildings to collect rainwater from roofs.

Crops matter too. Millets such as bajra and ragi need far less water than rice. Scientists expect climate change to make droughts more frequent in some regions, so how we use each drop will matter even more.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Droughts only happen in hot, dry places like deserts.

Truth: A drought means less rain than normal for that place. Rainy regions such as the Amazon rainforest and the United Kingdom have had serious droughts. A desert is dry all the time, which is a different thing.

Try this

Fill two pots with the same soil and water them equally. Cover the top of one with a layer of dry leaves or straw, called mulch. Check both every day by pushing a finger into the soil. The covered pot stays damp for longer, which is why farmers mulch fields in dry seasons.
