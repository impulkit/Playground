How are balloons made?

The first rubber balloons were made in 1824 by the scientist Michael Faraday, who used them to experiment with hydrogen gas.

The short answer

Most party balloons are made from latex, a milky liquid that comes from rubber trees. In a factory, rows of balloon-shaped moulds made of metal or glass are dipped into a liquid that helps rubber stick, then into a tank of latex. A thin skin of rubber clings to each mould.

The moulds pass through hot ovens to dry and toughen the rubber. Spinning brushes roll the open end into the neat lip you put your mouth on. Then jets of air or water pop the finished balloons off the moulds, and they are washed, dried and packed. A big machine can make many thousands an hour.

Surprising but true

A latex balloon is made by dipping a balloon-shaped metal mould into liquid rubber, much like dipping a finger into honey.

Milk from a tree

Latex comes from the rubber tree, which first grew in the Amazon rainforest. Workers make a slanting cut in the bark early in the morning, and a white, sticky liquid drips into a cup. It is not sap but a defence the tree uses to seal wounds.

India is one of the world's big natural rubber producers, and most of it comes from Kerala, where rubber plantations cover the hills. Much of India's rubber goes into tyres, but some ends up as gloves and balloons.

Raw latex goes soft when warm and brittle when cold. In 1839, the American Charles Goodyear found that heating rubber with sulphur made it strong and stretchy, a process called vulcanisation, part of the story of accidental inventions.

Faraday's bags of gas

Before rubber, people made balloons from animal bladders and intestines. Children in many places played with blown-up pig bladders.

In 1824, the scientist Michael Faraday made the first rubber balloons at the Royal Institution in London. He pressed two sheets of rubber together, with flour inside so they wouldn't stick, and filled them with hydrogen for experiments. A year later, a company began selling kits so people could make their own toy balloons.

The modern shape came in the 1930s, when Neil Tillotson in the USA dipped moulds into liquid latex. One of his first balloons had a cat's face with two ears.

Why balloons float

A balloon full of your breath sinks, because breath is a little heavier than air. Fill it with helium, which is lighter than air, and it floats up, pushed by the heavier air around it, the same way a cork bobs up in water.

But helium atoms are tiny. They slip through the gaps in a latex skin, and most helium balloons sag within a day. Foil balloons, made of nylon coated with a metal layer, hold helium for a week or more. That's why they are popular for birthdays.

Helium itself is precious. It is mined from underground gas fields, and doctors need it to cool MRI scanners. Some scientists argue it is too valuable to float away at parties.

Balloons that do serious work

Not all balloons are toys. Weather services release weather balloons twice a day around the world, including from India Meteorological Department stations. They carry instruments up to around 30 km, where the balloon swells to the size of a small house and bursts.

Doctors use tiny balloons inside blood vessels to open blockages near the heart. And in the Second World War, armies built fake tanks out of inflatable rubber to fool enemy spies. Those balloon tanks were part of a long history of disguise and trickery among spies.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Latex balloons are natural, so letting them go into the sky does no harm.

Truth: Latex does break down, but slowly, often taking months or longer, and released balloons can land in the sea or fields. Animals such as turtles and birds can swallow them or get tangled in their strings.

Try this

Blow up a balloon and tie it. Rub it on your hair or a woollen jumper for ten seconds, then hold it near small bits of torn paper. Watch them jump up. Then try sticking it to a wall and time how long it stays.
