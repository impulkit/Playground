How do people live in the Thar Desert?

Very little rain, scorching summers, and yet the Thar is one of the most crowded deserts on Earth.

The short answer

The Thar Desert in Rajasthan gets little rain, and only during the monsoon. In the far west, near Jaisalmer, a whole year's rain can be less than 25 cm, while summer days often pass 45 °C.

For centuries, people have survived by saving rainwater cleverly, in tanks, ponds and stepwells. They built thick-walled houses, herded animals that could cope with drought, and grew hardy crops like bajra, a kind of millet.

The golden city of Jaisalmer is built from local yellow sandstone. Rocks of the same region hold dinosaur footprints, left long before there was any desert at all.

Surprising but true

Chand Baori stepwell has about 3,500 steps going down 13 storeys to reach water.

Crowded for a desert

The Thar covers about 2 lakh km², mostly in Rajasthan, with parts in Gujarat, Haryana, Punjab and Pakistan. Many deserts around the world are almost empty. The Thar is one of the most crowded, with towns, villages and farms scattered across it.

Part of the reason is that it gets some rain each year, unlike the driest deserts. Another is that people here learned, over hundreds of years, how to make every drop count.

The desert is also full of colour. Women wear bright odhnis and heavy silver jewellery, and men wear tall turbans in reds, oranges and yellows. Folk singers called Manganiyars and Langas have sung ballads of desert kings and lovers for generations, often for the same families their ancestors sang for.

Every drop saved

Many homes and villages have a kund, or tanka: a round, covered underground tank. The ground around it slopes gently inwards, so rain runs in. The cover keeps the water cool and clean and stops it evaporating.

Villages also dug ponds called johads and talabs, and wealthy patrons built stepwells. Chand Baori at Abhaneri, near Jaipur, is more than a thousand years old. Its 3,500 steps zigzag down 13 storeys, about 30 m, to the water. People still visit it today.

How do camels survive the desert?A thirsty camel can drink about 100 litres of water in a quarter of an hour, about five big buckets' worth.

Animals of the sand

The camel has been the Thar's truck, tractor and taxi for centuries. It can go days without drinking, carry heavy loads and walk on soft sand with its wide, padded feet. Rajasthan made it the state animal in 2014.

Herding communities such as the Rabari move with their camels, goats and sheep to find grazing. Every year, the town of Pushkar on the desert's edge holds one of the world's largest camel fairs.

Keeping cool

Desert houses have thick walls of stone or mud that soak up the day's heat slowly, so rooms stay cooler inside. Small windows keep out hot winds and blowing sand. Courtyards give shade.

In Jaisalmer, carved stone screens called jaalis let breezes through while blocking the glare. Jaisalmer Fort, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, is one of the few forts in the world where thousands of people still live.

Guardians of the trees

The Bishnoi community of the Thar protects trees and animals as part of its faith. Blackbuck and chinkara deer often wander fearlessly near Bishnoi villages. Their story of sacrifice at Khejarli in 1730, to save khejri trees, later inspired India's Chipko movement.

A canal through the sand

Since the 1950s, the Indira Gandhi Canal has carried water from rivers in Punjab hundreds of kilometres into the desert. Fields of wheat, cotton and mustard now grow where there was once only scrub. It changed many lives, though it also brought problems, such as waterlogged and salty soil in some places.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Deserts are just empty sand dunes where nothing lives.

Truth: Only part of the Thar is shifting dunes. Much of it is scrubland with thorny trees, grasses and farms. It is home to blackbuck, desert foxes and the rare great Indian bustard, and to millions of people.

Try this

Fill two identical cups with the same amount of water and mark the levels. Cover one with a plate and leave the other open, both in a sunny spot. Check every day for a week. The open cup loses more water to evaporation, which is why desert people built covered tanks called kunds.
