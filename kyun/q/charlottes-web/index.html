What is Charlotte's Web about?

A spider saves a pig's life by weaving words into her web. E. B. White based her on a real spider in his barn.

The short answer

Charlotte's Web is a children's novel by the American writer E. B. White, published in 1952 with drawings by Garth Williams. It tells the story of Wilbur, a pig on a farm, and Charlotte, a grey barn spider who becomes his best friend.

When Wilbur learns he is going to be killed for meat at Christmas, Charlotte comes up with a plan. She weaves words into her web above his pen, starting with "SOME PIG". People flock to see the miracle pig, and Wilbur becomes too famous to kill.

The book is about friendship, loyalty and the turning of the seasons. It is also about death, handled honestly and gently, which is one reason so many readers remember it for life.

Surprising but true

E. B. White once carried a real spider's egg sac from his farm in Maine to his flat in New York in a box. Weeks later, hundreds of baby spiders hatched and spun threads across his dressing table.

Fern, Wilbur and a talking barn

The story begins when Fern Arable, an eight-year-old girl, stops her father from killing the runt of a litter of piglets. She names him Wilbur and raises him with a bottle. When he grows too big, he goes to live in her uncle's barn.

The barn is full of talking animals: geese, sheep, an old rat named Templeton who cares mostly about food, and, high in a corner of the doorway, Charlotte. At first Wilbur thinks she is cruel, because she catches and eats flies. Then he discovers how kind and clever she is.

Charlotte writes "SOME PIG", then "TERRIFIC", "RADIANT" and "HUMBLE". Templeton, grumbling, fetches words from old newspapers for her to copy.

The real Charlotte

E. B. White lived on a farm in Maine, in the north-east of the USA. He kept pigs, geese and sheep, and he spent a lot of time watching a big grey spider spin her web in his barn.

White was famous for writing for The New Yorker magazine, and he did careful research. He read books about spiders and checked facts with experts. Charlotte's full name, Charlotte A. Cavatica, comes from the scientific name of the barn spider, Araneus cavaticus.

He once cut down a real egg sac and took it back to New York in a box. Weeks later, tiny spiders hatched and spun threads all over his dresser.

Spiders as heroes

Many people are scared of spiders. White wanted readers to see them differently. The book describes Charlotte's work accurately. There is the sticky spiral, the dry spokes, the way she wraps her prey in silk, and the egg sac she spins at the end of summer.

Real orb-weaving spiders like Charlotte are useful garden helpers, catching flies, mosquitoes and other insects. Barn spiders usually live for about a year. Like Charlotte, many females lay a sac of hundreds of eggs in autumn and die before winter. The babies hatch in spring and float away on threads of silk, a trick called ballooning.

A sad ending, and a hopeful one

Charlotte dies at the end of the book, alone at the fair, after saving Wilbur. Many readers cry. White did not soften it, but he did not leave it there either. Wilbur carries her egg sac home, and in spring her babies hatch. Most float away, but three stay as his friends.

The book won a Newbery Honor in 1953 and is often listed among the best-selling children's paperbacks ever. White ends it with a line about Charlotte that many readers know by heart. He wrote that it is not often someone comes along who is a true friend and a good writer, and Charlotte was both.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Spiders are a kind of insect.

Truth: Spiders are arachnids, not insects. They have eight legs instead of six, no wings or antennae, and their bodies have two main parts instead of three.

Try this

Find a spider web outdoors, with an adult nearby, early on a dewy or misty morning, when the threads show up best. Draw its pattern without touching it. Count the spokes and see if the spiral is even all the way round. Come back the next day and check if the web has changed.
