How was America's first coast-to-coast railroad built?

Two railway companies started building from opposite sides of America and raced to meet in the middle. On 10 May 1869, a golden spike joined their tracks.

The short answer

In the 1860s, getting from the east coast of the United States to California meant months by wagon or by ship. So the government paid two companies to build a railroad across the continent. The Central Pacific started in Sacramento, California, and built east. The Union Pacific started near Omaha, Nebraska, and built west.

After six years of hard work, the two lines met at Promontory Summit in Utah on 10 May 1869. Now people and goods could cross the country by train in about a week. It changed America, though at a great cost to the people who built it and to the Native nations whose lands it crossed.

Surprising but true

Most of the workers who built the western half of the line were from China, yet none of them appear in the most famous photograph of the day the tracks met.

Lincoln's big gamble

Even while the Civil War was raging, President Abraham Lincoln signed the Pacific Railway Act in 1862. The government gave the companies loans and huge strips of land beside the track, which they could sell. The more track they laid, the more they earned, so each company rushed to lay as much as possible.

Since the Gold Rush, California had grown fast, but it was cut off from the rest of the country by deserts and mountains. A railroad would tie it to the East.

Through the Sierra Nevada

The Central Pacific faced the hardest job straight away: the Sierra Nevada mountains, with granite cliffs and snow many metres deep. Workers hung over cliffs to cut ledges and blasted tunnels through solid rock with black powder and later a dangerous explosive called nitroglycerin. Some tunnels advanced only about 30 cm a day.

Winters were brutal. Avalanches swept away camps, and some workers were buried in snow and not found until spring.

The Chinese workers

The Central Pacific could not find enough workers. So in 1865 it began hiring Chinese immigrants, many from Guangdong in southern China. Doubters thought they were too small for the work. They were wrong. At the peak, as many as 10,000 to 15,000 Chinese workers made up most of the Central Pacific's labour force.

They were paid less than white workers and often given the most dangerous jobs. Many died, though nobody kept a careful count. In 1867, thousands went on strike for better pay and shorter hours. The company cut off their food, and the strike ended after about a week, but it showed how much the railroad depended on them.

The race across the plains

The Union Pacific had flatter ground but its own problems. Its workers were largely Irish immigrants, Civil War veterans, and freed African Americans. Its lines crossed the hunting grounds of the Lakota, Cheyenne and other nations, who fought back against the invasion of their land.

The railroads also carried hunters who shot millions of bison, the animals many Plains nations depended on. Within about 20 years, the great herds were nearly gone.

The golden spike

On 10 May 1869, crowds gathered at Promontory Summit. A golden spike was tapped into place, and a telegraph message, DONE, flashed across the country. In a famous photograph, workers from both lines shake hands. Chinese workers are almost absent from it.

In 2019, for the 150th anniversary, descendants of Chinese railroad workers gathered at the same spot to recreate the photo, this time with their ancestors' story included. In India, the first passenger train had run from Bombay to Thane in 1853, a journey of 34 km. America's new track was more than 80 times longer.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The railroad was built mostly by machines.

Truth: It was built mostly by hand. Workers used picks, shovels, hammers, wheelbarrows, horses and explosives. Laying track meant carrying heavy iron rails and hammering spikes, one at a time.
