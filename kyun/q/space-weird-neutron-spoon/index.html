How heavy is a spoonful of a neutron star?

A single teaspoon of neutron star would weigh billions of tonnes, more than every person on Earth put together.

The short answer

A teaspoon of neutron star would weigh roughly a billion tonnes, and possibly several times that. That is heavier than every one of the 8 billion people on Earth put together. It would also weigh more than ten thousand of the largest aircraft carriers.

Neutron stars are the crushed leftover cores of giant stars that blew up. They pack more mass than our Sun into a ball about 20 km across, about the size of a city, or roughly the length of Manhattan in New York. The matter inside is squeezed so tightly that the empty space inside atoms has been crushed out.

Surprising but true

The fastest-spinning neutron star known turns 716 times every second, so a point on its equator moves at about a quarter of the speed of light.

Crushing the emptiness out of atoms

An atom is mostly empty. If the nucleus at the centre were a cricket ball in the middle of a stadium, the electrons would be like specks of dust in the outer stands. Everything in between is nothing.

Inside a neutron star, gravity is so strong that it crushes electrons into the nucleus. They combine with protons to make neutrons. The neutrons then pack together almost touching, with all the emptiness gone. Squeeze the whole Earth that way and it would shrink to the size of a large football stadium.

Born in an explosion

Stars much heavier than the Sun burn fast and die young. When the fuel at the centre of such a star runs out, the core collapses in under a second. The outer layers bounce off and blast into space as a supernova, which can briefly outshine a whole galaxy.

What remains of the core becomes a neutron star. In the year 1054, Chinese astronomers recorded a new star bright enough to see in daylight for weeks. Today, at that spot, sits the Crab Nebula, with a neutron star spinning 30 times a second at its heart.

The ticking stars

In 1967, a research student at Cambridge named Jocelyn Bell Burnell noticed a strange signal in her radio telescope data. It pulsed exactly every 1.3 seconds. The team half-jokingly labelled it LGM-1, for "little green men". It turned out to be a spinning neutron star sweeping a beam of radio waves past Earth like a lighthouse. These stars are now called pulsars.

Some spin unbelievably fast. One pulsar turns 716 times a second. A ceiling fan on full speed manages about five. Neutron stars also carry the strongest magnets known. The most powerful kind, called magnetars, have magnetic fields so strong that from halfway to the Moon they could wipe the strip on a bank card. Closer in, they would pull apart the atoms in your body.

Gravity you can't escape easily

On the surface of a neutron star, gravity is around 200 billion times stronger than on Earth. A mountain could not rise more than a few millimetres before collapsing under its own weight. The surface is the smoothest of any known object.

If you dropped something from just 1 metre up, it would hit the ground at about 2,000 km per second. To launch a rocket off a neutron star, you would need to reach roughly a third of the speed of light.

Where the spoon would go

Say you somehow scooped out a teaspoon and brought it to Earth. It wouldn't stay a teaspoon. Without the star's gravity holding it together, the neutrons would burst apart in a huge explosion of energy.

If it could stay squeezed, it would fall straight through your table, your floor and the ground. It would sink through the crust toward the centre of the Earth like a stone through water. Push gravity just a little further than a neutron star and you get something even stranger, an object so dense that not even light can climb back out.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A neutron star is a kind of black hole.

Truth: A neutron star is the crushed core of a dead star, with a solid surface you could in theory stand on (though you would be flattened). A black hole is even more squeezed, so nothing can escape it, not even light.
