Did chess begin in India?

A game called chaturanga, played in India about 1,500 years ago, became chess.

The short answer

Most historians think chess grew from a game called chaturanga, played in India around the 6th century CE. Chaturanga means "four parts", the four parts of an army: foot soldiers, horses, elephants and chariots. They became today's pawns, knights, bishops and rooks.

It was played on an 8 by 8 board, the same size as today's. A king sat at the centre of each army, with a counsellor beside him. The goal was the one chess players still chase: trap the enemy king so that it cannot escape.

Surprising but true

There are more possible games of chess than there are atoms in the observable universe.

A game that travelled

Traders and travellers carried the game along the trade routes to Persia, where it was called shatranj. From there it spread to the Arab world and Europe. Our word "checkmate" comes from the Persian shah mat: "the king is helpless."

The Persian poet Ferdowsi, writing about a thousand years ago in the Shahnameh, told a story of how chess arrived. An Indian king sent the game to the Persian court as a puzzle, daring the wise men to work out its rules. In the story, a minister solved it and sent back a game of his own, an ancestor of backgammon. It is a legend, but it shows that Persians themselves remembered chess as a gift from India.

The rice and the chessboard

A famous legend says a clever inventor asked a king for 1 grain of rice on the first square, 2 on the second, 4 on the third, doubling every time. The king laughed and agreed. But doubling grows terrifyingly fast: the last square alone needs over 9 quintillion grains.

Chess itself has the same explosive maths. After just three moves each, there are millions of possible positions. In 1950, the American engineer Claude Shannon estimated that the number of possible games is a 1 followed by about 120 zeros. Atoms in the observable universe come to roughly a 1 followed by 80 zeros.

The army on the board

Chaturanga copied the armies of ancient India. Kings went to war with four kinds of troops: foot soldiers, cavalry on horses, war elephants and chariots. Old Sanskrit texts call such an army chaturangini, meaning one with four limbs.

On the board, the elephant became the bishop in English, though in Hindi the bishop is still often called oont, the camel, and the rook is haathi, the elephant. Many Indian players also call the queen wazir, the minister. The names have shifted as the game travelled, but the army is still there.

How the queen got her power

For about a thousand years, the counsellor next to the king was one of the weakest pieces on the board. Around 1475, players in Spain gave it the power to sweep across the board in any direction, and renamed it the queen. Bishops gained long diagonal moves at about the same time.

Games suddenly became faster and fiercer. Some people called the new version "mad queen chess". Within a few decades it had spread across Europe, and the rules have barely changed since.

Machines and champions

In 1997, an IBM computer called Deep Blue beat world champion Garry Kasparov in a six-game match. Today, chess programs are far stronger than any human, and players use them to train.

India has produced many great players. Viswanathan Anand became India's first grandmaster in 1988 and later won the world championship several times. In 2024, 18-year-old D. Gukesh became the youngest undisputed world champion in history, one of a long line of India's chess champions. The game came home, and India's players are now among the best on the board.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The queen has always been the strongest piece in chess.

Truth: In chaturanga and the Persian game, the piece beside the king was a counsellor that moved just one square diagonally. Players in Europe turned it into the powerful queen around the end of the 1400s.
