How did cats come to live with people?

Nobody really tamed the cat. It moved in for the mice, and stayed.

The short answer

Pet cats come from the African wildcat, a small, striped wild cat that still lives in North Africa and the Middle East. Around 10,000 years ago, people in the Middle East began farming and storing grain. Grain stores attracted mice and rats, and the mice attracted wildcats.

The cats that were bold enough to hunt around villages, and calm enough to put up with people, got plenty to eat. People were happy to have pest control. Over many generations, these village cats became tamer.

So unlike dogs, cats were probably never trained or herded. They mostly chose to live with us, and they changed far less than dogs did along the way.

Surprising but true

Grown-up cats rarely meow at each other. Most of their meowing is aimed at people.

A cat buried with a person

The oldest clue comes from the island of Cyprus. At a village site called Shillourokambos, archaeologists found a cat buried close to a person, about 9,500 years ago.

Wildcats don't live naturally on Cyprus, so people must have carried cats there by boat. Taking a wild animal across the sea, and burying it near a person, suggests the cats were already special to them.

Cats also lived in villages in China about 5,300 years ago. But DNA showed those were leopard cats, a different wild species. That early partnership didn't last, and today's pet cats in China, like everywhere else, come from the African wildcat.

Cats of the pharaohs

Ancient Egypt loved cats. They appear in paintings, statues and jewellery. Cats protected granaries and killed snakes, and they were linked to Bastet, a goddess of the home, often shown with a cat's head.

Egyptians mummified huge numbers of cats as offerings to Bastet. So many were buried that in the late 1800s, a ship carried about 19 tonnes of cat mummies from Egypt to England, where most were ground up to spread on fields. A few survived in museums.

How were mummies made?Egyptian embalmers pulled out the brain through the nose with a hook, but left the heart exactly where it was.

Sailing around the world

Ancient DNA shows that cats spread in two big waves. The first moved with early farmers from the Middle East into Europe. Later, cats from Egypt spread along sea trade routes, carried on ships to keep rats away from food and cargo.

Ship's cats travelled with Romans, Vikings and European explorers. That is how cats reached the Americas, Australia and many islands. The tabby pattern with swirls and blotches, now very common, only became widespread in the Middle Ages.

Still half wild

A pet cat's body has barely changed from a wildcat's. It still hunts by sitting very still, then pouncing. Its eyes work best at dawn and dusk, the times when mice are most active. Many pet cats can survive on their own if they have to.

What did change was their behaviour. Pet cats are calmer around people and other cats. Grown-up cats rarely meow at each other, but they meow at people a lot, perhaps because it works.

In India, cats turn up in old stories. In the Panchatantra, a sly cat pretends to be a holy judge so that two quarrelling animals come close enough to catch. India also has its own wild relatives, like the Asiatic wildcat of the Thar Desert and the jungle cat.

Cats and wildlife

Cats are skilled hunters, and that can be a problem. On islands where birds and small animals never met a cat, pet and stray cats have helped wipe out some species. In the USA, studies suggest outdoor cats kill billions of birds every year.

That is why some wildlife experts ask owners to keep cats indoors, especially at night. The cat that moved in for the mice is still, at heart, a wildcat.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Cats need a saucer of milk.

Truth: Most adult cats can't digest the sugar in milk well, and it can upset their stomachs. Fresh water and proper cat food are what they need.

Try this

Stand in front of a mirror in a dim room and look at your pupils, the black circles in your eyes. Now switch on a bright light and watch them shrink. Your pupils stay round, but a cat's narrow into thin slits, which lets its sensitive night-time eyes cope with bright sunlight.
