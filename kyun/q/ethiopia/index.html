What makes Ethiopia unique?

In Ethiopia the year has 13 months, the clock starts at sunrise, and the calendar runs about seven years behind.

The short answer

Ethiopia is a big country in the Horn of Africa, the part of East Africa that sticks out towards Arabia. Much of it is a high plateau, with mountains and deep gorges, and the capital, Addis Ababa, sits about 2,350 metres above the sea. With around 13 crore people, it has the second-largest population in Africa.

Ethiopia is unusual in many ways. It was never turned into a European colony, apart from a short Italian occupation from 1936 to 1941. It has its own ancient script, its own calendar and its own way of telling the time. It is the homeland of arabica coffee, and some of the oldest fossils of human ancestors have been found in its dry north-east.

Surprising but true

Ethiopia's calendar has twelve months of 30 days and a thirteenth month of just five days, or six in a leap year.

Thirteen months of sunshine

The Ethiopian calendar has 12 months of 30 days each, plus a short 13th month of five days, or six in a leap year. Tourist posters once used the slogan "13 months of sunshine". New Year falls in September, after the long rains end. The calendar counts years about seven to eight years behind the Gregorian one used in India and the USA, because it dates the birth of Jesus differently.

The clock is different too. Near the equator, days and nights are almost the same length all year, so traditional Ethiopian time starts counting the hours at sunrise. One o'clock in the morning means one hour after dawn. Few calendars on Earth work quite like it.

Churches carved from rock

In the mountain town of Lalibela, eleven churches were carved straight down into solid volcanic rock, probably in the 12th and 13th centuries, under King Lalibela. Builders dug deep trenches to free a block of stone, then hollowed out rooms, windows, columns and roofs from inside it.

The best known, the Church of St George, is shaped like a cross when seen from above. It is a little like the Kailasa temple at Ellora in Maharashtra, which was also carved from the top down out of a single hill. Pilgrims in white robes still pray there today. Ethiopia was one of the earliest countries to adopt Christianity, in the 4th century, and about two-fifths of Ethiopians belong to the Ethiopian Orthodox Church. About a third are Muslim.

Lucy and the first coffee

In 1974, scientists in the Afar region found much of the skeleton of a human ancestor who lived about 3.2 million years ago. They nicknamed her Lucy, after a Beatles song playing at their camp. Ethiopians call her Dinkinesh, meaning "you are marvellous". She walked upright, and she helped show that our ancestors walked on two legs long before their brains grew large, an important clue in human evolution.

Wild arabica coffee still grows in Ethiopia's south-western forests. In a traditional coffee ceremony, the host roasts green beans over a small fire, grinds them by hand and brews them three times in a clay pot, while incense burns nearby.

Teff, Ge'ez and barefoot running

The national food is injera, a big, spongy, slightly sour flatbread made from teff, a grain even smaller than most millets. Stews are spooned on top, and everyone tears off pieces of injera to scoop them up.

Ethiopian writing uses the Ge'ez script, an abugida, in which each symbol stands for a consonant plus a vowel, a bit like Devanagari. And Ethiopia is famous for runners. In 1960, Abebe Bikila won the Olympic marathon in Rome running barefoot, and Ethiopian athletes have won long-distance races ever since.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Coffee was discovered when a goatherd named Kaldi saw his goats dancing after eating coffee berries.

Truth: Kaldi is a legend. The story was first written down in Europe in 1671, many centuries after coffee began to be used. Coffee plants do grow wild in Ethiopia's forests, but no one knows who first brewed them.
