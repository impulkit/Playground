How do robots work?

The word "robot" was invented for a play in 1920. Today millions of real ones weld cars, sweep floors and drive on Mars.

The short answer

A robot is a machine that can sense the world around it, decide what to do, and then move to do it. Engineers call this "sense, think, act".

To sense, a robot uses sensors: cameras, microphones, touch pads, or beams of light that measure distance. To think, it uses a computer running a program, a set of instructions written by people. To act, it uses motors that turn wheels, bend joints or squeeze grippers. Take away any one of the three and you have something simpler: a camera, a computer or a toy car.

Surprising but true

The word "robot" comes from the Czech word robota, which means forced labour. It was first used for machine workers in a 1920 play called R.U.R.

Arms that never get tired

The first robot to work in a factory was called Unimate. In 1961 it started lifting hot pieces of metal at a General Motors car plant in New Jersey, a job that was dangerous for people. It was basically a strong arm that repeated the same movements again and again.

Most robots today are still arms like that. In a modern car factory, rows of them weld the body together in showers of sparks, faster and steadier than any person. They don't need lunch breaks. But they are also not very clever. Move the car a few centimetres out of place, and an old-style robot arm will weld thin air.

Giving robots eyes

To work in messier places, robots must notice what is going on around them. A robot vacuum cleaner bumps into a sofa leg, feels the bump and turns away. Newer ones scan the room with cameras or lasers and build a little map, so they can clean in neat rows.

The hardest part is understanding what the sensors see. A camera gives a robot millions of coloured dots. Working out that some of those dots are a child, a ball or a step is difficult. This is where artificial intelligence comes in: programs that learn to recognise things by studying huge numbers of examples. The same problem faces self-driving cars, which are really robots on wheels.

Robots far from home

Some robots go where people can't. NASA's rovers have driven across Mars for years, steered by teams on Earth. Radio messages take several minutes to reach Mars, so the rovers must handle some decisions on their own, like stopping before a rock.

India has a robot on the Moon too. In August 2023, Chandrayaan-3 landed near the Moon's south pole and released the Pragyan rover. It was about the size of a large suitcase. Over roughly ten days it rolled about 100 metres and tested the soil for elements such as sulphur.

Why most robots don't look like us

In films, robots usually have two arms, two legs and a face. Real engineers build whatever shape suits the job. A robot that cleans a swimming pool looks like a small tank. One that operates inside the body is a set of thin arms that a surgeon controls from a console.

Walking on two legs, it turns out, is extremely hard. You balance without thinking, but a machine must correct its weight many times every second or topple over. A few companies are now testing human-shaped robots for warehouses. Even so, the humble wheel is still the robot's best friend.

Hands are another puzzle. Picking up an egg without crushing it, or a sock without dropping it, needs a delicate sense of touch. Your fingertips have thousands of nerve endings. Robot grippers are only starting to catch up, which is why folding laundry is still harder for a robot than welding a car.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Robots think and feel the way people do, like in the films.

Truth: Today's robots follow programs written by people. Some use artificial intelligence to recognise objects or plan a path, but none of them understand the world or have feelings the way you do.

Try this

Play "robot and programmer" with a friend. You are the programmer and must get your robot from one side of a room to a chair using only exact commands like "step forward" or "turn left". Your robot must do exactly what you say, nothing more. You will quickly see why real programs need to be so precise.
