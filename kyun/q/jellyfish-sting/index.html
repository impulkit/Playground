Why do jellyfish sting?

A jellyfish's sting fires a tiny coiled harpoon in less than a millionth of a second, one of the fastest movements known in nature.

The short answer

Jellyfish sting to catch food and to protect themselves. They have no teeth, claws or bones, and they drift with the currents, so they can't chase prey. Instead, their tentacles are covered in thousands of microscopic stinging cells. When something brushes against them, the cells fire tiny venom-tipped threads into it.

For a small fish or shrimp, the venom stuns or kills, and the tentacles pull it towards the jellyfish's mouth. For a human, most stings feel like a sharp burning or prickling, with red lines on the skin where the tentacle touched. A few species, especially in warm tropical seas, are dangerous.

Surprising but true

A jellyfish washed up dead on the beach, or even a broken-off piece of tentacle, can still sting you, because each stinging cell fires on its own.

A harpoon in every cell

The stinging cells are called cnidocytes. Inside each one is a tiny capsule holding a long, coiled thread, often with a barbed tip. A trigger on the outside of the cell responds to touch and to chemicals on the skin of prey.

When triggered, pressure inside the capsule shoots the thread out like a harpoon. It turns inside out as it goes, punches through skin and injects venom. The whole thing happens in less than a millionth of a second. Once fired, a cell is used up, and the jellyfish grows a new one.

No brain, still a hunter

Jellyfish have been drifting through the oceans for well over 500 million years, long before the first dinosaurs or trees. Their bodies are about 95 per cent water. They have no brain, heart or blood, only a loose net of nerves that lets them sense touch, light and which way is up.

They don't decide to sting you. Their cells react automatically to touch. That's why a dead jellyfish on a beach, or a broken bit of tentacle tangled in seaweed, can still sting. It is a good reason never to pick them up. Sometimes jellyfish gather in huge swarms called blooms, which can clog fishing nets and even block the cooling-water pipes of coastal power stations.

Which ones hurt

Most jellyfish are only mildly painful, and some, like the common moon jellyfish, barely sting people at all. The most dangerous is the Australian box jellyfish, whose tentacles can be three metres long. Beaches in northern Australia put up warning signs and nets during the box jellyfish season.

In India, a creature often called a "bluebottle", or Portuguese man o' war, sometimes washes up on beaches around Mumbai and Goa during the monsoon. It isn't a true jellyfish, but a colony of tiny animals living together, and its blue tentacles sting painfully.

What to do if you're stung

If you are stung, get out of the water calmly and tell an adult or lifeguard. The adult can rinse the area with sea water and lift off any tentacles using tweezers or a gloved hand. Don't rub it with sand or a towel, because that can make more stinging cells fire.

Soaking the area in water as hot as is comfortable, though never scalding, eases the pain for many people. On some Australian beaches, vinegar is kept to treat box jellyfish stings. Anyone who has trouble breathing, feels faint or has a large sting should get medical help immediately. Basic first aid training covers all of this.

Who isn't afraid of jellyfish

Some animals eat jellyfish without trouble. The leatherback, the largest of the sea turtles, eats almost nothing else. Its mouth and throat are lined with backward-pointing spines that let it swallow slippery jellies. Sadly, leatherbacks sometimes mistake floating plastic bags for jellyfish.

A tiny blue sea slug, the blue dragon, even eats the Portuguese man o' war and keeps its stinging cells for its own defence.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Peeing on a jellyfish sting makes it better.

Truth: This idea is everywhere, from TV shows to playgrounds, but it doesn't work. Urine can even trigger more stinging cells. Rinsing with sea water, carefully removing tentacles and getting help from an adult or lifeguard work much better.

Try this

Make a model stinging cell. Roll a strip of paper tightly around a pencil, slide it off and push the coil into a drinking straw. Blow sharply into the other end and watch the paper shoot out and unroll. A real nematocyst works a bit like that, but the thread is far tinier and has a barbed tip.
