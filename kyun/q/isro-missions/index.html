How did India reach Mars on its first try?

Its rocket wasn't strong enough to go straight to Mars. So ISRO got clever.

The short answer

In 2013, the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) launched the Mars Orbiter Mission, nicknamed Mangalyaan, which means "Mars craft".

Its rocket, the PSLV, was not powerful enough to throw it straight to Mars. So engineers fired Mangalyaan's own engine near Earth again and again, stretching its orbit a little more each time. Then one big push sent it on its way. On 24 September 2014, after 298 days in space, it reached Mars orbit. No country had ever done that on its first try.

Surprising but true

India was the first country to reach Mars orbit on its very first attempt.

Pumping the swing

Think of a child on a jhoola. Each push at the right moment sends the swing a little higher. Mangalyaan worked the same way. For almost a month, every time it came closest to Earth, its engine fired and its loop around Earth grew longer.

On 1 December 2013, the final burn sent it out of Earth's grip. After that, it coasted around the Sun for months with its engine off. ISRO had aimed it at the spot where Mars would be in ten months' time, not where Mars was on launch day.

The scariest moment

Arriving at Mars was the hardest part. Mangalyaan had to slow down enough for Mars's gravity to catch it. If the main engine failed, it would sail straight past and be lost forever.

That engine had been switched off for about 300 days. Two days before arrival, ISRO fired it for just four seconds as a test. It worked. On 24 September, it burned for about 24 minutes, and Mars captured the spacecraft. Radio signals took about 12 minutes to reach Earth, so the team in Bengaluru waited without knowing until the news arrived. Prime Minister Narendra Modi watched from the control centre.

Cheaper than a film

The whole mission cost about ₹450 crore, roughly 74 million US dollars. That was less than the budget of the Hollywood space film Gravity. ISRO saved money by using a proven rocket, a small spacecraft, and lots of careful planning instead of extra power.

NASA's MAVEN spacecraft reached Mars just two days earlier, on 21 September 2014. The two agencies' teams sent each other congratulations. A photo of women scientists at ISRO celebrating in saris was shared around the world, and helped many girls picture themselves working in space science. Mangalyaan's picture later appeared on the back of India's ₹2,000 note.

What it studied

Mangalyaan carried five small science instruments. Its colour camera photographed whole sides of Mars at once, including giant dust storms and the planet's two tiny moons, Phobos and Deimos. Other instruments studied the thin air and searched for methane gas.

Mangalyaan's orbit was long and stretched, so at its farthest it was tens of thousands of kilometres from Mars. From there, its camera could fit the whole planet into a single frame, volcanoes, clouds and all, something orbiters flying low cannot do.

It was designed to last six months. Instead, it kept working for about eight years. In 2022, ISRO lost contact with it, probably after its batteries ran down during a long eclipse, when Mars blocked the sunlight its solar panels needed.

What came next

Mangalyaan showed that a young space agency could reach another planet on a tight budget. Since then, ISRO has landed on the Moon with Chandrayaan-3 and sent Aditya-L1 to study the Sun. If you want to see what Mars is like from the ground, read about the rovers driving there.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Mangalyaan landed on Mars.

Truth: Mangalyaan was an orbiter. It circled Mars and studied it from above, from as close as about 420 km to as far as tens of thousands of kilometres. It never touched the ground.

Try this

Put a small plate in the middle of a big sheet of paper for the Sun. Draw Earth's path around it, and Mars's bigger path outside that. Mars takes about 687 days to go round once. Mangalyaan's trip took 298 days, so mark where Mars was at launch and where it had moved to on arrival. You have to aim at where Mars will be, not where it is.
