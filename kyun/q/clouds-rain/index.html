How does a cloud hold so much water?

A fluffy cloud can hold as much water as 100 elephants weigh.

The short answer

A cloud is made of billions of droplets far too small to see one by one.

Each droplet is so tiny and light that rising air holds it up.

A normal fluffy cloud holds around 500 tonnes of water, spread out over a huge space. That's about the weight of 100 elephants, floating over your head.

Surprising but true

One ordinary fluffy cloud weighs about as much as 100 elephants.

Heavy, but spread out

How can something that heavy float? The trick is that the water is spread very thin. A typical fluffy cumulus cloud might be a kilometre wide, a kilometre long and a kilometre tall. Each cubic metre of it holds only about half a gram of water, about ten drops from an eyedropper.

Each droplet is about 0.02 mm across, thinner than a hair. Droplets that small fall very slowly, so the gentle rising air that builds clouds keeps them aloft. The air around them is heavy too. A cubic kilometre of air weighs far more than the cloud's water, so the cloud is just a slightly misty part of the sky.

How droplets are born

Air always carries some water as an invisible gas called water vapour. When air rises, it cools. Cool air can't hold as much vapour, so some of it turns back into liquid, a process called condensation.

But vapour needs something to cling to. Every cloud droplet forms around a speck of dust, sea salt, pollen or smoke, far too small to see. Without these seeds, clouds would hardly form at all. Scientists have tried "seeding" clouds by spraying in extra specks, including in experiments in India, but the results are mixed.

From droplet to raindrop

A raindrop is about a hundred times wider than a cloud droplet, so it holds about a million times more water. Getting from one to the other takes time.

In warm clouds, droplets bump into each other and merge, growing bigger and heavier until rising air can't hold them up. In tall, cold clouds, tiny ice crystals grow by stealing water vapour from the droplets around them. They fall as snowflakes, then melt into rain on the way down. Much of the rain that falls even on hot Indian summer days started as ice high above.

Why some clouds are dark

Thin clouds look bright white because their droplets scatter all colours of sunlight equally. That's also why clouds are white while the sky is blue. Thick clouds full of big droplets let less light through, so from underneath they look grey or almost black. A dark sky means a lot of water overhead.

The biggest rain clouds, called cumulonimbus, can tower more than 12 km high, taller than Mount Everest. They bring downpours, hail and lightning.

Measuring the rain

Rain is measured with a simple rain gauge: a funnel and a container that records how deep the water would be if none of it drained away. India's monsoon brings most of the country's rain in just four months.

Mawsynram and Cherrapunji in Meghalaya are among the wettest places on Earth, with more than 11 metres of rain in an average year. Once rain lands, it soaks into the ground, runs into rivers or evaporates again, as part of the endless water cycle.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Raindrops are shaped like teardrops.

Truth: Small raindrops are almost perfectly round. Bigger ones get flattened on the bottom as they fall, more like the top half of a burger bun. The very biggest wobble and break apart into smaller drops.

Try this

Fill a glass with ice and water and leave it on a table. In a few minutes, tiny drops appear on the outside. They didn't leak through the glass. They are water vapour from the air, cooled by the glass until it turned back into liquid, just as it does to make a cloud.
