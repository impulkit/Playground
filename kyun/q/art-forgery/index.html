How do experts spot fake paintings?

In 1945, a Dutch painter faced prison for selling a national treasure to a Nazi leader. His defence was that it was a fake, and he had painted it himself.

The short answer

Experts use three kinds of clues. First, the style: does the brushwork, colour and drawing look like the artist's? Second, the history: can the painting be traced, owner by owner, back to the artist's studio? This record is called its provenance. Third, the science: are the paint, canvas and wood the right age?

A good forger can fool one of these tests. Fooling all three is much harder. Most forgers are caught when the story of a painting doesn't add up, or when a lab finds a material that didn't exist when the picture was supposedly made.

Even so, experts sometimes disagree, and some fakes probably still hang on museum walls without anyone knowing.

Surprising but true

To prove he was a forger, Han van Meegeren painted a brand new "Vermeer" while officials watched.

The man who faked Vermeer

Johannes Vermeer was a Dutch painter of the 1600s, famous for quiet scenes of light falling through a window. Only about 35 of his paintings are known, so a "new" one would be priceless. In the 1930s, a Dutch artist named Han van Meegeren began painting fresh "Vermeers", choosing religious subjects that some experts hoped Vermeer had once painted. He used old canvases and baked his pictures in an oven, using a kind of plastic in the paint so that it hardened and cracked like old paint. Leading experts were fooled.

After the Second World War, Dutch officials found one of his "Vermeers" in the collection of the Nazi leader Hermann Göring. Van Meegeren was arrested for selling a national treasure to the enemy. To save himself, he confessed it was a fake, and painted another "Vermeer" while officials watched. He was convicted of forgery in 1947 and died only weeks later.

Reading the brushstrokes

Experts who know an artist's work deeply are called connoisseurs. They study how a painter handled a brush, how they drew hands and eyes, and which colours they liked. A forger copying a style often works slowly and carefully, and that care can show up as stiff, hesitant lines.

Signatures are the easiest part to fake, so experts trust them least. Some researchers now use computers to measure the length, curve and thickness of brushstrokes, comparing them the way detectives compare fingerprints.

The paper trail

A genuine painting usually leaves a trail: letters, sales receipts, exhibition catalogues, old photographs and labels on the back of the frame. Forgers know this, so the cleverest ones fake the trail too.

The German forger Wolfgang Beltracchi invented "new" paintings in the style of famous early 20th-century artists, then claimed they came from a famous old collection. He even made fake old photographs, with his wife dressed up as her own grandmother sitting beside the paintings. His story fell apart in 2010. Scientists tested a painting dated 1914 and found titanium white, a paint that wasn't sold to artists until years later.

Science steps in

Labs can check the age of a painting's materials. A speck of paint smaller than a salt grain shows which pigments were used. X-rays show hidden layers and whether an old canvas was scraped and reused. Wood and canvas can be dated using carbon, the same half-life method used for ancient bones.

Science is good at proving a painting is too new. Proving that a painting is genuine is much harder, because an old canvas with old paint could still be the work of a skilled copyist from long ago. For that, experts must weigh all the clues together.

Some answers stay open. The Salvator Mundi, a painting of Christ, sold in 2017 for about 450 million US dollars as a work by Leonardo da Vinci. No painting has ever sold for more at auction. Some experts still doubt that Leonardo painted most of it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If a painting has the artist's signature on it, it must be real.

Truth: Signatures are the easiest thing to fake, and many genuine old paintings were never signed at all. Experts rely on style, ownership records and scientific tests instead.

Try this

Play forgers and detectives. One person draws a quick doodle and signs it. Two others each make a careful copy and sign it too. Mix up the three drawings, then let a "detective" study them with a magnifying glass. Copies are often drawn slowly, so look for shaky, hesitant lines and tiny blobs where the pencil paused.
