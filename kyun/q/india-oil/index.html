Where does India get its oil?

Almost every litre of petrol in an Indian scooter began its journey in another country, often on a giant ship that sailed for weeks to reach us.

The short answer

India gets most of its oil from other countries. It buys a thick, dark liquid called crude oil from places like Russia, Iraq, Saudi Arabia, the UAE and the USA, brings it home on huge ships, and turns it into petrol, diesel and cooking gas in its own refineries.

India is the world's third-biggest user of oil, after the USA and China. But it pumps only a small part of what it needs from its own wells. So where it buys oil, and how much it pays, affects prices for almost everyone.

Surprising but true

India buys more than 85% of the crude oil it uses from other countries. Only a small share comes from wells inside India, in places like Mumbai High, Assam and Rajasthan.

How it works

Oil is bought and sold all over the world, a bit like vegetables in one giant market. Prices are set in US dollars, and they go up and down every day.

Here is the journey of the oil in a scooter's tank:

  • 1. An oil company in another country pumps crude oil out of the ground.
  • 2. An Indian refinery buys it, often months in advance, and pays in dollars, a foreign currency.
  • 3. A giant ship called a tanker carries it across the sea to a port like Jamnagar, Paradip or Kochi.
  • 4. A refinery heats the crude and splits it into petrol, diesel, jet fuel, cooking gas and more.
  • 5. Pipelines, trains and trucks take the fuel to petrol pumps and gas agencies.

Think of a family that grows no wheat but eats rotis every day. It must keep buying wheat from the market, and if the price goes up, the family has to pay. India is in the same spot with oil.

Many ships pass through narrow sea lanes on the way. The most famous is the Strait of Hormuz, near Iran, where a large share of the world's oil sails out of the Gulf. When there is trouble there, oil prices can jump overnight.

Where it came from

Oil formed over millions of years from tiny sea creatures buried under mud and rock, as the story of how coal and oil were made explains.

India's own oil story began in 1889, when a well was drilled at Digboi in Assam. A refinery opened there in 1901, and it still works today. In 1974, scientists found a big oil field under the sea off Mumbai, called Mumbai High. In 2004, a large field was found in the desert of Barmer in Rajasthan.

But India's need for fuel grew far faster than its wells. More cars, buses, tractors, planes and factories meant more and more oil had to be bought from abroad. Goods bought from other countries like this are called imports.

India's story

For many years, India bought most of its oil from countries around the Gulf, such as Iraq, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait and the UAE. They were close, and their oil suited Indian refineries.

That changed after 2022, when Russia went to war with Ukraine. Western countries stopped buying much Russian oil, so Russia sold it cheaper to others. India bought a lot more, and Russia became one of its biggest suppliers.

This upset the USA. In August 2025, it put an extra 25% tariff on many Indian goods because of the Russian oil. That extra tariff was removed in February 2026, as the two countries worked on a trade deal. Later in 2026, a new US law allowed tariffs of up to 100% on the biggest buyers of Russian oil. In October 2026, the US paused that threat while it worked on a trade deal with India.

India's leaders say they buy oil where it is cheapest and most reliable for 140 crore people. They have also started buying more from the USA and from Venezuela, to spread the risk. Spreading purchases across many sellers is like a shop that keeps several suppliers, so one problem does not empty its shelves.

India also keeps emergency oil in huge underground rock caverns at Visakhapatnam, Mangaluru and Padur. These stores can keep the country going for some days if supplies are cut.

The numbers that matter

  • - More than 85%: the share of its crude oil that India buys from abroad. Fewer than 15 litres in every 100 come from Indian wells.
  • - 159 litres: the size of one barrel, the unit oil is sold in. India uses millions of barrels every single day.
  • - Third: India's place among the world's oil users, after the USA and China.
  • - About 157 dollars: the record price of a barrel of the crude India buys, reached in March 2026 during a war in West Asia that shut the Strait of Hormuz. In calmer years the price has often been well under 100 dollars.

What people argue about

One debate is about who to buy from. Some say India should always buy the cheapest oil, wherever it comes from, because that keeps prices low for families. Others say India must also think about its friendships with big trading partners like the USA, who may punish countries that buy from Russia.

Another debate is about the future. Some want India to cut its oil needs fast, with electric cars, solar power and fuel made from sugarcane. Others point out that buses, trucks and planes will need oil for many years, so India should also look for more oil at home.

Myths and mix-ups

"Crude oil and petrol are the same thing." Crude oil is a thick mix straight from the ground. It must be cleaned and split in a refinery before it becomes petrol.

"Oil prices only matter to people who drive." Trucks carry food, medicine and parcels. When fuel costs more, so do many things in the shops.

"India could just stop importing oil tomorrow." Switching would take many years and huge amounts of money. That is why governments plan these changes slowly.

What it means for you

Every time you ride a bus, eat vegetables that came by truck, or see the blue flame of a gas stove, oil from far away is part of the story.

When world oil prices rise, the government and oil companies must decide how much of the extra cost to pass on. Higher fuel prices can push up prices in general, which is why the news watches oil so closely. India pays for oil in dollars, so a weak rupee makes it dearer too. Learning why countries trade helps explain why one war or one deal far away can change what your family pays at the petrol pump.

Myth or fact?

Myth: India gets most of its petrol from its own oil wells.

Truth: India does pump some oil at home, but it buys more than 85% of its crude oil from other countries and then turns it into petrol and diesel in its own refineries.
