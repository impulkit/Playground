How did the potato travel the world?

The aloo in your samosa began its journey thousands of years ago, high in the Andes mountains of South America.

The short answer

The potato was first farmed in the Andes mountains of South America, near Lake Titicaca on the border of Peru and Bolivia, about 8,000 years ago. Farmers there grew hundreds of kinds, in every colour from yellow to deep purple.

In the 1500s, Spanish conquerors carried potatoes across the Atlantic to Europe. Over the next 300 years, the potato spread from Spain to Ireland, Russia, India and China. It gave more food per field than wheat, grew in cold places, and could be dug up only when needed. Today it is one of the world's most important food crops, and China and India grow the most.

Surprising but true

In 1995, potato plants grown aboard the US Space Shuttle Columbia became the first food crop grown in space.

The Inca's freeze-dried food

High in the Andes, nights are freezing and days are sunny and dry. The Inca and earlier peoples used this to make chuño. Potatoes were left out to freeze at night and thaw by day, then trampled underfoot to squeeze out water. After a few weeks, they became light, hard and could be stored for years.

Chuño fed armies and helped the Inca store food for bad harvests. It is one of the oldest freeze-dried foods in the world, a method still used in Peru and Bolivia.

Europe learns to love it

At first many Europeans didn't trust the potato. It was a strange lump from underground, related to poisonous nightshade plants, and its green parts really are toxic. Some people thought it caused illness.

Rulers had to push it. In the 1700s, King Frederick the Great of Prussia ordered farmers to grow potatoes to protect against famine. In France, the scientist Antoine-Augustin Parmentier, who had eaten potatoes as a prisoner of war, campaigned for them. Over time, the potato became an everyday food from Germany to Russia.

The Irish famine

In Ireland, many poor families came to depend almost entirely on potatoes. In 1845, a disease called potato blight arrived. It turned the plants black and rotted the potatoes in the ground.

The harvest failed for several years running. Between 1845 and 1852, about a million people died, and about another million left Ireland, many sailing to the USA. Millions of Americans today have Irish ancestors because of it, part of the immigration stories that shaped the country. Scientists now know blight is caused by a fungus-like microbe that spreads in damp weather.

Aloo comes to India

Portuguese traders brought potatoes to India in the 1600s, probably first to the west coast. British officials later planted them in hill stations such as Shimla, Darjeeling and the Nilgiris, where cool weather suited them.

The Hindi word aloo is older than the potato in India. It was once used for other tubers and roots. Today it's hard to picture Indian food without it: aloo paratha, dum aloo, vada pav and the filling of a samosa. India's national potato research institute, set up in 1949, is now based in Shimla. Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal grow the most potatoes in India.

Stems, not roots

A potato is a tuber, the swollen tip of an underground stem packed with starch. The plant stores food there to survive the winter and regrow in spring.

Because it is a stem, a potato turns green if left in the light. The green colour is harmless chlorophyll, but it's a warning sign: green patches can also hold solanine, a bitter natural poison. Cooks cut green bits away, and potatoes are best kept in the dark.

Potatoes in space

In 1995, scientists from the University of Wisconsin grew small potato plants inside the Space Shuttle Columbia. It was the first time a food plant was grown in space. Since then, astronauts have grown lettuce and other greens on the International Space Station, as you can read in space food.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Potatoes are roots.

Truth: A potato is a tuber, a swollen underground stem. It has buds (the eyes) and turns green in light, as stems do. Sweet potatoes, on the other hand, really are roots.

Try this

Put an old potato that has started to sprout into a pot of soil, with the shoots pointing up, and cover it with a few centimetres of soil. Keep it watered in a sunny spot. In a few months, dig gently around the plant with your hands to find the new potatoes.
