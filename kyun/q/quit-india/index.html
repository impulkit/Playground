What was the Quit India Movement?

On 8 August 1942, Gandhi stood before a huge crowd in Bombay and gave them a two-word mantra, "Do or die". By the next morning, he was in prison.

The short answer

The Quit India Movement was a mass campaign launched by Mahatma Gandhi and the Indian National Congress in August 1942, during the Second World War. It demanded that the British leave India immediately.

On 8 August 1942, at a meeting in Bombay, Gandhi told Indians to "do or die": to act as if they were already free. Within hours, the British arrested almost every Congress leader. Leaderless, people across India protested anyway, with strikes, marches and in some places attacks on railways and government buildings. The government crushed the movement within months and arrested more than 100,000 people. But it showed that Britain could no longer count on Indian cooperation.

Surprising but true

For a few months in 1942, a 22-year-old student named Usha Mehta helped run a secret radio station that broadcast news of the movement from hidden places in Bombay.

A war at the door

The Second World War had reached India's doorstep. Japan had conquered Burma and was near the eastern border. Britain had declared India part of the war in 1939 without asking Indian leaders.

In early 1942, the British sent Sir Stafford Cripps to offer India self-government, but only after the war. Congress leaders rejected the offer. Gandhi is said to have called it "a post-dated cheque". He argued that only a free India could defend itself properly, and that the British should leave now.

Do or die

On 8 August 1942, the All India Congress Committee passed the Quit India resolution at Gowalia Tank Maidan in Bombay, now Mumbai. Gandhi spoke for a long time. He told the crowd he was giving them a short mantra to carry in their hearts: "Do or die."

Before dawn the next day, police arrested Gandhi, Jawaharlal Nehru, Sardar Patel and many others. Gandhi was held at the Aga Khan Palace in Pune. His secretary, Mahadev Desai, died there that August. His wife, Kasturba, died there in February 1944.

The movement goes underground

With the leaders gone, younger people stepped forward. Aruna Asaf Ali raised the Congress flag at Gowalia Tank on 9 August, then went into hiding. Jayaprakash Narayan and Ram Manohar Lohia organised resistance in secret.

Usha Mehta and a few others ran the Congress Radio, a secret station that moved from place to place in Bombay to avoid detection. It broadcast news the censored newspapers would not print, until the police found it in November 1942. In a few places, such as Ballia in Uttar Pradesh, Satara in Maharashtra and Tamluk in Bengal, local people set up their own governments for weeks or even years.

Hard times

The government struck back hard. Police and troops fired on crowds, and many protesters were killed, including Matangini Hazra, a 73-year-old woman shot while leading a march in Tamluk. Whole villages were fined.

Not all Indians joined in. The Muslim League stayed away, as did the Communist Party, which supported the war against Nazi Germany after it invaded the Soviet Union. Many Indians were serving in the army. Meanwhile, Subhas Chandra Bose chose a different path, raising an army with Japanese help.

What it achieved

By early 1943 the open protests had been crushed. Gandhi was released in 1944 because of poor health. Yet the movement made clear that millions of Indians wanted freedom and were willing to suffer for it.

After the war, Britain was exhausted and short of money. Historians still argue over how far Quit India forced its hand. Five years after "do or die", on 15 August 1947, India became independent. The Bombay ground where it began is now called August Kranti Maidan, the August Revolution Ground.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Quit India Movement was entirely peaceful.

Truth: Gandhi asked for non-violent action, but with the leaders in jail, the movement had no central control. Some protesters cut telegraph wires, damaged railway lines and attacked police stations, and the government used heavy force in return.
