How many galaxies are there?

In 1995, the Hubble telescope stared for ten days at a patch of sky that looked completely empty, and found thousands of galaxies.

The short answer

Nobody knows exactly. Astronomers can't count every galaxy, so they count the ones in small patches of sky and multiply. Their best estimates for the part of the universe we can see run from a few hundred billion galaxies up to about two trillion.

Each galaxy is a vast family of stars, gas and dust held together by gravity. Our own, the Milky Way, has hundreds of billions of stars. Multiply that by hundreds of billions of galaxies, and the number of stars becomes so big that it's hard to write down.

Surprising but true

The Andromeda Galaxy is about 2.5 million light-years away, yet on a dark night you can see it without a telescope. It is the most distant thing most people can see with their own eyes.

The great debate

A hundred years ago, many astronomers thought the Milky Way was the whole universe. Faint, fuzzy patches in the sky, called nebulae, were thought to be clouds of gas inside it. Others argued that some were separate "island universes", very far away. In 1920, two American astronomers held a famous public debate about it in Washington.

The answer came from Mount Wilson Observatory in California. In 1923 and 1924, Edwin Hubble found a special kind of flickering star in the Andromeda nebula. These stars pulse at a speed that reveals how bright they really are. Comparing that with how faint it looked told him its distance. It was far outside the Milky Way. Andromeda was a whole galaxy.

That method came from Henrietta Swan Leavitt, who worked at the Harvard College Observatory measuring stars on glass photographic plates. She was paid very little and given little credit in her lifetime. Her discovery became one of the most useful tools in astronomy.

Spirals, blobs and messes

Galaxies come in a few main shapes. Spirals, like ours and Andromeda, are flat discs with curving arms full of young blue stars. Ellipticals are smooth, rounded balls of older, redder stars, some of them giants. Irregular galaxies have no neat shape at all, often because they've been pulled about by a neighbour.

Galaxies also bump and merge. Many of the biggest ellipticals are thought to be built from several smaller galaxies that crashed together. Most big galaxies, including ours, have a giant black hole at the centre.

Counting the uncountable

In December 1995, astronomers aimed the Hubble Space Telescope at a tiny patch of sky near the Big Dipper that seemed empty. They took exposures for about ten days. The picture, called the Hubble Deep Field, showed around 3,000 galaxies. Later deep pictures by Hubble and the James Webb Space Telescope found even more.

Then came the maths. If one tiny patch holds thousands of galaxies, and the whole sky is millions of times bigger, the total must be in the hundreds of billions. This is estimation, the same trick you would use to guess the number of people at a cricket match from one section of the stands.

In 2016, a team led by Christopher Conselice estimated about two trillion galaxies, counting many small, faint ones too dim to see. Later measurements of how dark space really is, using NASA's New Horizons spacecraft far out beyond Pluto, suggested the true number may be lower. The argument continues.

India's eye on galaxies

India's space telescope AstroSat, launched by ISRO in 2015, can see ultraviolet light, which our atmosphere blocks. In 2020, Indian astronomers reported that it had picked up ultraviolet light from a galaxy about 9.3 billion light-years away. That light left its galaxy long before Earth even existed.

Myth or fact?

Myth: When two galaxies collide, their stars smash into each other.

Truth: Stars are so far apart that they almost never hit. If the Sun were the size of a grain of sand, the nearest star would be about 30 km away. When galaxies merge, gas clouds crash and new stars form, but the stars themselves mostly glide past each other.
