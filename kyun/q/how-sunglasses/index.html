How do sunglasses protect your eyes?

Cheap dark sunglasses with no UV filter can be worse than none, because your pupils open wider behind them and let in more harmful rays.

The short answer

Sunglasses protect your eyes in two ways. First, good lenses block ultraviolet (UV) light, invisible rays from the Sun that can damage the eye over time. Second, tinted lenses cut down bright visible light and glare, so you can see comfortably without squinting.

The most important part is invisible. A label saying "UV400" or "100% UV protection" means the lenses stop almost all ultraviolet light. The colour and darkness of the lens only change how bright things look, not how much UV gets through.

Surprising but true

Inuit and other Arctic peoples carved snow goggles from bone, wood or antler at least 2,000 years ago, with narrow slits to cut the glare off snow.

Light you cannot see

Sunlight contains light of many wavelengths. We see only part of it, the colours of the rainbow. Just beyond violet lies ultraviolet light, which our eyes cannot see. UV is what causes sunburn on skin.

Eyes can get sunburnt too. Too much UV in one day can burn the clear front surface of the eye, causing pain, tears and a gritty feeling. Skiers and mountaineers call this snow blindness, because snow reflects so much sunlight back up. Over many years, UV also raises the risk of cataracts, a clouding of the eye's lens that is one of the main causes of poor sight in older people.

The danger of cheap dark lenses

When light is dim, your pupils, the black circles in the middle of your eyes, open wider to let more light in. Behind dark sunglasses, they do the same. If the lenses block light but not UV, the wide-open pupils actually let in more UV than before.

That is why dark toy sunglasses without a UV filter can be worse than no sunglasses at all. Good UV-blocking lenses can be any colour, even clear. Many ordinary spectacle lenses now include a UV filter too.

How polarised lenses kill glare

Sunlight bounces off flat surfaces like water, wet roads and car bonnets. Much of that reflected light becomes polarised, meaning its waves vibrate mostly in one direction, side to side. That is the dazzling glare that makes you squint.

Polarised lenses contain a filter with millions of tiny lined-up molecules, like the slats of a fence. They block light waves vibrating side to side and let through those moving up and down. Glare disappears, and you can often see fish under the surface of a pond. In the 1930s, American inventor Edwin Land made polarising filters cheap enough for sunglasses, and fishers and drivers loved them.

Goggles, film stars and pilots

People protected their eyes from glare long before modern lenses. Inuit and other Arctic peoples carved goggles with thin slits from bone, wood or antler. The slits let in only a sliver of light, enough to see but not enough to be blinded by the snow. Some found by archaeologists are around 2,000 years old.

Modern sunglasses became popular in the USA after 1929, when a businessman named Sam Foster began selling cheap ones on the beach at Atlantic City. Film stars wore them, and soon everyone wanted a pair. During the 1930s, American pilots got special green-tinted glasses for flying high in bright sunshine, a style still known as aviators.

UV is strongest at high altitudes, on snow, near water and around noon. In India's summer, and in places like Ladakh where the air is thin, it can be very strong indeed. So sunglasses with a UV400 label are a good idea outdoors, along with a hat. But no sunglasses are safe for staring at the Sun. During an eclipse, only special eclipse glasses that block almost all sunlight will do. And the reason less UV reaches the ground in the first place is a thin shield of gas high above us, the ozone layer.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The darker the sunglasses, the better they protect your eyes.

Truth: Darkness only reduces brightness. Protection from ultraviolet light comes from a special filter in the lens, which can be present even in light-coloured or clear lenses.

Try this

If someone at home has polarised sunglasses, hold them in front of a phone or laptop screen and slowly turn them sideways. At one angle the screen goes dark. That is because the screen gives off light waves lined up one way, and the lenses block that direction.
