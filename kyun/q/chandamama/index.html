What was Chandamama magazine?

For decades, every month, a king in Chandamama carried a corpse through a dark forest while a ghost on his shoulder told riddles.

The short answer

Chandamama was a monthly children's magazine full of folk tales, myths, legends and moral stories, beautifully illustrated in colour. It was started in July 1947 in Madras, now Chennai, first in Telugu and Tamil. It soon appeared in more than ten Indian languages, as well as English from 1955.

Its name means "Uncle Moon", the friendly moon of Indian lullabies, such as "Chanda mama door ke". For decades it was one of the most-read children's magazines in India. Families waited for each new issue, and children passed copies between houses until the pages fell apart. It finally stopped publishing in 2013, but many older Indians still remember its stories and pictures vividly.

Surprising but true

Chandamama was started by two film-makers in Madras in July 1947, weeks before India became independent, and it went on to appear in more than ten languages.

Film-makers who made a magazine

Chandamama was founded by B. Nagi Reddy and his partner Chakrapani. Together they also ran Vijaya Vauhini Studios in Madras, one of the biggest film studios in South India, which made many Telugu and Tamil films in South Indian cinema.

They wanted a magazine that brought India's old stories to children in their own languages. Chakrapani shaped what went into it in its early years. The magazine printed stories from the Ramayana and Mahabharata, from the Panchatantra and Jataka tales, and folk tales from many regions, along with new stories in the same style.

Vikram and Betal

Chandamama's most famous feature was the series about King Vikram and Betal, a ghostly spirit. Every story began the same way. On a dark, stormy night, the brave king climbs a tree in a cremation ground, lifts down a corpse in which Betal lives, and carries it over his shoulder.

As he walks, Betal tells him a story that ends with a puzzle about right and wrong. If Vikram knows the answer, he must speak. As soon as he does, Betal escapes and flies back to the tree. Next month, the whole thing starts again.

One riddle tells of three queens. The first faints when a lotus petal falls on her. The second gets blisters from moonlight. The third is bruised just by hearing a pestle pounding rice far away. Which queen is the most delicate? Vikram says the third, because the others were at least touched by something, and off flies Betal.

These tales are very old. They come from the Vetala Panchavimshati, "Twenty-five Tales of the Vetala", which appears in Somadeva's Sanskrit collection, the Kathasaritsagara, from the 11th century. Indian television also turned them into a popular series in the 1980s.

The art of Chandamama

The magazine's pictures were as loved as its stories. Artists such as M. T. V. Acharya and K. C. Sivasankaran, known as Sankar, drew kings, sages, demons and village folk in rich colour.

Sankar drew the Vikram and Betal pictures for decades: the determined king, the grinning spirit, the twisted tree. He kept working for the magazine into old age and died in 2020, aged 97.

Why it faded

From the 1990s, children had more choices: cable television, comics like Tinkle, and later the internet. Chandamama struggled to keep up. It was sold to a new owner in 2007, which tried to modernise it, but the business ran into money troubles and the magazine stopped in 2013.

Old issues are now collected and shared by fans, and many still turn up in family cupboards. For a generation of Indian children, the moon on the cover meant a new set of stories was waiting inside.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Vikram and Betal stories were invented by Chandamama.

Truth: They come from a much older collection, the Vetala Panchavimshati, "Twenty-five Tales of the Vetala". A version appears in Somadeva's Kathasaritsagara, written in Sanskrit in the 11th century.
