Why are some people left-handed?

About one person in ten is left-handed, in every country and every century that scientists have checked, and nobody knows exactly why.

The short answer

Around one person in ten prefers to use their left hand for writing, throwing and eating. It's been about the same through history and across the world. Nobody fully knows why.

Genes play a part. Left-handed parents are a little more likely to have left-handed children. But scientists haven't found a single left-handed gene. Instead, many genes each seem to nudge the odds a tiny bit. Things that happen as a baby grows in the womb matter too. Even identical twins, who share the same genes, are sometimes one left-handed and one right-handed. So handedness seems to be part inheritance and part chance.

Surprising but true

Babies already prefer one hand before they are born. Scans show most of them suck their right thumb in the womb.

Before you're born

Hand preference starts early. In the early 1990s, a team in Northern Ireland led by Peter Hepper watched babies in the womb using ultrasound scans. Most of them sucked their right thumb. Years later, the team checked on some of those children. Those who had sucked their right thumb had grown up right-handed. Those who had sucked their left were much more likely to be left-handed.

Most toddlers try both hands for a while. A clear preference usually shows by about age 2 to 4. Your brain is wired so that its left half controls the right side of the body, and the right half controls the left. How that links to handedness is still being worked out.

Twins and families

Because identical twins share all their genes, they're useful for studying handedness. If it were only genes, they'd always match. They don't. In roughly one pair in five, one twin is left-handed and the other right-handed.

That tells scientists that genes aren't the whole story. Tiny random differences in how a baby develops seem to play a part too. Being left-handed isn't linked to being smarter or less smart. Handedness also isn't all-or-nothing. Many people write with one hand but bat, kick or throw with the other, and a few are equally comfortable with both.

A right-handed world

For centuries, many people saw left-handedness as something to fix. The Latin word for left, sinister, came to mean evil in English. In many schools in Britain, the USA and India, left-handed children were made to write with their right hand, sometimes with their left hand tied behind them. In parts of India and many other places, eating with the left hand is still seen as impolite, because it is traditionally kept for washing.

Today, most schools let children use whichever hand comes naturally. Still, much of the world is built for right-handers: scissors, can openers, school desks with the writing arm on the right. Left-handers writing in English or Hindi move their hand across fresh ink, which is why many hook their wrist. With some writing tools, like fountain pens, smudges are a real problem.

Left-handers in sport

In one-on-one sports, left-handers turn up much more than one in ten. Many great tennis players, boxers and fencers have been left-handed. In cricket, left-handed batters such as Brian Lara and Sourav Ganguly gave bowlers headaches, because the ball's angle and the fielding positions are reversed.

The reason is surprise. Most players spend their lives facing right-handers. A left-hander's shots come from unexpected angles. Some scientists think this advantage in fights and games may be one reason left-handedness has never died out. International Left-Handers Day, on 13 August, has been marked since 1976.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Left-handed people are right-brained, which makes them more creative.

Truth: There's no good evidence that left-handers are more creative or that people are left-brained or right-brained. Both halves of the brain work together on almost everything.

Try this

Make a handedness survey. Ask ten people to write their name, throw a ball, kick a ball and look through a cardboard tube. Note which hand, foot and eye they use for each. You'll find many people aren't fully left or right, and some use their left foot or eye even if they write with their right hand.
