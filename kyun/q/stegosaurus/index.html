What were Stegosaurus's plates for?

Two rows of giant plates and a spiky tail, but its brain was tiny.

The short answer

Stegosaurus had two rows of tall, flat plates along its back. They were thin and covered in grooves for blood vessels, so they probably weren't good armour. Most scientists think they were for display: to look bigger, to recognise their own kind, and to show off to mates.

Its tail had four long spikes. Those were real weapons. A fossil Allosaurus has a hole in a tail bone that fits a Stegosaurus spike.

Surprising but true

The spiky tail end is nicknamed the ‘thagomizer’, a word first used as a joke in a 1982 newspaper cartoon. Scientists liked it and kept it.

Plates for show

The plates were made of bone with a spongy inside and a thin outer layer. They were not fixed to the backbone. Instead, they sat in the thick skin, standing up in two staggered rows.

From the side, they made the animal look much taller, a bit like a peacock's tail makes a peacock look bigger. The plates may have been brightly coloured, too, though colour is rarely kept in fossils. Some scientists once thought the plates helped warm up in the sun or cool down in the breeze, because of all those blood vessels. That idea is still discussed, but display is the most common view.

The thagomizer

In 1982, the American cartoonist Gary Larson drew a Far Side cartoon of a caveman teacher pointing at a Stegosaurus tail. He named the spikes after "the late Thag Simmons". Of course, no human ever met a Stegosaurus.

Scientists enjoyed the joke so much they began using "thagomizer" in talks and papers. It is now a common nickname. The spikes were serious, though. Swung by a strong tail, they could hit hard enough to break bone, as the injured Allosaurus shows. The spikes pointed out to the sides and slightly up, so a swing swept them through the air like a club.

Where it lived

Stegosaurus lived about 150 million years ago in what is now the western USA (Colorado, Utah and Wyoming), in rocks called the Morrison Formation. Giant long-necked sauropods like Diplodocus and Brachiosaurus lived there too, along with the hunter Allosaurus. Stegosaurus fossils have also been found in Portugal, which lay much closer to North America then, before the Atlantic Ocean grew wide.

It was named in 1877 by the American scientist Othniel Charles Marsh, during a bitter rivalry with another fossil hunter, called the Bone Wars. The name means "roof lizard", because Marsh first thought the plates lay flat on its back, like roof tiles.

What were the Bone Wars?Two American scientists hated each other so much that they spied, bribed and smashed fossils to beat each other.

A tiny brain?

Its brain was small for its body, no bigger than a lime, in an animal nearly as long as a school bus. Yet dinosaur brains can't be judged by size alone. Stegosaurus and its relatives lasted for millions of years, which suggests they were well suited to their lives.

Its head was low and narrow, with small, leaf-shaped teeth. It probably ate ferns, cycads and other low plants, perhaps rearing up on its back legs sometimes to reach higher.

Sophie, the best skeleton

The most complete Stegosaurus skeleton known is nicknamed Sophie. She was found in Wyoming and is now in the Natural History Museum in London. Scientists used her to work out how heavy Stegosaurus was: about the weight of a rhinoceros.

Deep time is deep

T. rex lived about 66 million years ago. Stegosaurus lived about 150 million years ago. So more time separates them than separates T. rex from you. "Dinosaur times" were not one moment. They were 165 million years long.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Stegosaurus had a second brain in its hips to help control its back legs.

Truth: There was a large space in its hip bones, which led to this old idea. Scientists now think the space held something else, possibly a store of energy-rich tissue like the one birds have there. It was not a brain.

Try this

Cut some paper triangles and squares of different sizes and fold a strip of card into a long "back". Try standing the plates in one row, two neat rows side by side, and two rows that zigzag. Which looks biggest from the side? Most Stegosaurus fossils suggest the zigzag pattern.
