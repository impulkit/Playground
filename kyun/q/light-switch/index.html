What happens when you flip a light switch?

The light comes on in a blink, yet the electrons in the wire crawl along slower than an ant.

The short answer

A light switch is a gate in a loop of wire. Electricity can only flow when the loop, called a circuit, is complete. When you flip the switch on, two pieces of metal inside press together and close the gap. Electric current flows from the supply, through the bulb and back again, and the bulb lights up.

Flip it off and a spring pulls the metal pieces apart. There's now a tiny air gap. Air doesn't carry electricity well, so the current stops and the room goes dark. Everything else, from the wires in your walls to the power station miles away, stays exactly as it was.

Surprising but true

Electrons in a household wire drift less than a millimetre a second, but the push that moves them travels close to the speed of light.

Inside the little box

Open up an ordinary switch (only an electrician should do this) and you find a small seesaw of brass or copper, a spring and two screws where wires attach. Pressing the switch tips the seesaw so its contacts touch.

The spring is there for a reason. It makes the contacts snap together or apart quickly. If they drifted slowly, electricity could jump across the narrowing gap as a spark, which heats and wears the metal. That's why most switches give a firm click.

Indian homes usually have rows of switches on a single board, often with a small red or orange dot that shows which way is on. In India you usually press a switch down to turn a light on, while in the USA you usually flip it up. The plastic case around them doesn't carry electricity, so your finger never touches the metal inside.

Electricians always put the switch on the live wire, the one that carries the push from the supply. When the switch is off, the bulb holder is cut off from that push, so it's safer to change a bulb.

Slow electrons, fast light

A copper wire is already packed with electrons, tiny charged particles that can move between atoms. When the circuit closes, every electron along the wire starts to move at once, like marbles in a full tube. Push one in at one end and one pops out at the other almost instantly.

Each electron, though, only drifts a fraction of a millimetre per second. In homes in India and the USA, the current is alternating, so electrons mostly jiggle back and forth. In India they switch direction 100 times a second, and in the USA 120 times.

What was the war of the currents?In the 1880s, two kinds of electricity fought to light America's cities. The loser's champion was Thomas Edison, the most famous inventor alive.

Lights on the stairs

In many homes you can turn on a staircase light at the bottom and switch it off at the top. That trick uses two-way switches. Each one can send the current down either of two wires. The light is on when both switches point to the same wire and off when they don't. Flip either one and you change the answer.

This is a simple bit of logic, the same yes-or-no kind computers use with binary. Hotels and long corridors use a third type, called an intermediate switch, so three or more places can control one light.

From the bulb's side

When the current arrives, an old-style bulb heats a thin wire until it glows white-hot. An LED bulb has a small circuit in its base that turns the mains supply into a gentle, steady flow for its light-emitting chips. That's why some LEDs take a fraction of a second to come on.

Every circuit in a house also passes through a fuse or circuit breaker. If too much current flows, it cuts the power before the wires get dangerously hot. Your light switch is the small gate. The breaker is the guard at the main door.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Switching a light off and on again uses more electricity than leaving it on.

Truth: For ordinary bulbs and LEDs, switching off saves electricity, even for a few minutes. The tiny surge when a light starts is far smaller than the energy it would use staying on.

Try this

With an adult's help, take a torch apart and find its switch. Watch how sliding it presses two pieces of metal together. Then look at the light switches at home and count how many places can turn the same light on and off.
