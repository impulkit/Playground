How did All India Radio begin?

In 1942 a 22-year-old student named Usha Mehta ran a secret radio station for the freedom movement, moving it from place to place to dodge the police.

The short answer

Radio in India began with amateurs. In 1923 a radio club in Bombay (now Mumbai) began broadcasting, and other clubs followed in Calcutta and Madras. In 1927 a private company, the Indian Broadcasting Company, opened stations in Bombay and Calcutta. It soon ran short of money, and the British government took over.

In 1936 the government service was renamed All India Radio. After independence it grew into one of the largest radio networks in the world, carrying news, music, farming advice and plays in many languages. Since 1957 it has also been officially called Akashvani, a Sanskrit-based word meaning "voice from the sky".

Surprising but true

The name Akashvani, "voice from the sky", was first used by a professor in Mysore for the small radio station he ran from his own home.

Voices through the air

Radio sends sound using radio waves, invisible waves that travel at the speed of light. A station turns voices and music into a changing radio signal and sends it from a tall tower. A radio set picks up the signal and turns it back into sound.

In the 1930s a radio set was expensive, so many villages had a single community set in a square or panchayat office. People gathered around it for news, the way families later gathered around one TV.

The secret station

In August 1942, during the Quit India Movement, the British arrested Congress leaders and silenced newspapers. A young student in Bombay, Usha Mehta, and a few friends set up a hidden transmitter. Their broadcasts began with the words "This is the Congress Radio calling".

They read out news the government was hiding and messages from underground leaders. To avoid being caught, they kept moving the equipment from building to building. After about three months, police tracked them down and Usha Mehta was jailed. She later became a professor and lived to see India celebrate 50 years of freedom.

What was the Quit India Movement?On 8 August 1942, Gandhi stood before a huge crowd in Bombay and gave them a two-word mantra, "Do or die". By the next morning, he was in prison.

Midnight, 1947

On the night of 14 August 1947, All India Radio carried Jawaharlal Nehru's "Tryst with Destiny" speech as India became independent at midnight. Families across the country heard it live.

Radio also became a teacher. From the 1960s, All India Radio ran farm programmes in local languages, telling farmers about new seeds, fertilisers and the weather, which helped spread the Green Revolution. It carried cricket too. Ball-by-ball commentary in English and Hindi let fans follow Test matches long before most homes had a television. In 2014 the prime minister began a monthly radio talk, Mann Ki Baat, on the same network.

Three months after independence, on 12 November 1947, Mahatma Gandhi made his only visit to a radio studio, in Delhi, to speak to refugees from Partition. That day is still marked as Public Service Broadcasting Day.

Film songs and a rival station

In the 1950s, the government decided that Hindi film songs were too lightweight and cut them back on All India Radio. Listeners switched to Radio Ceylon, broadcasting from Sri Lanka. Its weekly countdown show, Binaca Geetmala, hosted by Ameen Sayani, became a national habit.

All India Radio got the message. In 1957 it launched Vividh Bharati, a channel packed with film music, requests and radio shows. It is still on air. Private FM stations were allowed from the early 2000s, and big cities now have dozens of channels playing music and chat. News on radio is still mostly left to All India Radio. The name Akashvani has its own story. It was first used in 1936 by M.V. Gopalaswamy, a professor in Mysore who started a small station at his home. Today the network reaches almost every corner of India, and a farmer in a field and a commuter on the Delhi Metro can hear the same news bulletin at the same moment.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Hardly anyone listens to radio any more.

Truth: Radio still reaches places where phones and TV struggle, like remote villages, fishing boats and farms. All India Radio broadcasts in dozens of languages, and many people listen through mobile apps too.
