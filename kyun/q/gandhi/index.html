How did Gandhi fight without weapons?

A shy lawyer who was once thrown off a train in South Africa went on to challenge the British Empire with a spinning wheel and a handful of salt.

The short answer

Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi led India's struggle for freedom from British rule using a method he called satyagraha, "holding firmly to truth". It meant resisting injustice without violence.

Gandhi asked people to break unfair laws openly, refuse to cooperate with the government, boycott British goods and accept arrest or beatings without hitting back. He believed that if people suffered calmly for a just cause, they would win public support and shame their rulers into changing. His campaigns drew in millions of ordinary Indians, including farmers and women, who had never joined politics before. People called him Mahatma, "great soul", and Bapu, "father". His methods later inspired Martin Luther King Jr. and Nelson Mandela.

Surprising but true

Gandhi was nominated for the Nobel Peace Prize five times but never won. The Nobel committee later said publicly that leaving him out was a mistake.

A shy boy from Gujarat

Gandhi was born in 1869 in Porbandar, a small town on the coast of Gujarat. He grew up among Hindus, Jains and Muslims, and learned early about ahimsa, not harming living things, from the Jain tradition of Mahavira.

He was a shy student. In 1888 he sailed to London to study law. Back in India, he was so nervous in his first court case that he could not speak and sat down. In 1893, he took a job with an Indian firm in South Africa.

The night on the platform

In South Africa, Indians faced harsh discrimination. On a train to Pretoria in 1893, Gandhi refused to leave a first-class carriage for a white passenger and was thrown off at Pietermaritzburg station.

He spent the night shivering in the waiting room and decided to fight the injustice. He stayed 21 years. There he developed satyagraha, leading Indians to burn their identity passes and court arrest. He went to jail several times, but won some changes in the law.

The spinning wheel and the march

Gandhi came home in 1915. In 1917, he helped indigo farmers in Champaran, Bihar, who were being forced to grow the crop. It was his first big campaign in India.

He asked Indians to stop buying British cloth and spin their own thread on a charkha, a spinning wheel, to make khadi. Spinning became a daily act of protest. In 1930 he led the Salt March to the sea at Dandi to defy the British salt law. In 1942 he called on the British to "Quit India".

Fasting and prison

Gandhi often used fasting as a protest, refusing food to put pressure on opponents or to stop violence among his own people. In all, he spent years of his life in prison in South Africa and India.

He also had critics. B.R. Ambedkar thought Gandhi did too little to end caste discrimination, and the two clashed in 1932 over separate electorates for Dalits. Historians have criticised some of his early comments about Black Africans in South Africa. Revolutionaries thought his non-violence was too slow.

The last days

India became free on 15 August 1947, but Partition brought terrible violence between Hindus, Muslims and Sikhs. Gandhi did not celebrate. He was in Kolkata, fasting and praying for peace.

On 30 January 1948, on his way to a prayer meeting in Delhi, he was shot dead by Nathuram Godse, a Hindu extremist who opposed his efforts for Hindu–Muslim unity. His birthday, 2 October, is a national holiday in India, and the United Nations marks it as the International Day of Non-Violence.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Gandhi won India's independence on his own.

Truth: Gandhi was the best-known leader, but millions of people and many leaders with different ideas took part. Some, like Bhagat Singh and Subhas Chandra Bose, chose very different methods.

Try this

Try a "satyagraha of small things" for a day. Pick one rule you think is unfair at home or school, and instead of shouting, write a calm, polite letter explaining why and suggesting a fair change. Give it to the right person and see what happens.
