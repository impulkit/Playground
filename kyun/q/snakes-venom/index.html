How does snake venom work?

A snake's venom is really a kind of spit, turned over millions of years into one of nature's most powerful mixtures.

The short answer

Snake venom is a mixture of proteins made in special glands in a snake's head. These glands are changed versions of the glands that make spit. When a venomous snake bites, muscles squeeze the glands and push venom through hollow or grooved teeth called fangs, into the prey.

Different venoms attack different parts of the body. Some block the signals from nerves to muscles, so the prey can't move or breathe. Others stop blood from clotting, damage blood vessels or break down flesh. Many venoms do several of these things at once.

Snakes use venom mainly to catch and kill prey quickly, without a dangerous struggle. It also starts to digest the meal from the inside. Biting people is a last defence.

Surprising but true

A medicine for high blood pressure, used by millions of people, was developed from the venom of a Brazilian viper.

Venom and poison

People often mix up the words, but they mean different things. Venom has to be injected, by a bite or a sting. Poison works when it is eaten, swallowed or touched. A cobra is venomous. A poison dart frog is poisonous.

Most snakes have no venom at all. Of nearly 4,000 kinds of snake, fewer than one in five are venomous, and only a few hundred are dangerous to people. India has over 300 kinds of snake, and most of them, like the rat snake that hunts mice in fields, are harmless.

India's Big Four

Most serious snakebites in India come from four snakes, known as the Big Four. The spectacled cobra and the common krait have venom that mainly attacks nerves, causing weakness and trouble breathing. Russell's viper and the saw-scaled viper have venom that mainly stops blood from clotting.

Kraits hunt at night and sometimes slip into houses. Their bite can be almost painless, so a sleeping person may not notice. Sleeping on a raised bed under a tucked-in mosquito net helps keep them away.

A large study published in 2020 estimated that snakebites killed about 58,000 people a year in India between 2000 and 2019, mostly farm workers in villages. That is around half of all snakebite deaths in the world. In the USA, rattlesnakes and their relatives bite several thousand people a year, but only about five die, partly because hospitals with antivenom are usually close by.

How antivenom saves lives

The only proven treatment for a serious snakebite is antivenom. To make it, horses are given tiny, safe doses of venom, slowly increased over weeks. Their immune system makes antibodies that grab hold of the venom. These antibodies are collected from the horses' blood and purified. The doses are kept small so the horses stay healthy.

Much of the venom comes from the Irula people near Chennai. Their snake catchers' cooperative, started in 1978, catches snakes, gently collects their venom and releases them a few weeks later. The Irula are so skilled that in 2017, two of their snake catchers were invited to Florida to help track down invasive giant pythons.

Antivenom made for the Big Four doesn't work against every snake. Scientists in India are now working on antivenoms for other dangerous species too.

Medicine from venom

Venom is a toolbox of powerful chemicals, and scientists study it for new medicines. In the 1970s, researchers working with the venom of a Brazilian pit viper, which makes blood pressure drop, developed one of the first modern drugs for high blood pressure. Millions of people have taken medicines based on it.

Other venoms are being tested to make blood clot, to ease pain and to fight disease. A bite that can kill may one day help save lives. And if anyone is bitten, the best help is always calm, stillness and a fast trip to a hospital.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You should cut a snakebite and suck out the venom.

Truth: Cutting and sucking don't remove venom and can cause infection and more damage. So can tying a tight band. Keep the person calm and still, remove rings or bangles, and get them to a hospital with antivenom quickly.
