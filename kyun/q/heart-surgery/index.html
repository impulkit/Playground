How do doctors fix hearts?

One of the most important heart operations ever was worked out by Vivien Thomas, a lab technician who never went to medical school.

The short answer

Your heart is a muscle about the size of your fist. It beats around 1 lakh times a day, pushing blood through four chambers and out to the body. It can go wrong in a few main ways. The pipes that feed it can get blocked. Its valves can leak or stick. Its electrical rhythm can go haywire, or a baby can be born with a part shaped wrongly.

Doctors have a fix for each. Some need only a thin tube slid in through a blood vessel. Others need open-heart surgery, where the chest is opened and a machine keeps the patient alive while the heart is still. When a heart is too damaged to repair, a transplant from a donor may be the answer. Almost none of this was possible before the 1940s.

Surprising but true

During many heart operations, the patient's heart is stopped completely for an hour or more while a machine does its pumping and breathing.

The blue babies

About 1 baby in every 100 is born with some kind of heart problem. Some are born with a heart that doesn't send enough blood to the lungs. Their skin looks bluish, so they were called "blue babies". In the early 1940s, a doctor named Helen Taussig in Baltimore thought a new connection between two blood vessels might help.

Surgeon Alfred Blalock and his lab technician, Vivien Thomas, worked out how. Thomas, a Black man who had to give up his dream of college for lack of money, practised the operation on animals until it was reliable. In 1944, Blalock performed it on a tiny girl named Eileen Saxon, with Thomas standing behind him giving advice. She turned pink on the table. For many years Thomas got little credit, but Johns Hopkins later gave him an honorary doctorate.

Stopping the heart safely

Fixing a valve or a hole inside the heart is very hard while it beats and fills with blood. The answer was the heart-lung machine. It takes blood out of the body, adds oxygen, removes carbon dioxide and pumps it back in.

John Gibbon spent about 20 years building one in the USA. In 1953 he used it to close a hole in the heart of an 18-year-old woman, and she recovered. Today, surgeons often cool the heart and stop it with a special salty solution, then restart it when the repair is done. Patients usually go home within a week or two.

Bypasses, balloons and stents

The most common heart trouble in adults is a blocked coronary artery, one of the small pipes that feed the heart muscle itself. Fatty deposits build up inside it, as hard water leaves crust inside a kettle.

One fix is a bypass. The surgeon takes a spare piece of vein from the leg, or an artery from the chest wall. It is sewn around the blockage, like a new road built around a traffic jam. Many patients get three or four bypasses in one operation. A gentler fix is angioplasty, first done in Zurich in 1977. A tiny balloon is pushed up to the blockage and inflated. Often a little mesh tube called a stent is left in place to keep the artery open. The patient is usually awake throughout.

Keeping the beat

The heart has its own electrical system that tells it when to squeeze. If it slows down or skips, a pacemaker can help. This matchbox-sized device sits under the skin near the collarbone, with a wire to the heart, and sends small pulses to keep the rhythm steady.

The first implanted pacemaker went into a Swedish engineer named Arne Larsson in 1958. He needed many replacements over the years, and lived to 86, longer than both the doctor and the engineer who made his first one. In India, hospitals such as Narayana Health in Bengaluru have become known for doing heart surgery at much lower cost than many other countries.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In a heart transplant or bypass, doctors usually replace the whole heart with an artificial one.

Truth: Most heart operations repair the person's own heart, such as fixing a valve or adding new pipes around blocked arteries. Transplants use a donated human heart. Fully artificial hearts exist but are rare and are mostly used for a short time while someone waits for a donor.

Try this

Find your pulse on the inside of your wrist with two fingers. Count the beats for 15 seconds and multiply by four. Then do 30 star jumps and count again. Your heart is a pump made of muscle, and you can feel it speed up to send more blood to your legs.
