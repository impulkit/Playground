What is a veena?

The goddess Saraswati is almost always shown holding one. It is carved from a single tree and its notes can slide like a voice.

The short answer

The veena is a large string instrument from India. The kind most people mean today, the Saraswati veena, is used in South Indian Carnatic music. It has a big round wooden body, a long neck with 24 frets, and a second, smaller round shape near the top.

The player sits cross-legged with the veena held across the body, the big bowl resting on the floor or lap. She plucks the strings with her right hand and presses them against the frets with her left. Its deep, rich voice can slide smoothly from note to note, almost like singing.

Surprising but true

The word "vina" is so old that it once meant almost any string instrument, including the harp shown on a Gupta emperor's gold coin.

Carved from a tree

A Saraswati veena is usually made from jackfruit wood. Its main bowl, the kudam, is often hollowed from a single block. The neck, frets and decorations are added by hand. The town of Thanjavur in Tamil Nadu is especially famous for veena makers, and its veenas have special legal protection as a regional craft.

The frets are thin brass strips set into a ridge of black wax. Makers move them, little by little, until each note sounds right. A finished veena is about 1.2 metres long, roughly as long as a ten-year-old is tall.

Seven strings with different jobs

A veena has four main strings for playing melody. Three thinner strings run along the side. These are plucked with the little finger to mark the rhythm and to give a steady background note, called a drone.

The hollow wooden body works like a loudspeaker. The vibrating strings shake the wood, and the wood pushes far more air than a string alone could, making the sound fuller.

Notes that bend

Carnatic music is full of slides, wiggles and shakes between notes, called gamakas. A singer does them with the voice. A veena player does them by pulling the string sideways across a fret, which tightens it and raises the note.

Because the frets are smooth and set fairly high, the player can bend a single note up by several steps. This is one reason the veena is so close to the human voice, and why many great Carnatic singers also learned the veena. It suits the style of Carnatic music perfectly.

An instrument for a goddess

In Hindu tradition, Saraswati, the goddess of learning and music, is shown playing the veena. The sage Narada also carries one in many stories. The painter Raja Ravi Varma made a famous picture of Saraswati with her veena, copied in prints across India.

The word itself is ancient. In old texts, vina meant many kinds of string instruments, including harps. A gold coin of the Gupta emperor Samudragupta, from about 1,700 years ago, shows him seated and playing a harp-like vina. See the Gupta age.

Cousins in the north

North India has its own veena, the rudra veena. It has a long tube of wood or bamboo with two large gourds underneath, and makes a deep, slow sound. It is used in dhrupad, one of the oldest styles of Hindustani music.

Very few musicians play the rudra veena today, and teachers worry it could fade away. The Saraswati veena, though, is still learned by thousands of children in South India and in Indian communities from Chennai to New Jersey.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The veena and the sitar are basically the same instrument.

Truth: They are different. The veena is mainly played in South Indian music and has fixed frets set in wax, while the sitar belongs to North Indian music, has curved movable frets and many extra strings that ring on their own.

Try this

Stretch a rubber band around an open box and pluck it. Now, while it is still ringing, press down on one side of the band and push it slightly sideways. Listen to the note rise. That bend is the same idea a veena player uses.
