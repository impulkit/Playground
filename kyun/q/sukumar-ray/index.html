Who was Sukumar Ray, India's master of nonsense verse?

Sukumar Ray invented a creature that was half duck and half porcupine, and gave it a name to match.

The short answer

Sukumar Ray was a Bengali poet, writer and illustrator who lived from 1887 to 1923. He is best known for Abol Tabol, a book of nonsense poems full of strange creatures, odd characters and invented words. For over a century, Bengali children have learned these poems by heart, often before they can read.

Ray is often compared to Britain's Edward Lear and Lewis Carroll, who also wrote nonsense. Like them, he made up words that sound like they ought to mean something, and he drew his own pictures. He died young, at 35, but his rhymes are still recited at school functions and quoted by grown-ups across Bengal.

Surprising but true

Sukumar Ray's most famous book, Abol Tabol, came out in 1923, just days after he died at the age of 35.

A house full of ink

Ray was born in Calcutta, into a family of writers, artists and printers. His father, Upendrakishore Ray Chowdhury, wrote children's stories, played the violin, studied astronomy and ran a printing business. He was one of the first in India to experiment with halftone printing, a way of printing photos and shaded pictures using tiny dots.

Sukumar studied physics and chemistry, then went to England from 1911 to 1913 to learn the latest printing technology. When he returned, he joined the family business.

In 1913, his father started a children's magazine called Sandesh. After Upendrakishore died in 1915, Sukumar took it over. He filled its pages with his own poems, stories, drawings and articles.

Ducks, porcupines and a crying cat

Abol Tabol, roughly "gibberish" or "topsy-turvy", collects his best-known poems. In "Khichuri", animals get mashed together: a duck and porcupine become a hanshjaru, a heron and tortoise a bakachhap.

Other poems introduce a man who wears his moustache as a sign of dignity and panics when he thinks it has been stolen, and a kingdom run by twenty-one ridiculous laws. Ray drew the pictures for the book himself, in pen and ink, and his creatures look every bit as odd as they sound. The rhythm makes them fun to chant aloud, and the jokes often have a sting. Officials, know-it-alls and stuffy rules are frequent targets.

He also wrote HaJaBaRaLa, a prose story in which a boy dozes off on a hot afternoon. His handkerchief turns into a cat, a crow does strange sums, and he ends up in a very odd courtroom. It is often compared to Alice in Wonderland.

A short life

In 1921, Sukumar's only son, Satyajit, was born. Soon after, Sukumar fell ill with kala-azar, a disease spread by the bite of sandflies. There was no good cure then. He kept writing and drawing in bed, and prepared Abol Tabol for print. He died in September 1923, and the book was published days later.

Sandesh faded after his death. Decades later, in 1961, Satyajit Ray, by then a world-famous film-maker, revived the magazine and wrote his own detective and science stories for it. In 1987 he made a documentary film about his father.

Nonsense across languages

Nonsense is hard to translate, because so much of it depends on sound. Even so, Ray's poems have been translated into English, most famously by Sukanta Chaudhuri, and into other Indian languages.

Readers who enjoy Dr. Seuss in the USA often find a kindred spirit in Sukumar Ray. Both played with rhythm, invented words and drew odd creatures. Ray did it more than a decade before Dr. Seuss published his first children's book, in Bengali, for a magazine read by children in Calcutta.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Nonsense verse is just silly words thrown together, with no skill.

Truth: Good nonsense is hard to write. Sukumar Ray used careful rhythm, rhyme and wordplay, and many of his poems quietly poke fun at pompous adults, rules and officials.
