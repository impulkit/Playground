What is Beijing's Forbidden City?

For almost 500 years, ordinary people were not allowed inside this giant palace at the heart of Beijing, home to 24 emperors of China.

The short answer

The Forbidden City is the old imperial palace in the middle of Beijing, the capital of China. It was built between 1406 and 1420 for the Yongle Emperor of the Ming dynasty. It was home to 24 emperors, their families and thousands of servants until the last emperor gave up the throne in 1912.

It was called "forbidden" because no one could enter or leave without the emperor's permission. Behind a moat and walls about 10 metres high, it covers about 72 hectares, roughly the size of 100 football fields, with around 980 buildings. Since 1925 it has been the Palace Museum, and it is now one of the most visited museums in the world.

Surprising but true

Records from the Ming dynasty describe workers moving giant carved stones to the palace by sliding them over roads of ice in winter.

A palace on a line

The whole palace is laid out along a straight north–south line. The most important halls stand on that line, one behind another, and the buildings on the left and right mirror each other. This symmetry was meant to show order and harmony, with the emperor at the centre of everything.

Visitors entered from the south through the Meridian Gate. Beyond it lie three great halls on raised marble terraces. The biggest is the Hall of Supreme Harmony, where emperors held major ceremonies on a golden throne. The private living quarters lay further north, and at the very back is an imperial garden of rocks, old trees and pavilions.

Built by a million hands

Historians estimate that around 100,000 skilled craftsmen and perhaps a million labourers worked on the palace. The finest timber came from forests in south-west China, thousands of kilometres away. Huge logs were floated down rivers and could take years to arrive. Special bricks were fired in kilns near Suzhou, and they were so smooth they became known as "golden bricks".

Some stone carvings weighed hundreds of tonnes. Records describe workers in winter pouring water on roads so it froze, then dragging the stones on sledges over the ice.

Symbols everywhere

Almost nothing in the palace is decorated by accident. Yellow roof tiles were reserved for the emperor. Dragons, the symbol of the emperor, appear on roofs, walls, thrones and robes. Nine was an imperial number, so the big doors have rows of nine golden knobs, nine across and nine down.

Along the corners of each roof sits a parade of small animal figures. The more figures, the more important the building. The Hall of Supreme Harmony is the only one with ten.

The emperor who sent the treasure fleet

The Yongle Emperor, who built the palace, also sent the admiral Zheng He on giant voyages across the Indian Ocean. His fleets visited Calicut in Kerala and sailed as far as East Africa, bringing back gifts that included a giraffe.

When the last emperor, Puyi, gave up the throne in 1912, China became a republic. He was allowed to stay inside the palace for a few more years, until 1924. The palace then opened as a museum, and today its collection holds more than 1.8 million objects, from bronzes and paintings to clocks given to emperors by European visitors. Many of the finest treasures were shipped away during China's civil war, and they are now in the National Palace Museum in Taipei. A short drive north of the city, crowds now walk the Great Wall, another reminder of how China's rulers once guarded their land.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Forbidden City has exactly 9,999 and a half rooms.

Truth: This is a popular legend, linked to the idea that only heaven could have 10,000 rooms. Surveys count far fewer, around 8,700 room spaces in about 980 buildings.

Try this

Draw a palace on squared paper. Draw one straight line down the middle from top to bottom, then put the most important room on that line and make the left and right halves mirror images. You have just used the same idea as the Forbidden City's builders.
