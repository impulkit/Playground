How did Hollywood become the home of movies?

About 120 years ago, Hollywood was a quiet farm town of a few hundred people, surrounded by lemon and orange groves.

The short answer

Hollywood is a district of Los Angeles, in southern California. In the early 1900s, America's film companies were mostly based in New York and New Jersey. Over about ten years, many moved across the country to Hollywood and the towns around it.

They came for three main reasons. Southern California had sunshine nearly every day, which early cameras badly needed. Land was cheap, so studios could build huge outdoor sets. And within a day's drive there were beaches, deserts, mountains and forests, so one place could look like many. By the 1920s, "Hollywood" had become a name for the whole American film industry.

Surprising but true

The famous Hollywood sign was put up in 1923 as an advertisement for houses. It first read HOLLYWOODLAND, and the last four letters came down in 1949.

From orange groves to studios

In 1900, Hollywood was a small farming town of a few hundred people, with lemon and orange groves. In 1910 it joined the city of Los Angeles, mainly to get a reliable water supply.

That same year, the director D. W. Griffith shot a short film called In Old California in the area. In 1911 the Nestor company opened Hollywood's first film studio, in an old roadside tavern on Sunset Boulevard. Within a few years, dozens of companies had followed, and the sleepy town was crowded with cowboys, cameras and people looking for work as extras.

Running from Edison

Thomas Edison's company held many of the key patents for making and showing films. In 1908 Edison and other big companies formed the Motion Picture Patents Company. It demanded fees from anyone using their kind of camera or film, and took rule-breakers to court.

Some independent filmmakers found California handy because it was far from New Jersey, where Edison was based. Historians still argue about how much this mattered. The weather and the land were probably the bigger reasons, but escaping the patent fights helped.

The studio years

By the 1920s a few giant studios ran Hollywood, among them Paramount, MGM, Warner Bros. and Universal. Each worked like a factory. Writers, actors, set builders and camera crews were on long contracts, and a big studio could finish a film every week or two.

In 1927 Warner Bros. released The Jazz Singer, the first feature film with synchronised talking scenes. Silent stars who had strange voices or strong accents sometimes lost their careers almost overnight. Two years later, in 1929, the first Academy Awards were handed out at a dinner that lasted about 15 minutes.

A sign for selling houses

In 1923, property developers put up giant letters on a hillside reading HOLLYWOODLAND to advertise new homes. Each letter was about 15 metres tall, roughly as high as a five-storey building, and was lit with thousands of bulbs.

The sign fell into ruin over the years. In 1949 the last four letters were removed, leaving HOLLYWOOD. In 1978 the old wooden letters were replaced with steel ones paid for by donors, each sponsoring one letter.

Hollywood today

Studios still have offices in and around Hollywood, but filming happens all over the world. Computers now create cities, storms and creatures that would once have needed huge sets, as special effects explains. Streaming services also make films without ever showing them in cinemas.

India's film industries, led by Bollywood and the southern film industries, make more films each year than Hollywood does. Hollywood films, though, still earn the most money worldwide, and those white letters on the hill still mean "the movies" to people almost everywhere.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Most Hollywood films are still made in Hollywood.

Truth: Today many "Hollywood" films are shot in places such as Georgia, Canada, the UK and New Zealand, where costs are lower or the scenery fits. The word now means the American film business as a whole, not just one neighbourhood.

Try this

Use a torch and a toy or doll in a dark room to test lighting like an early film crew. Light the toy from above, from the side and from below. Notice how each one changes the toy's mood. Filmmakers still use these same tricks.
