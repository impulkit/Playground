What is deja vu, and why does it happen?

Some scientists now think the spooky "I've been here before" feeling is your brain catching its own mistake.

The short answer

Deja vu is the sudden, strong feeling that you've lived this exact moment before, even though you know you can't have. It usually lasts a few seconds and then fades. The name is French for "already seen". Surveys suggest about two out of three people have felt it at least once.

Nobody knows for sure what causes it. Most scientists think it's a hiccup in how your brain handles memory. A part of the brain shouts "familiar!" while another part knows that's not true. The gap between those two messages is the strange, floaty feeling.

Surprising but true

Scientists who studied the opposite feeling, when a common word suddenly looks strange after you repeat it many times, won a joke science prize, the Ig Nobel, in 2023.

Familiar, but from where?

One explanation comes from the psychologist Anne Cleary in the USA. She showed people scenes in a computer game, then later showed them new scenes with the same layout but different objects. A bowling alley might be arranged exactly like an earlier junkyard. People often felt deja vu in those new scenes, without remembering the old one.

Her idea is that your brain notices a pattern it has met before but can't find the original memory. So you get familiarity with no label attached. Walking into a new café that's arranged like your aunt's living room could be enough.

A brain checking its own files

In 2016 a team at the University of St Andrews in Scotland created a kind of deja vu in the lab. They read people lists of words like "bed", "pillow", "night" and "dream", but never "sleep". Later, people felt they had heard "sleep", while also knowing they hadn't.

Brain scans showed that the busiest parts were in the front of the brain, which helps make decisions, not the memory areas. The scientists think deja vu may be a sign of a healthy checking system. Your brain spots a false sense of familiarity and flags it as an error.

Why do optical illusions fool us?Your eyes don't send your brain a photograph. They send clues, and your brain guesses the rest. Illusions catch it guessing wrong.

Who gets it most

Deja vu is most common in teenagers and young adults, and becomes rarer as people get older. Being tired or stressed seems to make it more likely. Studies have also found that people who travel a lot report it more often, perhaps because they meet more new places that echo old ones.

For a small number of people, frequent deja vu can be part of a medical condition called epilepsy, when brain signals briefly misfire. If deja vu happens very often or comes with other odd feelings, it's worth telling a parent or doctor. For almost everyone, though, it is harmless.

Ideas that didn't work out

One older idea said deja vu happens when information reaches the brain by two routes, one a split second slower than the other. The second copy arrives and feels like a memory of the first. It sounds neat, but there's a problem. People who have been blind since birth also report deja vu, set off by sounds, smells or touch, so it can't depend on signals from the eyes arriving out of step.

Doctors operating on the brains of people with epilepsy have also caused deja vu by gently stimulating parts of the temporal lobes, areas near the ears that deal with memory and familiarity. That helps scientists narrow down where the feeling starts, even if they can't yet explain why.

The opposite feeling

There's a mirror image called jamais vu, French for "never seen". It's when something completely familiar suddenly seems strange, like your own name or a word you write every day. In 2023 researchers Chris Moulin and Akira O'Connor shared an Ig Nobel Prize, a jokey award for science that makes people laugh and then think, for studying it.

The word "deja vu" itself is a traveller, borrowed from French like ballet and café. English is full of words borrowed from other languages, and once you start spotting them, you'll feel like you've seen them everywhere before.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Deja vu means you have seen the future or lived the moment in a past life.

Truth: There is no scientific evidence for either. The best explanations involve memory and familiarity. In tests, people feeling deja vu can't predict what comes next any better than chance.

Try this

Try making the opposite feeling, called jamais vu. Write an ordinary word like "door" thirty times in a row, saying it in your head each time. Many people find that by the end the word looks odd, as if it might be spelled wrong. Your brain has stopped recognising something it knows perfectly well.
