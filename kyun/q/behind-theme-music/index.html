Why are some tunes so catchy?

Some of the scariest film music ever written is just two notes played over and over. Once you hear the shark theme from Jaws, it is hard to get it out of your head.

The short answer

Some tunes are catchy because they fit how our brains like to remember things. They tend to be simple, repeat a lot and follow a familiar shape, usually rising and then falling like a hill. But they also have one small surprise, such as an unexpected jump or a quirky rhythm, that makes the brain pay attention.

When a tune is easy to remember but slightly unusual, the brain keeps replaying it. Scientists call a song that loops in your head an earworm. Most people get one at least once a week. Film composers, advert makers and pop songwriters use these tricks on purpose.

Surprising but true

The famous Nokia ringtone, heard billions of times on phones, is a few bars from a guitar piece written in 1902 by the Spanish composer Francisco Tárrega.

Two notes and a shark

In 1975, the composer John Williams wrote the music for Jaws, a film about a giant shark. The director, Steven Spielberg, reportedly laughed when he first heard it. It was just two low notes, side by side, played slowly and then faster and faster.

But it worked. The two notes sound like a heartbeat speeding up or something approaching. Simple music is easy to recognise in a split second. Decades later, people still hum those two notes as a joke when someone creeps up behind them.

What makes a tune stick

In 2016, researchers in the UK asked 3,000 people which songs got stuck in their heads most often. Then they studied the music itself.

The catchiest songs usually had a fairly fast beat. Their melodies followed shapes common in Western pop, rising then falling, but they also had unusual intervals, meaning surprise jumps between notes. Lady Gaga's "Bad Romance" topped the list.

Repetition matters too. The more often you hear a song, the more firmly your memory stores it. A chorus repeated four times in a song, and the song played every day on the radio, adds up fast.

What does music do to your brain?When a favourite song reaches its best bit, your brain releases dopamine, a chemical linked to pleasure. It even starts releasing it a few seconds early, in anticipation.

Why tunes get stuck

An earworm is usually only a short piece of a song, often 10 to 20 seconds of the chorus. It loops because the brain likes to finish things. If you hear part of a tune and it stops, your mind may keep playing it, trying to complete it.

Earworms often appear when your mind is relaxed or doing something easy, like walking or brushing teeth. They can also be set off by a word, a smell or a place linked to the song.

Jingles and signature tunes

Advertisers know catchy tunes sell things. A short jingle linked to a brand name can stay with people for years. Many Indians can still hum old TV jingles from their childhood, along with the gentle signature tune of Doordarshan.

TV shows, sports events and news channels all use short theme tunes for the same reason. Hear a few notes and you know instantly what is coming. That's also why ringtones work. The Nokia tune, taken from a 1902 guitar waltz by Francisco Tárrega, became one of the most-played pieces of music in history.

Film songs that never leave

In India, film songs are the national soundtrack. Composers such as A. R. Rahman build songs with strong hooks, memorable rhythms and surprising sounds. Many songs are heard first in a film, then at weddings, festivals and on phones for years.

A good hook in a film song works just like a hook in a story. It grabs you early and won't let go.

How to get rid of one

Scientists have a few tips. Chewing gum may help, because it keeps your mouth too busy to silently sing along. Listening to the whole song all the way through can satisfy the brain's need to finish it. Some people swap in a different, "cure" song instead.

Music can do stranger things than loop in your head. At the best moment of a song, some people feel a shiver run down their spine, a reaction scientists call music goosebumps.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Only bad or annoying songs get stuck in your head.

Truth: Songs people love get stuck just as often as songs they dislike. In studies, the most commonly reported earworms are popular, well-liked songs that people have heard many times.

Try this

Run a catchiness test. Hum the first few notes of five tunes everyone in your family knows, such as a film song, an advert jingle or a school bell. Ask each person which one is still stuck in their head an hour later. Then look at the winner: is it short, repeated, or does it have a surprise jump?
