How did Dashrath Manjhi cut through a mountain?

For 22 years, a poor labourer in Bihar swung a hammer at a rocky ridge. When he stopped, a 55 km trip had shrunk to about 15 km.

The short answer

Dashrath Manjhi cut through the mountain with a hammer, a chisel and a crowbar. He did it alone, a few chips at a time, from around 1960 to 1982.

The result is a gap through a rocky ridge near his village, Gehlaur, in Bihar's Gaya district. It is about 110 m long, roughly the length of a football field, and about 9 m wide. In places it slices almost 8 m down into the rock, which is as deep as a two-storey house is tall. People in India now call him the Mountain Man.

Surprising but true

Dashrath Manjhi once walked from Bihar to Delhi, mostly along railway tracks, to ask the government for help with a road.

A ridge in the way

Gehlaur sat on one side of a long ridge of the Gehlour hills. Schools, markets and the nearest hospital were on the other side, near the town of Wazirganj. To reach them, people either climbed a narrow, dangerous track over the top or walked the long way round.

Manjhi belonged to the Musahar community, one of the poorest groups in Bihar. He worked as a labourer, often breaking stone and digging for others. So he knew how rock cracks and where it is weak.

Why he started

The story told by Manjhi and his family goes like this. His wife, Falguni Devi, was crossing the ridge with water or food for him when she slipped and was badly hurt. Some accounts say she later died because help was too far away. Others say she fell ill and died some years after.

Whatever the exact order, Manjhi decided nobody else should suffer because of that ridge. He sold his goats to buy tools. Then he started hitting the rock.

Twenty-two years of hammering

At first, villagers called him mad. He worked in the early morning and after his paid jobs, in the burning summer and the cold winter. A crowbar let him prise loose big slabs, the same idea as using a lever to lift a heavy box. The chisel did the rest.

There were no explosives and no machines. Picture moving a pile of rock bigger than a school bus, then doing it again, and again, hundreds of times. Slowly, a gap opened. Some villagers began to bring him food and help.

By 1982, the path was finished. The walk to Wazirganj that used to take most of a day could now be done in a couple of hours.

The walk to Delhi

Manjhi kept pushing for a proper road and other help for his village. He once walked to Delhi, mostly along the railway line, to take his request to the government. That is more than 1,000 km.

Officials were slow. Manjhi died in 2007, and a proper paved road through his cut came only years later. It is now known as the Dashrath Manjhi Path. India Post issued a stamp in his honour in 2016, and a Hindi film, Manjhi: The Mountain Man, told his story in 2015.

One man and one mountain

Modern tunnel machines can chew through rock at several metres a day. Manjhi managed a few centimetres. What he lacked in power he made up for in years.

Today, visitors come to Gehlaur to walk through the gap and touch the chisel marks still visible on its walls. A memorial to Manjhi stands close to the path he cut. Children from Gehlaur now take the short way to schools and markets their grandparents could reach only after a long, hot walk around the hills.

His story is often told alongside another Indian who refused to give up. Jadav Payeng planted trees on a bare sandbank in the Brahmaputra for decades, until the Forest Man had grown a jungle big enough for elephants and tigers.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Dashrath Manjhi dug a tunnel through the mountain.

Truth: He cut an open path, more like a deep trench or gap through the top of a rocky ridge. You walk through it under the open sky, with rock walls on both sides.

Try this

Feel what slow, steady work can do. Fill a cup with dry rice and move it grain by grain to another cup for two minutes. Count the grains, then work out how long the whole cup would take. Manjhi's job was like that, every day, for 22 years.
