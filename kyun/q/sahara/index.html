How big is the Sahara?

The Sahara is nearly three times the size of India, and only about a quarter of it is covered in sand dunes.

The short answer

The Sahara covers about 9.2 million square kilometres of North Africa. That is nearly as big as the whole of the United States, and almost three times the size of India. It stretches about 4,800 km from the Atlantic coast in the west to the Red Sea in the east, which is further than the whole length of India from Kashmir to Kanyakumari.

Parts of more than ten countries lie in it, including Egypt, Libya, Algeria, Morocco, Mali, Niger, Chad and Sudan. It is the largest hot desert on Earth. In some places, years can pass without a single shower of rain. Yet the Sahara is not empty. Millions of people live in its towns and oases, along with foxes, lizards, beetles and hardy plants that can wait out long droughts.

Surprising but true

Around 8,000 years ago the Sahara was green, with lakes full of hippos and crocodiles, and people painted pictures of swimmers on its rocks.

Not just sand

When people think of the Sahara, they think of golden dunes. Some dunes really are huge, taller than a 50-storey building. Wind piles them up and slowly pushes them across the land, grain by grain, as you can read in desert sand.

But most of the Sahara is not sand. It is rocky plateaus, plains of gravel, dry riverbeds and mountains. The Tibesti Mountains in Chad rise to about 3,400 m and sometimes get snow. Temperatures swing hard, too. A day can reach well over 40 °C, and the clear night sky lets the heat escape so fast that it can drop close to freezing before dawn.

When the Sahara was green

The Sahara has not always been a desert. Between roughly 11,000 and 5,000 years ago, the monsoon rains reached much further north. Grasslands and lakes spread across the region. Hippos, crocodiles, giraffes and elephants lived there, and so did people, who herded cattle and fished.

They left paintings and carvings on rocks, showing cattle, hunters and even people who appear to be swimming. The change happened because Earth's orbit wobbles slowly over thousands of years, shifting where the rain falls. As the rains moved south, the lakes dried up and people moved away, many towards the Nile.

Living in the desert

People still live in the Sahara, often near oases, where underground water reaches the surface and date palms grow. The Tuareg, a people of the central Sahara, have crossed it for centuries with camel caravans, trading salt, dates and cloth. Many Tuareg men wear a long blue or indigo veil wrapped around the head.

Camels suit the desert well. They can go days without drinking, then gulp down a huge amount of water in minutes. Their wide, padded feet stop them sinking in sand. Today trucks and four-wheel drives carry much of the trade, but camels are still used in many places.

How do camels survive the desert?A thirsty camel can drink about 100 litres of water in a quarter of an hour, about five big buckets' worth.

Dust that crosses an ocean

Every year, strong winds lift enormous amounts of Saharan dust high into the air. NASA satellites have tracked these clouds as they cross the Atlantic Ocean. Some of the dust falls on the Caribbean and the southern United States, turning sunsets hazy.

Some of it lands on the Amazon rainforest. The dust carries phosphorus, a nutrient that the Amazon's rain-soaked soil keeps losing. So in a strange way, the biggest hot desert on Earth helps feed the biggest rainforest. The Nile, meanwhile, flows along the Sahara's eastern edge, a ribbon of green fields between two walls of sand.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Sahara is the biggest desert in the world.

Truth: It is the biggest hot desert. Antarctica and the Arctic count as deserts too, because so little snow or rain falls there, and both are larger than the Sahara.
