How do skyscrapers stay standing?

For a very tall building, the biggest enemy isn't its own weight. It's the wind.

The short answer

A skyscraper stays up because of three things working together. A skeleton of steel or reinforced concrete carries the weight down through the building. Deep foundations spread that weight into the ground. And the design lets the tower resist, and sometimes soak up, the sideways push of wind and earthquakes.

Weight presses straight down, and steel and concrete are very good at carrying that. The harder problem is wind, which pushes from the side and tries to bend the tower over like a stalk of grass. The taller a building is, the stronger the wind it meets and the more it can sway. So engineers spend much of their effort making tall buildings stiff, well shaped and anchored deep.

Surprising but true

Near the top of Taipei 101 in Taiwan hangs a steel ball weighing about 660 tonnes. It swings against the wind to stop the tower from swaying too much.

Skeletons of steel

Old tall buildings had thick stone or brick walls holding up the floors above. The taller the building, the thicker the walls at the bottom had to be, until there was hardly room inside.

In 1885, the Home Insurance Building in Chicago used a frame of iron and steel to carry its weight instead. It was only ten storeys tall, but its walls no longer had to hold everything up. Many people call it the first skyscraper. Steel frames, along with safe lifts, let buildings climb higher and higher. New York's Empire State Building, finished in 1931, reached 102 floors.

A strong spine and deep feet

Many modern towers have a thick concrete core in the middle, where the lifts and stairs run. It works like a spine, stiffening the whole building. Columns around the outside help carry the floors. Concrete is very strong when squeezed but cracks when stretched, so steel rods are set inside it. This reinforced concrete combines the best of both materials.

Below the ground, foundations reach down into firm soil or rock. The Burj Khalifa in Dubai, 828 metres tall, stands on a thick concrete slab resting on nearly 200 concrete piles. They reach more than 40 metres into the ground, about as deep as a 13-storey building is tall.

Fighting the wind

Wind doesn't just push. As it flows around a tall tower, it can peel off in swirls, first on one side, then the other, rocking the building in a steady rhythm. To stop this, engineers shape towers carefully. The Burj Khalifa gets narrower in steps as it rises, so the wind never finds one steady pattern to push against.

Some towers carry dampers near the top. In Taipei 101, a 660-tonne steel ball hangs on cables. When the tower sways one way, the ball's weight swings the other, calming the motion. Visitors can see it from a viewing floor, painted gold. During typhoons it has been recorded swinging by a metre or more, while the tower around it moves far less.

Ground that shakes

In places with earthquakes, such as Japan, California and parts of north India, towers must survive the ground moving beneath them. Engineers design them to bend without breaking, using steel that stretches before it snaps and joints that can flex.

Some buildings sit on rubber-and-steel pads called base isolators. When the ground shakes, the pads move and the building above rocks much more gently. The tallest towers in India are in Mumbai, where dozens of residential towers now rise above 200 metres. Each one has to meet India's earthquake and wind codes before it can be built.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Tall buildings stand perfectly still.

Truth: Skyscrapers are designed to bend a little in the wind, like a tree. The top of a very tall tower can sway by tens of centimetres or more in a strong storm. A tower that couldn't flex at all would be more likely to crack.

Try this

Build a tower from drinking straws, paper and tape, as tall as you can. Test it by blowing on it, or by placing it in front of a table fan on low. Then build a second tower with a wider base and criss-crossing straws in triangles, and compare how much each one wobbles.
