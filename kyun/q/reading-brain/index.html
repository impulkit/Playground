What happens in your brain when you read?

Your brain was never built for reading. Writing is only about 5,000 years old, so every reader borrows brain parts that evolved to recognise faces and objects.

The short answer

When you read, light from the page or screen enters your eyes. Your brain spots the shapes of letters, groups them into words, links each word to its sound and meaning, and builds the sentence into an idea. All this happens in a fraction of a second, again and again, for every word.

Scientists can now watch it happen using brain scanners. They have found that reading uses a network of brain areas, mostly on the left side, that work together like a relay team.

Surprising but true

When you read, your eyes don't glide smoothly along the line. They jump in quick hops, and you only take in words during the tiny pauses between jumps.

A brain that learned a new trick

Humans have been talking for perhaps a hundred thousand years or more. But writing began only about 5,000 years ago, in places such as Mesopotamia and Egypt. That is far too recent for evolution to have built a special reading part of the brain.

So how do we read? The French scientist Stanislas Dehaene calls it "neuronal recycling". A patch of the brain that once only helped us recognise faces, tools and animals gets retrained to recognise letters. When a child learns to read, this patch slowly becomes a letter expert.

It takes years. A beginner sounds out each letter, c-a-t, slowly joining sounds into a word. With practice the brain stops spelling things out and recognises whole familiar words at a glance. That is why a seven-year-old reads aloud haltingly while an older sibling races through a comic, and why daily practice matters so much in the early years.

The letterbox

This patch is called the visual word form area. It sits low down on the left side of the brain, roughly behind your left ear. Some scientists nickname it the brain's letterbox.

It recognises a word whether it's in CAPITALS or small letters, handwritten or printed, in a newspaper or on a phone. In people who read Hindi, Tamil, English or Chinese, scans show the same area lighting up. Messages from it then travel to areas that handle sound and meaning. Every word you read sets off a quick chain of signals between millions of neurons.

Where did the alphabet come from?The letter A began as a picture of an ox's head. Turn a capital A upside down and you can still see the horns.

Eyes that jump

Your eyes don't scan smoothly. They make quick jumps called saccades, each about seven to nine letters long, then stop for about a quarter of a second. You only take in words during those stops. Short, common words like "a", "of" and "the" are often skipped completely.

Your brain glues these snapshots together so smoothly that reading feels continuous. A skilled adult reading silently manages about 200 to 300 words a minute.

The voice in your head

Many people hear a quiet inner voice as they read. Brain scans show that silent reading still switches on some of the areas used for speech. That is why tongue-twisters can feel awkward even when you read them in your head.

Reading also builds pictures. When you read about a kick, areas linked to movement become a little more active. When a story describes a smell, smell areas may join in too. Good books feel real partly because your brain is quietly acting them out.

When reading is harder

For some people, linking letters to sounds is much harder. This is called dyslexia, and by many estimates it affects roughly one person in ten to some degree. It has nothing to do with how clever someone is. Many scientists, artists and business leaders have dyslexia.

With the right teaching, people with dyslexia learn to read well. Some find audiobooks or special fonts helpful. People who are blind read with their fingers using Braille, and brain scans show their visual areas still get involved, even though no light reaches them. It's one more sign of how flexible the brain is.

Myth or fact?

Myth: With speed reading, you can read 1,000 words a minute and understand everything.

Truth: Studies find that reading much faster than normal means understanding and remembering much less. Skimming is useful for finding things, but it isn't the same as reading.

Try this

Ask a friend to read a paragraph silently while you watch their eyes from the side. Count how many jumps they make on one line. Then try reading a page held upside down and notice how much slower and harder it feels.
