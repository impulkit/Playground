Why does everyone have different fingerprints?

Your fingerprints were set months before you were born, and not even an identical twin has the same ones.

The short answer

Your fingertips are covered in fine raised lines called ridges. They began forming when you were a tiny baby in the womb, about three months into pregnancy. By about the sixth month, the pattern was fixed for life.

Your genes give a rough plan, which is why fingerprints in a family can share an overall shape. But the exact pattern depends on many tiny things: the shape of the fingertip, how fast the skin grows, the pressure around it and even the baby's position. Each fingertip forms in a slightly different setting, so the details come out differently. That's why no two fingers, even on the same hand, have ever been found to match.

Surprising but true

The world's first fingerprint bureau opened in Kolkata in 1897, and two Indian police officers worked out the system for sorting prints that the world then used for a century.

Loops, whorls and arches

Look closely at your fingertips and you'll see one of three main patterns. A loop curves in from one side and goes back out the same side, like a hairpin. A whorl makes circles or a spiral, like a tiny whirlpool. An arch flows from one side to the other with a little hump in the middle, like a wave.

Loops are the most common, found on roughly six fingers in ten. Whorls come next, and arches are rarest. If you cut a fingertip, the same pattern grows back as it heals, unless the cut is very deep.

A contract in Bengal

People pressed fingerprints into clay in ancient Babylon and China to mark business deals, but nobody used them scientifically until the 1800s. In 1858, a British official in Bengal named William Herschel asked a local businessman, Rajyadhar Konai, to press his inked hand onto a contract. He later found that the prints stayed the same over the years.

In 1880, a Scottish doctor working in Tokyo, Henry Faulds, suggested fingerprints could catch criminals. A few years later, Francis Galton studied thousands of prints and showed how varied they were.

The Kolkata system

The big problem was sorting. A police station with thousands of fingerprint cards needs a way to find a match fast. In the 1890s, in Bengal, two Indian police officers, Azizul Haque and Hem Chandra Bose, worked out a method of sorting prints by their patterns into groups. Their boss, Edward Henry, gave his name to it: the Henry system.

The world's first fingerprint bureau opened in Kolkata, then Calcutta, in 1897. Scotland Yard in London adopted the system in 1901, and versions were used across the world for about a hundred years. Today, computers do the matching, and India's Aadhaar system stores the fingerprints of more than a billion people.

What are they for?

For a long time, scientists assumed fingerprints helped us grip, like the tread on a tyre. But experiments in 2009 found that on smooth, dry surfaces, ridges actually reduce how much skin touches the object. They might help more on wet or rough surfaces.

Another idea is touch. When you slide your finger over a surface, the ridges vibrate. Nerve endings under the skin pick up those vibrations, which helps you feel fine textures, like the weave of a cloth. Koalas, which grip branches all day, have fingerprints so like ours that they could confuse an investigator. A very small number of people are born with no fingerprints at all, a rare condition passed down in families. Without prints, they can't use the sensor on a phone or a touchscreen lock.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Identical twins have identical fingerprints.

Truth: They don't. Fingerprints are shaped by genes and by tiny random differences as the skin grows in the womb. Twins' prints may look alike at a glance, but the details always differ.

Try this

Rub a soft pencil hard on a piece of paper to make a dark patch. Press your fingertip onto it, then onto a strip of clear sticky tape, and stick the tape on white paper. Look with a magnifying glass. Is your print a loop, a whorl or an arch? Compare all ten fingers and your family's prints.
