Why does Gujarat dance garba all night?

For nine nights every autumn, huge circles of dancers in Gujarat clap and spin around a clay pot with a lamp inside, often until well past midnight.

The short answer

Garba is a circle dance from Gujarat, performed during Navratri, the nine-night Hindu festival honouring the goddess Durga in her many forms. Dancers move around a clay pot with a lamp inside, clapping and turning in rhythm.

The dancing goes late for several reasons. Navratri is a night festival by tradition, with prayers, called aarti, in the evening. The nine nights are a time of devotion, but also of joy, music and meeting friends. Autumn nights are cool after a hot day. And once the drums get going and the circle speeds up, few people want to stop. In big cities, the music often runs past midnight, though local rules now set time limits for loudspeakers.

Surprising but true

In December 2023, UNESCO added garba to its list of the world's Intangible Cultural Heritage, alongside traditions like yoga and Kumbh Mela.

The pot at the centre

The word garba comes from garbha, the Sanskrit word for womb. The clay pot in the middle is called a garbo. It has holes punched all over it and a small lamp burning inside, so dots of light shine out in the dark.

Many people see the pot as a symbol of the body, the womb or the universe, and the flame as life and the goddess's power. Dancing in a circle around it is a form of worship. Circles can grow into rings within rings, hundreds of people moving together, always facing in towards the light.

Claps, spins and speed

A garba usually starts slow, with simple steps and two or three claps. As the drums and singers speed up, dancers add spins, bends and faster footwork. Popular step patterns have names like do taali, two claps, and teen taali, three claps.

The beat comes from the dhol and from singers, often with harmonium and, now, keyboards and electronic music. In Gujarat, live bands play for crowds of thousands. Learning to stay in step with everyone else is part of the fun. It is the same pull of rhythm that makes your feet tap to a song.

Mirror work and colour

Dancers dress up. Women often wear a chaniya choli, a wide, swirling skirt with a short blouse, covered in mirror work and embroidery from Kutch. Men wear a kediyu, a short pleated top, with a turban or cap. Many outfits use tie-dyed bandhani cloth.

Each of the nine nights has its own colour in many cities, and people try to match it. Shops and tailors in Gujarat stay busy for weeks before Navratri.

From villages to stadiums

Garba began as a dance by women in village courtyards and temple grounds. Over the last century, it moved into city squares, party plots and stadiums. Vadodara's big garba events draw tens of thousands of dancers on a single night. Rehearsal classes start months early. In Ahmedabad and Surat, garba classes fill school halls and rooftops on summer evenings as people learn the newest steps.

In 2023, UNESCO added "Garba of Gujarat" to its list of Intangible Cultural Heritage, which honours living traditions, not buildings. It was India's 15th entry on that list.

Garba around the world

Wherever Gujarati families have settled, garba has followed. New Jersey, Texas, London, Toronto and Nairobi all hold Navratri nights, some in hired sports halls packed with thousands. Many dancers there have never been to Gujarat, but they know every step.

During Navratri, the same nine nights are celebrated very differently across India. Bengal honours Durga with pandals and idols in Durga Puja, while Gujarat dances. And after nine nights of garba, many Gujarati families celebrate Dussehra, the tenth day, with a plate of crunchy fafda and syrupy jalebi, one of the best-loved pairings in Gujarati food.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Garba and dandiya are the same dance.

Truth: They are related but different. Garba is danced with hand claps and flowing turns, and was traditionally led by women. Dandiya raas is danced in pairs with decorated sticks that dancers strike together. Both are popular during Navratri.

Try this

Learn the basic two-clap garba step. Step to the right with your right foot, bring your left foot next to it and clap. Then step left and clap again, while slowly moving around in a circle with your family or friends. Play any garba song and try to keep in time as the beat speeds up.
