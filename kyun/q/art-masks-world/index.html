Why do cultures around the world make masks?

Some of the oldest masks ever found are about 9,000 years old, carved from stone, with holes that may have let them be tied on.

The short answer

People make masks to become someone, or something, else. Put on a mask and you can be a god, a demon, an animal, an ancestor or a hero. Masks appear in dance, theatre, religious ceremonies, festivals, funerals, war and sport on every inhabited continent.

They also protect. Surgeons, welders, firefighters and hockey goalies all wear masks. Some masks honour the dead, like the gold mask of Tutankhamun. Others are just for fun, like the costumes children wear at Halloween. A mask turns the human face, the first thing we read in other people, into something new.

Surprising but true

Tutankhamun's golden burial mask weighs about 10 kg, most of it gold, about as heavy as a full bucket of water.

The oldest faces

In 2014, the Israel Museum in Jerusalem displayed a group of stone masks about 9,000 years old, found in the Judean hills and desert. They have eye holes, mouths and small holes around the edge, perhaps for tying them on or hanging them up.

Nobody knows exactly what they were for. Many experts think they represented ancestors, and may have been worn in ceremonies or kept in homes. They come from a time when people were first settling into farming villages.

Gold for a king

About 3,300 years ago in Egypt, the young pharaoh Tutankhamun was buried wearing a mask of solid gold, inlaid with blue glass and stones like lapis lazuli. It weighs about 10 kg. Egyptians believed the mask helped the spirit recognise its body in the afterlife.

When the archaeologist Howard Carter found it in 1925, it dazzled the world. Today it's one of the most famous treasures of ancient Egypt, and millions of visitors have queued to see it.

Masks in Indian dance

In eastern India, Chhau dancers perform stories from the Ramayana, Mahabharata and local legends. In the Purulia style from West Bengal, dancers wear big, colourful masks with crowns of feathers, beads and tinsel, and leap and spin. In the Seraikela style from Jharkhand, the masks are smaller and gentler. A third style, from Mayurbhanj in Odisha, uses no masks at all.

On Majuli, a river island in Assam, monks in Vaishnava monasteries called satras make masks from bamboo, clay and cloth. Some are giant masks covering the whole body, used in plays about Krishna and Rama. In Karnataka, Yakshagana performers mostly use thick makeup instead, a painted mask on the face itself.

Masks across the world

In Japan, actors in Noh theatre, which began in the 1300s, wear wooden masks. Each is carved so carefully that tilting it slightly changes its expression. In ancient Greek theatre, actors wore masks with wide mouths that helped carry their voices to thousands of people.

Venice in Italy holds a famous carnival where people wear elaborate masks. In Bhutan, monks perform masked dances at festivals called tsechu. Across West and Central Africa, wooden masks are part of ceremonies honouring spirits and ancestors, and artists such as Picasso were inspired by them.

Masks that save lives

In 1959, the Canadian goalkeeper Jacques Plante was hit in the face by a puck. He returned to the game wearing a mask, and became the first goalie in the NHL to wear one regularly. Others soon followed. Today, sports masks and visors are as normal as helmets.

Doctors began wearing surgical masks in the late 1800s to stop germs. During the COVID-19 pandemic from 2020, billions of people wore masks every day. Scientists found that masks make it a little harder to read emotions, because we read so much from the mouth, though our eyes do much of the talking too.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Masks are mainly for hiding who you are.

Truth: Hiding is only one use. Across cultures, masks let wearers become someone else, such as a god, an animal or an ancestor. They are used in dance, theatre, healing, festivals, funerals and sport.

Try this

Cut a mask shape from a paper plate and cut eye holes with an adult's help. Draw a face that is calm, neither happy nor sad. Hold it up to a mirror in front of a lamp and tilt it up and down slowly. Watch how shadows change its mood, just like a Noh mask.
