Is Majuli the world's biggest river island?

Majuli, an island in the Brahmaputra in Assam, has lost a large part of its land to the river in about a hundred years, yet more than 1.5 lakh people still call it home.

The short answer

Majuli is an island in the middle of the Brahmaputra River in Assam, in northeast India. In 2016, Guinness World Records recognised it as the largest river island in the world, and that is the title most people know.

But the answer is not so simple. Bananal Island in Brazil is much bigger, and it too sits between rivers. Experts argue over whether it counts as a true river island. Meanwhile, Majuli itself keeps shrinking, because the Brahmaputra eats away at its edges every monsoon.

Surprising but true

In 2016 Majuli became India's first island district, with its own district headquarters on the island.

An island made of mud

Majuli is not made of rock. It is a giant bank of sediment: sand and silt carried down from the Himalayas and dropped by the river. The Brahmaputra is one of the widest rivers in the world in flood, and in places it spreads into many channels around the island.

Soft land like this is easy to wash away. Records suggest that in the early 1900s Majuli covered about 1,250 square kilometres. Today it is much smaller, though measurements differ because the banks shift every year. A big earthquake in 1950 changed the river's flow and is thought to have made the floods and erosion worse.

Life on the island

More than 1.5 lakh people live on Majuli, in well over a hundred villages. Many belong to the Mising community, who build their houses on bamboo stilts called chang ghar so that floodwater can pass underneath. Rice fields, fishing and handloom weaving are part of everyday life. Potters on the island shape clay by hand, without a wheel, in a style that is centuries old.

There is no bridge yet from the mainland. People cross by ferry, sometimes with bicycles and motorbikes balanced on the deck. In 2016 Majuli became a district of its own, the first island in India to be given that status.

The satras

In the 1500s, the saint and reformer Srimanta Sankardev visited Majuli. He spread a form of Hindu devotion to Krishna that used music, drama and dance. His followers built monasteries called satras on the island, and some of them still stand.

The satras are famous for Sattriya dance and for mask-making. At Samaguri Satra, craftsmen shape huge masks of demons and gods from bamboo, clay and cloth for plays about the Ramayana and the Mahabharata. Some masks are so big that they cover the actor's whole body.

The record question

So is Majuli the biggest? Bananal Island in Brazil sits between the Araguaia and Javaés rivers and covers about 19,000 square kilometres, an area bigger than the whole of Majuli many times over. Britannica describes Bananal as one of the largest river islands in the world.

Guinness looked at it differently and named Majuli the record holder in 2016. Part of the argument is about what counts. Is an island formed where a river splits and rejoins the same as one built up in the middle of a single wide river? Geographers don't fully agree, so both claims appear in books.

Saving the island

Assam's government and the Brahmaputra Board have built embankments, dropped sandbags and planted grass and trees along the banks to slow the erosion. Some stretches have held. Others have been swept away in a single bad flood. A road bridge linking Majuli to the mainland has been planned, which would change daily life for islanders who now depend on ferries.

Each winter, Majuli's wetlands fill with migrating birds, including ducks and geese that fly in from colder lands to the north. Visitors come for the birds, the satras and the quiet. Whether or not it holds the world record, there is no other place quite like it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Majuli is definitely the biggest river island on Earth.

Truth: Guinness World Records gave Majuli that title in 2016, but Bananal Island in Brazil, between two rivers, covers about 19,000 km², many times more than Majuli. Some experts don't count Bananal because of how its rivers split and join, so the record depends on the definition.
