What is on the far side of the Moon?

The far side of the Moon isn't dark at all. When we see a new moon, the side facing away from us is in full sunshine.

The short answer

The far side of the Moon is the half that always faces away from Earth. It gets just as much sunshine as the near side, but nobody on Earth had seen it until a space probe flew behind the Moon in 1959.

It turned out to look very different. The side we see has large dark patches, called maria, Latin for "seas". These are old lava plains. The far side has hardly any. Instead it is covered in craters on top of craters, including the South Pole–Aitken basin, about 2,500 km across and one of the biggest impact basins in the Solar System.

Surprising but true

The two sides of the Moon look like different worlds. The near side has big dark plains, while the far side is almost all bright, rough, crater-covered highlands.

Why we only see one face

The Moon does spin, but slowly. It turns once on its axis in about 27 days, the same time it takes to go once around Earth. So the same face always points towards us, just as a dancer circling a partner keeps looking at them.

This didn't happen by chance. Long ago the Moon spun faster. Earth's gravity stretched it slightly into an egg shape, the same kind of pull that raises tides in our oceans. Earth's tug on that bulge slowly braked the Moon's spin until the two matched. Because the Moon wobbles a little in its orbit, we actually get to see about 59% of its surface over time. The other 41% stays hidden.

A first look in 1959

In October 1959, the Soviet probe Luna 3 swung behind the Moon and took fuzzy photographs. It developed the film on board, scanned it and radioed the images home. People finally saw the hidden side: bright, crater-covered and almost without seas. Soviet scientists named some of its features. That is why the far side has a crater called Tsiolkovsky, after a Russian rocket pioneer, and a dark patch called Mare Moscoviense, the Sea of Moscow.

The first humans to see it with their own eyes were the three Apollo 8 astronauts in December 1968. As they flew behind the Moon, they also lost radio contact with Earth, because the Moon blocked the signal. For about 45 minutes on each orbit, they were cut off from every other person.

Why the two sides differ

Scientists think the answer lies in the crust, the Moon's outer rocky layer. On the far side it is much thicker. When giant space rocks hit the near side billions of years ago, melted rock from deep inside could push through the thin crust and flood the craters, making dark maria. On the far side, the thick crust mostly kept the lava down.

Why the crust is thicker there is still being studied. One idea is that heat from the young, glowing Earth kept the near side warmer for longer while the Moon cooled. The Moon's own birth, probably from a giant crash between Earth and another world, may also have played a part.

Landing on the hidden side

Landing on the far side is hard because there is no direct radio link to Earth. In 2018, China placed a relay satellite called Queqiao beyond the Moon to pass messages back and forth. In January 2019, its Chang'e 4 lander touched down in Von Kármán crater, the first landing on the far side. Its little rover, Yutu-2, kept exploring for years.

In June 2024, Chang'e 6 brought about 2 kg of far-side rock and soil back to Earth, the first ever. Scientists are studying them to learn when the far side's few lava flows happened. India's Chandrayaan-2 orbiter, circling since 2019, has mapped both sides from above.

Astronomers have another dream for the far side. Because the Moon blocks the noise of radios, phones and TV from Earth, it could be the quietest place nearby to build a radio telescope and listen to the early universe.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The far side of the Moon is always dark, so it's called "the dark side".

Truth: Both sides get sunlight in turn as the Moon goes around Earth. "Dark" in the old phrase meant unknown, not unlit. During a new moon, when the side facing us looks black, the far side is in full daylight.

Try this

Try tidal locking yourself. Put a chair in the middle of a room to be Earth. Walk in a circle around it while always keeping your face towards the chair. When you finish, notice that you have also turned all the way round once, and the wall behind you saw your back the whole time.
