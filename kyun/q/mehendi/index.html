How does mehendi stain your hands?

Mehendi goes on green, comes off orange and turns deep red-brown over the next two days, all thanks to one molecule in a leaf.

The short answer

Mehendi, also spelt mehndi, is art painted on skin with a paste made from the dried, crushed leaves of the henna plant. The leaves contain a reddish-orange dye called lawsone. When the paste sits on your skin for a few hours, the lawsone seeps into the dead outer layer and grips onto a tough protein there called keratin.

When you scrape the dried paste off, the pattern underneath is bright orange. Over the next day or two, the dye reacts with the air and darkens to a rich red-brown. The design then slowly fades over one to three weeks, as your skin sheds its outer cells and takes the colour with it.

Surprising but true

Mehendi stains darkest on your palms and the soles of your feet, because the skin there has the thickest outer layer for the dye to soak into.

A plant from hot, dry places

Henna is a small shrub that grows well in hot, dry lands, from North Africa through the Middle East to India. Sojat, a town in the Pali district of Rajasthan, is famous for its henna, and in 2021 Sojat Mehndi received a GI tag. That label means the name can only be used for henna from that area.

Farmers cut the leafy branches, dry them in the sun and grind the leaves into a fine green powder. Mixed with water, lemon juice and sometimes tea or a few drops of oil, the powder becomes a thick paste. Artists roll it into cones, like piping bags for cake icing, and squeeze out thin lines.

Patterns with meaning

Mehendi designs are full of motifs, small shapes that repeat. Peacocks, lotus flowers, mango-shaped paisleys, vines, dots and nets of fine lines all appear often. Good artists use symmetry, mirroring shapes on each side so the whole design feels balanced.

There are many regional styles. Rajasthani mehendi often covers the whole hand and arm in very fine detail, sometimes with figures of a bride and groom. Arabic-style designs use bolder flowers and leaves trailing diagonally across the hand, leaving more bare skin. Indo-Arabic designs mix the two.

Weddings, festivals and hidden names

In many Indian families, a bride's mehendi ceremony is held a day or two before her wedding. Relatives and friends gather, sing and get their own hands decorated. A popular game is to hide the groom's name or initials inside the bride's design, and he has to find it.

Mehendi is part of many celebrations across religions and regions. Women and girls apply it for Eid, Karva Chauth, Teej and Diwali, and at weddings in India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, the Gulf and North Africa. In many places a dark stain is said to be a sign of luck or love, which may be why people leave the paste on for so long.

The chemistry of a darker stain

The stain keeps darkening long after the paste is gone. Lawsone sticks to the keratin in your skin cells, and once there it slowly reacts with oxygen in the air. That reaction, called oxidation, shifts the colour from bright orange towards deep reddish brown over about 24 to 48 hours.

Warmth helps the dye soak in, which is why some people hold their hands near a warm cup after the paste dries. Fresh henna powder gives darker results than old powder, because lawsone breaks down over time. Hands that are washed often or scrubbed with soap lose their colour faster, as the dyed outer cells are rubbed away.

Older than you think

People have used henna for thousands of years. Some ancient Egyptian mummies have hair or fingernails tinted orange-red, which researchers think came from henna. It has been used to colour hair, nails, leather and even horses' manes and tails.

Henna also cools the skin, which may be one reason it became popular in hot lands. Getting a deep stain takes patience. Artists suggest keeping the paste on for several hours, dabbing on a little lemon and sugar to keep it moist, and avoiding water on the first day. After that, all you can do is wait for the orange to turn red-brown.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Black henna is just a darker kind of natural henna.

Truth: Natural henna never stains black. So-called black henna often contains a chemical hair dye called PPD, which can cause painful skin reactions and blisters. Health agencies warn people to avoid it, so check with an adult before trying any dark-coloured paste.

Try this

Trace around your hand on a sheet of paper. Draw a circle or flower in the middle of the palm, then repeat one small shape, like a leaf or a dot, around it in rings. Fill each finger with a different pattern of lines and dots, the way mehendi artists do, and see how symmetry makes it look finished.
