Who was Rembrandt?

Rembrandt's most famous painting is called The Night Watch, but it shows a scene in daylight. Centuries of darkening varnish fooled everyone.

The short answer

Rembrandt van Rijn was a Dutch painter and printmaker who lived from 1606 to 1669. He is often counted among the greatest artists who ever lived. He painted portraits, Bible stories and huge group scenes, and he made beautiful prints called etchings.

What made him special was the way he used light. In his pictures, a beam seems to fall on one face or one hand while the rest sinks into warm shadow. He also painted himself about 80 times, from a cocky young man to a wrinkled old one. Looking at those self-portraits in order is like flipping through a diary of a whole life.

Surprising but true

Rembrandt never travelled far from home, yet he made a set of drawings copying Mughal miniature paintings from India.

A miller's son from Leiden

Rembrandt was born in Leiden, a university town, the son of a miller. He was sent to the university but soon left to train as a painter. In the early 1630s he moved to Amsterdam, then one of the richest cities on Earth, full of merchants who had made fortunes in trade.

Those merchants wanted portraits of themselves, and Rembrandt was soon in demand. In 1634 he married Saskia van Uylenburgh, and he bought a large, expensive house. He collected shells, armour, prints and art from around the world, which he used as props in his paintings.

Painting with light and dark

Artists call strong contrast between light and shadow chiaroscuro, an Italian word meaning "light-dark". Rembrandt was a master of it. He often painted a dark background and then built up the lit parts with thick paint, sometimes so heavy that it stands out from the canvas like icing on a cake.

Up close, his brushwork can look rough. Step back and it turns into skin, gold thread or the shine in an eye. His etchings, made by scratching a design into a metal plate and printing it, spread his name across Europe long before his paintings did.

The Night Watch

In 1642, Rembrandt finished a huge painting of a company of Amsterdam's city guards. It is about 3.6 metres tall and over 4 metres wide, taller than a basketball hoop. Instead of lining the men up neatly, he showed them spilling into action, with a drummer, a dog and a girl in a gold dress caught in a pool of light.

The picture was later trimmed on all four sides to fit a wall. Over the years, its varnish darkened so much that people named it The Night Watch. Today it hangs in the Rijksmuseum in Amsterdam. Since 2019, scientists there have scanned it with X-rays and other tools, studying every layer in a project called Operation Night Watch.

A surprise from India

In the late 1650s, Rembrandt made around two dozen drawings based on Mughal miniatures, small paintings made for India's emperors. He copied figures of Shah Jahan, Jahangir and court nobles, probably from paintings that Dutch traders had brought back from the East. He kept the poses but used his own loose style.

Nobody knows exactly why he made them. He may simply have admired the fine detail and rich costumes. Either way, it shows how trade linked a painter in Amsterdam to the court of the Mughal emperors thousands of kilometres away.

Hard times

Rembrandt's life was full of loss. Saskia died in 1642, the year of The Night Watch. Three of their four children died as babies. He spent more than he earned, and in 1656 he went bankrupt. His house and his collection were sold.

He kept working. Some of his finest paintings come from his last, poorest years, including late self-portraits that show every line on his face without flattery. His old house is now the Rembrandt House Museum, where visitors can see the room where he once painted.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Night Watch shows soldiers on patrol at night.

Truth: It shows a city militia company setting off in daylight. The painting got its nickname long after Rembrandt died, because old varnish had made it so dark.

Try this

Sit near a window in a dark room, or shine one torch from the side at a friend's face. Draw what you see using only a soft pencil, pressing hard for the shadows and leaving paper bare for the bright parts. You are trying Rembrandt's trick of shaping a face with light.
