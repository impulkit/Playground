What is the Barefoot College?

In a Rajasthan village, grandmothers who never finished school learn to build solar lights, then carry them home to villages across Africa and Latin America.

The short answer

The Barefoot College is an organisation in Tilonia, a village in Rajasthan, that trains rural people with little or no schooling to do skilled jobs. Its students learn to be solar engineers, water engineers, teachers, health workers and builders. Bunker Roy, a social worker, started it in 1972 as the Social Work and Research Centre.

The name comes from an idea. The college believes knowledge from experience, the kind held by village people who often walk barefoot, matters as much as degrees. Its best-known project trains grandmothers from India and dozens of other countries to build and repair solar lighting systems.

Surprising but true

Women from different countries who share no common language learn solar engineering together at Tilonia, using colours, gestures and hands-on practice.

Grandmothers become engineers

The college chose older women on purpose. Young men who learned a skill often left their villages for jobs in cities. Grandmothers usually stayed, and their neighbours already trusted them.

The women come to Tilonia for about six months. Many cannot read and do not share a language with each other. Trainers use colour-coded wires and parts, hand signals and repeated practice. By the end, the women can put together solar panels, batteries, charge controllers and lamps, and fix them when they break. Women have come from Africa, Latin America, the Pacific and Asia, with India's government paying for many of them.

Lights that change evenings

When the women go home, they set up solar systems in houses that have never had electricity. A child who had to stop reading at sunset can study at night. Families stop buying kerosene, whose smoke harms lungs and whose flames can start fires.

People in the village pay a small monthly fee to the solar engineer for repairs. So the grandmother earns a living, and a broken lamp gets fixed instead of thrown away. In some villages, she becomes the most important technical expert for many kilometres around, the person people call when anything electrical stops working.

Schools at night

Many children in Rajasthan's villages herd goats or help on farms during the day. So the college runs night schools, lit by solar lamps, where lessons fit around work. In 2008, about 3,000 children went to 150 such schools, and girls far outnumbered boys.

The night schools also run a children's parliament. Pupils elect their own members and even a prime minister, who check whether schools have teachers, water and lights, and report problems to adults.

What is rainwater harvesting?One millimetre of rain falling on one square metre of roof gives you exactly one litre of water, and over a monsoon that adds up to tankers' worth.

A campus built by villagers

The college campus in Tilonia was designed with help from a professional architect, but villagers built it themselves using local stone, mud and simple methods. In 2001, the buildings won the Aga Khan Award for Architecture, one of the world's best-known prizes for architecture.

Water is precious in the Thar region. The roofs are built to collect rain and send it into large underground tanks, a technique called rainwater harvesting. A single good monsoon can fill them with enough water to last the dry months.

Puppets and other messages

To spread ideas about health, water and rights, the college uses puppetry, songs and street plays. Puppets made from recycled paper can say things a stranger cannot, and a crowd will gather to watch them where nobody would come to a lecture.

The college has its critics. Some ask how well its solar systems keep working years after the training, when spare parts are far away, and whether its methods can grow without losing their spirit. Supporters point to the grandmothers who still keep their villages lit. Their stories have travelled widely, and the same sunshine that powers a single lamp in Tilonia now powers giant solar parks across India.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Solar power only works in rich countries or at giant power plants.

Truth: Small solar kits with one panel, a battery and a few lamps can light a home anywhere sunny. In villages far from the power grid, they often arrive before electricity cables do.

Try this

On a sunny day, fill two identical cups with the same amount of water. Wrap one in black paper and one in white paper and leave them in the sun for an hour. Feel which is warmer. Dark surfaces soak up more sunlight, which is why solar water heaters are painted black.
