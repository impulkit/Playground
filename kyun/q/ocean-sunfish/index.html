What is the ocean sunfish?

The heaviest bony fish ever weighed was as heavy as two cars, and it looked like a giant swimming head with no tail.

The short answer

The ocean sunfish, or mola, is one of the strangest fish in the sea. It looks like a big, flat disc, as if someone took a fish and cut off the back half. It has a tall fin on top, another underneath, and a short, frilly edge where a tail should be.

Sunfish are also giants. The heaviest bony fish ever weighed was a bump-head sunfish found floating dead near the Azores islands in the Atlantic in 2021. It weighed 2,744 kg, about as much as two cars, and was more than 3 metres long. Sunfish live in warm and mild seas around the world.

Surprising but true

A sunfish starts life smaller than a grain of rice and can end up around 60 million times heavier.

A fish without a back end

As a baby sunfish grows, its tail never forms properly. Instead, the back edges of its top and bottom fins fold together into a stiff, wavy flap called a clavus, which it uses as a rudder.

To swim, the sunfish flaps its two tall fins from side to side, together, like a bird flapping wings sideways. It looks clumsy, but it works. Tagging studies show sunfish can cruise at a steady pace and dive hundreds of metres down.

When the top fin sticks out of the water, beach-goers sometimes panic and shout "shark". But sharks swim with their fin steady, while a sunfish's fin flops slowly from side to side.

From speck to giant

A newly hatched sunfish is only about 2.5 mm long, smaller than a grain of rice and covered in tiny spines. By adulthood, some grow around 60 million times heavier. No other backboned animal is known to grow so much.

A big female may carry hundreds of millions of eggs. One female measured in the early 1900s was estimated to hold about 300 million. Almost all the babies are eaten by other animals, which is why the seas are not packed with sunfish.

A diet of jelly

Sunfish mainly eat jellyfish, plus comb jellies, small squid and fish. Jellyfish are about 95 per cent water and very low in food value, so a sunfish has to eat a huge amount to grow so big. Its mouth is fused into a beak, and teeth in its throat shred the slippery food before swallowing.

They hunt in cold, deep water, often down to the dim twilight zone, where light barely reaches. Then they come back up.

Sunbathing with cleaners

After a cold dive, a sunfish often lies flat on its side at the surface, soaking up warmth. That habit gave it its English name. Warming up after a dive helps its muscles and brain work faster, the way you might sit in the sun after a cold swim.

While it lies there, seabirds such as gulls land and peck parasites off its skin. Sunfish can carry dozens of kinds of parasites. Small fish called wrasse also clean them in the kelp forests nearer shore. The Latin name, Mola, means millstone, after the fish's round, grey, rough shape.

Dangers for a gentle giant

Sunfish are harmless to people. Their beaky mouths are made for slurping soft food, not biting swimmers. But they are often caught by accident in fishing nets, especially in the Mediterranean and off California.

Plastic is another danger. A floating bag looks a lot like a jellyfish. Sunfish, and sea turtles too, sometimes swallow bags, which can block their guts. The IUCN listed the ocean sunfish as Vulnerable in its 2015 assessment. And the sunfish is not the only giant that lives on soft, small food. The biggest of all the ocean giants, the blue whale, feeds on shrimp-like krill no longer than your finger.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Sunfish are lazy drifters that float wherever the currents take them.

Truth: Tagging studies show sunfish swim actively, dive hundreds of metres to feed and travel long distances. Their lying-at-the-surface habit made people think they were helpless.

Try this

Put a scrap of thin, clear plastic bag into a bowl of water and stir gently. Watch how it floats and ripples. To a hungry sunfish or sea turtle, it looks a lot like a jellyfish, which is why plastic in the sea is so dangerous for them.
