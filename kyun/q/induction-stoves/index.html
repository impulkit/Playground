How does an induction stove heat without a flame?

Put your hand on a working induction stove and it may feel only warm. The heat is made inside the pan itself.

The short answer

Under the smooth glass top of an induction stove sits a flat coil of copper wire. When you switch it on, electricity flows through the coil, changing direction tens of thousands of times a second. That creates a magnetic field above the stove that flips back and forth just as fast.

When you put an iron or steel pan on top, the flipping field makes electric currents swirl around inside the base of the pan. The metal resists these currents, and that resistance turns into heat, right in the pan. The glass only gets warm because the hot pan sits on it.

No flame, no glowing coil. The pan is the heater.

Surprising but true

An aluminium or glass pan won't heat up on most induction stoves at all, but a steel pan sitting right beside it can boil water in a few minutes.

Faraday's discovery

The idea goes back to 1831, when the English scientist Michael Faraday showed that a changing magnetic field can make electricity flow in a nearby wire. This is called electromagnetic induction. It is how power stations make electricity: spinning magnets near coils of wire push currents into the grid.

An induction stove runs that trick in reverse and in miniature. The coil makes a changing magnetic field, and the field makes currents inside the pan. Engineers call these swirling currents eddy currents, after the little whirlpools you see in a stream. Faraday himself never saw an induction stove. Electric cooking of this kind only reached homes in the second half of the 1900s, and became cheap enough for most families much later.

Why the pan matters

Iron and some kinds of steel are magnetic, so the field grips them strongly and heats them fast. Aluminium, copper and glass don't work the same way, so on most induction stoves they stay cold. That's why induction pans often have a thick steel disc on the bottom.

There's an easy test, which you can try with any fridge magnet. If a fridge magnet sticks firmly to the base, the pan will work. Many Indian kitchens found this out when they bought an induction stove and their old aluminium pressure cooker refused to whistle. Companies now sell "induction-base" cookers and kadhais.

Less wasted heat

A gas flame heats the air around the pan as well as the pan itself. A lot of the heat escapes up the sides and into the kitchen. The US Department of Energy estimates that only about a third to two-fifths of a gas flame's energy reaches the food.

Induction is far better at this, at roughly 85 per cent, because the heat starts in the pan. Water can boil faster than on gas, and the kitchen stays cooler. Turning the dial changes the heat instantly, much like gas, which cooks appreciate.

Cleaner air indoors

Burning gas, wood or kerosene inside a home releases smoke and gases. In many Indian villages, cooking over wood or dung fires has been a major cause of indoor air pollution, which harms the lungs. Gas is much cleaner than wood, and induction adds no fumes at all. In the USA, some cities have started asking new buildings to use electric cooking instead of gas, partly for cleaner air and partly to cut the gases that warm the planet.

Induction has downsides. It needs electricity, which isn't always reliable, and the right pans, which cost money. The stove top stays hot for a while after cooking, from the pan's heat, so it still needs care. But a stove that heats only the pan is one of the quietest bits of physics in the house.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Induction stoves give off dangerous radiation.

Truth: The magnetic field is weak and fades within a few centimetres of the stove top. Health agencies treat normal home use as safe. People with heart pacemakers are usually told to check with their doctor and keep a little distance.

Try this

Go on a magnet hunt in the kitchen with an adult. Hold a fridge magnet against the bottom of different pots and pans. The ones it sticks to would work on an induction stove. Check whether your family's pressure cooker passes the test.
