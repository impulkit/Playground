How do oceans control the climate?

The top three metres of the ocean hold as much heat as the whole of Earth's atmosphere above them.

The short answer

The ocean is Earth's giant heat battery. Water takes a lot of energy to warm up and gives that energy back slowly. Because the ocean covers about 71% of the planet, it soaks up sunlight in summer and in the tropics, then releases warmth in winter and carries it towards the poles.

Ocean currents move that heat around like a slow conveyor belt. Water evaporating from warm seas feeds clouds and rain, including India's monsoon. The ocean also swallows carbon dioxide from the air. Without it, summers would be hotter, winters colder and the planet warming faster.

Surprising but true

London is farther north than Calgary in Canada, yet its winters are far milder, partly because of warm water and winds from the Atlantic.

A heat sponge

Sit on a beach at noon and the sand burns your feet, while the water stays cool. At night the sand is cold and the water feels warm. Water simply needs more energy than sand or air to change its temperature.

That is why coastal cities have gentler weather. Mumbai mostly stays between about 16°C and 35°C all year. Delhi, hundreds of kilometres from the nearest sea, can swing from about 45°C in May to under 5°C on a January morning. In the USA, San Francisco and Kansas City show the same pattern: one is cooled by the Pacific, the other bakes and freezes in the middle of the continent.

Rivers in the sea

The ocean moves. Winds push surface currents across the sea, and differences in temperature and saltiness drive slower, deeper ones. Together they form a loop sometimes called the global conveyor belt.

The best known part is the Gulf Stream. It carries warm water from the Gulf of Mexico up the east coast of the USA and across the Atlantic. Along with westerly winds blowing in off the ocean, it helps explain why Britain and Norway are mild for their latitude. London lies farther north than Calgary, yet snow there is a rare event. Some scientists worry that melting Arctic ice could slow these Atlantic currents, though how much and how soon is still being studied.

The sea makes the monsoon

India's summer rain is an ocean story. In April and May, the land heats up faster than the Arabian Sea and the Bay of Bengal. Hot air rises over the land, and moist air from the sea flows in to replace it. That wet sea air brings the monsoon.

The ocean far away matters too. Every few years, a stretch of the Pacific near South America becomes unusually warm. This is El Niño. It shifts winds and rain around the globe, and in many El Niño years India's monsoon is weaker than normal. Weather forecasters in Pune and in Maryland watch Pacific temperatures for clues about the coming season.

Fuel for storms

Warm sea water is the energy source for tropical cyclones. A cyclone needs sea surface temperatures of roughly 26°C or more. As water evaporates, it releases heat high in the clouds, which powers the spinning winds. The Bay of Bengal, often very warm, produces many of the cyclones that hit Odisha, Andhra Pradesh and Bangladesh. The same process drives hurricanes that reach Florida and Texas.

Taking in our pollution

Since the 1970s, the ocean has soaked up more than 90% of the extra heat trapped by greenhouse gases. It has also absorbed about a quarter of the carbon dioxide people release each year. That has slowed warming on land.

But it comes at a cost. Warmer water expands, so sea levels rise. Heatwaves in the sea bleach coral reefs. When carbon dioxide dissolves in water, it makes the water slightly more acidic, which makes it harder for shellfish and corals to build shells. The heat the ocean has already stored will keep warming the planet for decades, even if emissions fall. Scientists measure all this using about 4,000 floating robots, called Argo floats, which drift and dive across every ocean.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The ocean is too big to be changed by humans.

Truth: The ocean has absorbed more than 90% of the extra heat trapped by greenhouse gases since the 1970s, and about a quarter of our carbon dioxide. That has made sea water warmer and slightly more acidic, which harms corals and shellfish.

Try this

Fill one bowl with water and another with the same amount of sand or soil. Leave both in the sun for an hour, then feel them. Now put both in the shade and feel again after an hour. The water warms more slowly and cools more slowly, just like the sea.
