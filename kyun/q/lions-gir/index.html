Why are Asiatic lions found only in Gir?

Lions once roamed from the Middle East to Bihar. Today the last wild Asiatic lions all live in one corner of Gujarat.

The short answer

Asiatic lions once lived across a huge stretch of the world, from the Middle East through Iran to much of northern and central India. Over the centuries, people hunted them, cleared their land for farms and killed the deer and antelope they ate. By the late 1800s, lions had vanished from everywhere except one patch of dry forest in Gujarat: Gir.

They survived there partly by luck and partly because the rulers of the region, the Nawabs of Junagadh, began protecting them in the early 1900s. After India's independence, Gir became a sanctuary in 1965.

From a few dozen lions, the population has grown to about 890 in the 2025 count. Every one of them lives in Gujarat.

Surprising but true

The four lions on India's national emblem, from Emperor Ashoka's pillar at Sarnath, are Asiatic lions.

A close call

Around 1900, some reports claimed fewer than 20 lions were left. Some historians think that figure was too low, and may even have been kept low on purpose to discourage hunters. Either way, the Asiatic lion came very close to vanishing.

British officers and Indian princes had shot lions for sport for decades, often in large numbers, and a lion skin was a prized trophy. Protection came just in time. Today there are more wild lions in Gujarat than at any time in over a century.

How Asiatic lions are different

Asiatic lions are the same species as African lions, and are closely related to the lions of West and Central Africa. But they look a little different. Males usually have shorter, thinner manes, so their ears stay visible. Most Asiatic lions have a long fold of skin running along the belly, which African lions rarely show.

Male and female lions in Gir mostly live apart. The females live in small family groups and raise cubs together, while the males form small gangs and join the females mainly to mate. They hunt chital, sambar, nilgai and wild boar, and sometimes cattle.

Living with lions

Gir is not empty of people. The Maldhari herders have lived inside the forest for generations, in small settlements called nes, grazing buffaloes and cows. Lions sometimes take their animals, and the government pays some compensation. Yet many Maldharis are proud of the lions and tolerate them as neighbours.

As their numbers grew, lions spread far beyond the forest. They now roam farmland, hills and even beaches along the coast of Saurashtra. People have filmed lions strolling past temples, crossing highways and resting in mango orchards.

One home is risky

Having every lion in one place has a big weakness. In 2018, more than 20 lions in one part of Gir died within a few weeks, from a virus caught from dogs and a disease spread by ticks. A larger outbreak could hit the whole population.

In 2013, India's Supreme Court ordered that some lions be moved to Kuno in Madhya Pradesh to start a second population. That has not happened. Gujarat argues that its lions are thriving and already spreading into new areas. Many wildlife scientists say a home far away would be safer. Kuno later received cheetahs from Africa instead.

Lions on the national emblem

Lions have stood for strength and power in India for over 2,000 years. When India became a republic, it chose the lions carved on Emperor Ashoka's pillar at Sarnath as its emblem.

Those stone lions are Asiatic lions, carved when they still lived across much of the country. Today their real-life cousins have one forest to call home, and a country deciding where they should go next.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Lions are the "king of the jungle" and live in thick rainforests.

Truth: Lions prefer open country such as grasslands, scrub and dry forests, where they can spot prey and stalk it. Gir is a dry forest that loses many of its leaves in the hot summer.

Try this

Find an Indian coin or banknote and look for the lion emblem. Count how many lions you can see. The real carving at Sarnath has four lions sitting back to back, but from the front you can only ever see three. If you don't have Indian money, look up a picture of the Lion Capital of Ashoka.
