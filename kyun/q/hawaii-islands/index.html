How did volcanoes build Hawaii in the middle of the ocean?

Hawaii sits on a conveyor belt of rock, sliding over a hot spot in Earth.

The short answer

Deep under the Pacific Ocean there is a hot spot, a place where hot rock rises from deep inside Earth. Melted rock punches up through the sea floor and piles up as volcanoes. Over hundreds of thousands of years, they grow tall enough to break the surface as islands.

Surprising but true

Measured from its base on the sea floor, Mauna Kea in Hawaii is over 10,000 m tall, taller than Mount Everest.

A moving conveyor belt

The sea floor is part of the Pacific plate, which slides north-west by about 7 to 10 cm a year, a little faster than your fingernails grow. The hot spot stays put. So each volcano is slowly carried away, and a new one forms over the hot spot.

That is why Hawaii is a chain of islands: the oldest in the north-west, the youngest (the Big Island) in the south-east. A brand-new island is growing underwater right now.

The chain doesn't stop at the islands you see on a map. A line of drowned volcanoes, called seamounts, stretches about 6,000 km across the Pacific, almost to Russia. The oldest are around 80 million years old. Halfway along, the line bends sharply. That bend shows the plate changed direction about 47 million years ago.

Islands that grow, then shrink

Once a volcano drifts off the hot spot, it stops erupting. Then rain, waves and wind start wearing it down. The heavy island also slowly sinks, pressing down on the plate beneath it.

So the islands tell a life story. The Big Island is young, tall and still growing. Maui is a bit older. Kauaʻi, far to the north-west, is older still, carved by rain into steep green cliffs. Beyond it, the old volcanoes have sunk until only coral reefs and tiny atolls are left at the surface.

Taller than Everest

Mauna Kea rises about 4,200 m above the sea, but its base is about 6,000 m below the waves. From bottom to top it is over 10,000 m, taller than Everest. Its neighbour, Mauna Loa, is the largest active volcano on Earth by volume.

The air on top of Mauna Kea is so dry, still and clear that it is one of the best places on Earth to study the stars. Over a dozen telescopes stand there. Many Native Hawaiians consider the summit sacred, and plans to build new telescopes there have led to long protests and debate.

What is Hawaiian culture like?The first Hawaiians crossed thousands of kilometres of open Pacific in canoes, steering by stars, waves and birds, and found some of the most remote islands on Earth.

Kīlauea and Pele

Kīlauea, on the Big Island, is one of the most active volcanoes in the world. It erupted almost without stopping from 1983 to 2018. Its lava is runny, so it usually flows rather than explodes, and you can sometimes watch it from a safe distance. In 2018, lava flows destroyed hundreds of homes in the Puna district.

In Hawaiian tradition, Pele is the goddess of volcanoes and fire, said to live in the crater of Kīlauea. Many people on the island still leave offerings and speak of her with respect.

The next island

About 35 km off the Big Island's south-east coast, a new volcano called Kamaʻehuakanaloa is growing on the sea floor. It used to be called Lōʻihi. Its top is still about 1,000 m below the surface. If it keeps growing, it may rise above the waves in tens of thousands of years.

India also rode a moving plate, all the way from near Antarctica to Asia.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Hawaii's volcanoes sit on the edge of a plate, like most volcanoes.

Truth: Most volcanoes do form at plate edges, but Hawaii is in the middle of the Pacific plate, thousands of kilometres from any edge. It is fed by a hot spot rising from deep below.

Try this

Model a hot spot. Hold a felt pen still, pointing up, under a sheet of paper. Slowly slide the paper sideways in one direction, and every few seconds press the paper down onto the pen tip. You'll get a line of dots. The first dot you made has travelled farthest, just like Hawaii's oldest islands.
