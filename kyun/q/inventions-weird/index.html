What are the weirdest inventions ever patented?

In 2001, a man in Australia was granted a patent for a "circular transportation facilitation device". It was the wheel.

The short answer

Patent offices have approved some very strange ideas. There's a patent for exercising a cat with a laser pointer, one for swinging on a swing and one for a comb-over hairstyle. There was even one for the wheel itself, renamed a "circular transportation facilitation device".

A patent is a legal right that lets an inventor stop others copying their invention, usually for about 20 years. To get one, an idea must be new, not obvious to experts, and described clearly. It doesn't have to be a good idea. That's why the record books are full of odd ones, and why some were later cancelled.

Surprising but true

Abraham Lincoln is the only US president to hold a patent. It was for a gadget to lift riverboats over sandbanks, and it was never built.

Patenting the wheel

In 2001, Australia started a fast-track "innovation patent". It was granted quickly, without a full check by an examiner. A patent lawyer named John Keogh thought this was a bad idea. To prove it, he applied for the wheel, describing it in fancy legal language.

It was granted. The story spread around the world, and later that year Keogh and the patent office shared an Ig Nobel Prize, a joke award for research that "makes people laugh, then think". The patent was later cancelled. Australia eventually scrapped innovation patents altogether.

A president's invention

In 1849, Abraham Lincoln, then a lawyer in Illinois, got US patent number 6,469 for a device to lift boats over sandbanks in rivers. He had seen riverboats get stuck. His idea used inflatable air chambers on the sides of a boat, pumped up to make it float higher.

It was never built for real, but a wooden model of it is kept at the Smithsonian. He is still the only US president to hold a patent. In speeches, Lincoln praised patents for adding "the fuel of interest to the fire of genius".

Who invented the wheel?The wheel came surprisingly late. People were farming, making pots and building towns for thousands of years before anyone rolled a cart.

The cat, the swing and the comb-over

In 1995, two Americans received a US patent for a "method of exercising a cat". It describes shining a laser pointer on the floor so a cat chases the dot. Plenty of cat owners had done it for years.

In 2002, a five-year-old named Steven Olson got a patent for a "method of swinging on a swing", by pulling sideways on the chains. His father, a patent lawyer, wanted to teach him about patents. The patent office later re-examined it and cancelled it. More young inventors appear in the story of kid inventors.

In 1977, a father and son from Florida patented a method of combing hair to cover a bald spot. It also earned an Ig Nobel Prize, in 2004.

Why weird patents matter

Not every strange patent is useless. Many inventions seemed silly at first: Velcro, the microwave oven and the first aeroplanes all drew laughs. But badly checked patents can cause trouble. Companies sometimes collect patents on obvious ideas and threaten to sue others, which is why patent offices try hard to reject them.

India's patent office has its own rules. Indian law doesn't allow patents on things like traditional knowledge already known for centuries. In the 1990s, a US patent on using turmeric to heal wounds was challenged by India's Council of Scientific and Industrial Research, which showed that the idea appeared in old Indian texts. The patent was cancelled in 1997.

Funny ideas, serious lessons

Odd patents remind us that inventing is part creativity and part paperwork. Many brilliant inventions never got patents, and many patents never became anything. And the laser in that cat-exercising patent is itself one of the great inventions, a story told in how lasers work.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You can only get a patent for something useful and clever.

Truth: Patent offices check whether an idea is new and not obvious, and whether it could work. They don't judge whether it's sensible, so some very silly ideas have been granted. Others, like the wheel, slipped through by mistake and were later cancelled.
