Who was Mark Twain?

He was born when Halley's Comet was in the sky, predicted he would leave with it, and died the day after it came back.

The short answer

Mark Twain was the pen name of Samuel Clemens, an American writer and humorist who lived from 1835 to 1910. He wrote The Adventures of Tom Sawyer and Adventures of Huckleberry Finn, two of the most famous American novels.

Twain wrote the way people really talked, with their slang and accents. Before him, a lot of American writers tried to sound like the English. He was also one of the funniest writers of his time. He gave lectures all over the world, and people paid to hear him tell stories.

Surprising but true

Mark Twain's pen name is a shout from the old riverboats on the Mississippi. It means the water is two fathoms deep, about 3.7 metres, and safe to steam through.

A boy on the river

Clemens grew up in Hannibal, a small town in Missouri on the banks of the Mississippi, one of the longest rivers in North America. Steamboats churned past, and every boy in town, he later wrote, wanted to work on one.

His father died when he was eleven, and he left school to work for a printer. In his twenties he trained as a steamboat pilot, memorising every sandbar and bend over more than 1,000 km of river. He got his licence in 1859. The river shaped his best books and gave him his name.

Tom and Huck

The Adventures of Tom Sawyer (1876) is about a mischievous boy in a river town much like Hannibal. In one famous scene, Tom is told to whitewash a fence. He pretends it is such fun that other boys pay him for a turn.

Adventures of Huckleberry Finn (1884) is darker. Huck, Tom's friend, runs away from his violent father and floats down the river on a raft with Jim, a man escaping slavery. As they travel, Huck slowly realises that everything his society taught him about Jim is wrong. The novelist Ernest Hemingway later said that all modern American writing comes from this one book.

The book has also caused arguments. It uses a racist word many times, because that is how people in the story spoke before the Civil War. Some schools in the US have removed it from reading lists. Others teach it as a powerful attack on racism and slavery. Readers still disagree about how it should be taught.

A traveller in India

In 1895 and 1896, deep in debt, Twain went on a lecture tour around the world. He spent about two months in India in 1896, visiting Bombay, Calcutta, Varanasi, Agra and Darjeeling. He wrote about it in his travel book Following the Equator (1897).

He was dazzled by India's crowds, colours and history, and wrote that it was the most extraordinary country under the sun. He also wrote about the Taj Mahal, the ghats of Varanasi and a ride down the mountain from Darjeeling on a little open railway car. He called that the most enjoyable day he had ever spent.

Born and gone with a comet

Twain was born in November 1835, just after Halley's Comet passed close to Earth. The comet returns about every 76 years. In 1909 he joked that he had come in with it and expected to go out with it. He died on 21 April 1910, one day after the comet's closest approach to the Sun.

Twain lived much of his later life in Hartford, Connecticut, where his house is now a museum. His quick sayings are quoted so often that many lines he never said get stuck to his name too.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Mark Twain said, "The reports of my death are greatly exaggerated."

Truth: In 1897 a newspaper wrongly reported that he was dying. He wrote that "the report of my death was an exaggeration". The punchier version became popular later.
