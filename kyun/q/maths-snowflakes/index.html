Why do snowflakes have six sides?

In 1611 an astronomer gave his friend a New Year's present that cost nothing, a little book asking why snowflakes always have six points.

The short answer

Snowflakes have six sides because of the shape of water itself. A water molecule is made of one oxygen atom and two hydrogen atoms, joined in a bent shape like a wide "V". When water freezes, those molecules link up in a pattern of rings, and each ring has six sides.

So every ice crystal begins as a tiny hexagon. As it falls through a cloud, water vapour freezes onto its six corners, which stick out the most. The corners grow into arms. Because all six arms grow in the same air at the same time, they usually end up looking alike. That gives a snowflake its six-fold symmetry.

Surprising but true

A snowflake's six arms don't talk to each other. They match because each arm grows through exactly the same air at exactly the same moment.

A gift from an astronomer

In 1611, the German astronomer Johannes Kepler wanted to give his friend a New Year's gift. He was short of money, so he wrote a little book instead. It asked why snowflakes always have six corners.

Kepler had no microscope strong enough to see molecules. He guessed the answer might lie in how tiny round pieces pack together, like cannonballs stacked on a ship. He was on the right track. It took about 300 years, and X-ray machines, for scientists to see the six-sided arrangement of molecules inside ice.

The farmer who photographed snow

Wilson Bentley was a farm boy in Jericho, Vermont, USA. In 1885, aged 19, he became the first person known to photograph a single snowflake. He attached a camera to a microscope and worked in freezing sheds, catching flakes on black velvet.

Over his life he photographed more than 5,000 snowflakes. No two of his pictures looked exactly the same. Neighbours called him "Snowflake Bentley". His pictures showed the world that six arms, again and again, were nature's rule.

Why the arms match

People often think the arms somehow copy each other. They don't. A snowflake is so small, about the width of a pencil eraser, that all its arms meet the same temperature and dampness as it tumbles. When the air gets colder, all six arms change their growth together. When it gets damper, all six sprout side branches together.

So a snowflake's shape is a record of its trip through the cloud. Two flakes take different paths, so they look different. With billions of molecules in each flake and countless possible paths, finding two exactly alike is almost impossible.

Temperature decides the shape

In the 1930s, the Japanese scientist Ukichiro Nakaya became the first to grow snowflakes in a lab. He found that temperature and dampness decide the shape. Near −2 °C, crystals form flat plates. Near −5 °C, they grow as needles and columns. Near −15 °C, in very moist air, they become the large, branching stars we draw on cards.

So skiers in Gulmarg and children in Buffalo, New York, may see totally different snow on the same day. Nakaya called snowflakes "letters from the sky", because their shape tells you about the clouds they came from. Snowflakes drift down slowly, at roughly walking pace, which gives them time to grow on the way. And although each crystal is clear ice, snow looks white because its countless tiny faces scatter every colour of light.

Hexagons everywhere

Six-sided shapes turn up all over nature. Basalt rock can crack into hexagonal columns. Bees build their honey stores from six-sided wax cells. Mathematicians have even invented a snowflake that never ends: each side keeps sprouting smaller bumps forever, a kind of fractal shape. And the strangest part? Bees chose the hexagon for a completely different reason than ice did, as the story of the honeycomb shows.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Every snowflake is a perfect six-pointed star.

Truth: Most snowflakes are lumpy, broken or stuck together in clumps. The neat stars form only in certain conditions. But underneath, the ice in every one is arranged in a six-sided pattern.

Try this

Fold a square of paper in half, then fold it into three equal wedges so you get a narrow triangle. Snip small shapes out of the edges with safety scissors and unfold it. You will get a six-sided pattern, because each cut is copied six times, just like a snowflake's arms.
