How do you become a game designer?

Shigeru Miyamoto, who created Mario and Zelda, has said that exploring caves and fields as a boy near Kyoto gave him ideas for his games.

The short answer

A game designer decides how a game works. They choose the goal, the rules, the challenges and the rewards. They decide what happens when you press a button, how hard the third level should be and what makes a player want to try just one more time.

Designers work with programmers, artists, writers and musicians. There is no single path into the job. Many studied computer science, art or design, and some studied something else entirely. What nearly all of them have in common is that they started young, by making small games of their own and showing them to other people.

Surprising but true

Many video game designers test their ideas first with paper, dice and cardboard, because it is far quicker than writing code.

What a designer actually does

The American designer Sid Meier, who made the Civilization games, has described a game as "a series of interesting decisions". A designer's job is to make those decisions interesting. Should you take the safe path or the risky one? Spend your coins now or save them?

On a big team, there are many kinds of designer. A level designer builds the maps and puzzles. A systems designer balances numbers, such as how much damage a sword does. A narrative designer works on story and characters. In a tiny studio, one person might do all three.

Paper first, code later

Designers rarely start with a computer. They sketch ideas in notebooks, cut out cardboard pieces or borrow dice from board games. A rough prototype on a table can show in an afternoon whether an idea is fun.

When the idea works, the team builds a simple digital version, often with plain grey boxes instead of characters. Most use a game engine, a ready-made toolkit that handles things like physics, lighting and sound. That lets designers try ideas without writing every line of code from scratch.

What is a game engine?The same software used to build a video game was used to create the glowing backgrounds of a Star Wars TV series, live on set as the actors performed.

Watching people play

The most important part of the job is playtesting. A designer sits behind a player and watches, without helping. Where does the player get lost? When do they look bored? What makes them laugh or sit up?

Often the answers are surprising. A puzzle the designer thought was easy might stump everyone. A small jump that was added as a joke might become everyone's favourite part. The team makes changes and tests again, sometimes hundreds of times before a game is finished.

Ideas come from everywhere

Shigeru Miyamoto grew up in a small town near Kyoto in Japan. He has said that wandering the hills, woods and caves there, and the excitement of finding something unexpected, fed into The Legend of Zelda. Tetris was made in 1984 by Alexey Pajitnov, a computer scientist in Moscow, who loved a puzzle with shapes made of squares.

Indian designers draw on their own stories. Raji: An Ancient Epic, made by a studio in Pune and released in 2020, sends a young girl through a world shaped by Indian myths and temple art. Good music and sound also shape how a game feels, so designers work closely with composers.

How to start now

You don't have to wait until you're grown up. Free tools such as Scratch let you make games with drag-and-drop blocks, and many teenagers move on to engines like Godot or Unity. Learning a little programming helps, but so do maths, art, writing and even psychology.

Game jams are events where people make a whole game in a weekend around a theme. They work much like hackathons and are a fast way to learn. Keep your games small and finish them. A short game that works teaches you more than a huge one that never ends.

Designers also play widely: card games, sports, puzzles and games they don't like. Each one shows a different way to make a decision interesting.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Game designers spend their days playing games.

Truth: They play a lot, but most of their time goes on writing ideas down, building rough versions, testing them with players and fixing what doesn't work. Much of the job is careful problem-solving and teamwork.

Try this

Make a one-page board game. Draw a path of 30 squares, add three squares with a twist (such as "swap places with another player"), and play it with someone. Afterwards, ask what was boring and what was exciting, change one rule, and play again.
