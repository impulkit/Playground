What are some African folk tales?

In a story from Ghana, a clever spider named Anansi tricked a python, a leopard and a swarm of hornets to buy every story in the world from the sky god.

The short answer

Africa has an enormous treasure of folk tales, told for thousands of years in more than 2,000 languages. Many were never written down until recently. Instead, they were spoken, sung and acted out, passed from grandparents to children around fires and in village squares.

Some characters appear again and again. There's Anansi, a crafty spider from the Akan people of Ghana. There's Tortoise, a sly schemer in Yoruba and Igbo stories from Nigeria, and Hare, a quick-witted trickster across East and Southern Africa. There are also grand epics about real kings, like Sundiata, who founded the Mali Empire about 800 years ago.

Surprising but true

Some American trickster tales about a rabbit called Brer Rabbit grew from African hare stories, carried across the Atlantic by enslaved Africans.

Anansi buys the stories

In one tale from Ghana, all the stories in the world belonged to Nyame, the sky god. Anansi asked to buy them. Nyame laughed and set a price no one could pay: bring me a python, a leopard, a swarm of hornets and a fairy who can't be seen.

Anansi tricked each one. He fooled the python into stretching along a pole to "measure" himself, then tied him up. He trapped the leopard in a pit and caught the hornets by pretending it was raining. Nyame kept his word. Ever since, Akan stories are often called anansesem, "spider stories", even when Anansi isn't in them. Real spiders aren't quite so cunning, but they are clever builders.

Tortoise goes to a feast

In a Nigerian tale, the birds were invited to a feast in the sky. Tortoise couldn't fly, so he persuaded each bird to lend him a feather. On the way, he told them that everyone at a feast takes a new name, and he chose "All of you".

When the hosts served the food, saying "This is for all of you", Tortoise ate the best of it. The angry birds took back their feathers. Tortoise had to jump down, and his shell cracked into the patchwork pattern it has today. The Nigerian writer Chinua Achebe included this story in his famous novel Things Fall Apart.

Keepers of memory

In West Africa, especially in Mali, Guinea, Senegal and Gambia, families of griots have kept history alive for centuries. A griot is a storyteller, singer and historian. Many play the kora, a harp-like instrument with 21 strings.

Griots tell the epic of Sundiata, a prince who couldn't walk as a child and was mocked for it. One day he pulled himself up using an iron bar, grew strong and later defeated a cruel sorcerer-king to found the Mali Empire. Griots still perform the epic today, and some versions take many hours to tell.

Tales that crossed the ocean

From the 1500s to the 1800s, millions of Africans were captured and taken as enslaved people to the Americas. They were forced to leave almost everything behind, but they kept their stories. Anansi became "Anancy" in Jamaica and "Aunt Nancy" in parts of the southern USA. The African hare grew into Brer Rabbit, the small trickster who outsmarts bigger animals.

These trickster tales mattered. A small creature beating a powerful one with brains was a story of hope.

Why the sky is far away

Many African stories explain how the world got its shape. In one, told in Nigeria, the sky once hung so low that people tore off pieces to eat. The sky warned them not to waste it, but people grew greedy, so it rose up high out of reach.

African tales are also loved worldwide in picture books. In 1976 Why Mosquitoes Buzz in People's Ears, a West African tale retold by Verna Aardema, won America's top award for illustration, the Caldecott Medal. And the strangest part? Trickster animals and talking-animal lessons turn up in India too, in the Panchatantra, and in Greece, in the fables of Aesop.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Africa has one set of folk tales, all much the same.

Truth: Africa has 54 countries and more than 2,000 languages, each with its own stories. A Zulu tale from South Africa can be as different from an Akan tale from Ghana as a Norse myth is from a Tamil one.

Try this

Try call-and-response storytelling, popular across Africa. Choose a short phrase your listeners must shout back whenever you pause, such as you saying "Are you there?" and them answering "We are here." Tell a short story and pause to call out every few sentences. Notice how it keeps everyone listening.
