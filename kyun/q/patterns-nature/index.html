Where do patterns appear in nature?

The man who helped crack secret wartime codes spent his last years working out how a leopard gets its spots.

The short answer

Nature is full of repeating shapes. Sunflower seeds sit in spirals. Tigers and zebras wear stripes. Leopards and cheetahs have spots. Bees build hexagons. Trees, rivers and lightning branch again and again. Sand dunes and the sea form waves.

These patterns aren't painted on by design. They grow out of simple rules. Things push and pull, spread and stop, crack and shrink, and the same shapes keep appearing because they are the easiest or most efficient result. That is why mathematicians, physicists and biologists all study them.

Surprising but true

The code-breaker and the leopard

Alan Turing is famous for helping to crack German codes during the Second World War, and for early ideas about computers. In 1952 he published a paper about something quite different: how living things get their patterns.

He imagined two chemicals spreading through a growing animal's skin. One switches on colour and also switches on more of itself. The other spreads faster and switches the first one off. Working through the maths, Turing showed that two such chemicals, left alone, could form spots or stripes all by themselves.

At the time there was little evidence. Decades later, biologists found this kind of process at work in the stripes of zebrafish and in the ridges inside a mouse's mouth. Scientists still debate how much it explains tiger stripes and other coats, but Turing's idea is now a major part of the story.

Stone columns from cooling lava

When thick lava from a volcano cools slowly, it shrinks. As it shrinks, it cracks. The cracks spread down into the rock and meet each other, often at angles of about 120 degrees. That makes tall columns, many of them six-sided.

The most famous are at the Giant's Causeway in Northern Ireland, with about 40,000 columns. In the United States, Devils Tower in Wyoming is a huge stump of them. India has its own: the St Mary's Islands, near Udupi in Karnataka. Their columns formed around 88 million years ago, when India was breaking away from Madagascar. They are a national geological monument.

Spirals that count

Look at the middle of a sunflower and you'll see two sets of spirals, one turning clockwise and one anticlockwise. Count them and you often get neighbouring numbers in the Fibonacci sequence: 34 and 55, or 55 and 89.

This happens because each new seed grows at a fixed angle from the last, about 137.5 degrees. That angle packs seeds tightly with no wasted gaps. Pine cones, pineapples and the leaves on many plants follow the same rule, as you can read in Fibonacci in nature.

Branches everywhere

Rivers split into streams, which split into smaller streams. Trees split into branches and twigs. Your lungs split into airways, then smaller ones, then tiny air sacs. Lightning forks across the sky.

Branching is a good way to reach every part of a big space from a single starting point. Each small part looks a little like the whole, a property that mathematicians call self-similarity. Shapes like these are known as fractals.

Waves and ripples

Wind blowing across sand piles it into ripples, and on a bigger scale into dunes. In the Thar Desert, dunes march slowly across the land in rows. On a beach, ripples in wet sand form where the water flows back and forth.

Even a snowflake is a pattern made by simple rules, with six arms that grow as water molecules lock into ice. The same few shapes keep turning up, in stone and in skin, because nature keeps following the same few rules.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A zebra is a white animal with black stripes.

Truth: A zebra's skin underneath its fur is dark. Its stripes form where the hair cells make pigment or don't, so scientists usually describe a zebra as dark with white stripes. Why zebras have stripes at all is still being studied.

Try this

Spread a thin layer of wet mud or a flour-and-water paste on a plate or tray and leave it somewhere warm for a few days. Watch it crack as it dries. Count how many sides the pieces have, and look at the angles where the cracks meet.
