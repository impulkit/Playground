Who was India's ‘big-leg lizard’?

Its name comes from Bengali-Hindi words: ‘bara pa’, big leg.

The short answer

Barapasaurus was one of the earliest long-necked sauropods. It lived about 190 million years ago, long before T. rex, in what is now the Godavari valley of southern India.

Scientists dug up about 300 of its bones near the Pranhita and Godavari rivers between 1958 and 1961. It was named in 1975. Like other sauropods, it walked on four pillar-like legs and ate plants all day long. At about 14 m, it was longer than a city bus, with leg bones taller than a grown-up.

Surprising but true

Barapasaurus lived more than 120 million years before Rajasaurus. That gap is far longer than the time since the dinosaurs died out.

Big leg, big find

The name means "big-leg lizard". "Bara" means big, and "pa" means leg in Bengali. The scientists who named it were from the Indian Statistical Institute in Kolkata, so a Bengali name made sense. Its thigh bone is about 1.7 m long.

The team found the bones near the village of Pochampalli, in what is now Telangana. They came from at least six animals, of different sizes, buried together in ancient river sand. Almost every part of the skeleton was found except the skull. Only teeth and a few small pieces of the head turned up. The teeth were shaped like spoons, with rough edges for stripping leaves.

A poet's dinosaur

The full name is Barapasaurus tagorei. The digging went on in the years leading up to 1961, the 100th birth anniversary of Rabindranath Tagore. The team decided to honour the poet, who was also from Bengal.

A mounted skeleton of Barapasaurus stands in the geology museum of the Indian Statistical Institute in Kolkata. Because the skull was never found, the head on display is a careful reconstruction based on other early sauropods.

Who was Rabindranath Tagore?He wrote the national anthems of two countries, won Asia's first Nobel Prize, and only started painting seriously in his sixties.

An early giant

Barapasaurus lived in the early Jurassic period, when sauropods were just starting to grow huge. Earlier relatives had been smaller and could walk on two legs. Barapasaurus walked firmly on four, with thick limbs like columns. Its neck was long, but not as long as those of later giants like Brachiosaurus or the titanosaurs.

It did not chew. It stripped leaves with its teeth and swallowed them whole, leaving its huge gut to break them down. There was no grass and not a single flower in its world. It ate conifers, ferns, cycads and other plants that reproduce without flowers.

Its backbones had hollows on the sides, an early version of the air-filled bones that let later sauropods grow even bigger without becoming too heavy. In that way, Barapasaurus is a snapshot of sauropods on their way to becoming the largest land animals ever.

India in the Jurassic

When Barapasaurus was alive, India was not where it is today. It was part of a giant southern landmass, joined to Africa, Madagascar, Antarctica and Australia. The rivers it lived beside flowed across a land with no Himalayas at all. India only broke away and began drifting north much later.

The rocks it was found in, called the Kota Formation, also hold fossil fish and some of the earliest mammals known from India.

A long time ago, even for dinosaurs

Barapasaurus lived more than 120 million years before Rajasaurus, the royal hunter of Gujarat. That is roughly twice as long as the time since the last giant dinosaurs died out. The age of dinosaurs lasted so long that the first and last dinosaurs of India never came close to meeting.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Long-necked dinosaurs like Barapasaurus were so heavy that they had to live in lakes and swamps to hold up their weight.

Truth: That was a popular idea a century ago. Their pillar-like legs, their footprints on dry land and the shape of their feet show they lived on land. Deep water would actually have made breathing hard.
