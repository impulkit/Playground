Can people make it rain?

In 1946, a scientist dropped dry ice from a plane into a cloud over Massachusetts, and snow began to fall from it within minutes.

The short answer

Sometimes, a little. Clouds are made of billions of tiny water droplets, too small and light to fall. For rain to form, droplets must grow, either by freezing into ice crystals or by bumping together into bigger drops. Each droplet or crystal needs a speck to form around, like dust or salt.

Cloud seeding adds extra specks. Planes, rockets or ground machines release tiny particles, often silver iodide or salt, into suitable clouds. If conditions are right, the cloud may drop more rain or snow than it would have. But seeding can't make clouds, and it's hard to measure how much extra rain it really brings. Scientists usually estimate somewhere between none and about 20%.

Surprising but true

Cloud seeding can't create clouds. It only works when suitable clouds are already there, which is why a much-publicised trial over Delhi in October 2025 produced only a trace of rain.

Dry ice in a freezer

The story starts in 1946 at the General Electric lab in New York State. Vincent Schaefer was studying how ice forms in clouds, using a home freezer filled with his own breath to make a mini cloud. One hot July day, he dropped dry ice into it to cool it down. Instantly, the cloud turned into thousands of sparkling ice crystals.

In November 1946, Schaefer flew a small plane over Mount Greylock in Massachusetts and dropped dry ice into a cloud. Snow fell from the cloud. His colleague Bernard Vonnegut then found that silver iodide, whose crystals are shaped much like ice, worked even better. Bernard's younger brother was the famous novelist Kurt Vonnegut, who later wrote a story about a substance that froze the world.

How much does it help?

This is the tricky part. You can't run the same cloud twice, once with seeding and once without. So scientists compare many seeded and unseeded clouds and use statistics.

In India, the Indian Institute of Tropical Meteorology ran a big experiment called CAIPEEX over Solapur, in a dry part of Maharashtra, in 2018 and 2019. Planes seeded selected monsoon clouds. Researchers reported that seeded clouds gave up to about 18% more rain, but only from the right kind of clouds, and not enough to solve a drought alone.

Delhi's experiment

In late October 2025, IIT Kanpur and the Delhi government tried cloud seeding over parts of Delhi, partly hoping rain would wash polluted air clean. Planes released seeding flares, but the clouds had very little moisture. Only a trace fell, about 0.1 to 0.2 mm at a couple of spots near Delhi, too little for official weather stations to record.

Scientists said this showed a basic rule. Seeding needs moist clouds. In Delhi's dry winter season, those are rare, and polluted air usually comes back within days after rain anyway.

Who seeds clouds?

More than 50 countries have tried cloud seeding. China runs one of the largest programmes. The United Arab Emirates seeds clouds often to boost scarce rain. In the western USA, seeding is used over mountains to add to winter snow, which melts into rivers in spring.

Some airports and ski resorts have used it to reduce fog or boost snow. Some people worry about the silver iodide, but scientists have found the amounts used are very small. A bigger worry is fairness: if one region squeezes rain from a cloud, does a neighbour get less? So far, studies haven't shown this, but the question keeps coming up.

Weather is still far too big to control. One ordinary thunderstorm holds more energy than many atomic bombs. India's farmers will keep depending on the real monsoon, not rain made to order.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Cloud seeding can make it rain whenever and wherever people want.

Truth: It only works when the right kind of clouds are already overhead, with enough moisture. Even then, it may increase rain by a modest amount. It can't end a drought or create rain from a clear blue sky.

Try this

On a cold day, breathe onto a cold mirror or window and watch it mist up. Then breathe onto a slightly dusty patch and compare. Water vapour needs surfaces to condense on. In clouds, specks of dust, salt and smoke do that job, and cloud seeding adds more.
