Who invented negative numbers?

In ancient China, accountants used red rods for money coming in and black rods for money going out, the opposite of how banks use colours today.

The short answer

Negative numbers are numbers less than zero, like −1, −5 or −273. Nobody invented them in one moment. Chinese mathematicians were using them about 2,000 years ago to solve problems about trade and money. In 628 CE, the Indian mathematician Brahmagupta wrote the first full set of rules for adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing them.

Europeans took much longer to accept them. Many thought it made no sense to have less than nothing. Today you meet negative numbers on thermometers, bank statements and in lifts that go below the ground floor.

Surprising but true

Some European mathematicians called negative numbers "absurd" as late as the 1500s and 1600s, centuries after Indian and Chinese mathematicians were using them.

Red and black rods

In ancient China, people calculated by laying small bamboo or bone rods on a board. A famous book called The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art, put together about 2,000 years ago, used red rods for positive amounts and black ones for negative amounts.

This let people solve problems with several unknowns, such as working out the price of different grains from a few market sales. The book gave rules for adding and subtracting the coloured rods, so negative amounts could appear partway through a sum and then cancel out.

Fortunes and debts

Brahmagupta, working in Rajasthan, wrote his great book in 628 CE. He called positive numbers dhana, meaning fortunes, and negative numbers rina, meaning debts. Thinking about money made the rules easy to picture. If you owe a friend 10 rupees and pay back 4, you still owe 6, so −10 + 4 = −6.

A debt taken from zero becomes a fortune. A fortune times a debt is a debt. And a debt times a debt is a fortune, which is why two minuses multiplied make a plus. Brahmagupta also wrote rules for zero, treating it as a number of its own.

"Absurd numbers"

Indian and Arab mathematics reached Europe slowly. Even in the 1500s, the German mathematician Michael Stifel called negative numbers absurd. Others called them false or fictitious. If a problem gave a negative answer, they often said it had no answer.

Gradually, bankers and merchants found negatives useful for debts. Scientists needed them for temperatures and positions. By the 1700s most mathematicians accepted them as ordinary numbers. Number lines, with negatives stretching left of zero, became common in schoolbooks much later.

Sport is full of them too. In golf, a player who takes fewer shots than expected has a score like −3, and the lowest score wins. In football league tables, a team that has let in more goals than it scored has a negative goal difference. Cricket's net run rate, used to split teams level on points in tournaments, can also dip below zero.

Below zero

Negative numbers are everywhere once you look. In winter, the temperature in Leh, in Ladakh, can drop below −20 °C. Water freezes at 0 °C, which is 32 °F, so a Delhi morning at 5 °C feels very different from one at −5 °C. Different temperature scales put zero in different places. The coldest temperature possible, called absolute zero, is −273.15 °C.

Lifts use negatives too. In some buildings a button marked −1 or B1 takes you below the ground floor, and elevators in deep metro stations go further still. The shore of the Dead Sea, between Jordan and Israel, is more than 430 metres below sea level, the lowest dry land on Earth. The salty water there makes swimmers float, as you can read in salty lakes. In the United States, Badwater Basin in Death Valley sits about 86 metres (282 feet) below sea level.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Zero is the smallest number.

Truth: Zero is the smallest counting number, but there are numbers below it. Minus one is smaller than zero, minus a hundred is smaller still, and negative numbers go on forever.

Try this

Make a number line on the floor with masking tape or chalk, marking 0 in the middle and steps from −10 to 10. Stand on 0. Walk forwards for plus and backwards for minus. Try −3 + 5, or 2 − 6, and see where you land.
