What are Meghalaya's living root bridges?

In the rainiest hills on Earth, villagers don't build bridges. They grow them, guiding the roots of a fig tree across a river, one season at a time.

The short answer

A living root bridge is a bridge grown from the roots of a living tree. In the hills of Meghalaya, in north-east India, Khasi and Jaintia villagers plant rubber fig trees on riverbanks. These trees send out aerial roots, roots that grow from the trunk and branches into the air.

Builders guide those roots across a stream, along a frame of bamboo or hollow palm trunks. When the roots reach the other side, they dig into the soil. Over years, they thicken and grow into one another, forming a living, walkable bridge. Stones are pushed into the gaps to make a path. Some bridges can carry dozens of people at once.

Surprising but true

Unlike a bridge of wood or steel, a living root bridge gets stronger as it gets older, because the roots keep thickening and fusing together.

Why grow a bridge?

Meghalaya means "abode of the clouds", and the name fits. Its southern slopes face the Bay of Bengal and catch some of the heaviest monsoon rain in the world. Towns such as Mawsynram and Cherrapunji (Sohra) often get more than 11,000 mm of rain a year.

In that much rain and damp, a wooden bridge rots in a few years. Raging monsoon streams sweep away flimsy crossings. A living bridge has the opposite problem: the wetter it is, the better the tree grows. Its roots grip the riverbanks and stay strong for decades, even centuries, as long as the tree stays healthy.

How do bridges hold up so much weight?Every bridge is a tug-of-war between two forces, a push and a pull. Good engineers decide which parts get which.

The double-decker at Nongriat

The most famous root bridge is near the village of Nongriat, a steep walk of a few thousand steps down from the nearest road. It has two levels, one above the other, both made of living roots. Visitors climb down to see it, and to swim in the blue pools below.

Researchers have counted dozens of these bridges across the Khasi and Jaintia hills, from short spans to ones longer than a cricket pitch. Some villages grow ladders, platforms and even viewing towers from roots too. In 2022, India put the root bridges on UNESCO's tentative list, the first step towards becoming a World Heritage Site.

A slow kind of engineering

Building a root bridge takes patience. It often takes 15 years or more before a new bridge is strong enough to cross, and many keep strengthening for long after. Older people start bridges that their grandchildren will use.

Engineers and architects from other countries have studied them. They are interested in how roots fuse together where they touch, making a strong mesh, and in how a living structure repairs itself. Some think the same ideas could one day help design greener structures in cities. The rubber fig is a cousin of the banyan, another tree famous for its aerial roots.

More of Meghalaya

The root bridges are only one part of Meghalaya's story. Its limestone hills hide some of the longest caves in India, carved out by rainwater over millions of years. Krem Liat Prah, one of the longest known, has passages running for more than 30 km.

The Khasi, Jaintia and Garo communities have their own languages and customs. In Khasi families, children usually take their mother's clan name. Mawlynnong, a small village near the Bangladesh border, became famous after a travel magazine called it Asia's cleanest village in 2003. Paths are swept daily and bamboo dustbins stand by the road. Above it all, the clouds roll in, as they have for as long as anyone remembers.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The root bridges are natural, and grew across the rivers by themselves.

Truth: They are made by people. Families spend years training the roots, tying, twisting and weaving them, and keep repairing the bridges over generations. Without that care, the roots would just hang or spread into the forest.

Try this

Put a sweet potato or a cutting of a money plant in a glass of water and keep it near a window. Over a few weeks, watch the roots grow. Try gently guiding one root along a straw or a lolly stick, and see whether it follows.
