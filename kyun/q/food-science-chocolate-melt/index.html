Why does chocolate melt in your mouth?

Good chocolate is built from fat crystals designed to melt at almost exactly the temperature inside your mouth, and not a few degrees below.

The short answer

Chocolate gets its melt from cocoa butter, the natural fat in cacao beans. In a well-made bar, this fat is set into tiny crystals that melt at about 34°C. Your mouth is about 36 to 37°C, slightly warmer. So on your tongue the crystals collapse, and the solid square becomes a smooth, thick liquid in seconds.

Your fingers are usually a few degrees cooler, so chocolate holds its shape in your hand for a little while. In a hot car or a summer pocket it gives up completely. Chocolate makers work hard to get that melting point just right.

Surprising but true

The fat in chocolate can form six different kinds of crystal. Only one of them gives chocolate its shine, its snap and its smooth melt.

Six kinds of crystal

Cocoa butter is fussy. As it cools, its fat molecules can stack up in six different patterns, called crystal forms. Each form has a different melting point, from about 17°C to about 36°C.

Only one, known as form V, gives everything people want. It melts at about 34°C, has a glossy shine and snaps cleanly when broken. The other forms make chocolate that is dull, soft, crumbly or greasy. Chocolate left to cool any old way ends up with a messy mix.

Tempering: training the crystals

To get form V, chocolate makers temper it. They melt it fully, cool it while stirring so crystals start to form, and then warm it slightly, to around 31°C for dark chocolate. The gentle reheating melts away the weak crystals and leaves form V, which then acts like a template, teaching the rest of the fat to line up the same way.

Get it wrong by even a degree or two and the bar turns out streaky. Professional chocolatiers check by smearing a little on a cold knife or a strip of paper and watching how it sets.

How does your body stay at the right temperature?Whether you're in a Delhi summer or a Chicago winter, the inside of your body stays within about one degree of 37°C. A tiny part of your brain makes sure of it.

Why it feels cool

Melting takes energy. As chocolate turns liquid on your tongue, it soaks up heat from your mouth, so it can feel slightly cool, like a tiny ice cube. The same thing happens when ice melts in a drink, or when sweat evaporates from your skin.

Chocolate also melts smoothly because its particles are ground very fine. In 1879, the Swiss maker Rodolphe Lindt invented conching, where liquid chocolate is churned for hours, sometimes days. Before that, chocolate bars were gritty. After it, they melted like silk.

From hot drink to solid bar

For most of its history, chocolate was not eaten at all. The Olmec, Maya and Aztecs of Mexico and Central America drank it, often frothy, bitter and spiced with chilli. Cacao beans were valuable enough to be used as money.

Europeans sweetened the drink with sugar. Then, in 1847, the English company J. S. Fry and Sons sold one of the first moulded chocolate bars. Milk chocolate followed in Switzerland in the 1870s. Today, cacao also grows in southern India, especially in Kerala, Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh, often in the shade of coconut and areca palms.

Chocolate that won't melt

Melting is a problem in hot countries. During the Second World War, the US Army asked Hershey's to make a ration bar that would not melt in a soldier's pocket. It was thick, hard and, by most accounts, not very tasty.

Modern companies have tried heat-resistant chocolates for tropical markets, usually by changing the recipe or the fats. But the melt is half the pleasure. Chocolate also contains a mild stimulant called theobromine, which is why it is poisonous to dogs. And the strangest part is where it starts: the pods grow straight out of the trunk of the cacao tree, as if someone had glued them on.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Chocolate with white or grey streaks has gone bad.

Truth: Those streaks are called bloom. Fat or sugar has risen to the surface after the chocolate got warm or damp. It looks dull and may feel crumbly, but it is safe to eat.

Try this

Put one square of chocolate in the fridge, leave one on a plate, and hold one in your closed palm. After five minutes, check all three. Then place a square on your tongue without chewing and count the seconds until it melts. Notice whether it feels cool as it goes.
