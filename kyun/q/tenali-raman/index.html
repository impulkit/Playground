Who was Tenali Raman?

A poet at the court of a great southern king became the hero of a thousand jokes, some of them played on the king himself.

The short answer

Tenali Raman was a poet who, by tradition, lived in the early 1500s at the court of Krishnadevaraya, king of Vijayanagara. In Telugu he is called Tenali Ramakrishna, and in Tamil, Tenali Raman. His name comes from Tenali, a town in today's Andhra Pradesh.

He is remembered for his wit. In the stories, he fools greedy priests, proud scholars, thieves and sometimes the king himself. The tales are told in Telugu, Tamil and Kannada homes, and in comics and TV shows across India.

Surprising but true

Tenali Raman's best-known stories are about tricks, but his surviving written work is a long and serious Telugu poem about a temple and its god.

A kingdom with clever courtiers

Vijayanagara was a powerful kingdom in southern India from the 1300s to the 1500s. Its capital, near today's Hampi in Karnataka, was one of the largest cities in the world at the time. Travellers from Portugal and Persia wrote about its markets, temples and horses.

Krishnadevaraya, who ruled from 1509 to 1529, was a soldier and also a poet. His court was famous for writers. Later tradition speaks of eight great Telugu poets there, called the Ashtadiggajas, or "eight elephants holding up the directions". Tenali Ramakrishna is usually counted among them, though historians still argue about exactly when he lived.

His best-known poem, Panduranga Mahatmyam, is about the god Vitthala, also called Panduranga, and his temple at Pandharpur in today's Maharashtra. It is long, learned and full of wordplay. Scholars of Telugu admire it. Very few of the children who laugh at his tricks have read a line of it.

The cat that hated milk

In one tale, rats are eating the city's grain. The king gives every household a cat and a cow, so the cat can drink milk and grow strong. Tenali's cat grows thin while everyone else's grows fat.

The truth is sneakier. On the first day, Tenali gave his cat a bowl of milk so hot that it burnt its mouth. After that the cat ran from milk. When the king comes to inspect, Tenali sets down a bowl, and the cat flees. The king learns that a gift with silly rules can backfire. Tenali keeps the cow.

Many stories, one hero

In another favourite, the goddess Kali offers Tenali a choice of two bowls: milk for wisdom or curd for wealth. He gulps down both. The goddess is annoyed, then amused.

Many of these tales are much older than Tenali. Similar tricks appear in stories of Akbar and Birbal in the north, and in the Panchatantra, where clever animals beat stronger ones. Storytellers often attach a good plot to the most famous trickster in the area. In the south, that trickster was Tenali. Because the tales were passed on by mouth for centuries, every region has its own versions, and the details change with each teller.

Why he still wins

The stories share a pattern. Someone powerful is proud, greedy or careless, and a sharp mind sets things right without a fight. Tenali is not always kind. He tricks people who annoy him, and sometimes the joke goes too far.

That is part of the charm. He is clever and also a little naughty, which makes him easy to like. Since the 1990s, Tenali has appeared in several television series and animated films. A poet whose serious work is read mostly by scholars is now known to millions of children for a burnt-mouthed cat.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Tenali Raman was only a court jester who told jokes.

Truth: He is remembered as one of the poets of Krishnadevaraya's court. His Telugu poem Panduranga Mahatmyam is still studied. The jester image comes mostly from folk tales and television.

Try this

Tell a Tenali story to someone younger, but stop just before the trick is revealed. Ask them to guess how Tenali gets out of trouble. You may find their answer is cleverer than the real one.
