Who pollinates our food?

Every vanilla pod in the world owes something to a 12-year-old boy on an island in the Indian Ocean in 1841.

The short answer

Many of the foods we eat come from flowers that need pollen carried from one flower to another. That job is done by pollinators. Most are insects, especially bees, but flies, butterflies, moths, beetles, birds and even bats help too. They visit flowers for sweet nectar or protein-rich pollen and get dusted with pollen as they feed.

A 2016 global report found that more than three-quarters of the leading kinds of food crops benefit at least partly from animal pollinators. Rice, wheat and maize mostly don't need them, because wind or the plant itself does the job. But many fruits, vegetables, nuts, oils and spices do.

Surprising but true

Cocoa flowers, which give us chocolate, are pollinated mainly by tiny flies called midges, smaller than a grain of rice.

Bees and more bees

There are about 20,000 kinds of bees in the world. Honeybees are the best known because people keep them in hives. India has several native honeybees, including the giant rock bee, which builds huge hanging combs on cliffs, trees and even city buildings.

Most bees don't live in hives at all. Many are solitary, nesting alone in tiny holes in the ground or in hollow stems. Bumblebees can "buzz pollinate", shaking their flight muscles so hard that pollen falls out of flowers like tomatoes and aubergines. Honeybees can't do this.

In the United States, California's almond orchards need so many bees that beekeepers truck in well over a million hives each spring. In India, apple growers in Himachal Pradesh often rent hives during flowering, because a good apple needs plenty of bee visits.

Flies, beetles, birds and bats

Flies are some of the most important pollinators after bees, especially in cooler places. Tiny midges pollinate cocoa flowers, the source of chocolate. Beetles were probably among the first pollinators, visiting flowers over 100 million years ago. Every kind of fig depends on its own tiny fig wasp.

Birds pollinate too. In the Americas, hummingbirds hover at red, tube-shaped flowers. In India, sunbirds do a similar job. Bats visit flowers that open at night, such as those of the agave in Mexico, which is used to make tequila, and the durian in Southeast Asia.

How do hummingbirds hover?A hummingbird can hang perfectly still in front of a flower, then zip backwards out of it like a tiny helicopter.

The boy who pollinated vanilla

Vanilla comes from the pod of an orchid native to Mexico. People took vanilla plants to other tropical lands, but the flowers rarely made pods. The bees that pollinate it in Mexico weren't there.

In 1841, on the island of Réunion in the Indian Ocean, an enslaved 12-year-old boy named Edmond Albius found a solution. Using a thin stick and his thumb, he could pollinate a vanilla flower by hand in seconds. His method spread across the world and is still used today. Albius gained his freedom when slavery was abolished on Réunion in 1848, but he received little reward for his discovery.

Helping pollinators

Pollinators face many problems: loss of wild flowers, pesticides, diseases and a changing climate. Anyone can help a little. Planting native flowers, leaving patches of bare soil for ground-nesting bees and keeping a shallow dish of water with pebbles all give them a boost. Even a few pots of flowers on a balcony can feed visiting bees.

Farmers can help too, by leaving hedges and strips of wild flowers between fields. In parts of China where wild bees became scarce, some apple and pear growers have had to pollinate blossoms by hand, with brushes and feathers. It is slow, costly work, and it shows how much free help pollinators normally give.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Honeybees do almost all the pollinating.

Truth: Honeybees are important farm pollinators, but there are about 20,000 kinds of bees, plus flies, butterflies, moths, beetles, birds and bats. Wild pollinators do a large share of the work, and for some crops they do better than honeybees.

Try this

Find a flowering plant in a garden or park on a sunny morning. Watch it from a safe distance for ten minutes and count every visitor, from bees and flies to butterflies, beetles and birds. Write down which flowers each one visits. You may be surprised how many visitors are not bees at all.
