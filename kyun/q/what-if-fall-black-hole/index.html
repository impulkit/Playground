What would happen if you fell into a black hole?

Fall into a small black hole and you'd be stretched like a noodle, but fall into a giant one and you might not notice the edge at all.

The short answer

A black hole is a place where so much matter is squeezed into so small a space that its gravity stops even light from escaping. Its edge is called the event horizon. Cross it, and there is no way back out, not even at the speed of light.

What happens to you depends on the size of the black hole. A small one, a few times heavier than the Sun, would stretch you into a long, thin strand before you reached the edge. Scientists really call this "spaghettification". A giant one, millions of times heavier than the Sun, would let you sail through the edge without feeling a thing. After that, nobody knows for sure.

Surprising but true

To a friend watching from far away, you would never seem to fall in. You'd appear to slow down, freeze at the edge and slowly fade red.

Stretched like a noodle

Gravity gets stronger the closer you are. Falling feet first, your feet would be pulled harder than your head. On Earth the difference is far too tiny to feel. The Moon's version of it is what raises the tides.

Near a small black hole, the difference is enormous. Your body would be stretched lengthwise and squeezed from the sides, like toothpaste out of a tube. This would happen hundreds of kilometres before the edge, so you'd never even see the event horizon up close.

The gentle giant

At the centre of our Milky Way is a black hole called Sagittarius A*, about four million times heavier than the Sun. Its event horizon lies about 12 million kilometres from its centre, roughly 30 times the distance from Earth to the Moon.

Because the edge is so far out, the stretching there is mild. You could, in theory, drift across it and feel nothing special at that moment. The universe behind you would look bent and brightened as light from outside piled in. The stretching would come later, as you fell deeper.

What your friend would see

Black holes bend time as well as space, an idea that comes from Einstein's theory of gravity. The stronger the gravity, the slower clocks tick compared with clocks far away.

So a friend watching from a safe distance would see something strange. You'd seem to fall slower and slower as you neared the edge, then freeze there. Your light would stretch into redder and dimmer colours until you faded from view. From your side, though, you'd fall straight in.

How close is the nearest?

There's no danger of falling into one by accident. One of the closest known black holes, called Gaia BH1, was found in 2022 about 1,560 light-years away. Light leaving it today won't reach Earth for more than 1,500 years. Sagittarius A* is much further, about 27,000 light-years from us.

Astronomers think our galaxy may hold around 100 million black holes. Most are invisible, because nothing nearby is falling in to make them glow.

Inside, where physics runs out

Our best theories say everything inside falls towards the centre, where matter is crushed into a point of endless density called a singularity. Most physicists suspect that answer is a sign our theories break down there, rather than the full truth. A theory that joins gravity with the physics of tiny particles might explain it, but nobody has one yet.

We do know black holes are real. In 2019 astronomers released the first image of one, in the galaxy M87, and in 2022 they photographed Sagittarius A*. Both look like glowing orange rings around a dark middle.

And the strangest part? Many black holes are the leftover cores of giant stars that exploded, which is why their story starts with how stars live and die.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Black holes are cosmic vacuum cleaners that suck in everything around them.

Truth: From far away, a black hole pulls like any other object of the same mass. If the Sun became a black hole of the same mass, Earth would keep orbiting just as before, only in the cold and dark.

Try this

Show stretching with a slinky or a long rubber band. Hold one end still and pull the other end gently, then hard. The far end moves much more than the near end. That difference in pull along a body is what scientists call a tidal force.
