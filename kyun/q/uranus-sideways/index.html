Why does Uranus spin on its side?

Uranus rolls around the Sun like a ball, so each of its poles gets about 42 years of daylight followed by 42 years of night.

The short answer

Most planets spin like slightly tilted tops. Earth leans over by about 23 degrees. Uranus is tipped by about 98 degrees, so it lies almost on its side and rolls around the Sun like a ball rolling along a track.

Nobody saw this happen, so nobody knows for sure why. The most popular idea is that, billions of years ago, something about the size of Earth crashed into young Uranus and knocked it over. Other scientists think several smaller crashes, or the long, slow pull of a moon that no longer exists, might have done the job.

Surprising but true

Uranus was spotted by astronomers at least as early as 1690, but they all mistook it for a faint star.

A planet found by accident

On 13 March 1781, the musician and astronomer William Herschel was studying stars from his garden in Bath, England. He was using a telescope he had built himself. One "star" looked like a tiny disc instead of a point, and over the next nights it moved.

Herschel first thought it was a comet. Other astronomers worked out its path and realised it was a planet, the first discovered with a telescope. Herschel wanted to name it after King George III. In the end the name Uranus won, after the Greek god of the sky.

The discovery doubled the size of the known solar system overnight, because Uranus is about twice as far from the Sun as Saturn. The king gave Herschel a yearly salary so that he could give up music and study the sky full time.

Forty-two years of daylight

Because Uranus is on its side, its seasons are extreme. A year there lasts about 84 Earth years. For about half of that, the north pole points roughly towards the Sun and never sees night. For the other half, it sits in darkness while the south pole gets the sunshine.

Picture a child born at Uranus's north pole at the start of its long summer. They would be in their forties before they saw a sunset. Only near the equinoxes, when the Sun shines on Uranus's middle, does the planet get something like ordinary days and nights, each lasting about 17 hours.

An ice giant

Uranus is about four times as wide as Earth. Astronomers call it an ice giant. Underneath its clouds is a deep, hot, slushy layer of water, ammonia and methane, much thicker than any ocean on Earth. Methane gas high in its air soaks up red light, giving the planet its pale blue-green colour.

It is also bitterly cold. Uranus has recorded the lowest temperature of any planet's atmosphere, about −224°C. That puzzles scientists, because its near twin, Neptune, is even farther from the Sun but gives off much more heat from inside.

One quick visit

Only one spacecraft has ever been to Uranus. Voyager 2 flew past in January 1986. In a few hours of close-up work it measured the planet's magnetic field and found ten new moons. Uranus now has more than two dozen known moons. Most are named after characters from the plays of William Shakespeare, such as Miranda, Titania and Oberon, with a couple from a poem by Alexander Pope.

Uranus also has 13 known rings, first detected in 1977. They are thin and dark, very different from Saturn's bright icy ones.

Scientists in the United States have named a mission to orbit Uranus as a top priority. If it flies, it would be the first proper look at this tilted world in more than half a century.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Uranus has always been known, like the other planets.

Truth: The ancients knew five planets besides Earth, all bright enough to see easily. Uranus is so faint that it was recorded as a star until William Herschel noticed in 1781 that it moved.

Try this

In a dark room, mark the top and bottom of an orange as its poles. Shine a torch on it from across a table, then walk the orange in a slow circle around the torch, keeping it tipped right over on its side. Watch how one pole faces the light for half the trip, then the other.
