How many languages does India speak?

In the 2011 census, Indians gave more than 19,000 different names for their mother tongue.

The short answer

It depends how you count. In the 2011 census, Indians named 19,569 different mother tongues. Many were the same language under different names, or local dialects. After grouping them, the census listed 121 languages spoken by at least 10,000 people each.

India's Constitution gives special recognition to 22 languages in its Eighth Schedule, from Assamese to Urdu. Hindi and English are used for the work of the central government, and each state chooses its own official languages.

A separate count, the People's Linguistic Survey of India, found about 780 languages. Whichever number you pick, India is one of the most linguistically varied countries on Earth.

Surprising but true

Indian banknotes show their value in 15 languages on a panel at the back, as well as in Hindi and English.

Four big families

Most Indian languages belong to four families, groups of languages descended from a common ancestor. Indo-Aryan languages, such as Hindi, Bengali, Marathi, Gujarati and Punjabi, are spoken across the north, west and east. Dravidian languages, including Tamil, Telugu, Kannada and Malayalam, are spoken mostly in the south.

Austroasiatic languages include Santali and Mundari in central and eastern India and Khasi in Meghalaya. Tibeto-Burman languages, such as Bodo, Manipuri and many Naga languages, are spoken across the Himalaya and the north-east.

A few languages fit no family at all. Nihali, spoken by a small community on the border of Maharashtra and Madhya Pradesh, seems unrelated to any other language. On the Andaman Islands, the Great Andamanese languages now have only a handful of speakers.

Counting a country's voices

From the 1890s to 1928, an Irish scholar named George Grierson led the first Linguistic Survey of India. His team collected samples of hundreds of languages and dialects, often by asking local people to translate the same short story, the Biblical parable of the Prodigal Son. Gramophone recordings made for the survey can still be heard today.

Every ten years, the census asks each person their mother tongue. In 2011, Hindi came first, with about 53 crore speakers, followed by Bengali, Marathi, Telugu and Tamil. Many Indians speak two or three languages every day: one at home, one at school and one at the market or on the cricket field.

Languages that drew the map

After independence, people in many regions asked for states based on the language they spoke. In 1952, Potti Sriramulu fasted for a separate Telugu-speaking state and died after 58 days without food. His death led to the creation of Andhra State in 1953. Three years later, the States Reorganisation Act of 1956 redrew much of India's map along language lines.

That is why Karnataka is largely Kannada-speaking, Kerala largely Malayalam-speaking, and so on. It is also why an ordinary Indian banknote is a small lesson in languages, with its value printed in 15 of them on a panel at the back.

Languages in danger

Some of India's languages have tens of millions of speakers. Others have only a few hundred, often elders in a single village. When children stop learning a language, it can vanish within a generation, taking songs, stories and knowledge of local plants and animals with it. In 2009, UNESCO listed nearly 200 Indian languages as vulnerable or endangered.

Communities are fighting back. Santali now has its own script, Ol Chiki, created in 1925 by Raghunath Murmu, and is taught in schools. Linguists are recording elders' speech, and young people are making videos, songs and dictionaries in their own endangered languages.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Everyone in India speaks Hindi.

Truth: Hindi is the most spoken language, but in 2011 well over half of Indians named a different mother tongue. Many people in the south and north-east speak little or no Hindi, using their state language and English instead.

Try this

Make a language map of your class or building. Ask each person which languages their family speaks at home, at school and with grandparents. Draw a chart with a bar for each language. Count how many people speak two or more. You may be surprised how many languages share one corridor.
