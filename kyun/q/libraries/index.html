How did libraries begin?

One of the oldest libraries ever found was made of clay. When enemies burned it, the fire baked the tablets hard and helped them survive.

The short answer

Libraries began soon after writing did. In ancient Mesopotamia, temples and palaces kept collections of clay tablets: records, prayers, laws, stories and lists of words. As kingdoms grew, rulers collected texts to look powerful and wise.

For most of history, libraries were private. They belonged to kings, temples, monasteries and universities, and only a few scholars could use them. Public libraries, open to anyone for free, are a much newer idea, mostly from the last 200 years.

Surprising but true

The US Library of Congress was burned by British troops in 1814. Thomas Jefferson then sold the government his own collection of about 6,500 books to restart it.

The king who collected clay

In the 600s BCE, King Ashurbanipal of Assyria gathered a huge library at his capital, Nineveh, in today's northern Iraq. He sent scribes across his empire to copy texts on medicine, astronomy, magic, history and poetry. One copy of the Epic of Gilgamesh, among the world's oldest stories, comes from there.

The tablets were written in cuneiform, a script of wedge-shaped marks pressed into clay. Many ended with a short note, called a colophon, saying that they belonged to the king. In 612 BCE enemies sacked Nineveh. Its tablets lay buried until archaeologists dug them up in the 1800s, and many are now in the British Museum in London.

Alexandria and Nalanda

About 300 BCE, the Greek rulers of Egypt founded a great library at Alexandria. They wanted a copy of every important book in the known world. A famous story says ships in the harbour had their books copied, and the owners were given back copies while the library kept the originals.

Nobody knows exactly how many scrolls it held. Numbers from ancient writers vary wildly, and the great lost libraries attract plenty of legends.

In India, the Buddhist university of Nalanda in Bihar had a famous library, remembered by the name Dharmaganja. Students and monks came from China, Korea and Tibet to study and copy texts on palm leaves. Nalanda was attacked and ruined around 1200 CE, and much of its learning was lost. Its ruins are now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

What happened to the Library of Alexandria?The ancient world's most famous library did not vanish in a single dramatic fire. It faded away over hundreds of years.

Books for everyone

Libraries open to the public grew as printing made books cheaper. In 1731, Benjamin Franklin and friends in Philadelphia set up the Library Company, where members shared the cost of books. In the late 1800s and early 1900s, the American businessman Andrew Carnegie paid for more than 2,500 library buildings around the world.

The Library of Congress in Washington, D.C., was founded in 1800. British troops burned the Capitol, and the library inside it, in 1814. Thomas Jefferson sold Congress his personal collection to rebuild it. Today it is one of the largest libraries in the world, with well over 100 million items.

How librarians find things

A big library is useless if nobody can find anything. In 1876, the American librarian Melvil Dewey published a system that gives every subject a number, using decimals to split topics more finely. Science books might sit in the 500s, with animals around 590.

In India, the mathematician-librarian S. R. Ranganathan invented another system, Colon Classification, and wrote the Five Laws of Library Science in 1931. The first is simple: "Books are for use." His birthday, 12 August, is celebrated as National Librarians' Day in India.

Today, libraries also lend e-books, offer computers and help people sort good information from bad. A modern library catalogue works a bit like a search engine, but every entry was checked by a person.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Library of Alexandria was destroyed in one great fire.

Truth: Julius Caesar's fire in 48 BCE may have burned some books, but most historians think the library faded slowly over centuries, as funding stopped and the city went through wars.

Try this

Organise a shelf of books at home like a librarian. Sort them into groups, such as stories, science and history, and give each group a number. Write the number on a small slip in each book, then ask someone to find a book using only your system.
