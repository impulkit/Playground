Does it rain glass on some planets?

On a blue planet 64 light-years away, the wind may blow at about 7,000 km/h, and the rain could be made of glass.

The short answer

Probably, yes. In 2013, the Hubble Space Telescope measured the colour of a planet called HD 189733b and found it was a deep cobalt blue, a bit like Earth seen from space. But the blue doesn't come from oceans. Astronomers think it comes from tiny bits of silicate, the stuff glass is made from, floating in its clouds.

The planet is a giant, bigger than Jupiter, and it circles its star so closely that one year lasts just over two days. Its air is around 1,000 °C. If those glass particles fall as rain, they would be whipped sideways by winds estimated at about 7,000 km/h, about eight times faster than a passenger jet.

Surprising but true

On Venus, it rains sulphuric acid, but the drops evaporate in the hot air long before they reach the ground.

How do we know, from 64 light-years?

Nobody has seen the rain. Astronomers study planets like this when they pass in front of their star. Some starlight shines through the planet's thin outer air on the way to us, and different chemicals block different colours. By reading what is missing, scientists can guess what the air is made of, a method explained more in the story of exoplanets.

That's why the glass rain is an educated guess, not a photograph. In 2024, the James Webb Space Telescope found hydrogen sulphide on the same planet, the gas that makes rotten eggs smell. So this blue world might also stink.

Rain of iron and diamonds

HD 189733b isn't the only strange weather report. On WASP-76b, about 640 light-years away, one side always faces its star and reaches over 2,000 °C, hot enough to turn iron into a gas. In 2020, astronomers reported signs that iron vapour drifts to the cooler night side, condenses and may fall as iron rain.

Closer to home, scientists think deep inside Neptune and Uranus, crushing pressure may squeeze carbon into diamonds that sink like hailstones. Lab experiments with powerful lasers have made tiny diamonds from similar chemicals. The details live in the tale of diamond rain.

Is there a planet made of diamonds?In 2012, astronomers suggested that a scorching planet 41 light-years away might be one-third diamond. Then the arguments began.

Weird rain in our own solar system

On Venus, clouds of sulphuric acid drop acid rain. But the planet's surface is around 465 °C, so the drops evaporate long before landing. Rain that never reaches the ground has a name on Earth too: virga, those grey streaks you sometimes see hanging under clouds.

Saturn's moon Titan is colder than any freezer, about −180 °C. There, methane and ethane, which are gases on Earth, fall as rain. The drops are thought to be bigger than Earth raindrops and fall slowly, drifting down like snowflakes because Titan's gravity is weak and its air is thick. The rain fills real lakes and rivers.

Why Earth's rain is special

Earth's rain is water because our planet sits at just the right temperature for water to be a gas, a liquid and a solid. Move Earth much closer to the Sun and the oceans would boil. Move it farther away and they would freeze.

Every planet has its own "rain" that matches its temperature. Super-hot worlds rain rock and metal. Freezing worlds rain chemicals we use as fuel. On ours, it is the plain water cycle that fills wells and rivers, described in the story of clouds and rain. And astronomers say that HD 189733b's rotten-egg air is one of the strangest smells in space ever detected.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Rain is always made of water.

Truth: Rain just means liquid or solid drops falling from clouds. On Earth it is water, but on other worlds it may be methane, sulphuric acid, iron or even glass, depending on how hot or cold the planet is.

Try this

Put a few drops of food colouring on a plate and blow on them through a straw, gently and then hard. Watch how the drops streak sideways when the "wind" is strong. Now picture winds hundreds of times stronger, carrying drops of melted rock.
