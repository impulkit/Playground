How is chocolate made in a factory?

Chocolate starts as bitter, purple seeds wrapped in sweet white pulp, and they must rot a little before they taste of chocolate at all.

The short answer

Chocolate is made from the seeds of the cacao tree. On the farm, the seeds, called beans, are scooped out of their pods with the white pulp, left in heaps to ferment for about a week, and then dried in the sun. Only after this do they start to develop the flavour of chocolate.

At the factory, the beans are roasted, cracked and stripped of their shells. The pieces, called nibs, are ground into a thick paste called cocoa mass. Sugar, extra cocoa butter and sometimes milk powder are mixed in. Machines grind and stir the mixture for hours to make it smooth. Finally it is tempered, poured into moulds and cooled into bars.

Surprising but true

Cocoa butter melts at just below body temperature, which is why a good chocolate melts in your mouth but stays solid in the wrapper.

Seeds from the rainforest

The cacao tree grows in hot, wet places near the equator, in the shade of taller trees. Its pods sprout straight from the trunk.

Cacao comes from the upper Amazon. Scientists found traces of it in pots in Ecuador about 5,300 years old. Later the Olmec, Maya and Aztec peoples of Mexico and Central America drank cacao as a bitter, frothy drink, often spiced with chilli. Aztecs even used cacao beans as money.

Who were the Aztecs and the Maya?The Aztec capital stood on an island in a lake, with floating gardens, and it was bigger than any city in Spain when the Spanish arrived.

Fermenting and drying

Fresh cacao beans are bitter and don't taste of chocolate at all. On the farm, the beans and pulp are piled under banana leaves or put in wooden boxes. Microbes feed on the sugary pulp, the pile heats up, and chemical changes begin inside the beans.

After about five to seven days, the beans are spread out to dry. Then they are packed into sacks and shipped to factories. Most of the world's cacao is grown by small farmers in West Africa, especially Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana.

Inside the factory

In 1828, the Dutch chemist Coenraad van Houten invented a press that squeezed out cocoa butter, the pale fat in cacao, leaving cocoa powder. In 1847, the English company J.S. Fry & Sons mixed cocoa powder, sugar and cocoa butter to make the first solid chocolate bar.

In 1875, the Swiss chocolate maker Daniel Peter added condensed milk, made by his neighbour Henri Nestlé, to create milk chocolate. In 1879, Rodolphe Lindt invented the conche, a machine that stirs liquid chocolate for hours or days. Conching makes chocolate smooth and mellows its flavour. This was all part of the age of factory machines, the Industrial Revolution.

The perfect snap

Cocoa butter can harden into six different crystal shapes. Only one gives chocolate a glossy shine and a crisp snap. Factories temper chocolate by heating it, cooling it, and warming it slightly, so the right crystals form.

If a bar gets too warm and then cools, the wrong crystals grow and white streaks appear on the surface. This is called bloom. It looks odd but is safe to eat.

Chocolate in India

Cacao is grown in India too, mainly in Kerala, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu, often among coconut and areca palms. India grows only a small share of the world's cacao, but chocolate sales have grown fast.

The hard side of chocolate

Many cocoa farmers in West Africa earn very little. Researchers and charities have found that many children there work on cocoa farms, some doing dangerous jobs with machetes or chemicals, instead of going to school. Chocolate companies and governments have promised to end this, but progress has been slow.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A little chocolate is a harmless treat for a dog.

Truth: Chocolate contains theobromine, which dogs process very slowly. Even small amounts can make a dog ill, and dark chocolate is the most dangerous. If a dog eats chocolate, call a vet.

Try this

Take two small squares of chocolate. Put one on a plate and hold the other between your fingers in your closed palm for two minutes. Which melts first? Then put a square in your mouth without chewing and count how long it takes to melt.
