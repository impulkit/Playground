How did people make the first maps?

The oldest map of the whole world fits in your hand, and it is made of clay.

The short answer

People made maps long before they had paper. The oldest known map of the whole world is a small clay tablet from Babylon, in what is now Iraq, made about 2,600 years ago. It shows Babylon in the middle, a river running through it, and a ring of salty ocean around the edge.

Early maps were drawn from what travellers remembered and what merchants said. Over time, people learned to measure angles and distances, and maps became less like stories and more like accurate pictures of the ground. Today a satellite does the looking, but the big ideas are thousands of years old.

Surprising but true

In 1154, the mapmaker al-Idrisi drew a famous map of the known world for a king of Sicily, and he put south at the top.

Maps made of memory

The Babylonian tablet is not accurate, and it was never meant to be. It is a picture of how its makers saw their world: their great city at the centre, and strange far-off lands beyond the sea.

Other peoples made very different maps. In the Marshall Islands, navigators tied palm ribs and shells into stick charts that showed how ocean swells bend around islands. Many Indigenous Australian groups kept maps in songs, where each verse named the next waterhole on a long walk. A map, it turns out, is anything that helps you get from here to there.

The Greeks add maths

About 2,200 years ago, a scholar named Eratosthenes, who ran the great library in Alexandria, worked out the size of the Earth. He compared the length of shadows in two Egyptian cities at noon and used simple geometry. His answer was close to the real figure.

Around 150 CE, Claudius Ptolemy wrote a book called Geography. He listed thousands of places with two numbers each, much like the latitude and longitude we use now. His idea of a grid of lines over the Earth is the reason two numbers, called coordinates, can pin down any spot.

A map with south on top

In 1154, the scholar al-Idrisi finished a world map for King Roger II of Sicily. He gathered reports from sailors and traders for about 15 years. His map put south at the top, which was normal for many mapmakers in the Islamic world.

Which way is "up" on a map is a choice, not a rule. North went on top in Europe much later, partly because of the compass.

Measuring a whole country

In 1802, the British began the Great Trigonometrical Survey of India near Chennai. Teams carried a huge instrument called a theodolite, weighing about half a tonne, to measure angles between hilltops. Step by step, they built a chain of triangles across the subcontinent.

The survey took decades. In 1852, an Indian mathematician named Radhanath Sikdar, working on the survey's figures, found that a peak called Peak XV was the highest anywhere. It was later named Mount Everest, after a former head of the survey. The mountain became a target for climbers, which you can read about in the first climb of Everest.

Naming a new continent

In 1507, a German mapmaker named Martin Waldseemüller printed a huge world map on 12 sheets. It was the first map to use the name "America", after the explorer Amerigo Vespucci. Only one copy survives. The Library of Congress in Washington bought it in 2003, and it is on display there.

Paper maps still matter, but most journeys now begin on a screen. Online maps mix satellite photos with data from millions of phones, so they can show a traffic jam on a Mumbai flyover or a Texas highway within minutes.

Myth or fact?

Myth: People in the Middle Ages thought the Earth was flat, and their maps showed it.

Truth: Greek thinkers had shown the Earth was round more than 2,000 years ago, and educated people in medieval Europe, the Islamic world and India knew it. Round-world maps were simply hard to draw on flat parchment.
