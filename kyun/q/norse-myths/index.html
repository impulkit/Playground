What are the Norse myths?

Four days of your week are named after gods worshipped by the Vikings, including a red-bearded thunder god with a magic hammer.

The short answer

The Norse myths are the stories of the gods worshipped by the peoples of Scandinavia, the lands now called Norway, Sweden and Denmark, and later Iceland. These were the people we often call Vikings. Their gods included Odin the wise, Thor the thunderer, and Loki the shape-changing trickster.

The myths tell how the world was made from the body of a giant, and how the gods fought giants and monsters. They also tell how it would all end, in a great battle called Ragnarok. Most of the stories survive because a few Icelandic writers recorded them about 800 years ago.

Surprising but true

Almost everything we know about the Norse gods was written down in Iceland in the 1200s, more than 200 years after most Icelanders had become Christian.

Gods of a hard land

Odin was the chief god, a wanderer who gave up one of his eyes to drink from a well of wisdom. Two ravens, Huginn and Muninn, whose names mean "thought" and "memory", flew round the world each day and told him what they saw.

Thor, Odin's son, was strong and short-tempered. With his hammer, Mjolnir, he protected gods and humans from giants. People wore small hammer-shaped charms, and archaeologists have found many of them. Freya was a goddess of love, gold and magic who rode in a chariot pulled by cats.

Loki is the hardest to pin down. Sometimes he helps the gods out of trouble with his cleverness. Sometimes he causes the trouble, and in the end he fights against them.

Nine worlds on one tree

At the centre of the Norse universe stood Yggdrasil, a giant ash tree. Its branches and roots linked nine worlds. Asgard was home of the gods, Midgard the home of humans, and Jotunheim the land of giants. A rainbow bridge called Bifrost joined Asgard and Midgard.

A squirrel was said to run up and down the trunk carrying insults between an eagle at the top and a dragon gnawing the roots. Details like this show that the myths could be funny as well as grand. In another story, giants steal Thor's hammer, and Thor gets it back by dressing up as a bride, veil and all.

Who were the Vikings?Vikings reached North America about 500 years before Columbus, and a small bronze Buddha from South Asia turned up on an island in Sweden.

Saved in Iceland

The Norse did write, using letters called runes, but runes were mostly carved as short messages on stone, wood or bone. The long stories were passed on by poets who learned them by heart.

After Scandinavia and Iceland became Christian, around the year 1000, the old poems could easily have been forgotten. In about 1220, an Icelandic chieftain and scholar called Snorri Sturluson wrote the Prose Edda, partly as a guide for young poets who needed to understand the old stories. A collection of older poems, now called the Poetic Edda, survives in a manuscript from the late 1200s. Most of what we know about Norse myths comes from these two works.

Thor, Odin and the end of the world

The myths end with Ragnarok, the "fate of the gods". Winter lasts three years, monsters break free, and Thor and the great serpent of the sea kill each other. Then a new green world rises from the water, and a few gods and two humans survive to begin again.

The stories have travelled far. Just as the planets carry Roman names from the Greek myths, English weekdays carry Norse ones. Thor became a Marvel comic hero in 1962 and later a film star, part of the long story of superheroes. The comic Thor has blond hair, but the Thor of the old poems was usually described with a red beard.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Vikings wore helmets with horns on them.

Truth: No Viking-age helmet with horns has ever been found. The horned look became popular in the 1800s, partly through costumes for Richard Wagner's operas based on Germanic myths.
