Why are so many Indians vegetarian?

India has more vegetarians than any other country, but the reasons, and the numbers, change a great deal from state to state.

The short answer

For many Indian vegetarians, the main reason is ahimsa, the idea of not harming living beings. It is central to Jainism and important in Buddhism and many Hindu traditions. Over centuries it shaped what families cooked, and many kept the habit for generations.

Other reasons play a part too: family custom, caste and community traditions, cost, health and simple taste. But vegetarianism is far from universal. A 2021 survey found that about four in ten Indian adults call themselves vegetarian. The rest eat some meat, fish or eggs, and the mix varies hugely from one state to the next.

Surprising but true

Most Indians are not vegetarian. Even so, India probably has more vegetarians than the rest of the world put together.

An old idea about harm

The idea of ahimsa is very old in India. Jain teachers, including Mahavira about 2,500 years ago, taught that every living thing has a soul and should not be hurt. Many Jains today avoid meat, fish and eggs, and some also skip onions, garlic and potatoes, because pulling up roots can harm tiny creatures in the soil.

Who was Mahavira?Some Jain monks sweep the ground before every step so they won't crush an ant. The idea goes back to a teacher who lived 2,500 years ago.

The Buddha also spoke against harming animals. Emperor Ashoka, after turning to Buddhism, had rock edicts carved that limited the killing of animals, and said his own kitchen would cut back on meat.

Many Hindu views

Hinduism has no single rule about meat. Some Hindu communities have been strictly vegetarian for centuries, often for religious reasons, while many others eat fish, chicken or mutton. In Bengal, fish is part of many festival meals.

Many Hindus avoid beef because the cow is treated as sacred, and others avoid meat on certain days of the week or during festivals like Navratri. So one person might be vegetarian on Tuesdays and not on Sundays.

Milk, not meat

Most Indian vegetarians are lacto-vegetarians. They avoid meat, fish and often eggs, but eat milk, curd, ghee and paneer. That is different from vegans, who avoid all animal foods.

Indian vegetarian cooking is rich because it had to be. Protein comes from pulses, dairy and nuts. Spices, souring agents and many vegetables keep meals interesting. A Gujarati or Udupi thali can hold a dozen dishes without a gram of meat.

Green dot, brown mark

Packaged food in India carries a symbol by law: a green dot inside a green square for vegetarian, and a brown mark for non-vegetarian. Visitors are often surprised to see it on biscuits, chips and soft drinks. In the United States, no such mark is required, and vegetarians check ingredient lists instead.

A map that changes colour

If you coloured a map of India by how many people are vegetarian, it would be patchy. Surveys find the highest shares in Rajasthan, Gujarat, Haryana and Punjab. Along the coasts and in the east, where rivers and the sea provide plenty of fish, the shares are much lower.

In Kerala, West Bengal, Odisha and most of the Northeast, only a small minority avoid meat and fish. Even within one city, a Marwari family and a Malayali family living next door may eat very differently. Food habits follow history, religion and geography all at once.

Vegetarians around the world

About 5% of American adults told Gallup in 2018 that they were vegetarian. In the West, people often give up meat for the environment, animal welfare or health. A young Mohandas Gandhi, studying law in London from 1888, joined the London Vegetarian Society and wrote for its magazine. He found that English vegetarians had very different reasons from his family back in Gujarat, though they ended up at the same dinner table.

Myth or fact?

Myth: All Indians are vegetarian.

Truth: Most Indians eat at least some meat, fish or eggs. Vegetarianism is very common in states such as Rajasthan, Gujarat and Haryana, and much less common in Kerala, West Bengal and the Northeast.
