Who was Charles Dickens?

At twelve, Charles Dickens was sticking labels on pots of boot polish in a rat-infested factory. He grew up to become the most famous writer of his age.

The short answer

Charles Dickens was an English novelist who lived from 1812 to 1870. He wrote Oliver Twist, A Christmas Carol, David Copperfield, Great Expectations and many more. In his lifetime he was the most popular writer in the English-speaking world, and people queued to buy each new instalment of his stories.

Dickens wrote about London in the time of the Industrial Revolution, when the city was crowded, smoky and unfair to the poor. He created hundreds of unforgettable characters, from the miser Scrooge to the pickpocket the Artful Dodger. His stories are funny and sad at once, and they made readers angry about cruelty to children.

Surprising but true

Most of Dickens's novels first came out in cheap monthly parts, like episodes of a TV series, so readers waited weeks to find out what happened next.

The boy in the blacking factory

When Charles was twelve, his father was sent to the Marshalsea, a prison for people who could not pay their debts. Much of the family went with him, as was common then. Charles was sent to work at Warren's Blacking, a factory near the River Thames. For about ten hours a day he pasted labels on pots of boot polish.

He worked there for about a year, but he never forgot the shame and loneliness. He kept it secret for most of his life, even from his own children. Many of his books are about children who are abandoned, overworked or treated badly.

Stories in instalments

Dickens's first big success, The Pickwick Papers, came out in 1836 and 1837 in monthly parts. Each part was a slim booklet with green paper covers and two pictures. It cost one shilling, much less than a full book.

That way of publishing made reading cheaper, and it made Dickens a celebrity. Families who couldn't afford books would club together or listen as one person read aloud. He also published stories in weekly magazines that he edited himself. Writing in parts meant ending chapters on cliffhangers, a trick now used by every TV drama.

A Christmas Carol

In 1843, short of money, Dickens wrote A Christmas Carol in about six weeks. It tells of Ebenezer Scrooge, a mean old businessman visited by three ghosts on Christmas Eve, who wakes up a kinder man.

The book was an instant hit. It helped make Christmas in Britain and America a festival of family, feasting and giving to the poor. Scrooge's name became an everyday word for a stingy person, and the story has been filmed dozens of times, once with the Muppets.

Fighting for the poor

Dickens used his fame to campaign. He visited schools, prisons and workhouses, and wrote about what he saw in his novels and magazine articles. He supported a home for homeless women and spoke up for education for poor children. Britain later passed laws limiting child labour and making schooling compulsory, and many readers felt Dickens had helped change attitudes.

He was not a perfect man. He separated from his wife, Catherine, in 1858 and treated her unkindly in public. Some of his characters, like Fagin in Oliver Twist, are now seen as unfair stereotypes, and he changed parts of that book after a reader complained.

He visited America twice. On the second trip, in 1867 and 1868, he gave hugely popular public readings, acting out all the voices. When he died in 1870, he was buried in Westminster Abbey, in a corner reserved for writers called Poets' Corner. His last novel, The Mystery of Edwin Drood, was left half-written, and nobody knows how he planned to end it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Dickens was paid by the word, so he made his books extra long.

Truth: He was paid for each part or instalment, not for each word. His books are long because serial stories needed many parts, and readers loved spending months with the characters.

Try this

Write a serial story with a friend or family member. Each of you writes one short chapter a day and ends it on a cliffhanger. Swap every day for a week and see where the story goes.
