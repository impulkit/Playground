Why do some inventions fail?

In 1964, visitors in New York queued to try a telephone with a TV screen. Almost nobody bought one, yet today billions of people video call.

The short answer

Most inventions fail. Some don't work. But many that work fine still flop, because people don't want them, can't afford them or aren't ready for them yet.

A product has to solve a real problem, at a price people will pay, at a moment when they are ready to change their habits. Miss any one of those and even a clever machine ends up in a cupboard. The good news is that failure often teaches inventors what to try next. Some of the most common things you use today grew out of ideas that flopped first.

Surprising but true

The sticky notes on your fridge exist because a scientist made a glue that was a failure. It was too weak to hold anything for long.

Too early for its time

In 1964, at the World's Fair in New York, the American phone company AT&T showed off the Picturephone. You could see the person you were talking to on a small screen. People loved the demo. But it was costly, the screen was tiny, and very few people had one, so there was nobody to call. It faded away.

Fifty years later, video calls on phones became everyday life. The idea was right. The timing, the price and the networks were wrong. Electric cars had a similar story. They were common in American cities around 1900, then lost out to cheap petrol cars for most of a century.

A problem nobody had

Some inventions solve a problem people don't actually feel. The Segway, launched in 2001, was a two-wheeled scooter that balanced itself with gyroscopes and sensors. Its makers hinted it would change how cities moved. It worked, but it cost about as much as a small motorbike, and people weren't sure where to ride it. Segway stopped making it in 2020.

In 2016, an American company launched a $700 machine, later cut to $400, that squeezed juice out of sealed packets. Reporters then showed you could squeeze the packets just as well by hand. The company shut down within about a year and a half.

When people don't like the feel of it

Some products bump into feelings. In 1985, Coca-Cola changed its famous recipe. Taste tests had said people liked the new flavour, but many customers were furious about losing the old one. After 79 days, the original came back.

Google Glass, smart glasses with a tiny screen and camera, went on sale to early users in 2013. Many people felt uneasy being near someone who might be filming them. Shoppers didn't take to it, and Google later stopped selling it to the public.

Cheap but not wanted

In 1985 in Britain, inventor Clive Sinclair launched the C5, a small electric tricycle. It was low to the ground, slow and open to rain, and drivers felt unsafe next to lorries. Only a few thousand were sold.

In India, the Tata Nano was unveiled in 2008 as the world's cheapest car, at about one lakh rupees. It was meant to move families off risky scooters. But many buyers didn't want to be seen in "the cheapest car", and sales stayed far below plans. Production ended in 2018.

Failing forward

In 1968, a scientist at the company 3M made a glue that stuck only lightly and peeled off easily. Nobody knew what to do with it. Years later, a colleague used it to keep bookmarks in his hymn book from falling out, and Post-it notes were born.

Thomas Edison and his team tested thousands of materials before finding a long-lasting light bulb filament. A museum in Sweden, the Museum of Failure, now displays flops so people can learn from them. And the strangest part? Most inventions protected by patents, the legal rules that guard new ideas, never earn their inventors any money at all.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A good invention always succeeds if it works well.

Truth: Plenty of inventions that worked perfectly still failed. They cost too much, came too early, solved a problem nobody had or clashed with how people wanted to live. Success depends on people as much as on machines.

Try this

Invent something useful from a cardboard box, string and tape. Before you build, write down who would use it and what problem it solves. Then let three people test it and note what goes wrong. Change one thing and test again, the way real inventors do.
