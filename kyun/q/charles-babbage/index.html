Who designed a computer 200 years ago?

In the 1830s, an English mathematician designed a steam-age computer of brass gears. It took until 1991 for anyone to build one of his machines.

The short answer

Charles Babbage was an English mathematician and inventor, born in 1791. He designed two great calculating machines made of brass gears, wheels and levers.

The first, the Difference Engine, would calculate mathematical tables automatically. The second, the Analytical Engine, was far more ambitious. It could be programmed with punched cards, had a memory and a separate unit to do calculations, and could make decisions based on its results. Those are the key parts of a modern computer.

Babbage never finished either machine. But because of these designs, he is often called the father of the computer.

Surprising but true

Half of Charles Babbage's brain is kept at the Science Museum in London, and the other half at the Hunterian Museum of the Royal College of Surgeons.

A world full of mistakes

In Babbage's time, engineers, sailors and bankers relied on printed books of numbers: tables of logarithms, tides and star positions. These were worked out by hand by human computers, then copied and typeset. Mistakes crept in at every step, and a wrong number in a ship's navigation table could wreck a ship.

In 1821, while checking tables with his friend the astronomer John Herschel, Babbage found error after error. He is reported to have wished the calculations could be done by steam. That idea, a machine that never makes careless mistakes, drove the rest of his life.

The Difference Engine

Babbage's first design used a clever trick called the method of differences. Many tables, such as squares or times tables, can be built using only addition, if you start with the right differences. Adding is easy for gears.

The British government gave him money, and his engineer Joseph Clement began building it in the 1820s. In 1832, a small section was assembled and worked perfectly. But Babbage and Clement fell out over costs, and the government eventually stopped paying. Only a fraction of the machine was ever built in his lifetime.

The Analytical Engine

By then, Babbage was already thinking of something bigger. From 1834, he designed the Analytical Engine. It had a "store" to hold numbers, like a computer's memory, and a "mill" to do the arithmetic, like a processor.

Instructions would be fed in on punched cards, an idea borrowed from the Jacquard loom, which used cards to weave complex patterns into silk. The mathematician Ada Lovelace wrote about the engine and described how it could be programmed. It would have been driven by steam, a true child of the Industrial Revolution.

What was the Industrial Revolution?About 250 years ago, machines began doing work that hands had done for thousands of years, and the way people lived changed faster than ever before.

A busy, prickly genius

Babbage had many interests. He held the same professorship of mathematics at Cambridge that Isaac Newton once held. He wrote about railways, lighthouses, codebreaking and factories, and is often credited with inventing the cowcatcher, a frame on the front of trains that pushes obstacles off the track.

He was also famously grumpy. He campaigned for years against the street musicians and organ grinders outside his house in London, whose noise, he said, ruined his concentration. They sometimes played under his window on purpose.

Built at last

In 1991, the 200th anniversary of Babbage's birth, the Science Museum in London finished building his Difference Engine No. 2, a later and simpler design, using his original drawings. Its calculating section has about 4,000 parts and is turned by a hand crank. It worked. A printer, also designed by Babbage, was added in 2002, and together they weigh about 5 tonnes.

A second copy was made for the Computer History Museum in California. The Analytical Engine has never been built, though a project in Britain called Plan 28 has been studying his drawings in the hope of one day building it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Babbage's machines could never have worked with the technology of the 1800s.

Truth: The Science Museum built his Difference Engine No. 2 to his drawings, using tolerances achievable in his time, and it works. His projects stalled mainly because of money, arguments and his habit of redesigning, not because the ideas were impossible.

Try this

Make a "difference table" like Babbage's engine. Write the square numbers 1, 4, 9, 16, 25 in a row. Underneath, write the differences between them (3, 5, 7, 9), then the differences between those (2, 2, 2). Now use only adding to extend the squares as far as you can. Babbage's engine did this with gears.
