Do heavy things fall faster than light things?

An astronaut dropped a hammer and a feather on the Moon to find out.

The short answer

No. If there is no air in the way, everything falls at exactly the same speed. Heavy things are pulled harder by gravity, but they are also harder to get moving. The two effects cancel out.

On Earth, air pushes back on falling things. A feather is light and spread out, so air slows it a lot. That's why a feather floats down while a hammer drops.

Near Earth's surface, gravity makes any falling thing speed up by about 9.8 metres per second, every second, if air doesn't get in the way.

Surprising but true

In 1971, astronaut David Scott dropped a hammer and a feather on the Moon. With no air to slow the feather, they hit the ground together.

The Moon test

In 1971, Apollo 15 astronaut David Scott dropped a hammer and a falcon feather on the Moon, where there is almost no air. They hit the ground at the same moment. A TV camera filmed it, and you can still watch the clip. Scott joked that it showed Galileo had been right.

Gravity on the Moon is only about a sixth of Earth's, so both fell slowly. But they fell slowly together.

Galileo's idea

For nearly 2,000 years, many people followed the Greek thinker Aristotle, who taught that heavy things fall faster. About 400 years ago, the Italian scientist Galileo argued that falling objects speed up at the same rate. There's a famous story that he dropped balls from the Leaning Tower of Pisa. We don't know if he really did.

What he certainly did was roll balls down long, gentle ramps and time them, sometimes with a water clock. Slowing the fall down let him measure it. He found that the distance grew with the square of the time, whatever the ball's weight.

From apples to the Moon

Isaac Newton realised that the same force pulling an apple down also keeps the Moon going around Earth. He published his law of gravity in 1687. It says every object pulls on every other object, and the pull gets weaker with distance.

The Moon is falling towards Earth all the time. It's just moving sideways so fast that it keeps missing.

The pull works both ways. The Moon tugs on Earth too, heaving the oceans into gentle bulges that give coasts from Gujarat to Maine their rising and falling tides.

Who was Isaac Newton?Sent home from university by a deadly plague, a young man in a farmhouse worked out ideas about light, motion and gravity that still guide spacecraft.

Ideas from India

Long before Newton, thinkers in many places wondered why things fall. The Indian mathematician Brahmagupta, writing in the 600s, is often credited with saying that the Earth attracts objects towards itself. He didn't have Newton's mathematics, but he saw falling as a pull from the Earth.

A giant vacuum chamber

You don't have to go to the Moon to see the hammer test. NASA has a huge vacuum chamber in Ohio, big enough to hold a whole spacecraft. In 2014, scientists pumped out nearly all its air for a TV programme, then dropped a bowling ball and a bunch of feathers. The feathers didn't drift or flutter. They fell like stones, exactly alongside the ball.

Floating astronauts

Astronauts on the space station aren't floating because gravity has switched off. They're falling, just like the Moon, always around Earth and never into it. Skydivers feel something similar for a moment before the air slows them to a steady speed of about 200 km/h. Open a parachute, and its wide canopy catches so much air that the speed drops to a gentle landing, the feather trick on a human scale.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Astronauts float in the space station because there is no gravity in space.

Truth: At the height of the space station, gravity is still about 90% as strong as on the ground. Astronauts float because they and the station are falling around Earth together, so nothing pushes against them.

Try this

Drop a coin and a flat sheet of paper together from the same height. The coin wins. Now scrunch the paper into a tight ball and drop them again. They should land at almost the same moment. The paper didn't get heavier; it just has less air to push aside.
