Is bottled water better than tap water?

In many cities, bottled water is no cleaner than tap water, costs hundreds of times more, and some of it actually comes from the tap.

The short answer

It depends on where you live. In many cities in the United States, Europe and parts of India, tap water is treated, tested many times a day and safe to drink. There, bottled water is not healthier. It just costs far more and creates plastic waste.

In places where tap water is not safe, because pipes leak, rivers are polluted or treatment is patchy, sealed bottled water can protect people from illness. That is why many Indian families boil water, use filters at home or buy large cans of purified water. So the honest answer is that it depends on the tap.

Surprising but true

A 2024 study found that a litre of bottled water held, on average, about 240,000 tiny bits of plastic, most of them far too small to see.

Tap water in America

In the United States, the Safe Drinking Water Act of 1974 lets the government set limits for germs, metals and chemicals in public water. Water companies must test regularly and report the results.

Bottled water is treated as a packaged food and checked by a different agency. Its rules are similar, but tap water is often tested more often. Several big bottled brands even start with city water and filter it again. A litre of bottled water can cost hundreds of times more than a litre from the tap.

When the tap fails

Tap water is only as good as the pipes and plants behind it. In 2014 the city of Flint, Michigan, switched its water source to save money. The new water ate into old pipes and released lead, a metal that harms children's brains. For years many families had to drink bottled water.

In India, many towns do not have water that is safe straight from the tap. In 2019 the Government of India started the Jal Jeevan Mission, which aims to bring piped water to every rural home. Making that water safe to drink is the next big task.

How Indian homes clean water

Many Indian homes treat their own water. Boiling kills germs. Candle filters strain out dirt. UV purifiers kill microbes with light. Reverse osmosis machines, known as RO, push water through a very fine membrane that blocks salts and many chemicals.

RO systems have a cost: they usually waste a good share of the water that goes in, and can remove useful minerals too. Bottled water sold in India must meet standards set by the Bureau of Indian Standards and carry its mark.

Plastic in the bottle

Most water bottles are made of a plastic called PET. In 2024, scientists at Columbia University used a new laser method to look for very tiny plastic particles. They found on average about 240,000 bits in each litre of bottled water, most of them far smaller than a speck of dust.

Nobody yet knows whether these particles harm people. Scientists are studying the question. What is clear is that bottles themselves add up. Many end up in landfills, rivers and the ocean instead of being recycled.

Where the water came from

Every drop, from a tap or a bottle, has travelled through the water cycle. It has fallen as rain, run through rivers or soaked underground, and evaporated again, over and over for billions of years.

Some bottling plants pump large amounts of groundwater from one place, which can upset local people who depend on the same wells. In parts of India and the United States, communities have protested against plants taking water during droughts.

Choosing wisely

A good rule is to find out about your local water. Many cities publish test reports. If your tap water is safe, a refillable steel bottle is cheaper and creates almost no waste. If it is not, bottled water or a reliable home purifier keeps you healthy. Either way, drinking enough water matters far more than which bottle it comes from.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Bottled water is always purer than tap water.

Truth: Bottled water is not automatically cleaner. In places with good water treatment, tap water is tested often and meets strict rules. Where tap water is unsafe, though, sealed bottled water or purified water at home is a sensible choice.

Try this

With an adult, set up a blind taste test. Pour the drinking water your family normally uses at home into one cup and bottled water into another, and mark the bottoms. Ask family members to taste both and guess which is which. Count how many guess correctly.
