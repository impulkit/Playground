What is Bangladesh like?

Bangladesh fits about 17 crore people into a country smaller than the state of Odisha, most of it built by rivers.

The short answer

Bangladesh is a country to India's east, almost wrapped around by West Bengal, Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram, with the Bay of Bengal to the south. It is one of the most crowded countries on Earth. About 17 crore people live in an area a little smaller than Odisha.

Almost the whole country is a giant delta, the low, flat land built up where the Ganga, the Brahmaputra and the Meghna rivers reach the sea. It is green, wet and full of rivers. People here speak Bangla, the same language spoken in West Bengal, and share much of the food, music and poetry of Bengal.

Surprising but true

Rabindranath Tagore wrote the songs that became the national anthems of both India and Bangladesh.

A country made by rivers

The Ganga and Brahmaputra carry silt down from the Himalayas, more than a billion tonnes of it each year. Where they slow down near the sea, the mud settles and builds land. Over thousands of years, this has made the largest delta in the world.

Bangladesh has hundreds of rivers, and boats are a normal way to travel. The soil is so rich that farmers can grow two or three rice crops a year. In the south lies part of the Sundarbans, the largest mangrove forest on Earth, shared with India and home to the Bengal tiger. Some families keep homes and schools on boats in the flood season, and charities have run floating schools that sail from village to village to pick up children.

Fighting for a language

In 1947, British India was divided, as told in Partition. The area that is now Bangladesh became East Pakistan, part of Pakistan, though it lay more than 1,500 km from West Pakistan, with India in between.

In 1948 the government announced that Urdu alone would be the national language. Most people in East Pakistan spoke Bangla, and many felt their language and culture were being pushed aside. On 21 February 1952, police opened fire on student protesters in Dhaka, killing several. That day is remembered with flowers and songs at the Shaheed Minar in Dhaka.

Becoming Bangladesh

Tensions grew over the next twenty years. In 1971, after a brutal crackdown by the Pakistani army, a war of independence broke out. Millions of refugees fled to India. India joined the war in December, and on 16 December 1971 the Pakistani forces in the east surrendered. Bangladesh, meaning "land of the Bengalis", became independent.

The national anthem, "Amar Shonar Bangla" ("My Golden Bengal"), is a song by Rabindranath Tagore. He wrote it in 1905, long before Bangladesh existed. He also wrote "Jana Gana Mana", so one poet gave words to two neighbouring countries.

Rickshaws, jute and T-shirts

Dhaka is one of the most crowded cities in the world, and its streets are famous for cycle rickshaws painted with bright pictures of film stars, birds and flowers. Jute, a tough plant fibre used for sacks and ropes, was once Bangladesh's main export and was nicknamed the "golden fibre".

Today the biggest industry is clothing. Bangladesh is one of the world's largest exporters of ready-made clothes, after China. There is a fair chance that a T-shirt in your cupboard was stitched there, a story you can follow in cotton and clothes.

Living with water

Cyclones from the Bay of Bengal sometimes strike the coast. In 1970, a terrible cyclone killed hundreds of thousands of people. Since then, Bangladesh has built thousands of cyclone shelters on stilts and set up warning systems run partly by volunteers with megaphones.

Death tolls from big storms have dropped hugely as a result. Bangladesh has become a place that other countries study to learn how to live with rising water.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Floods are always a disaster for Bangladesh.

Truth: Ordinary monsoon floods are part of farming life. They spread fresh silt over the fields and make the soil rich. It is the rare, extreme floods that destroy homes and crops, and Bangladeshis use different words for the two kinds.

Try this

Fill a long baking tray with sand or soil, raise one end slightly, and slowly pour water at the top. Watch how the water spreads out into many small channels and drops sand where it slows down at the bottom. That fan of mud is a tiny delta.
