How does hand sanitiser kill germs?

Hand sanitiser kills germs by ripping apart their outer coats and wrecking their insides, all in about 20 seconds of rubbing.

The short answer

Most hand sanitisers contain alcohol, usually ethanol or isopropyl alcohol, making up 60 to 95% of the liquid. Alcohol kills germs by breaking open their outer layers and making the proteins inside them unravel and clump, a bit like an egg white turning solid in a hot pan.

A germ with a ruined coat and scrambled proteins can't survive or multiply. That works against many bacteria and viruses, including those that cause colds and flu. You need enough sanitiser to cover your hands, and you need to rub until it has all dried, which takes about 20 seconds.

Surprising but true

Pure alcohol is worse at killing many germs than alcohol mixed with some water, because water helps it soak into germs before it evaporates.

What alcohol does to a germ

Many viruses, like the flu virus, are wrapped in a thin, fatty envelope. Alcohol dissolves that envelope the way soap dissolves grease, and the virus falls apart. Bacteria have tougher walls, but alcohol slips through and wrecks the proteins inside.

Strangely, pure alcohol is not the best killer. It evaporates in seconds and can harden a germ's surface into a shell before getting inside. Mixed with some water, the alcohol works more slowly and more thoroughly. That is why many sanitisers contain around 60 to 80% alcohol, not 100%.

Why it feels cold

Sanitiser feels cold because alcohol evaporates very fast. As it turns from liquid to gas, it takes heat from your skin. This is the same reason sweat cools you down, only faster.

Alcohol also dries out skin, since it strips away natural oils. So most sanitisers add glycerine or aloe vera to keep hands soft. Gel versions add a thickener, so the liquid does not drip off before you can rub it in.

Using too little is a common mistake. You need enough to wet both hands completely, and you should rub between the fingers, over the backs of the hands and around the thumbs. If your hands are dry in under 10 seconds, you probably did not use enough.

Where sanitiser falls short

Sanitiser is not magic. It can't get through mud, food or grease, since germs hide underneath where the alcohol does not reach. It also struggles with a few tough germs. Norovirus, which causes nasty stomach bugs, has no fatty envelope to dissolve. Some bacteria make hard-shelled spores that alcohol cannot crack.

Soap works differently. It does not need to kill germs. It loosens them from the skin so running water can wash them away, along with the dirt. That is why doctors say soap and water is best whenever you have a sink. Sanitiser is the backup for buses, school trips and picnics.

Who came up with it

Doctors have known for centuries that alcohol fights infection. In the 1840s, the Hungarian doctor Ignaz Semmelweis showed that washing hands with a chlorine solution saved mothers' lives in a Vienna hospital. Many other doctors laughed at him, a sad story told in the history of handwashing.

Alcohol gels for hospitals became common in the late 1900s. In the 2000s, a team led by Swiss doctor Didier Pittet worked with the World Health Organization to publish a cheap sanitiser recipe that anyone could make, with no patent. Hospitals from India to Africa used it. During the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, sales went up so fast that shops ran out, and even perfume makers and breweries switched to making sanitiser.

Your skin is not supposed to be germ-free, though. Millions of friendly microbes live on it all the time, and they help keep harmful germs away. And the germs that sanitiser misses are no match for plain soap, whose clever molecules have one end that loves water and another that grabs grease.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Hand sanitiser kills 99.9% of all germs, so it is just as good as washing.

Truth: Lab tests on a few germs give that number, but real hands are messier. Sanitiser misses some germs, and does not work well on dirty or greasy hands. Soap and water is better whenever you can get to a sink.

Try this

Rub a few drops of cooking oil and a pinch of glitter over your hands, with an adult's permission. Try cleaning one hand with sanitiser and the other with soap and water. Which one gets rid of the glitter better? It shows why soap is best for dirty hands.
