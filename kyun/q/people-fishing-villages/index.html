What is life like in a fishing village?

Fishers along India's 11,000 km coast often head to sea in the dark, and are back on the beach selling the catch before most children wake up.

The short answer

Life in a fishing village runs on the sea's timetable. Boats often leave in the dark, long before sunrise, and come back in the early morning with their catch. By the time the sun is fully up, the beach becomes a busy market, with fish laid out in rows and auctions shouted at top speed.

India has thousands of fishing villages along its coastline of about 11,000 km. Fishing feeds millions of families and brings fish to plates far inland. But it is hard work, it depends on weather and seasons, and it is changing fast.

Surprising but true

The English word "catamaran" comes from the Tamil word kattumaram, meaning "tied wood", a boat made by lashing logs together.

Before the sun comes up

Many fishers set out around 3 or 4 in the morning. Some use small wooden boats or catamarans close to shore. Others go out on motor trawlers for days at a time. Fishers read the wind, the colour of the water, the birds diving overhead and the phase of the moon to guess where fish will be.

Back on land, the work keeps going. Nets are untangled and mended by hand, often by older men who sit in the shade with a wooden needle. Mending a big net can take days. Children grow up knowing how to tie dozens of knots before they can ride a bicycle. Most nets today are made of nylon, which lasts much longer than the cotton and coir nets of the past.

The women who run the market

In many Indian fishing villages, men catch the fish and women sell it. Fisherwomen buy from the boats, then carry baskets to markets, sometimes travelling by bus or train. In Mumbai, Koli women in bright saris have sold fish in the city's markets for generations. The Kolis were among the first people to live on the seven islands that became Mumbai.

Women also dry, salt and pickle fish. On some beaches, long rows of small fish lie drying on mats in the sun. Dried fish lasts for months and travels well, so it reaches villages far from the sea.

Rules of the sea

Every year, Indian states ban most fishing at sea for about two months. On the west coast, it usually runs from 1 June to 31 July. On the east coast, it falls in April to June. This gives fish time to breed, and keeps boats safe from stormy monsoon seas.

Fishers spend the break repairing boats and nets, and many take other jobs. Families save money in the good months to get through the lean ones. Fishing communities also have their own festivals. On Narali Purnima, the Kolis offer coconuts to the sea to mark the end of the monsoon and the start of the new fishing season.

A changing coast

India produces more fish than almost any other country. It ranks second in the world, with around 8% of the global total in recent years, counting fish from both sea and farms. Seafood from India's coast is exported to the USA, Europe and Asia.

But the sea is under pressure. Big trawlers catch huge amounts, and some kinds of fish are getting harder to find. Rising seas and stronger storms threaten beachside homes. Some villages now plant mangroves, whose tangled roots protect the shore and shelter baby fish.

Fishing villages exist on every coast. In Gloucester, Massachusetts, one of the oldest fishing ports in the USA, a famous bronze statue of a fisherman at the wheel remembers those lost at sea. In Kochi, giant cantilevered fishing nets dip into the water like huge spiders, and the question of who brought them to Kochi is still debated. And every fishing village lives by the tides, the Moon's twice-a-day tug on the ocean.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Fishers go out to sea every day of the year.

Truth: Weather, tides, festivals and fishing bans decide when boats go out. On India's coasts, motor boats mostly stay in harbour for about two months each monsoon.

Learn a fisher's knot. Take two pieces of string and tie the ends together with a simple knot, then try a reef knot (right over left, left over right). Test which one holds better when you tug, and which one is easier to untie.
