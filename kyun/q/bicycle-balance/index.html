Why doesn't a moving bicycle fall over?

Stand still on a bicycle and you topple. Ride it and you're fine. Scientists argued about this for over 100 years.

The short answer

When a bicycle leans left, steering left moves the wheels back under you, and the bike comes upright again. You make these small corrections all the time without thinking about them. Watch a cyclist from behind and you'll see the front wheel twitching gently from side to side.

A bicycle's front wheel is also designed to turn into a lean by itself. So even with no hands on the handlebar, a moving bike tends to steer itself back under its own weight.

Even today, scientists are still studying exactly how a riderless bike can stay up.

Surprising but true

A bicycle with no rider, pushed fast enough, can balance itself for a while.

Why speed helps

Standing still, a bicycle is like a pencil balanced on its point. Any small tip, and it falls. The trick of steering into the lean only works if the bike is rolling forward, because turning the wheel moves the bottom of the bike sideways, back under its top.

The faster you go, the less you need to steer to make the same correction. That's why a wobbly beginner feels steadier once they pick up a little speed, and why riding very slowly through a crowded market lane takes real skill.

Your inner ear and eyes keep reporting which way you're tipping. Your brain uses those signals to steer, much as it does when you stand on one foot.

A puzzle for a century

Scientists have been arguing about bicycles since the 1800s. Many believed the spinning wheels did the job, acting like a spinning top. Others pointed to the shape of the front fork.

In 1970, a British chemist named David Jones tried to settle it by building bikes that shouldn't work. He made one with an extra front wheel spinning backwards to cancel the top-like effect. He could still ride it. He then changed the front fork's angle so the wheel no longer swung into a lean, and that bike was almost impossible to ride hands-free.

The strange bike of 2011

In 2011, a team of engineers from the Netherlands and the USA built an odd-looking bike. Its wheels had extra discs spinning the opposite way to cancel the top effect, and its fork was set up so the wheel didn't trail behind in the usual way. Pushed along with no rider, it still balanced itself.

The secret was where its weight sat. A heavy weight placed high and forward on the front made the front end swing into a lean quickly. The lesson was that many details of a bike's design work together, and no single one explains everything.

How was the bicycle invented?The first bicycle, built in Germany in 1817, had no pedals. You sat on it and pushed along the road with your feet.

Steer right to turn left

Here's a surprise most riders never notice. To turn left at speed, you first push the handlebar very slightly to the right. The bike starts to lean left, and only then do you steer into the turn. Motorcycle riders call this countersteering.

The tyres grip the road thanks to friction, so the bike's base moves where the wheels point instead of sliding sideways.

Learning to balance

Bicycles began in 1817, when Karl Drais of Germany built a wooden "running machine" with no pedals. Riders pushed along with their feet.

Today many toddlers learn the same way, on small balance bikes with no pedals. They discover steering into a lean first, so moving to pedals later is much easier. Simple machines like the pedal crank and the handlebar are both kinds of lever, which let your legs and arms push with less effort. Once your brain has learned the balancing trick, it rarely forgets it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A bike stays up mainly because its spinning wheels act like a gyroscope or a spinning top.

Truth: Spinning wheels help a little, but they aren't the main reason. In 2011, researchers built a bike whose wheel spin was cancelled out, and it still balanced itself. Steering into the lean does most of the work.

Try this

Roll a coin along a smooth floor and watch it. When it starts to lean, it curves towards the side it's leaning to, and that turn keeps it upright for longer. A bicycle's front wheel does something similar. Try rolling the coin slowly and then faster to see which stays up longer.
