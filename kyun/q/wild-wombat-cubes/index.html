Why do wombats make cube-shaped poop?

A wombat can leave about 80 to 100 little cubes of poop in a single night, and scientists won a prize for working out how.

The short answer

Wombats are stocky, burrowing animals from Australia, about the size of a medium dog. They are the only animals known to make poop shaped like cubes, with flat sides and fairly sharp corners.

For a long time, nobody knew how. In 2018, a team of scientists in the USA and Australia studied the guts of wombats that had been killed on roads. They found that the cubes form inside the last part of the intestine. That part of the gut wall has bands that are stiff and bands that are stretchy. As dry poop is squeezed along, the stretchy bands push in harder, carving flat faces, while the stiff bands make corners.

Why wombats make cubes is less certain. The best idea is that they use them as signposts, and cubes do not roll away.

Surprising but true

The team that worked out how wombats make cubes won an Ig Nobel Prize in 2019, a prize for science that makes people laugh, then think.

A very slow kitchen

Wombats eat tough grasses, roots and bark. To get every bit of goodness out, they digest very slowly. Food can take up to about two weeks to pass through a wombat, much longer than in humans, who usually take one to three days.

All that time lets the gut soak up almost all the water. So wombat poop comes out very dry and firm, which helps the cubes keep their shape. Each cube is about 2 cm across, roughly the size of a sugar cube or a big dice.

A single wombat can drop around 80 to 100 of them in one night.

Messages on rocks

Wombats cannot see well, but they have an excellent sense of smell. They pile their cubes on rocks, logs and the tops of small mounds, often near their burrows. Each pile is a smelly message that says something like "this patch is mine".

Here, shape matters. A round dropping on a sloping rock would roll off. A cube sits still. Many scientists think that's the reason cube poop evolved, although no one has proved that cubes help wombats find mates or defend their patch better.

The prize-winning science

The engineer Patricia Yang and her colleagues did something careful and slightly gross. They compared wombat intestines with pig intestines, stretched them like long balloons and measured how stiff the walls were. They then built computer models of the squeezing.

Their findings won the 2019 Ig Nobel Prize in physics. These prizes, handed out for many years at Harvard University, honour research that first makes people laugh, then makes them think. The team suggested factories could one day learn from wombats, because making cubes usually needs moulds or cutting, and wombats do it with soft tissue.

Cubes are rare in nature. Salt crystals make them, and so do some minerals, but living things almost never produce them.

Built like a tank

Wombats are tough. They have strong legs and long claws for digging burrows that can run for tens of metres. A female's pouch faces backwards, towards her hind legs. That way, when she digs, soil does not fly into the pouch and onto her baby, called a joey.

Their rump is covered by a hard plate of bone and cartilage under thick skin. When chased into a burrow, a wombat may block the entrance with its bottom, which is very hard for a predator to bite. Despite their chunky shape, wombats can run at about 40 km/h for short bursts, about as fast as a top human sprinter.

Burrows in the bushfires

During the huge bushfires in Australia in 2019 and 2020, stories spread online that wombats had herded other animals into their burrows to save them. That did not happen on purpose. But wombat burrows stayed cooler and safer than the burning ground, and other animals did use them to shelter.

Wombats share their home country with other unusual marsupials, animals that carry their babies in pouches. One of them, the koala, spends most of its life asleep, for a reason that also comes down to a very slow, very picky gut.

Myth or fact?

Myth: During Australia's 2019–2020 bushfires, wombats herded other animals into their burrows to save them.

Truth: This story spread widely online, but there is no evidence wombats deliberately rescued anyone. Other animals did shelter in wombat burrows, which stayed cooler than the burning ground, so the burrows really did help.

Try this

Fill two balloons with damp sand, with an adult's help. Wrap rubber bands tightly around one balloon in a few places, leaving gaps, and leave the other plain. Squeeze both gently in your hands. Notice how the bands change the shape where the balloon pushes out. Wombat guts work on a similar idea.
