What are the seven continents?

About 20 crore years ago, all the land on Earth was joined in one giant continent. It has been breaking apart ever since.

The short answer

A continent is one of Earth's very large land masses. Schools in India and the USA usually teach seven: Asia, Africa, North America, South America, Antarctica, Europe and Australia.

Asia is the biggest, with about 30% of all the land on Earth and more than half its people. Australia is the smallest. Antarctica is the coldest and the only one with no permanent population.

The number seven is partly a choice. Europe and Asia are really one connected land mass, and North and South America are joined too. So in some countries, children learn six continents, or even five. The land doesn't change, only the way people group it.

Surprising but true

India was once an island drifting north across the ocean. When it crashed into Asia, about 5 crore years ago, the collision began pushing up the Himalaya.

Lines drawn by people

Look at a globe and you will see no gap between Europe and Asia. The usual dividing line runs along the Ural Mountains in Russia, down to the Caspian Sea. That line was chosen by geographers centuries ago, partly for cultural reasons.

In much of Latin America and in countries like Spain, students learn that America is a single continent. The five Olympic rings, designed in 1913, were said to stand for the five inhabited continents, with the Americas counted as one.

The giant that broke apart

About 30 crore years ago, nearly all of Earth's land was joined in one supercontinent called Pangaea, meaning "all lands". Around 20 crore years ago it began splitting up.

The continents sit on huge slabs of rock called plates, which move a few centimetres a year, about as fast as your fingernails grow. Over millions of years, that is enough to open oceans. The Atlantic is still getting wider.

India's long voyage

India once sat far south, near Africa, Antarctica and Australia. When the giant southern land broke apart, the Indian plate drifted north across the ocean, faster than most plates.

About 5 crore years ago, it crashed into Asia. The collision crumpled the land and began pushing up the Himalaya, which are still rising by a few millimetres a year. That is why geographers often call India a subcontinent.

The one nobody owns

Antarctica is bigger than India and China combined. Almost all of it lies under ice, in places more than 4 km thick. In 1959, twelve countries signed the Antarctic Treaty, which keeps the continent for peace and science. Many more countries have signed since.

India runs research stations there, called Maitri and Bharati. Scientists study ice, weather and ancient air trapped in bubbles.

Continents of life

Because continents drifted apart long ago, each has its own animals. Kangaroos and koalas live wild only in Australia, which has been cut off from other land for tens of millions of years. Lions and giraffes roam Africa. Llamas come from South America, and the bald eagle soars over North America.

People spread across the continents too. Scientists think modern humans first lived in Africa and then moved out, reaching Asia, Australia, Europe and finally the Americas. They probably reached the Americas over land that joined Siberia and Alaska during the last Ice Age.

A hidden eighth?

Some geologists argue there is an eighth continent, Zealandia, around New Zealand. About 94% of it is under the sea. In 2017, a team of scientists published a paper arguing it should count. Not everyone agrees, which shows that even continents can be up for debate.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Africa is a country.

Truth: Africa is a continent with 54 countries, more than any other continent. It is home to over a thousand languages and is large enough to fit India, China and the USA inside it at once.

Try this

Trace the continents from an atlas onto a sheet of paper and tear them out roughly with your fingers. Try fitting the east coast of South America against the west coast of Africa. Scientists noticed this jigsaw fit hundreds of years ago, and it was one of the first clues that continents move.
