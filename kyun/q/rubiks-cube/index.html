How do people solve a Rubik's Cube so fast?

The fastest people solve a scrambled Rubik's Cube in under three seconds, quicker than it takes most of us to read this sentence.

The short answer

Fast solvers don't work it out from scratch each time. They learn a method that breaks the puzzle into stages, and for each stage they memorise short move sequences, called algorithms. Then they practise until their fingers do the moves without thinking.

Top "speedcubers" know well over a hundred of these sequences. Before each solve they get 15 seconds to look at the scrambled cube and plan the first steps. They use special cubes that turn smoothly, often with tiny magnets inside. The best solve one in under three seconds.

Surprising but true

It took Ernő Rubik, the cube's own inventor, about a month to solve it for the first time.

A puzzle that began as a lesson

Ernő Rubik was a young architecture teacher in Budapest, Hungary. In 1974 he built a cube of wooden blocks held together by a clever hidden core, so each face could turn without the whole thing falling apart. He made it to help his students think about shapes in three dimensions.

Then he scrambled it, and couldn't get it back. It took him about a month. The "Magic Cube" went on sale in Hungary in 1977, and from 1980 a US toy company sold it worldwide as the Rubik's Cube. Hundreds of millions have been sold since.

Why it's so hard

The cube has about 43 quintillion possible positions. That's 43 followed by 18 zeros. If you made one random twist every second, you would almost never stumble on the solution, even in billions of years.

Yet every one of those positions can be solved in 20 moves or fewer. Mathematicians proved this in 2010 with help from Google's computers. Humans can't find those perfect short solutions, so they use longer but reliable methods instead.

How speedcubers do it

The most popular fast method is often called CFOP, after its four stages: Cross, First two layers, Orientation and Permutation. You first make a cross on one face, then fill in the first two layers, then fix the top layer in two steps.

The last two steps are where memory matters. There are 57 cases for orienting the top layer and 21 for arranging it, and serious cubers learn a sequence for each. Many top solvers trained by solving thousands of times, a bit like a bowler practising one delivery in the nets until it becomes automatic.

The cube itself

The original cube was stiff and tended to jam. Speed cubes are built differently. Their pieces have rounded edges so a face can turn even when it is slightly out of line, and solvers add silicone lubricant to make them glide. Many now have small magnets that click each layer gently into place.

Solvers also learn "finger tricks": flicking a face with one fingertip instead of gripping and twisting with the whole hand. Watch a slow-motion video of a top solve and the hands barely seem to move. The cube's neat symmetry helps too, because a move sequence learned for one face works the same way on any other.

Competitions and records

The World Cube Association runs official contests around the world, including many in India and the USA. Judges scramble the cubes by computer so nobody gets an easy one. Results usually take the average of several solves, so a lucky single doesn't decide the winner.

Records keep falling. In 2023 the American cuber Max Park set a single-solve record of 3.13 seconds. In February 2026, a nine-year-old from Poland, Teodor Zajder, became the first to go under three seconds, with 2.76.

Beyond the 3×3

Cubers also race on bigger cubes, up to 7×7, and on oddly shaped cousins. Some solve with one hand, and some blindfolded. A blindfolded solver studies the cube, puts on the mask, and solves it entirely from memory.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You need to be a maths genius to solve a Rubik's Cube.

Truth: Most people can learn a beginner's method in a few days. It uses a handful of memorised move sequences and lots of practice. Many children solve the cube in under a minute.

Try this

Turn one face of a solved cube, then turn the next face, and write each move down. Now try to undo the moves in reverse order. You have just used the same idea speedcubers use: every sequence of moves can be run backwards.
