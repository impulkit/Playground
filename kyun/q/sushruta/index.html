Who was Sushruta, the ancient Indian surgeon?

Long before modern hospitals, an Indian text taught surgeons to practise their cuts on watermelons and to rebuild a lost nose.

The short answer

Sushruta is the name attached to one of the oldest books on surgery in the world, the Sushruta Samhita (Sushruta's Collection). It was written in Sanskrit and is one of the founding texts of Ayurveda, India's traditional medicine.

Tradition says Sushruta was a surgeon who learned from a teacher in Kashi, the city now called Varanasi. The book describes about 300 operations and more than 100 instruments. It covers setting broken bones, removing cloudy lenses from the eye, stitching wounds and, most famously, rebuilding a damaged nose.

Who Sushruta really was, and exactly when he lived, nobody knows. The book is what survives.

Surprising but true

In 1794, a London magazine described Indian practitioners near Pune rebuilding a man's nose with skin from his forehead, and European surgeons soon copied the method.

An ancient text that kept growing

Historians think the earliest parts of the Samhita go back well over 2,000 years, but the book was not written in one go. Later authors added chapters and edited older ones. One scholar, Nagarjuna, is often named as an editor. The version printed today probably took its shape many centuries after the earliest parts.

That makes the Samhita less like a single book and more like a school's handbook that teachers kept updating.

What is Sanskrit?About 2,400 years ago, a scholar named Panini described the whole of Sanskrit grammar in about 4,000 short rules. Linguists still study his work.

Watermelons, leather bags and practice

The Samhita is strict about training. A student had to study the texts and also watch and practise. Cuts were to be practised on gourds, cucumbers and melons. Puncturing was practised on leather bags filled with water or mud. Stitching was tried on cloth and soft leather. Students learned to probe on hollow plant stems.

It also lists instruments, many shaped like the beaks or jaws of animals and birds, and describes how to keep them sharp and clean.

Rebuilding a nose

In ancient India, cutting off a person's nose was sometimes used as a cruel punishment. So a way of rebuilding noses was in demand. The Samhita describes cutting a flap of living skin from the cheek, swinging it round while it stays attached, and shaping it over the nose. Two small tubes were placed to keep the nostrils open.

Over time, Indian practitioners used skin from the forehead instead. In 1794, the Gentleman's Magazine in London printed a report of this operation done on a man near Pune. European surgeons copied it, and it became known as the "Indian method". Plastic surgeons still use forehead flaps today.

Eyes, bones and herbs

The Samhita covers far more than noses. It describes a way of treating cataract, a clouding of the lens inside the eye, by using a fine needle to push the cloudy lens out of the line of sight. This method, called couching, was used in many parts of the world for centuries.

The book also explains how to set broken bones with splints and how to remove arrows stuck in the body. It lists hundreds of medicines made from plants and minerals.

Sushruta among the world's healers

Many ancient cultures had skilled healers, from Egypt to Greece to China, and you can read about them in the history of doctors. Sushruta's book stands out for how practical it is: it reads like a working surgeon's guide.

Not everything in it matches modern science. It explains illness through Ayurvedic ideas about balance in the body, and some treatments would not be used now. But its attention to training, cleanliness and careful wound healing still impresses doctors.

A statue of Sushruta stands at the Royal Australasian College of Surgeons in Melbourne, far from Kashi, a sign of how widely surgeons know his name.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Sushruta wrote the whole Sushruta Samhita by himself at one moment in history.

Truth: The text grew over a long time. Scholars think an early core was later added to and revised by other authors, and the version we read today took shape many centuries after it began.

Try this

Ask an adult for a soft fruit like a banana. Draw a short line on the peel with a pen, then try to "stitch" along it with a blunt plastic needle and wool, keeping every stitch the same size. It is much harder than it looks, which is exactly why surgeons practise.
