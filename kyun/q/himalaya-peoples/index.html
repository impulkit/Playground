Who lives in the Himalayas?

Some families in the Himalayas live higher than the top of any mountain in Europe, and their bodies have adapted to the thin air over thousands of years.

The short answer

The Himalayas stretch about 2,400 km across Asia, from Pakistan in the west, through India, Nepal and Bhutan, to Tibet in the east. Millions of people live among them, from villages in warm green valleys to high pastures far above the tree line.

They belong to many different peoples. There are Ladakhis and Kashmiris in the west, Gaddi shepherds in Himachal Pradesh, and Garhwalis and Kumaonis in Uttarakhand. Nepal is home to Sherpas, Gurungs, Tamangs and many others. Further east live the Lepchas and Bhutias of Sikkim, the people of Bhutan and the Monpa of Arunachal Pradesh, with Tibetans on the high plateau to the north. They speak dozens of languages and follow Hindu, Buddhist, Muslim and older local traditions.

Surprising but true

Scientists have found that many Tibetans carry a version of a gene that helps them live with less oxygen, and it seems to have come from an ancient human group called the Denisovans.

Bodies built for thin air

At 4,000 m, each breath holds only about 60% of the oxygen it would at sea level. Newcomers get headaches and feel breathless. People whose families have lived high up for thousands of years often cope much better.

Tibetans, for example, tend to breathe faster and have bodies that use oxygen in a slightly different way. In 2014 scientists reported that many Tibetans carry a version of a gene called EPAS1 that seems to help. Surprisingly, it appears to have come from the Denisovans, an ancient group of humans whose DNA survives in some living people. Sherpas, who descend from Tibetan migrants, share some of these advantages.

Farming on a slope

Flat land is rare in the mountains, so people carve hillsides into terraces, steps of flat fields held up by stone walls. They grow barley, buckwheat, potatoes, rice in lower valleys, and apples in places like Himachal and Kashmir.

Animals matter enormously. Yaks give milk, wool, meat and dung for fuel, and they carry loads at heights where horses struggle. In Ladakh and Tibet, people drink salty butter tea to keep warm. Houses are built for the cold, with thick stone or mud walls, small windows and flat roofs where grain, apricots and firewood dry in the sun. Shepherds take their flocks up to high meadows in summer and bring them down before the snows, a seasonal journey that has gone on for centuries.

Holy mountains

For many people, the mountains are sacred. Hindu pilgrims travel to Kedarnath, Badrinath and Amarnath. Buddhists build monasteries on ridges, such as Hemis in Ladakh and Tawang in Arunachal Pradesh, the largest monastery in India. Colourful prayer flags flutter on passes, printed with prayers that, by tradition, the wind carries away.

People have protected their mountains, too. In 1973, villagers in what is now Uttarakhand began hugging trees to stop them being cut down. Women such as Gaura Devi of Reni village led the way, and the Chipko movement became famous across the world.

Sherpas and the summit

The Sherpa people of Nepal's Khumbu region became famous through mountaineering. Tenzing Norgay, a Sherpa, reached the top of Everest with Edmund Hillary of New Zealand in 1953, as told in the first Everest climb.

Today many Sherpas work as climbing guides and high-altitude workers, a dangerous and skilled job. Some of the most experienced have climbed Everest more than twenty times. The mountains themselves are still growing by a few millimetres a year, because India keeps pushing into Asia, as explained in how the Himalayas formed.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The word "Sherpa" means a mountain porter or guide.

Truth: Sherpa is the name of a people, an ethnic group from eastern Nepal. Many Sherpas work on mountains, but many do other jobs, and many mountain workers come from other communities.

Try this

Count how many times you breathe in one minute while sitting still. Then jog on the spot for a minute and count again. At high altitude, each breath brings in less oxygen, so newcomers feel like this even when walking slowly.
