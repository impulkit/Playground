What is the Qutub Minar, and why is it so tall?

The Qutub Minar in Delhi is about as tall as a 24-storey building, and it was built more than 800 years ago without a single machine.

The short answer

The Qutub Minar is a tall stone tower in the south of Delhi. It is about 73 metres high, roughly a 24-storey building, and narrows from about 14 metres wide at the bottom to under 3 metres at the top. It is made of red and buff sandstone, with white marble in its upper storeys.

Work on it began in the 1190s, under Qutb ud-din Aibak, the first ruler of what became the Delhi Sultanate. A minaret, or minar, is a tower beside a mosque from which the call to prayer can be given. This one was also a victory tower, built to announce that a new power had arrived in north India.

Surprising but true

An iron pillar in the same courtyard is about 1,600 years old and has hardly rusted, even though it has stood outdoors through centuries of Delhi monsoons.

Built by three rulers

Aibak lived to see only the first storey finished. His successor, Iltutmish, added three more. In 1368, lightning struck the top and damaged it. The ruler Firoz Shah Tughlaq repaired it, rebuilding the top floor and adding a fifth storey faced with white marble.

You can still see the change if you look up. The lower storeys are deep red and buff, while the top ones are paler. Each storey is ringed by a balcony held up on carved stone brackets, and the outer walls swirl with fluted ribs, some rounded and some sharp-edged.

Words in stone

The tower is wrapped in bands of carved writing. They are verses from the Quran and inscriptions in Arabic and Persian, telling who built what and when. Workers carved the letters in deep relief, with flowers and vines curling around them. It is calligraphy on a giant scale, and it had to be readable from far below.

Many of the carvers were probably local Indian craftsmen, and some of the decoration shows lotus and bell patterns found in older temples. An inscription at the nearby Quwwat-ul-Islam mosque says it was built using material from 27 Hindu and Jain temples that once stood on the site. Carved temple pillars can still be seen in its courtyard.

The pillar that hardly rusts

In the courtyard stands the Iron Pillar, about 7 metres tall. An inscription in Sanskrit suggests it was made around 1,600 years ago, in the time of the Gupta kings, probably to honour the god Vishnu. It was moved to Delhi centuries later.

Most iron left outdoors flakes into rust within years. This pillar has survived about 16 centuries with only a thin coat. Metal scientists, including R. Balasubramaniam of IIT Kanpur, found that its iron is rich in phosphorus. This helped a thin, tight protective layer form on its surface, slowing rusting almost to a stop. It also shows how skilled Indian ironworkers were.

Stairs, storms and a dome on the lawn

Inside the tower, a spiral staircase of 379 steps climbs to the top. Visitors once climbed it, but in 1981 a crowd accident on the stairs killed dozens of people, many of them schoolchildren. The tower has been closed inside ever since.

The Qutub Minar has survived lightning and earthquakes. After an earthquake in 1803 damaged the top, a British officer, Major Robert Smith, added a small domed pavilion as a new crown in 1828. People thought it looked out of place, and it was taken down about 20 years later. It now sits on the lawn nearby, sometimes called "Smith's Folly".

The tower that never grew

Near the Qutub Minar stands a wide, stumpy ring of rubble about 25 metres high. This is the Alai Minar. In the early 1300s, Sultan Alauddin Khalji began it, planning a tower twice as big as the Qutub Minar. He died in 1316 and nobody finished it.

Together, these buildings form the Qutb complex, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1993. Like the Red Fort, it draws visitors from across India and the world, and the sight of the tower against the evening sky has hardly changed in more than 600 years.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Delhi's Iron Pillar never rusts at all.

Truth: It does rust, but extremely slowly. A thin protective layer on its surface keeps the rusting from eating deep into the metal, so after 1,600 years it is still standing strong.
