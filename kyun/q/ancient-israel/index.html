What do we know about ancient Israel?

The oldest known mention of the name "Israel" is carved on a stone made for an Egyptian pharaoh more than 3,200 years ago.

The short answer

Ancient Israel was the land and people of the Israelites, who lived in the hills of Canaan, at the eastern end of the Mediterranean Sea, about 3,000 years ago. They spoke Hebrew, and over time they came to worship one god alone, which made them unusual among their neighbours. Their stories, laws, poems and history were later gathered into the Hebrew Bible, a book that matters deeply to Jews and that Christians call the Old Testament.

Historians piece together this past from three main sources. One is the Bible itself. Another is the records of powerful neighbours like Egypt, Assyria and Babylon. The third is what archaeologists dig up: walls, pottery, seals and inscriptions.

Surprising but true

A water tunnel more than 500 metres long, cut through solid rock under Jerusalem about 2,700 years ago, still carries water today. An inscription found inside in 1880 describes two teams of diggers meeting in the middle.

A name on an Egyptian stone

Around 1208 BCE, Pharaoh Merneptah of Egypt had a large stone slab, or stele, carved to boast of his victories. Near the end, it lists peoples in Canaan he said he had defeated. One line reads, roughly, "Israel is laid waste, its seed is not."

It is the oldest known mention of Israel outside the Bible. It shows that a people called Israel was living in Canaan by then. It doesn't say how they got there, which is one of the questions scholars still debate.

Two kingdoms

According to the Bible, the Israelites were once united under kings named Saul, David and Solomon. Later the land split in two: Israel in the north, with its capital at Samaria, and Judah in the south, with its capital at Jerusalem.

How big and powerful David and Solomon's kingdom was is hotly argued. In 1993, archaeologists found a stone at Tel Dan with an inscription from the 9th century BCE that mentions the "House of David". Many scholars take this as evidence that a king called David founded a ruling family. What his kingdom was like is still debated.

How do historians know what's true?Historians work like detectives, questioning every letter, coin and diary, because even old documents can be wrong, biased or completely fake.

Empires from the east

From about 900 BCE, the picture gets much clearer, because Assyrian and Babylonian records mention Israelite kings by name. Around 722 BCE, the Assyrian empire conquered the northern kingdom of Israel and moved many of its people away.

Judah survived for a while. King Hezekiah prepared Jerusalem for an Assyrian attack by cutting a tunnel through solid rock to bring water inside the city walls. In 1880, an inscription was found inside the tunnel describing two teams digging from opposite ends until they met. Water still flows through it.

In 587 or 586 BCE, the Babylonians captured Jerusalem and destroyed its temple, known as the First Temple. Many people were taken to Babylon in exile. About 50 years later, the Persian king Cyrus conquered Babylon and allowed exiles to return. They built a new temple, the Second Temple, which stood until the Romans destroyed it in 70 CE.

Letters and scrolls

The Hebrews wrote using an alphabet closely related to the one used by their northern neighbours, the Phoenicians. Later they switched to a square script borrowed from Aramaic, the ancestor of the Hebrew letters used today.

In 1947, Bedouin shepherds exploring caves near Qumran, by the Dead Sea, found clay jars full of ancient scrolls. These Dead Sea Scrolls, written over about 300 years before and around the time of Jesus, include the oldest known copies of books of the Hebrew Bible. They showed that the text had been copied with great care for more than a thousand years.

A long legacy

The traditions of ancient Israel shaped Judaism, and through it influenced Christianity and Islam, which also honour figures such as Abraham and Moses. Billions of people today belong to these faiths.

Jewish communities spread across the world, including to India. The Cochin Jews lived on the Kerala coast for many centuries, and the Paradesi Synagogue in Kochi, built in 1568, is still in use. The Bene Israel of Maharashtra have their own long history in India too.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Archaeology has either proved the whole Hebrew Bible true or proved it all false.

Truth: Neither is the case. Some kings and events, such as King Hezekiah of Judah and the Assyrian attack on his land, appear in outside records too. Other stories have no outside evidence yet, and scholars continue to debate them.

Try this

Use a lolly stick or a blunt pencil to carve a short message into a bar of soap, the way ancient people carved inscriptions into stone. Keep it to one line. Notice how much effort each letter takes, and think about why ancient inscriptions are usually short.
