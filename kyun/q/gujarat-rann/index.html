What is the Rann of Kutch?

For part of the year it is a shallow sea. For the rest it is a white plain of salt so flat and bright that it seems to have no end.

The short answer

The Rann of Kutch is a vast salt marsh in Gujarat, near India's border with Pakistan. It has two parts: the Great Rann in the north, about 7,500 square km by a common estimate, and the smaller Little Rann to the south-east. The word rann comes from an old word for a salty, swampy place.

For a few months after the monsoon, much of it lies under shallow water. Then the sun bakes it dry, and the salt that was dissolved in the water is left behind as a white crust. In winter, on a full-moon night, the Great Rann glows almost silver. It is one of the strangest landscapes in Asia.

Surprising but true

In the middle of the salt desert sits Dholavira, a city of the Indus civilisation that was busy about 4,500 years ago.

A sea that went away

Long ago, the Rann was an arm of the Arabian Sea, deep enough for boats. Over thousands of years, rivers carried in mud and the land slowly rose, partly because of earthquakes along faults under Kutch. The sea retreated, leaving a huge flat basin barely above sea level.

The ground still moves. In 1819, a powerful earthquake pushed up a long ridge of land that locals named Allah Bund, meaning "dam of God". In 2001, the Bhuj earthquake badly damaged towns across Kutch. People rebuilt, and many towns are larger now than before.

Wild asses and flamingos

The Little Rann is home to the Indian wild ass, a fast, tough relative of the horse with a sandy coat and a dark stripe along its back. In the 1960s, only a few hundred were left. A sanctuary was set up in 1972, and a 2024 count found about 7,600.

When the Great Rann floods, it becomes a nursery for birds. Tens of thousands of greater flamingos gather in some years to nest on mud flats, at a spot nicknamed "Flamingo City". Cranes, pelicans and other water birds visit too. Wolves, foxes and desert cats hunt around the edges.

Salt farmers of the Little Rann

Gujarat produces about three-quarters of India's salt, and a lot of it comes from the edges of the Little Rann. Families called Agariyas spend the dry months there, pumping up salty water from under the ground into flat pans. The sun does the rest, drying it into crystals.

The work is hard. Temperatures go above 45 °C in summer, and the white salt reflects the glare. Agariyas have done this for generations, living in simple shelters out on the flats until the rains return and wash the pans away. It is a lot like the salt pans on coasts, only far from the sea.

A city in the salt

On an island of higher ground in the Great Rann lies Dholavira, one of the largest cities of the Indus civilisation. It was busy around 4,500 years ago. Its builders cut stone reservoirs and channels to catch every drop of rain, because fresh water was precious even then.

Archaeologists found a row of ten large Indus signs near one of its gates, possibly a kind of signboard, though nobody can read the script yet. In 2021, UNESCO listed Dholavira as a World Heritage Site.

The white desert festival

Every winter, Gujarat holds the Rann Utsav, a festival near the village of Dhordo, at the edge of the white desert. Visitors stay in tents, buy embroidery, mirror-work and pottery made by local craftspeople, and walk out onto the salt at sunrise. Months later, the rain comes back, and the white plain vanishes under water again.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Rann of Kutch is a sandy desert, like the Thar.

Truth: It is a seasonal salt marsh. It floods in the monsoon and dries into a hard, flat layer of salt and clay, with very little sand. Grassy patches and islands of higher ground dot its edges.

Try this

Dissolve two spoonfuls of salt in a cup of warm water. Pour a thin layer into a dark plate and leave it in a sunny window for a few days. As the water evaporates, watch the white crust form, just like a tiny Rann.
