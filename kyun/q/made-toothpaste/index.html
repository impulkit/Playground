How is toothpaste made?

Toothpaste tubes are filled from the wrong end, and the paste inside is partly made of crushed rock.

The short answer

Toothpaste is a thick mix of a few simple things. There is a gentle scrubbing powder, often chalk or silica, to polish away the sticky film on teeth. There is water and a syrupy liquid to keep it moist, a gum to make it thick, a foaming agent, a sweet flavour like mint, and usually fluoride to make teeth stronger.

In a factory, giant mixers blend all of it under a vacuum so no air bubbles get trapped. Then machines squirt the paste into tubes through the open bottom end, squash that end flat and seal it. A single machine can fill well over a hundred tubes a minute.

Surprising but true

Factories fill toothpaste tubes through the flat bottom end, then crimp it shut. The cap end is closed the whole time.

Rock in your mouth

The scrubbing part is the surprise. Calcium carbonate is the same stuff as chalk, limestone and seashells. Silica is a cousin of sand and quartz. Both are ground into a powder much finer than flour, fine enough to polish teeth without scratching the hard outer coat, the enamel.

Toothpaste makers test how scratchy each paste is. Too rough, and over years it could wear teeth down. Too gentle, and it won't remove stains from tea, coffee or turmeric. So toothpaste is a careful balance, like using a very soft scrubber on a steel pan.

Why the foam

The bubbles come from a detergent, often sodium lauryl sulfate, which is a relative of the cleaners in shampoo and soap. Foam doesn't clean teeth much on its own. It spreads the paste around the mouth and makes brushing feel like it is working, which helps people keep going.

Sweeteners such as sorbitol make toothpaste taste nice without feeding the germs that cause tooth decay. Those germs love sugar. Sorbitol and similar sweeteners give them almost nothing to eat.

The fluoride trick

Fluoride is the real hero. Every day, acid made by mouth bacteria pulls tiny amounts of minerals out of your enamel. Fluoride helps put minerals back and makes the surface tougher against the next acid attack. In the 1950s, large studies in the United States showed that fluoride toothpaste and water cut tooth decay sharply, and fluoride toothpaste spread around the world.

That's also why dentists say to spit, not rinse, after brushing. A little fluoride left on the teeth keeps working.

Ash, eggshells and neem

People cleaned their teeth long before tubes existed. Ancient Egyptians used powders that included ash and crushed eggshells. Romans used crushed bones and shells. In India, people have chewed twigs of neem and babool for thousands of years. The frayed end works like a brush, and neem has bitter chemicals that bacteria dislike. Many Indian toothpastes still advertise neem, clove or salt.

The first toothpaste sold in jars appeared in the 1870s. In the 1890s, an American dentist named Washington Sheffield began selling toothpaste in squeezable metal tubes, borrowed from the tubes painters used for oil paint. Plastic tubes took over much later.

The tube nobody can recycle

Most toothpaste tubes are made of thin layers of plastic and aluminium pressed together, which keeps the paste fresh but makes them very hard to recycle. Companies are now testing tubes made of one kind of plastic so they can be melted down and reused.

The paste itself is one of the last steps in keeping a mouth healthy. The other half is the brush, and the story of the toothbrush goes back to a Chinese invention made with pig hair.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The more toothpaste you use, the cleaner your teeth get.

Truth: The brushing does most of the cleaning, and fluoride works in small amounts. Dentists suggest a smear the size of a grain of rice for children under 3 and a pea-sized blob for ages 3 to 6. Swallowing large amounts of fluoride toothpaste is not good for children.

Try this

Put a pea-sized blob of toothpaste on a dark plate and look at it with a magnifying glass. Then rub a tiny bit between finger and thumb. You might feel the faint grit of the scrubbing powder. Compare two brands if you have them.
