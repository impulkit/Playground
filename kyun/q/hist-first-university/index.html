What was the world's first university?

More than 1,500 years ago, students from China and Korea walked for months to reach Nalanda in Bihar, where monks taught everything from grammar to medicine.

The short answer

It depends on what you call a university. If you mean a big place where students lived and studied many subjects with expert teachers, one of the earliest was Nalanda, in what is now Bihar, India. It began in the 5th century CE and ran for about 700 years.

If you mean the oldest one still working today, the usual answer is al-Qarawiyyin in Fez, Morocco, founded in 859. And if you mean the first place to call itself a university, with degrees, students' groups and the word universitas, that was Bologna in Italy, from 1088. All three answers are true in their own way.

Surprising but true

The oldest institution of learning still working today was founded by a woman, Fatima al-Fihri, in Fez, Morocco, in 859.

Nalanda, the great monastery

Nalanda started as a Buddhist monastery and grew under the Gupta kings and later rulers. At its height it had huge brick buildings, temples, lecture halls, gardens, ponds and rooms for thousands of monks. Its library was said to fill three buildings, one of them nine storeys tall, though that detail comes from later Tibetan tradition.

Students studied Buddhist teachings, but also grammar, logic, medicine, mathematics and astronomy. Debate was a big part of learning. A student had to defend ideas against sharp questions in front of others.

The Chinese monk Xuanzang studied there in the 600s. He walked from China, across deserts and the Himalayas, and stayed for years. He wrote that around 10,000 monks and students lived there, though some historians think that number is too high. Others came from Korea, Tibet, Central Asia and Southeast Asia. Another Chinese monk, Yijing, studied at Nalanda later in the same century and copied hundreds of texts to take home.

Takshashila, even older

Even earlier, there was Takshashila, near today's Islamabad in Pakistan. By around 500 BCE, it was famous for its teachers. Tradition links it to the grammarian Panini, the physician Jivaka and the political thinker Chanakya.

But Takshashila was more like a town full of independent teachers than one organised institution. Students went to live with a teacher of their choice. That is why many historians save the word "university" for Nalanda instead.

A woman founds a school in Fez

In 859, a wealthy woman named Fatima al-Fihri, whose family had moved to Fez from Tunisia, used her inheritance to build a mosque in the city. It grew into a great centre of learning, with teachers of religion, law, grammar, astronomy and medicine. Scholars travelled across North Africa, Spain and beyond to study there.

Today it is the University of al-Qarawiyyin. Guinness World Records lists it as the oldest existing educational institution in the world. Its library, one of the oldest anywhere, still holds handwritten books more than 1,000 years old.

Bologna, Oxford and beyond

In Europe, the University of Bologna began around 1088. Students, many of them adults studying law, banded together into groups to hire teachers and protect themselves. Their group was called a universitas, and that is where the word "university" comes from.

Paris and Oxford followed in the 1100s. The oldest university in the USA, Harvard, was founded in 1636. India's first modern universities, in Calcutta, Bombay and Madras, opened in 1857.

Nalanda rises again

Around 1200, Nalanda was attacked and burned. Many accounts blame the army of Bakhtiyar Khalji, though historians still discuss exactly what happened. The monks scattered, and the great library was lost. Its ruins were rediscovered in the 1800s and became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2016.

A new Nalanda University now stands nearby. Its campus opened in 2024, with students from many countries, just as in Xuanzang's day. Along with Bodh Gaya and the other old Buddhist sites of Bihar, it draws visitors from across Asia who come to see where some of the world's first great universities began.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Universities were invented in medieval Europe.

Truth: The word "university" comes from Europe, but large centres of higher learning existed much earlier in India, and in the Islamic world institutions like al-Qarawiyyin and al-Azhar began before Europe's first universities.

Try this

Hold a mini "Nalanda debate" at home or in class. Pick a simple question, such as "Is it better to be a morning person or a night person?" One side argues yes, the other no, each with three reasons. Swap sides halfway through. Nalanda students practised debating like this.
