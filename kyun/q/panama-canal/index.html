How does the Panama Canal lift ships?

The Panama Canal lifts giant ships 26 m up and over a country using no pumps at all, only rainwater and gravity.

The short answer

The Panama Canal cuts across Panama, a narrow country in Central America, to link the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans. Before it opened in 1914, a ship sailing from New York to San Francisco had to go all the way around the tip of South America. The canal cut about 13,000 km off that journey.

Panama is hilly, so the canal doesn't run at sea level. Instead it works like a staircase made of water. Ships enter giant boxes called locks. Gates close behind them, and water flows in from a lake high above, lifting the ship step by step, about 26 m in all. They cross the lake, then step back down to the other ocean. A full trip usually takes about 8 to 10 hours.

Surprising but true

A ship crossing the canal from the Atlantic to the Pacific actually travels from northwest to southeast, because of the way Panama bends.

A staircase of water

At each end of the canal there is a flight of locks. Each lock is a concrete chamber with huge steel gates at both ends. The original chambers are 305 m long, about as long as three football fields.

To raise a ship, workers open valves that let water from Gatun Lake pour through tunnels in the walls into the chamber. As the water rises, the ship floats up with it, a bit like a person in a rising lift. When it matches the next step, the gates open and the ship moves forward. To go down, the water is let out.

In the old locks, small electric railway engines called mules run along the walls. Ropes from the mules keep each ship centred, because some ships fit with only about half a metre to spare on each side.

A lake made by a dam

The middle of the canal is Gatun Lake. It was created by building a dam across the Chagres River. When it filled, it was the biggest artificial lake in the world.

Rain feeds the lake. Every ship that passes through the old locks uses about 200 million litres of fresh water, which ends up in the sea. In 2023 and 2024, a severe drought lowered the lake, and the canal had to cut the number and size of ships it allowed. In the 2024 financial year it handled about 11,200 transits. After the rains returned, it handled about 13,400 in 2025.

Mosquitoes and mudslides

France tried to build a canal first, starting in 1881. It was led by Ferdinand de Lesseps, who had built the Suez Canal. His plan was a sea-level canal with no locks. Landslides kept filling the cuttings, and yellow fever and malaria killed many thousands of workers. The French gave up in 1889.

The USA took over in 1904. Doctors had recently shown that mosquitoes spread these diseases. Teams drained swamps, covered water tanks and fumigated houses, and deaths fell sharply. Tens of thousands of workers came from the Caribbean, especially from Barbados, to do much of the hardest and most dangerous digging.

Why are mosquitoes so dangerous?No shark, snake or tiger comes close. Through the diseases they carry, mosquitoes cause more human deaths than any other animal.

Bigger ships, bigger locks

The USA ran the canal until 31 December 1999, when it passed to Panama. In 2016, Panama opened a second set of larger locks for the biggest modern ships. These newer locks save water by storing some of it in side basins and reusing it.

Tolls can reach hundreds of thousands of dollars for a large ship. The cheapest toll ever paid was 36 cents, in 1928, by the American writer Richard Halliburton, who swam the canal over several days. Officials weighed him and charged him as a ship. The Suez Canal in Egypt is longer, but it has no locks at all, because the land there is flat.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Giant pumps fill the canal's locks with seawater.

Truth: The old locks use fresh water from Gatun Lake and Alajuela Lake, which flows down by gravity. Each ship that passes lets millions of litres run out to sea, which is why droughts can force the canal to limit traffic.

Try this

Make a lock with two plastic bottles cut in half, a tray and a toy boat. Fill one container higher than the other and connect them with a bendy straw or tube, pinched shut. Put the boat in the lower one, then open the tube and watch the water flow down and the boat rise, with no pump at all.
