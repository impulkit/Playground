How does a helicopter fly?

A helicopter's blades are really wings, whirling round several times every second.

The short answer

A helicopter flies because its long, thin rotor blades are wings. Instead of the whole aircraft rushing forward to move air over its wings, like a plane, a helicopter spins its wings round and round. Each blade is shaped and tilted to push air downwards, and the air pushes the blade up. That push is called lift.

Spin the blades fast enough and the lift becomes greater than the helicopter's weight, so it rises. That's why a helicopter can take off straight up, hover in one place and land on a rooftop, a ship or a mountainside, places no plane could reach.

Surprising but true

In 2005, a French test pilot landed a helicopter on the summit of Mount Everest, where the air is about a third as thick as at sea level.

Steering with tilting blades

The pilot has two main controls for the blades. One, the collective, changes the angle of all the blades at once. More angle means more lift, so the helicopter rises. Less angle and it sinks.

The other, the cyclic, tilts the spinning disc of blades slightly, so it pushes air down and a little backwards. The helicopter then moves forwards. Tilt it to the side, and it slides sideways. It is a bit like tilting an umbrella in a strong wind. The whole aircraft follows the direction of the tilt.

Why there's a tail rotor

When an engine spins the big rotor one way, the helicopter's body wants to spin the other way. You can feel this on a swivel chair: twist your arms hard to the left and the chair turns right.

The small rotor on the tail pushes sideways to stop this. Pedals let the pilot change how hard it pushes, to turn the nose left or right. Some helicopters, like the big twin-rotor Chinook, have two main rotors turning in opposite directions instead, so their spins cancel out.

Centuries of dreaming

Children in China played with spinning bamboo toys, the bamboo-copter, more than 1,500 years ago. Around 1490, Leonardo da Vinci sketched an "aerial screw", a spiral sail meant to twist its way into the sky. It could never have flown, but the idea was there.

Real helicopters were very hard to build. Early machines shook, spun and tipped over. In 1939, the Russian-American engineer Igor Sikorsky flew the VS-300 in Connecticut, USA. It had one main rotor and a small tail rotor, the design most helicopters still use today.

Helicopters to the rescue

Helicopters go where planes can't. They lift sick people from accident sites, pluck sailors off sinking ships and fight forest fires with giant buckets of water.

In June 2013, floods and landslides struck Uttarakhand, around Kedarnath. Indian Air Force helicopters flew hundreds of trips through narrow mountain valleys and helped rescue many thousands of people. India builds its own helicopters too. The HAL Dhruv, designed and made by Hindustan Aeronautics in Bengaluru, first flew in 1992.

Highest and farthest

Thin air is hard for helicopters, because the blades have less air to push. In May 2005, French test pilot Didier Delsalle landed a specially prepared helicopter on the summit of Mount Everest, 8,848 m up, a record that still stands.

In 2021, a helicopter flew somewhere even stranger. NASA's tiny Ingenuity made the first powered flight on another planet, on Mars, where the air is about a hundredth as thick as Earth's. The same idea, smaller and with four rotors, now lifts the drones you see over weddings and cricket grounds.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If a helicopter's engine stops, it falls like a stone.

Truth: Pilots train for this. They tilt the blades so the air rushing up through them keeps them spinning, like a sycamore seed. This is called autorotation, and it lets the helicopter glide down and land.
