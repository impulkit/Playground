How do eagles see so far?

An eagle weighs about as much as a small cat, yet its eyes are almost as big as a grown-up's.

The short answer

An eagle sees far because its eyes are huge for its size and built for detail. A golden eagle weighs about as much as a small cat, yet its eyeballs are close to the size of a grown-up's. Bigger eyes throw a bigger picture onto the back of the eye, so there is more room for detail.

The back of the eye, the retina, is also packed far more tightly with light-sensing cells than ours. Where you have one sharp spot in each eye, an eagle has two. Together these let it pick out a hare moving across a hillside long before the hare notices anything above.

Surprising but true

Each eagle eye has two especially sharp spots, one for looking ahead and one for looking to the side.

Eyes bigger than they look

From the outside you see only a small, fierce circle of an eagle's eye. Most of the eyeball is hidden inside the head, and it is not a neat ball. It is slightly stretched from front to back, partway between a ball and a short tube. The two eyes fill a large part of the skull.

That size comes at a price. The eyes can hardly move in their sockets. An eagle that wants to look sideways must turn its whole head, which is why birds of prey make those sudden, jerky glances. A bony ridge above each eye works like the peak of a cap, shading the eye from the glare of the sun.

Two sharp spots in each eye

Your eyes have a tiny pit at the centre of the retina called the fovea. It is where your vision is sharpest, and it is what you use to read these words. An eagle has two foveae in each eye. One looks forward, where both eyes overlap. The other looks out to the side, towards the horizon.

In birds of prey, the cells in these spots are crowded together much more densely than in humans. More cells in the same space means finer detail, a bit like a phone camera with more pixels. The deep pit may also act like a small lens, stretching the middle of the picture so it looks bigger.

How do your eyes see?The picture at the back of your eye is upside down. Your brain flips it the right way up without you ever noticing.

How much sharper?

Popular books often say an eagle sees four to eight times better than you, and can spot a rabbit from 3 km away. Scientists who have actually tested eagles are more careful. In the 1980s, a researcher in Australia trained a wedge-tailed eagle to fly towards one of two screens with fine stripes. The bird could tell stripes apart when they were about twice as fine as the best human could manage.

Twice as sharp is still enormous. It means an eagle could read a car's number plate from roughly twice as far as the sharpest-eyed person you know. Other eagles may do even better, but few have been tested.

Eyes that hunt

Seeing far is only half the job. An eagle must also keep its target in focus while it dives. A golden eagle can drop at well over 200 km/h, and its eyes keep adjusting as the ground rushes up. A see-through third eyelid sweeps across each eye to clean it and protect it during the strike.

In India, you can watch this skill in steppe eagles that arrive every winter, and in crested serpent eagles that watch from treetops for snakes. In the United States, the bald eagle does much of its hunting over water. It spots a fish under the ripples before it folds its wings and drops.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Hawks and eagles find mice by seeing the ultraviolet glow of their trails of urine.

Truth: A 1995 study of kestrels suggested this. Later tests found that the eyes of birds of prey let through very little ultraviolet light, so most scientists now doubt it. Eagles mostly rely on extremely sharp ordinary vision.

Try this

Stand at one end of a playground or long corridor and ask a friend to hold up a page of large printed letters. Walk backwards until you can no longer read them, and measure that distance in steps. If you had eagle eyes, you could probably read them from at least twice as far.
