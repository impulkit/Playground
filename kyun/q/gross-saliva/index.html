How much spit do you make?

You make somewhere between half a litre and a litre and a half of spit every day, enough over a lifetime to fill a couple of water tankers.

The short answer

Most people make about half a litre to a litre and a half of saliva a day. That's somewhere between a small water bottle and a big one, every single day. Over a lifetime, it adds up to tens of thousands of litres, roughly enough to fill a couple of the water tankers you see on Indian streets.

Saliva is about 99% water, but the other 1% is busy. It contains an enzyme that starts digesting starch, slippery substances that help you swallow, minerals that repair your teeth, and germ fighters. You make most of it when you eat, a little all day, and very little when asleep.

Surprising but true

Without saliva you couldn't taste dry food. Flavour chemicals have to dissolve in liquid before your taste buds can detect them.

Where spit comes from

You have three big pairs of salivary glands. The largest sit just in front of your ears. Another pair sits under your jaw, and the smallest pair under your tongue. Hundreds of tiny glands dot the lining of your mouth too.

The glands work harder when you smell, see or even think about food. Picture biting into a sour lemon or a raw mango with salt and chilli, and you may feel your mouth water right now. That is your brain switching on the glands before any food arrives.

Digestion starts in your mouth

Saliva begins breaking down food before you swallow. Its enzyme, amylase, chops starch from rice, roti and potatoes into smaller sugars. That's why chewing plain bread for a long time makes it taste sweet.

Saliva also wets and softens food so it slides down your throat as a soft ball. Without it, swallowing a dry biscuit would feel like swallowing sand. The rest of the journey is the story of your gut.

Saliva also matters for taste. Flavour chemicals have to dissolve before the taste buds on your tongue can detect them.

How does your tongue taste things?Your taste buds are replaced every week or two, and most of what you call "taste" actually happens in your nose.

A guard for your teeth

After you eat, mouth bacteria make acids that attack your teeth. Saliva washes away bits of food, weakens the acid and carries minerals like calcium that help repair tiny damage to the enamel.

At night, saliva slows down a lot. Bacteria have a party, which is one reason for morning breath. It's also why brushing before bed matters so much for teeth.

Saliva also contains lysozyme, a substance that breaks open some bacteria. Alexander Fleming discovered it in 1921, a few years before he discovered penicillin.

Spit in other animals

Plenty of animals use spit in clever ways. Some swiftlets in South-East Asia and the Andaman Islands build their nests almost entirely from their own hardened saliva. People collect these nests to make an expensive soup, which has made some swiftlets endangered.

Dogs drool when they expect food. In the early 1900s, the Russian scientist Ivan Pavlov studied this and found dogs would drool at sounds they linked with food, a famous discovery about learning. Vampire bats have saliva that stops blood from clotting while they feed.

When spit goes wrong

Some medicines, illness and stress can cause a dry mouth, which makes talking, eating and tasting hard. Doctors can also test saliva instead of blood for some illnesses, because it carries traces of many things in the body.

Saliva even shows up when your body is about to empty your stomach. Just before throwing up, your mouth floods with extra spit to protect your teeth, part of the strange drill of vomiting.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A dog's mouth is cleaner than a human's mouth.

Truth: Dogs and humans each carry hundreds of kinds of bacteria in their mouths, many of them different. A dog's mouth isn't cleaner, it just has different germs. Washing your hands and face after a dog licks you is a good idea.

Try this

Chew a small piece of plain roti, bread or a plain cracker slowly for about a minute without swallowing. Notice when it starts tasting sweeter. Then try drying the tip of your tongue with a clean tissue and place a few grains of sugar on it. See how long it takes to taste them until saliva returns.
