What is a dhole?

India's forests hide a red wild dog that whistles to its pack, and it is rarer than the tiger.

The short answer

A dhole is a wild dog of Asia's forests. It has a rusty-red coat, a bushy black-tipped tail and rounded ears. A grown dhole weighs about as much as a medium-sized pet dog, around 12 to 20 kg.

Dholes live and hunt in packs, usually of 5 to 12, chasing deer such as chital and sambar. India has more dholes than any other country. They live in the Western Ghats, central Indian forests like Tadoba, Pench and Kanha, and parts of the Northeast.

They are endangered. The IUCN estimates that fewer than 2,500 adults are left in the wild worldwide, which makes them rarer than tigers.

Surprising but true

Dholes are sometimes called whistling dogs, because they keep in touch in thick forest with sharp, bird-like whistles.

The whistling hunters

Dholes are famous for their whistle. In thick forest, where pack members cannot see each other, they whistle to regroup. They also yap, scream, chatter and mew, a much bigger range of calls than wolves.

Unlike many wild dogs, they hunt mostly by day, running prey down over long distances instead of ambushing it. A pack can bring down a sambar weighing more than ten times as much as a single dhole. Working together is everything. Some members chase, while others cut corners to head the prey off, often driving it into water.

Dholes eat fast. A pack can finish a chital in well under an hour. Eating quickly means bigger predators, such as tigers and leopards, have less chance to steal the meal. The relationship works both ways. There are records of dhole packs mobbing a tiger and even driving one away from a kill, though a tiger can easily kill a single dhole.

A pack that shares everything

In a dhole pack, usually only one pair has pups. But the whole pack raises them. Adults bring food back to the den by swallowing meat at the kill and bringing it up again for the pups. The pups also get to eat first at a kill, ahead of the adults.

Dholes do not usually fight over food. Biologists have watched them share kills with very little squabbling, which is unusual among predators. A dhole pack also has a strict morning routine. Members greet each other with whines, licks and play, then set off together at dawn. Their teeth are a little different from other dogs' too. They have fewer molars, with sharp shearing edges that help them cut meat quickly.

Red dogs in stories

Rudyard Kipling wrote about dholes in a story called "Red Dog", in The Second Jungle Book. In it, a huge pack sweeps through the forest and Mowgli leads the wolves against them. The story made dholes sound like monsters.

For a long time, many people treated them that way. In British India, there were cash rewards for killing dholes, because hunters saw them as rivals for deer. Thousands were shot and poisoned.

Why dholes are in trouble

Today, dholes face new problems. Forests are being cut into pieces, so packs cannot roam as far as they need to. The deer they hunt are fewer in many places. Diseases such as rabies and distemper can spread from village dogs and wipe out a whole pack.

Dholes have vanished from roughly three-quarters of their old range, which once stretched from Siberia to Java. India's tiger reserves have helped, because protecting forests for tigers also protects the deer that dholes eat.

They are cousins of the wolves that roam India's grasslands, but the two have very different lives. A wolf pack howls across open country. A dhole pack whistles under the trees, and you could walk past one without ever knowing it was there.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Dholes are just stray dogs that went wild.

Truth: Dholes are a separate wild species with their own genus. They split off from the ancestors of wolves and dogs long ago, and they cannot be tamed like pet dogs.

Try this

Play a whistle-tag game outdoors with an adult watching. One person hides behind a tree or a wall and gives short whistles. The rest must find them only by sound. Try a high whistle and a low one, and notice which is easier to follow.
