What is a camel library?

In north-eastern Kenya, a library arrives on the backs of camels, and children run out to meet it under a shady tree.

The short answer

A camel library is exactly what it sounds like: books carried on camels to people who live far from any town. The most famous one began in 1996 in Garissa, in north-eastern Kenya, near the border with Somalia. Many families there are herders who move with their animals to find water and grass. There are few roads, and a van would get stuck in the sand.

So librarians loaded boxes of books onto camels and walked them out to villages and camps. They set up under a tree, spread out mats, and children chose books to borrow until the camels came back.

Surprising but true

In the 1930s, librarians in the mountains of Kentucky, USA, delivered books on horseback, riding up to about 160 km a week.

How it works

In Garissa, Kenya's camel library used six camels, in two caravans of three, each led by a handler, with librarians travelling alongside. Books are packed into wooden or metal boxes strapped on each side of a camel's hump. A camel can carry a load heavier than two grown men, walk long distances in heat and go days without drinking.

At each stop, the librarians open the boxes, set out a reading tent or mat and help children pick stories, schoolbooks and picture books. Many of the books are in English and Swahili. The camels chew on thorny bushes nearby while readers browse. A camel can drink well over 100 litres of water in a few minutes. Then it can walk for days in the heat without drinking again, which is just what a library on legs needs.

Rain, drought and moving families make the work hard. Books get lost when a family moves on, and dust and sun are tough on paper. Still, for many children in the area, the camel library has been the only place to borrow a book.

How do camels survive the desert?A thirsty camel can drink about 100 litres of water in a quarter of an hour, about five big buckets' worth.

Donkeys, horses and boats

Kenya's camels have lots of company. In Colombia, a teacher named Luis Soriano started riding two donkeys, Alfa and Beto, into the hills with books in the 1990s. He called it the Biblioburro, "donkey library". In Ethiopia, a donkey-drawn cart library has visited villages around the country.

In Mongolia, a children's author named Jambyn Dashdondog has taken books across the Gobi Desert by camel and other means for years. In Bangladesh, where floods cut off villages every monsoon, boats carry libraries along rivers, and some even double as floating schools.

Pack horse librarians of Kentucky

The USA had its own version. During the Great Depression of the 1930s, many families in the Appalachian mountains of Kentucky lived far from roads, schools or libraries. In 1935, a government programme hired local people, mostly women, to deliver books on horseback and mule.

The pack horse librarians rode up to about 160 km a week, crossing creeks and muddy trails in all weather. They carried books, magazines and scrapbooks of recipes and patterns made by the readers themselves. The project ran until 1943.

Some librarians rode routes so remote that they left books at a central house, and families from further up the hollows walked down to collect them. Readers especially asked for magazines and books that taught practical skills, along with children's picture books. At its peak, the project reached about 100,000 people in rural Kentucky.

Why it matters

Reading changes what a child can learn and do, but books are expensive and heavy, and many places have no shop or library. Moving libraries solve that problem by bringing books to readers instead of the other way round. India has its own versions: library buses in cities, cycle libraries in villages, and volunteers who carry books to tribal hamlets.

All of these follow the same simple idea as the very first libraries: books are worth more when they are shared. The ancient world's most famous collection, in Alexandria, is the starting point for the story of the world's great libraries.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Libraries need a building.

Truth: A library is a shared collection of books that people can borrow. Around the world, libraries travel on camels, donkeys, horses, boats, buses and bicycles to reach readers who live far away.

Try this

Start a "travelling library" with friends. Each person puts two books in a bag or box. Every week, pass the bag on to the next person, who takes one book out and adds one back. Keep a list inside the bag of who read what.
