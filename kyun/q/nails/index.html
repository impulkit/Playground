What are fingernails made of?

Your fingernails are made of the same tough protein as a rhino's horn and a horse's hoof.

The short answer

Fingernails are made of keratin, a tough protein that also makes up your hair and the outer layer of your skin. In nails, the keratin is packed into hard, flat layers.

Your nail starts under the skin at the base of your finger, in a pocket called the matrix. Cells there keep dividing. The older cells are pushed forward, flatten, fill with keratin and die. They harden into the nail plate that slides slowly along your fingertip.

That's why cutting your nails doesn't hurt. The part you trim has no nerves and no blood. The pink colour under the nail comes from the living skin beneath it, which is full of tiny blood vessels.

Surprising but true

Why we have them

Nails protect the soft tips of your fingers and toes. They also help you pick up small things, like a coin from a smooth floor or a grain of rice from a plate. Without nails, scratching an itch or peeling a banana sticker would be much harder.

Nails make your fingertips more sensitive too. When you press on something, the nail gives the soft pad something firm to push against, so the nerves feel more.

Slow and steady

A study of young adults in the USA found fingernails grow about 3.5 mm a month. Toenails grow less than half as fast. If you lose a fingernail, a new one takes about six months to grow back fully. A big toenail can take a year or more.

Nails often grow faster on your writing hand, in summer and in younger people. The nail on your middle finger usually grows faster than the one on your little finger.

Half-moons and white spots

The pale half-moon at the base of many nails is called the lunula. It's the front edge of the matrix, showing through.

Small white spots are usually old, harmless bumps that are growing out. A doctor can sometimes spot clues about health in nails, such as unusual colours or ridges. If a nail suddenly changes a lot, it's worth showing a trusted adult.

Cousins of claws and horns

Keratin is used all over the animal kingdom. The claws of cats, the hooves of horses and cows, the beaks of birds and the scales of reptiles are all made of it. So is the horn of an Indian one-horned rhino. Unlike a deer's antlers, which are bone, a rhino horn is a solid mass of keratin, the same stuff as your nails.

Most mammals have claws. Monkeys, apes and humans have flatter nails instead, which may have helped our tree-climbing ancestors grip branches and handle fruit with sensitive fingertips. A langur sitting on a temple wall has nails much like yours.

The longest nails

In 1952, a schoolboy in Pune named Shridhar Chillal stopped cutting the nails on his left hand. He kept them growing for 66 years. They curled into long spirals and, by the end, measured more than 9 metres added together, longer than three autorickshaws parked end to end. In 2018 he finally had them cut in New York, and they went on display at a Ripley's museum there.

A myth about the end of life

People sometimes say nails and hair keep growing after a person dies. They don't. Growing needs living cells. What happens is that skin dries and pulls back a little, so the nails can look longer than before.

Myth or fact?

Myth: White spots on your nails mean you need more calcium.

Truth: Small white spots usually come from minor knocks to the base of the nail weeks earlier. They grow out on their own. Lack of calcium is not the usual cause.

Try this

With an adult's permission, put a tiny dot of washable marker or nail paint at the very bottom of one fingernail, just above the skin. Measure the distance from the skin to the dot with a ruler every week for a month. Work out how many millimetres your nail grew each week.
