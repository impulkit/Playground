Why is Bodh Gaya so important to Buddhists?

In a small town in Bihar, a peepal tree marks the spot where a young prince is said to have become the Buddha.

The short answer

About 2,500 years ago, a prince named Siddhartha Gautama left his comfortable life to find out why people suffer. After years of searching, he sat down under a peepal tree near the Niranjana river and decided not to get up until he understood. Buddhists believe that there, on a full-moon night, he reached enlightenment, a deep understanding of life. From then on he was called the Buddha, "the awakened one".

That spot is now Bodh Gaya, in Bihar. For Buddhists it is the holiest place on Earth. Monks and visitors arrive from Sri Lanka, Thailand, Japan, Tibet, Myanmar, the USA and dozens of other countries to sit, chant and walk around the tree.

Surprising but true

The Bodhi tree at Bodh Gaya is thought to be a grandchild of the original. A branch was taken to Sri Lanka over 2,000 years ago, and a cutting from that tree was brought back in the 1880s.

The temple and the tree

Beside the tree rises the Mahabodhi Temple, a brick tower about 55 metres tall, roughly as high as an 18-storey building. Its walls are covered in carved niches with small Buddha figures. Inside sits a large golden statue of the Buddha touching the ground with his right hand, a gesture that recalls the moment he called the Earth to witness his awakening.

Behind the temple, between its wall and the tree, lies a stone slab called the Vajrasana, or "diamond throne". It marks the place where he is said to have sat. Prayer flags hang from the railings, and pilgrims leave flowers, candles and saffron cloth.

What are the Jataka tales?Before he became the Buddha, Buddhists say, he lived hundreds of lives, as a monkey king, a deer, an elephant and a generous hare.

A tree with a family history

The Emperor Ashoka, who became a Buddhist after a terrible war, visited Bodh Gaya in the 3rd century BCE. Buddhist tradition says his daughter Sanghamitta carried a branch of the Bodhi tree to Sri Lanka, where it was planted at Anuradhapura. That tree still grows there and is one of the oldest planted trees with a recorded history.

Over the centuries, the tree in Bodh Gaya was damaged and replaced more than once. In the 1880s, a cutting was brought back from Sri Lanka and planted on the old spot. So the tree you see today is probably a grandchild of the first.

Lost and found

After Buddhism faded in much of India, Bodh Gaya was neglected for hundreds of years. The temple fell into ruin and was partly buried. In the 1880s, British archaeologists led by Alexander Cunningham, with help from the king of Burma, repaired it.

A Sri Lankan Buddhist named Anagarika Dharmapala visited in 1891 and was upset to find the temple in poor shape. He started the Maha Bodhi Society to campaign for Buddhists to look after it. Since 1949, a committee with both Buddhist and Hindu members has managed the temple. Some Buddhists still campaign for it to be run by Buddhists alone, while others are content with the shared arrangement.

A town of many countries

Walk around Bodh Gaya today and it feels like a world tour. Thailand, Bhutan, Japan, Tibet, Vietnam and other countries have built monasteries here, each in its own style. One looks like a Thai palace with a gold roof, while another has a Japanese garden.

The ruins of Nalanda, once a great centre of Buddhist learning, are about 80 km away. Pilgrims often visit both, along with Rajgir nearby, where the Buddha spent many rainy seasons teaching.

The busiest full moon

The biggest day of the year in Bodh Gaya is Buddha Purnima, the full moon of the month of Vaishakha, which usually falls in April or May. Many Buddhists remember the Buddha's birth, enlightenment and death together on that day. The town fills with monks in maroon, saffron and grey robes, chanting in Pali, Tibetan, Japanese and many other languages.

Winter is busy too, when the weather is cool and Tibetan Buddhists arrive in large numbers. Nearby stands the Great Buddha Statue, a seated figure about 25 metres tall that was unveiled in 1989. Children from local villages sell beads and lotus flowers to visitors, and the sound of bells carries across the town.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Bodhi tree at Bodh Gaya is the very same tree the Buddha sat under.

Truth: The original tree died long ago. The tree there today is believed to have grown from a cutting of the Sri Maha Bodhi in Anuradhapura, Sri Lanka, which itself grew from a branch of the first tree.

Try this

Find a peepal leaf on the ground and look at its long, pointed tip. Hold the leaf flat and drip a little water near the top. Watch how the water slides down and drips off the tip, the way it does in heavy rain.
