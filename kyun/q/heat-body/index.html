What happens to your body on a very hot day?

On a scorching day, your body can sweat out more than a litre of water an hour, and it only cools you if the sweat dries.

The short answer

Your body works best at about 37°C. On a very hot day, it has to work hard to stay there. Blood vessels near your skin widen, which is why your face goes red, so more warm blood can lose heat to the air. And you sweat. As sweat evaporates from your skin, it carries heat away.

If you lose too much water through sweat and don't drink enough, you become dehydrated: tired, dizzy and headachy. If your body cannot cool itself at all, its core temperature can climb to dangerous levels. That is heatstroke, a medical emergency.

Surprising but true

Humid heat is more dangerous than dry heat at the same temperature, because sweat cannot evaporate easily from your skin when the air is already full of water.

Your built-in cooling system

Deep in your brain, a small region called the hypothalamus acts like a thermostat. It notices when your blood is getting too warm and switches on cooling. Your heart pumps faster to push more blood to the skin. Millions of sweat glands start releasing salty water.

Humans are unusually good at sweating. We have more sweat glands than most mammals, and very little fur. Many scientists think this let our ancestors hunt and travel in the heat of the African midday, when other animals had to rest in the shade.

Why humid heat feels worse

Sweat only cools you when it evaporates. In dry air, it disappears quickly. In humid air, already full of water vapour, sweat sits on the skin or drips off without cooling you much.

So 38°C in coastal Chennai or Kolkata in June often feels worse than 38°C in dry Jaipur. Scientists combine heat and humidity into a measure called the wet-bulb temperature. When it gets close to 35°C, even a healthy person resting in the shade struggles to cool down. Such conditions are still rare, but they have been recorded along parts of the Persian Gulf and in South Asia for short periods.

Why are heatwaves getting worse?A summer heatwave that might have come once in a lifetime a century ago now arrives far more often, and scientists can measure why.

Warning signs

Heat exhaustion comes first. Signs include heavy sweating, weakness, headache, feeling sick and cramps. Moving somewhere cool, resting and sipping water usually help.

Heatstroke is more serious. The body's temperature may rise to 40°C or higher, the skin may feel hot, and a person may become confused or faint. They need emergency medical help straight away. While waiting, adults can move them into shade and cool them with water and fanning. Babies, older people, outdoor workers and people with some illnesses are most at risk, which is why checking on neighbours during a heatwave matters.

Record heat in India and the USA

India's highest temperature on record is 51.0°C, measured at Phalodi in Rajasthan on 19 May 2016. Cities across north India regularly pass 45°C in May and June. The India Meteorological Department issues heatwave warnings, and many cities now have heat action plans. Ahmedabad was one of the first in South Asia, in 2013, after a deadly heatwave in 2010. Its plan includes text-message alerts, cool shelters and painting some roofs white to reflect sunlight.

In the USA, Death Valley in California holds the official world record of 56.7°C, from 1913, though some climate scientists question that old reading. Phoenix, Arizona, had 31 days in a row at or above 110°F (about 43°C) in July 2023.

Staying cool

The simplest advice works. Drink water regularly, even before you feel thirsty. Wear loose, light-coloured cotton clothes and a hat. Stay in the shade during the hottest hours, usually from about noon to 4 pm. Old Indian tricks help too: buttermilk, lime water with a pinch of salt, and water kept in clay pots that cool it through evaporation.

Animals have their own methods. Dogs pant, elephants flap their huge ears, and many desert creatures simply sleep through the day and come out at night.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You only need to drink water when you feel thirsty.

Truth: On very hot days, especially when you are active, your body can lose water faster than thirst warns you. Sipping water regularly, before you feel very thirsty, helps prevent dehydration.

Try this

Wet a cloth and wrap it around one thermometer, or just around the end of one finger. Leave another thermometer or finger dry. Sit in front of a fan for two minutes. The wet one feels or reads cooler, because evaporating water carries heat away, just like sweat.
