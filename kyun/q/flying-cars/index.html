Will we ever have flying cars?

People have built cars that fly since the 1930s. The hard part has never been getting them in the air. It is making them safe, quiet and cheap enough for ordinary streets.

The short answer

Flying cars already exist, in a way. Inventors have built cars that sprout wings, and small aircraft that can drive on roads, for almost a hundred years. Some have flown well.

What we don't have yet is the dream from cartoons: millions of people zipping over traffic jams in their own family car. That runs into big problems. Flying is far harder than driving, aircraft are expensive and noisy, and a crash in the air over a city is much more dangerous than a bump on the road.

The idea most likely to arrive soon is different. It is the air taxi, a small electric aircraft that takes off straight up like a helicopter and carries a few passengers across a city, flown by a trained pilot.

Surprising but true

In 2021 a car with wings that fold out, called the AirCar, flew for about 35 minutes between two airports in Slovakia, then drove into town on ordinary roads.

Cars with wings

In 1937 an American inventor, Waldo Waterman, flew his Arrowbile, a car with detachable wings. In the 1940s and 1950s other roadable aircraft followed. One, the Aerocar, was approved to fly in the USA in 1956, but only a handful were built.

The problem was always the same. A good car needs to be heavy and strong to survive bumps. A good plane needs to be light. A machine that does both usually does both badly.

In 2021, a Slovakian inventor, Stefan Klein, flew his AirCar from the airport in Nitra to the airport in Bratislava in about 35 minutes. On landing, the wings folded away in a few minutes, and he drove into the city. It is a road car that can fly, but it still needs a runway.

The air taxi idea

New technology has changed the picture. Batteries and electric motors have become lighter and more powerful. Computers can keep a wobbly aircraft steady, as they do in drones.

So many companies are building eVTOLs, which stands for electric vertical take-off and landing. Most look like a cross between a small plane and a giant drone. They have several propellers that tilt or lift, so they can rise straight up from a rooftop, then fly forward on wings.

Firms in the USA, Europe, China and India are testing them. In 2024 the US company Joby agreed with Dubai to start air taxi rides there, with first paying passengers planned for about 2026. In India, start-ups such as the ePlane Company, which began at IIT Madras, and Bengaluru's Sarla Aviation are designing air taxis for crowded cities.

The hard problems

Batteries are the biggest limit. They store far less energy for their weight than fuel does, so most air taxis can fly only short trips, often tens of kilometres, before recharging. That is fine for crossing a city, but not for a long journey.

Then there is safety. Every new aircraft must pass years of tests before passengers can ride in it. Cities would need landing pads called vertiports, and a new way to manage busy skies, a bit like air traffic control for low-flying taxis.

Noise matters too. Electric air taxis are quieter than helicopters, but hundreds of them over a city would still be heard. And at first, rides are likely to cost much more than a cab.

So, will we?

Probably, but not quite the way old films showed it. For a while, flying taxis will most likely be a pricey extra in a few rich cities, with pilots on board and fixed routes between airports and city centres.

Later, if batteries improve and the aircraft prove safe, the price could fall and pilots might be replaced by computers. Some experts think that could take decades. Others think it will never be cheap enough for everyday use. Either way, the family car parked on the roof is still a long way off, and most of us will keep using roads, metro trains and buses for years to come.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Flying cars have never actually been built.

Truth: Dozens have been built and flown since the 1930s, and some have been certified to fly. The trouble is that most were expensive, noisy or awkward as both a car and a plane.
