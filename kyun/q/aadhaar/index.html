What is Aadhaar?

The first Aadhaar number went to a woman in a small village in Maharashtra in 2010. Today well over 130 crore people have one.

The short answer

Aadhaar is a 12-digit number given to people who live in India. Each number belongs to just one person, for life. The word "aadhaar" means foundation or support in Hindi.

When you enrol, your name, date of birth, address and photo are recorded, along with all ten fingerprints and scans of both irises, the coloured rings in your eyes. These body measurements are called biometrics. Later, a fingerprint or iris scan can check that you are really the person linked to that number. The system is run by the Unique Identification Authority of India, known as UIDAI.

Surprising but true

Aadhaar records your fingerprints and eyes, but it deliberately does not record your religion, caste or income.

The first number

On 29 September 2010, in the village of Tembhli in Nandurbar district, Maharashtra, a woman named Ranjana Sonawane received the first Aadhaar number. Within about a decade, the great majority of people in India had one.

That makes Aadhaar the largest identity system of its kind in the world. Enrolment is free, and the number itself does not expire. Collecting fingerprints and eye scans from more than 130 crore people meant setting up enrolment centres in post offices, banks and schools in every corner of the country.

Why India built it

Before Aadhaar, many people, especially the poor, had no official papers at all. Without proof of who you are, it was hard to open a bank account, get a SIM card or receive government help like subsidised grain.

The idea was to give everyone a single, simple way to prove their identity. Government money for pensions and scholarships could then go straight into a person's bank account, linked to their Aadhaar. This helped millions open bank accounts, and it made payment systems like UPI easier to spread.

Children and Aadhaar

Children can have Aadhaar too. Under the age of five, a child gets a blue "Baal Aadhaar" card based on a photo and a parent's details, with no fingerprints taken, because small children's fingerprints are still changing.

At five and again at fifteen, the child's fingerprints and iris scans must be updated. Your face and hands change a lot as you grow, and the system needs a fresh record to recognise you. Schools sometimes hold special camps so that students can get these updates done in one go.

The privacy debate

Not everyone was comfortable with one giant database. Critics worried that linking Aadhaar to bank accounts, phones and travel could let someone track a person's whole life, and that a data leak could expose millions. Others pointed out that fingerprint machines sometimes fail for elderly people or manual workers with worn fingertips, who then struggled to get rations. To get around that, many offices also accept a one-time password sent to a registered phone, or a scan of the eyes.

Supporters argued that Aadhaar reduced fraud, saved government money and helped people who had been left out.

The Supreme Court stepped in. In 2017 it ruled that privacy is a fundamental right of every Indian. In 2018 it said Aadhaar itself was allowed, but it could not be made compulsory for things like school admission, opening a bank account or getting a mobile connection. It is still required for some things, such as filing income tax and many government benefits.

The United States has a different system: a nine-digit Social Security number, started in the 1930s, with no fingerprints attached.

Myth or fact?

Myth: An Aadhaar card proves that you are an Indian citizen.

Truth: Aadhaar proves who you are, not your nationality. Residents of India, including some foreign nationals, can get one. Citizenship is shown by other documents, such as a passport.

Try this

Make a fingerprint card. Rub a soft pencil on a scrap of paper until it is dark, press your fingertip on it, then press a piece of clear sticky tape onto your finger and stick the tape on white paper. Compare your print with a family member's. Look for loops, whorls and arches.
