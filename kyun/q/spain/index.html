What is Spain known for?

Every August, in one small Spanish town, thousands of people throw over 100 tonnes of ripe tomatoes at each other for an hour.

The short answer

Spain is a large country in south-western Europe that covers most of the Iberian Peninsula. It has hot, dry plains in the middle, snowy mountains in the north and south, and long coasts on the Mediterranean and the Atlantic. Madrid, the capital, is almost exactly in the centre.

Spain is known for its sunshine and beaches, its football clubs, its late dinners, flamenco, tapas and festivals. It is also known for its history. Muslim rulers governed much of it for centuries, and later Spanish ships built an empire across the Americas. That is why Spanish is now the first language of nearly 50 crore people, more than any other language except Mandarin Chinese.

Surprising but true

Spain sits in the same longitude band as the UK and Portugal, but since 1940 it has kept its clocks on Central European Time, one reason Spaniards eat dinner so late.

Seven centuries of al-Andalus

In 711, armies from North Africa crossed the narrow strait from what is now Morocco. Within a few years they ruled most of the peninsula. The Arabs and Berbers called it al-Andalus, a name that lives on in the southern region of Andalusia.

For hundreds of years, cities like Córdoba were centres of learning, where Muslim, Jewish and Christian scholars translated Greek books and studied medicine and astronomy. Christian kingdoms in the north slowly pushed south. The last Muslim kingdom, Granada, surrendered in January 1492.

Granada's hilltop palace, the Alhambra, survives, with courtyards of fountains and walls carved like lace. Spanish still has thousands of words that came from Arabic, from aceite (oil) to azúcar (sugar), a reminder of how words travel with people.

1492 and the Americas

The same year Granada fell, Queen Isabella and King Ferdinand paid for an Italian sailor, Christopher Columbus, to sail west, hoping to reach Asia. He reached the Caribbean instead. Over the next century, Spain conquered huge areas of the Americas, from Mexico to Peru, with terrible losses for the Indigenous peoples who lived there.

Ships brought back gold and silver, but also new plants: potatoes, chillies, maize, chocolate and tomatoes. Spanish food was never the same again.

Tomatoes as weapons

On the last Wednesday of August, the town of Buñol near Valencia holds La Tomatina. Trucks tip over 100 tonnes of overripe tomatoes into the streets, and for one hour about 20,000 people throw them at each other. Afterwards, fire hoses wash the town clean. The festival started in 1945, reportedly with a scuffle at a parade.

Tomatoes are usually eaten, of course, in dishes like gazpacho, a cold soup, and pa amb tomàquet, bread rubbed with tomato and olive oil. Spain produces more olive oil than any other country, often close to half the world's supply.

Dinner at ten

Spaniards often eat lunch at 2 or 3 in the afternoon and dinner at 9 or 10 at night. One reason may be the clock. Geographically, Spain lines up with Britain and Portugal. But in 1940, the dictator Francisco Franco moved Spain's clocks forward one hour to match Germany and much of Europe. They never moved back, so the sun rises and sets late by the clock. Spain's time zone is still debated today.

In Andalusia, flamenco blends Romani, Andalusian and other traditions into fierce singing, guitar and stamping dance. UNESCO lists it as part of the world's intangible cultural heritage. And on the far side of the country, in Barcelona, the giant church of the Sagrada Família, begun in 1882 and shaped by the architect Antoni Gaudí, is still being finished.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Everyone in Spain takes a long nap every afternoon.

Truth: The siesta is a real tradition, especially in hot southern areas in summer, but surveys suggest only a minority of Spaniards nap regularly. Most people in cities work through the afternoon like people anywhere else.
