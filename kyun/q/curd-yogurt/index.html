How does milk turn into curd?

Every bowl of dahi is made by billions of bacteria, and many families keep the same family of microbes going for years, one spoonful at a time.

The short answer

Milk turns into curd because of bacteria. You warm some milk, let it cool until it feels just warm on your finger, and stir in a spoonful of curd from yesterday. That spoonful contains huge numbers of lactic acid bacteria.

Over the next few hours, the bacteria feed on lactose, the natural sugar in milk, and turn it into lactic acid. The acid makes the milk proteins unravel and link up into a soft, wobbly gel that traps the water. By morning the liquid milk has become thick, tangy curd. The sour taste is the acid. The thickness is the protein net.

Surprising but true

The bacterium that helps make most of the world's yogurt was first identified in 1905 by a Bulgarian medical student named Stamen Grigorov, which is why it is called Lactobacillus bulgaricus.

The magic spoonful

In Indian homes, the spoonful of old curd is called the starter or jaamun. It carries a whole community of microbes. Families pass starters to neighbours, and a new bride sometimes brings one from her mother's kitchen.

Because each starter has its own mix of microbes, curd can taste a little different from house to house. Scientists who study traditional Indian curd find many kinds of lactic acid bacteria in it, along with some yeasts. Commercial yogurt is usually made with just two carefully chosen bacteria.

Why warmth matters

The bacteria in curd grow best between about 30 and 45°C. That is why curd sets quickly in May and slowly in December. If the milk is too hot, the bacteria die. If it is too cold, they hardly move.

So families keep curd somewhere snug in winter, wrapped in a cloth or tucked inside a closed casserole. In the past, some kept clay pots of curd near the warm kitchen fire. Clay also soaks up some water, which helps make the curd extra thick, as in Bengal's famous sweet mishti doi.

A student and his microscope

In 1905, a 27-year-old Bulgarian medical student named Stamen Grigorov was studying in Geneva. He looked at yogurt from his home country under a microscope and identified the rod-shaped bacterium that helps it set. It was named Lactobacillus bulgaricus.

How did microscopes reveal a hidden world?In the 1670s, a Dutch cloth seller peered through a lens smaller than a pea and saw tiny creatures swimming in a drop of pond water. Nobody had ever seen them before.

Around the same time, the Russian scientist Élie Metchnikoff noticed that some Bulgarian villagers lived long lives and suggested yogurt might be part of the reason. His idea made yogurt popular in Europe and America. Scientists still don't know whether yogurt helps people live longer.

Curd in Indian meals

Curd cools a meal in hot weather and calms a fiery curry. It becomes raita with cucumber, chaas or lassi when whisked with water, and kadhi when cooked with gram flour. In Tamil Nadu and Karnataka, a meal often ends with curd rice.

In India, "curd" means dahi. In Britain and America, "curds" usually means the lumps that form when making cheese, so Indian visitors abroad sometimes find the word confusing.

Curd and your stomach

The acid in curd keeps many harmful germs from growing, which is why it was a safe way to store milk before fridges. Some of its lactose has already been used up by bacteria, so many people who get tummy aches from milk can eat curd.

Live bacteria in curd may also join the friendly microbes in your gut for a while. Researchers are studying how much they help. What is clear is that a bowl of curd is a good source of protein and calcium, and it has been part of Indian meals for thousands of years.

Myth or fact?

Myth: All curd and yogurt contains living bacteria.

Truth: Fresh homemade curd does. But some shop yogurts are heated after they set, which kills the bacteria. Labels that say "live cultures" mean the bacteria are still alive.

Try this

With an adult, set two small bowls of curd from the same warm milk and starter. Wrap one in a towel and keep it in a warm cupboard. Leave the other uncovered in a cool room. Check both every two hours and note which sets first and which tastes more sour.
