How do you tell crocodiles from alligators?

Look at the smile. If you can see a big lower tooth poking up when its mouth is shut, it's a crocodile.

The short answer

Crocodiles and alligators are cousins, both members of a group called crocodilians. The quickest way to tell them apart is the snout and the teeth.

An alligator has a wide, rounded snout shaped like a U. When it shuts its mouth, the upper jaw covers the lower teeth, so you mostly see a row of top teeth. A crocodile's snout is narrower and more pointed, like a V. Its upper and lower jaws are about the same width, so teeth from both stick out when its mouth is closed. The giveaway is a big fourth tooth on each side of the lower jaw, which sits in a notch and pokes up like a fang.

Surprising but true

South Florida is the only place on Earth where wild alligators and crocodiles live side by side.

More clues

Colour helps a little. Alligators are usually dark, almost black. Crocodiles tend to be lighter, olive or tan.

Look closely at the skin. Crocodiles have tiny dark dots on almost every scale. These are pressure sensors that feel ripples in the water. Alligators have them only around the jaws. Those jaw sensors are very sharp. In experiments, alligators in a dark tank turned towards a single drop of water landing near their heads.

Crocodiles also handle salt water better. Glands on their tongues push out extra salt, so many crocodiles live in mangroves and river mouths. Alligators have much weaker salt glands and stay mostly in fresh water.

Where they live

There are only two kinds of alligator. The American alligator lives in the south-eastern United States, from the Carolinas to Texas. The Chinese alligator is small and very rare, surviving along the lower Yangtze River.

Crocodiles are far more widespread, across Africa, Asia, Australia and the Americas. South Florida is the one place where both live in the wild together. In the Everglades, a lucky visitor can see a dark American alligator in a freshwater pond and a pale American crocodile in a salty bay on the same day.

India's three

India has no alligators at all, but it has three crocodilians. The mugger, or marsh crocodile, lives in rivers, lakes and even reservoirs across most of the country. It has the broadest snout of any crocodile, so it is often mistaken for an alligator. In winter, muggers dig burrows in riverbanks and lie up in them on cold days.

The saltwater crocodile is the largest living reptile. Big males can grow longer than 6 m, about the length of a small truck. In India it lives in the Sundarbans, the Andaman and Nicobar Islands and Bhitarkanika in Odisha, where the 2025 count found 1,826 of them.

The third is the gharial, with a thin snout like a long pair of tongs. It catches fish with a quick sideways snap. Strictly speaking it isn't a true crocodile but belongs to its own family. It is critically endangered, and most survive in the Chambal River.

Old, but not old-fashioned

People sometimes call crocodilians living fossils, and their ancestors did live alongside the dinosaurs. Yet their closest living relatives are birds. Like birds, crocodilians build nests, and the mothers of many species guard them.

A mugger mother will gently carry her hatchlings to water inside her mouth. Those same jaws that can crush a turtle can hold a baby crocodile without a scratch.

Crocodilians were hunted heavily for their skins in the twentieth century, and many kinds became rare. In the 1970s India started breeding muggers, gharials and salties and releasing them into protected rivers and wetlands. The American alligator made a similar comeback after hunting was controlled. It was once endangered, and now there are more than a million in Florida alone.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The big reptiles in Indian rivers are alligators.

Truth: India has no wild alligators. Its three crocodilians are the mugger, the saltwater crocodile and the gharial. Alligators live only in the south-eastern United States and a small part of eastern China.
