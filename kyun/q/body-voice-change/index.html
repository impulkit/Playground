Why do voices change as kids grow?

In 1749, a choirboy in Vienna named Joseph Haydn was sent away from his famous choir because his voice had cracked. He grew up to be one of history's great composers.

The short answer

Your voice comes from two small bands of muscle and tissue in your throat, called vocal folds. They sit inside the voice box, or larynx. When you speak, air from your lungs makes them vibrate hundreds of times a second, and that buzz becomes sound.

During puberty, hormones tell the body to grow in many ways, and the larynx grows too. The vocal folds get longer and thicker. Longer, thicker folds vibrate more slowly, and slower vibrations make lower notes. So the voice gets deeper.

In boys, the larynx grows much more than in girls, so their voices drop by about an octave. Girls' voices usually drop by just a few notes.

Surprising but true

Girls' voices change at puberty too. Their voices drop by a few notes, much less than boys' voices, so most people never notice.

Why it cracks and squeaks

The change does not happen overnight. For several months, the folds are growing faster than the muscles that control them can adjust. It is like learning to play a guitar whose strings keep getting longer every week.

So a boy might start a sentence in a deep voice and suddenly squeak in the middle. That crack happens when control slips and the voice jumps to its old, higher setting. It is completely normal. For most people it settles down within a few months to a year. Girls go through a milder version too, and their voices can sound husky or wobbly for a while.

The hidden bump in the neck

As the larynx grows in boys, the front of it sticks out more. You can often see it as a bump in the neck, called the Adam's apple. Everyone has one. In girls and younger children, the larynx is smaller and the angle at the front is wider, so it barely shows.

A child's vocal folds are tiny, often under a centimetre long. In grown men they are about 1.5 to 2.5 cm, roughly the width of a big coin. In grown women they are a little shorter.

Measured in buzzes per second

Scientists measure how fast vocal folds vibrate in hertz, which means buzzes per second. A young child's voice often sits around 250 to 300 hertz. An adult woman's speaking voice averages around 200 hertz, and an adult man's around 100 to 120.

So a man's vocal folds, when he talks, are flapping open and shut about 100 times every second. Hold a note for one minute and they will have opened and closed around 6,000 times.

Sound travels from your folds as waves of squeezed air. Sound moves at about 343 metres per second through air, so a word you say reaches a friend across a classroom almost instantly.

Choirboys and changing voices

For centuries, famous choirs relied on boys with high, clear voices. The trouble was that a boy's best singing years often ended just when he got good. In 1749, the teenage Joseph Haydn was let go from the choir of St Stephen's Cathedral in Vienna because his voice had broken. He went on to write more than 100 symphonies.

Today, many choirs help singers through the change rather than sending them away. Voice teachers suggest singing gently in a comfortable range, drinking water and not shouting, while the voice settles. Some boys who sang high notes as children turn into strong tenors or deep basses later.

If a voice stays hoarse for weeks, or speaking hurts, it is a good idea to talk to a parent and a doctor. Usually, though, a cracking voice is just a sign that the body is right on schedule. Myth or fact?

Myth: Only boys' voices change during puberty.

Truth: Everyone's voice changes. Girls' voices usually drop by a few notes and become fuller, while boys' voices drop by about an octave, which is why their change is easier to hear.

Try this

Stretch a rubber band between two fingers and pluck it. Now stretch it longer, or use a thicker band, and pluck again. Listen to how the note changes. Then put your fingers gently on your throat and hum a high note and a low note to feel your own vocal folds buzzing.
