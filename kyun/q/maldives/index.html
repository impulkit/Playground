Why are the Maldives in danger from the sea?

The Maldives is the lowest country on Earth, and most of its land is less than a metre above the sea.

The short answer

The Maldives is a country of about 1,190 small coral islands in the Indian Ocean, south-west of India and Sri Lanka. The islands are grouped into 26 rings called atolls. Its land is flat and very low: about 80 percent of it lies less than a metre above sea level, and the highest natural point is only about 2.4 metres. No country on Earth is lower.

That makes it extremely sensitive to sea level rise. As the planet warms, ice on land melts and seawater expands, slowly lifting the ocean. Even a rise of half a metre would mean more flooding, salty water in wells and beaches washing away. Scientists expect the sea to rise somewhere between about 30 centimetres and a metre by 2100, depending on how much pollution the world produces.

Surprising but true

The English word "atoll", for a ring-shaped coral island, comes from Dhivehi, the language of the Maldives.

Islands made by animals

The Maldives sits on top of an underwater mountain range. Over millions of years, coral reefs grew on the old volcanic peaks, in rings near the surface. Waves broke off bits of coral and shells and piled them up as sandbanks, which slowly became islands with palm trees.

Much of the white sand has a funny source: parrotfish. They nibble algae off the coral with tough beaks and grind up the coral as they go, then pass it out as sand. A single big parrotfish can make hundreds of kilograms of sand a year.

The word for these rings, "atoll", came into English from Dhivehi, the language of the Maldives. It is one of many borrowed words in English that started life in South Asia.

A cabinet meeting under water

In October 2009, President Mohamed Nasheed and his ministers put on scuba gear and held a cabinet meeting on the seabed, about 6 metres down. They signed a letter calling on all countries to cut the gases that cause climate change. The pictures went around the world.

For the Maldives, warming oceans bring a second danger. When the sea gets too hot, corals push out the tiny algae that live inside them, turn white and may die. This happened across the Maldives in 1998 and again in 2016. Without living coral, there is no new sand, and the reefs that break the force of waves get weaker.

Building higher ground

The capital, Malé, packs well over a lakh of people onto an island of about 2 square kilometres, one of the most crowded places on Earth. A sea wall surrounds it. In December 2004, the Indian Ocean tsunami flooded much of the city and many other islands.

Next to Malé, the government has built a new island called Hulhumalé by pumping sand from the lagoon onto a reef. It stands about 2 metres above sea level, higher than most natural islands, and tens of thousands of people already live there. Other ideas include floating homes and moving people to a few bigger, raised islands.

Life in the atolls

Most Maldivians are Muslim and speak Dhivehi, written in a script called Thaana that runs from right to left. Fishing for tuna, often with poles and lines rather than big nets, has fed families for centuries. Today tourism is the biggest business. Many resorts take up a whole island each, with wooden villas on stilts over the lagoon.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Maldives are sinking.

Truth: The islands themselves are not sinking. The sea around them is slowly rising. Coral islands can even grow, as reefs make new sand, but scientists worry that the sea may rise faster than healthy reefs can keep up.
