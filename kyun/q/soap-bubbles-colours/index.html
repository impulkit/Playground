Why do soap bubbles have rainbow colours?

A soap bubble has no colour in it at all. Its rainbow comes from light bouncing off a skin thinner than light's own waves.

The short answer

A soap bubble is a ball of air wrapped in a very thin skin of water held between two layers of soap. When light hits that skin, some of it bounces off the outer surface and some bounces off the inner surface.

These two reflections travel slightly different distances. When they meet again, some colours line up and get brighter, while others cancel each other out. Which colours win depends on how thick the film is at that exact spot. Since the thickness keeps changing, the colours swirl and shift.

Scientists call this thin-film interference. It needs a film whose thickness is close to the size of a light wave, which is why a bubble shows colours and a glass of soapy water does not.

Surprising but true

Just before a bubble pops, the top of it can turn black, because the film has become too thin to reflect visible colours.

Light is a wave

Light travels as waves, and each colour has its own wavelength. Red waves are the longest that our eyes can see, at about 700 nanometres. Violet waves are shortest, at about 400. A nanometre is a millionth of a millimetre.

A bubble's skin is often only a few hundred nanometres thick. That is hundreds of times thinner than a hair on your head. It is also just about the same size as the light waves landing on it.

When the light reflected from the back of the film comes out exactly in step with the light from the front, that colour looks bright. When the two come out of step, the peaks of one wave fill the dips of the other and that colour fades away. Change the thickness by a tiny amount and a different colour comes out in step.

A bubble that is always draining

A bubble is never still. Gravity pulls the water in the film downwards, so the bottom grows thicker while the top grows thinner. Water also evaporates into the air. Puffs of air swirl the film around, which is why the colours seem to flow like oil paint.

Sugar or glycerine in bubble mix slows the drying, so bubbles last longer and the colours have more time to play. The soap itself matters too. It lowers the water's surface tension and keeps the skin stretchy instead of letting it snap. That stretchiness is also why bubbles pull themselves into spheres.

When a bubble turns black

Watch a bubble long enough and you may see a dark patch near the top. The film there has become thinner than about 30 nanometres. At that thickness, the two reflections cancel almost every colour we can see, so very little light comes back to your eye. The patch looks black against the shining colours around it.

A black patch is a warning. The film is close to its limit, and the bubble usually pops within seconds.

Newton's soap bubbles

More than 300 years ago, Isaac Newton spent hours watching soap bubbles. In his book Opticks, published in 1704, he described how the colours appear in rings and bands and change in a fixed order as the film thins. He measured the patterns carefully, even though nobody yet understood light as waves. Newton thought light was a stream of tiny particles, and later scientists used the wave idea to explain what he had seen.

The same effect paints the swirl of colours on a puddle with a little oil on it, after rain on a city road. A peacock's shimmering tail uses a related trick, with layers built into its feathers instead of a film of soap. A rainbow is different, because raindrops bend light rather than bouncing it between two surfaces.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Bubble colours come from dye or colour in the soap.

Truth: Clear soap and coloured soap make the same rainbow bubbles. The colours come from the thickness of the film, not from anything in it.

Try this

Mix a cup of water with a big squirt of washing-up liquid and a spoon of sugar or glycerine. Dip the rim of a plastic cup in it so a flat film forms across the opening, then tilt it upright near a window. Watch the coloured bands slide down and the top go dark.
