Who was Thomas Jefferson?

Thomas Jefferson wrote that "all men are created equal", yet he enslaved more than 600 people during his lifetime.

The short answer

Thomas Jefferson was one of the Founding Fathers of the United States. He wrote most of the Declaration of Independence in 1776, which announced that the American colonies were breaking away from Britain. Later he was the third president, from 1801 to 1809.

He was a lawyer, a farmer, an architect, an inventor and a collector of books. He doubled the size of the US with the Louisiana Purchase and founded the University of Virginia. But the man who wrote that all people have a right to liberty also enslaved hundreds of people. Both facts are part of his story.

Surprising but true

Jefferson and John Adams, his old friend and rival, both died on 4 July 1826, exactly 50 years after the Declaration of Independence was adopted.

The words that started a country

In June 1776, the Continental Congress asked a committee to write a statement explaining why the colonies were declaring independence. The committee, which included Benjamin Franklin and John Adams, handed the job to Jefferson, then 33.

He wrote it in about two weeks, in rented rooms in Philadelphia. The most famous line says that "all men are created equal" and have rights to "life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness". Congress made changes, including cutting a section where Jefferson blamed the British king for the slave trade. On 4 July 1776, they approved it.

Those words became a promise that later movements would point to, from campaigners against slavery to women seeking the vote.

President and explorer

As president, Jefferson made a huge deal. In 1803, France's leader Napoleon needed money for his wars and agreed to sell the vast Louisiana territory. For about 15 million dollars, the US roughly doubled in size.

Jefferson sent Meriwether Lewis and William Clark to explore it. Their journey, from 1804 to 1806, reached the Pacific Ocean, with help from Native American guides including a young Shoshone woman named Sacagawea. The Lewis and Clark expedition mapped rivers and recorded plants and animals new to science. For the Native nations already living there, though, the purchase was the start of losing their land.

Why are presidents carved into Mount Rushmore?Four faces, each about as tall as a six-storey building, were blasted out of a granite mountain with dynamite. Not one worker died in 14 years.

A house full of ideas

Jefferson designed his own home, Monticello, on a hilltop in Virginia, and kept changing it for 40 years. It had a dome, clever dumbwaiters for carrying wine between floors, and a clock that showed the day of the week. He loved gadgets, including a machine that copied his letters as he wrote them.

He owned one of the largest book collections in America. When British troops burned the Library of Congress in 1814, Jefferson sold his roughly 6,500 books to rebuild it, which is why libraries in the US owe him a debt.

The contradiction

Jefferson enslaved more than 600 people over his life. They built and ran Monticello, farmed his land and served his family. He called slavery wrong and a danger to the nation, but freed only a few of the people he enslaved.

Sally Hemings was an enslaved woman at Monticello. Evidence, including a DNA study in 1998, has led most historians to conclude that Jefferson fathered her six children, four of whom survived. Monticello, now a museum, tells the stories of the enslaved families alongside his.

Remembered and argued about

Jefferson's face is on the US nickel and on Mount Rushmore, and the Jefferson Memorial stands in Washington, DC. He asked that his gravestone list only three things: the Declaration of Independence, a Virginia law on religious freedom, and the University of Virginia. It doesn't mention that he was president.

Today, Americans still debate how to remember him. Many admire his ideas about freedom and democracy. Many also point out that he did not live up to them. Both views are common, and historians keep studying the gap between his words and his life.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Declaration of Independence was signed by everyone on 4 July 1776.

Truth: Congress voted for independence on 2 July and approved the final wording on 4 July. Most delegates signed the official copy on 2 August, and a few signed even later.
