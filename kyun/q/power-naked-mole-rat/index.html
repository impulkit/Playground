Why don't naked mole rats get cancer?

A naked mole rat is about the size of a mouse but can live for more than 30 years, and cancer in these animals is extremely rare.

The short answer

Naked mole rats are wrinkly, pink, almost hairless rodents that live underground in East Africa. They are about the size of a mouse. A mouse might live two to four years. A naked mole rat can live for more than 30.

Even more surprising, cancer is extremely rare in them. Scientists have found several reasons. Their cells make a thick, gooey substance that seems to stop them clumping into tumours. Their cells are also very quick to stop dividing when they get crowded. And their bodies seem extra good at fixing damaged proteins.

They are not completely cancer-proof. But they are so good at avoiding it that labs around the world study them.

Surprising but true

In a lab test, naked mole rats survived 18 minutes with no oxygen at all, by switching their bodies to run on a different sugar.

A sticky secret

Your body is made of trillions of cells, and most of them copy themselves now and then. Cancer starts when some cells keep copying when they shouldn't, piling up into a lump.

In 2013, scientists at the University of Rochester in New York found something odd. Naked mole rat cells were making huge amounts of a substance called hyaluronan. This is a sugary material found in many animals, including us, in joints and skin. But the mole rat version was about five times bigger, with extra-long molecules.

It made their cells very sensitive to crowding. As soon as cells touched each other, they stopped dividing. When the researchers removed this thick hyaluronan, the cells became much more likely to turn cancerous.

Growing old slowly

Most animals get weaker and more likely to die as they get older. Naked mole rats seem to break this rule. In 2018, scientists studied records from thousands of animals and found that their risk of dying barely rose with age, even after 25 years.

That makes them strange champions of ageing. Old mole rats still breed, stay strong and keep their bones healthy. Scientists hope that learning their secrets might one day help people stay healthier for longer, although that is a long way off.

Life in the dark

Naked mole rats live in big underground colonies, digging tunnels with their long front teeth. Their lips close behind their teeth, so they can dig without swallowing soil.

Like bees and ants, they live in a colony with one breeding queen. A few males breed with her. Everyone else works: digging, finding roots to eat, guarding against snakes or caring for pups. A colony can hold up to about 300 animals. They even share a toilet chamber, and when one fills up, they seal it off and dig a new one.

Their burrows have very little oxygen. In 2017, scientists found that naked mole rats survived 18 minutes with no oxygen at all. Their bodies switched to burning fructose, a sugar found in fruit, which needs no oxygen to release energy. They also feel very little pain from some acids that would hurt other animals.

Each colony has its own accent

Naked mole rats chirp to each other all the time. In 2021, scientists recorded thousands of soft chirps from different colonies. They found that each colony had its own dialect, a slightly different way of chirping.

Mole rats answered more often to the chirps of their own colony. When pups were raised in a different colony, they learned that colony's dialect. The queen may help keep the dialect steady.

This is one of the clearest examples of a "culture" of calls in rodents, a topic explored in animal communication. And the strangest part? These wrinkly diggers live in the same dry lands of Kenya where elephants and lions roam above them, completely unaware of the busy cities under their feet.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Naked mole rats can never get cancer.

Truth: For years scientists thought so. Then in 2016, researchers reported a few cases in zoo animals. Cancer is still extremely rare in them, which makes them very useful to study, but they are not completely immune.
