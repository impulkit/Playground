How does an ATM work?

The world's first cash machine opened in a London suburb in 1967. It didn't take cards at all, only special paper cheques marked with a faint radioactive substance.

The short answer

An ATM, or automated teller machine, is a computer in a strong steel box, connected to banks over a secure network. When you put in your card and type your PIN, the machine reads your card, scrambles your PIN and sends both to your bank. The bank's computer checks that the PIN is right and that there's enough money in your account.

If everything matches, the bank sends back a yes. The ATM counts out notes from locked boxes inside, pushes them out of the slot and records the withdrawal. Your bank balance drops at the same moment.

The whole conversation between machine and bank usually takes just a few seconds.

Surprising but true

The inventor of the cash machine first planned a six-digit PIN, but chose four after his wife said six was too many to remember.

A bath-time idea

The story goes that John Shepherd-Barron, a British engineer, got the idea in the bath after arriving at his bank just after it had closed. If chocolate bars could come out of a machine, he thought, why not cash?

His machine opened at a Barclays branch in Enfield, north London, on 27 June 1967. Customers bought special paper cheques in advance. Each carried a mark of carbon-14, a very faintly radioactive substance, which the machine could detect. You put in the cheque, typed a number and got ten pounds.

Shepherd-Barron wanted a six-digit code, using his army number. His wife, Caroline, said she could only remember four. So four digits it was, and four-digit PINs are still the most common worldwide.

Inside the box

A modern ATM has a few main parts. A card reader takes information from the magnetic stripe or, more securely, the small gold chip. A keypad with a metal shield lets you type your PIN. A screen shows instructions.

Deep inside is a safe holding cash cassettes, boxes of notes sorted by value. A dispenser pulls notes out one at a time. Sensors measure each note's thickness with light, so two stuck together get rejected rather than handed out by mistake.

The PIN never travels as plain numbers. It's scrambled using encryption before it leaves the keypad, so even if someone tapped the line, they'd see only gibberish.

ATMs come to India

India's first ATM was opened by HSBC in Mumbai in 1987. For years ATMs stayed mainly in big cities. Then banks spread them to towns and villages, and many now take cards from any Indian bank through shared networks.

By March 2025, India had about 2,51,000 ATMs, according to the Reserve Bank of India. That was slightly fewer than a year before, because more people now pay by phone using UPI instead of carrying cash.

Indian ATMs can be found in surprising places, from railway stations and ferry jetties to towns high in the Himalayas. In the USA, many ATMs are owned by private companies and sit in petrol stations, shops and even bars, charging a fee for each withdrawal.

Keeping it safe

Thieves try tricks such as fake card readers stuck over the real slot, called skimmers, and tiny cameras aimed at the keypad. That's why banks tell customers to cover their hand while typing a PIN and to check that nothing looks loose or odd.

Chip cards help, because they're much harder to copy than magnetic stripes. Some new ATMs work with no card at all, by scanning a QR code with a phone.

What comes next

As passwords and phones take over, cash use is falling in many countries. But ATMs still matter for people without smartphones, or in places where the internet is patchy. The basic job hasn't changed since 1967: check who you are, check what you have, and count out notes without a single mistake.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If you type your PIN backwards at an ATM, it secretly calls the police.

Truth: This story has spread by email and social media for years, but banks and police say no such system is in use. If you're ever threatened at a cash machine, the safest thing is to hand over the money and tell an adult or the police straight away.
