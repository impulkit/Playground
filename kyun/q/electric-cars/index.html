How do electric cars work?

Electric cars aren't new. Around the year 1900, about a third of the cars on American roads ran on batteries.

The short answer

An electric car has no petrol tank and no engine full of pistons. Instead, it has a big battery, usually a flat pack stretched under the floor, and one or more electric motors.

When you press the accelerator, electricity flows from the battery to a box of electronics. That box controls how much power the motor gets. Inside the motor, the electricity creates magnetic forces that push a central shaft round and round. Gears pass that spin to the wheels. There is no burning, no exhaust and hardly any noise.

To refill, you plug the car into a charger, at home or at a charging station. Electricity flows back into the battery, ready for the next trip.

Surprising but true

An electric car can recharge its own battery a little every time it slows down, by running its motor backwards as a generator.

Electricity into motion

Electric motors are very simple compared with petrol engines. A petrol engine has hundreds of moving parts, from pistons and valves to the gearbox. An electric motor has one main spinning part. That means less to wear out and fewer oil changes.

Motors are also very efficient. Roughly 85–90% of the battery's energy ends up moving the car. A petrol car turns only about a quarter of its fuel's energy into motion. The rest is lost as heat.

Electric motors also give their full pushing force from the moment they start turning. That's why many electric cars shoot forward so quickly when the light turns green.

Braking that makes power

When a petrol car brakes, its pads squeeze the wheels, and all that moving energy is wasted as heat. An electric car can do better. When the driver lifts off the pedal, the motor switches roles and becomes a generator. The wheels spin it, it makes electricity, and the battery soaks some of it up. This is called regenerative braking.

In stop-and-go city traffic, this can save a lot of energy. Some drivers barely use the brake pedal at all. Electric trains, including many of India's newer locomotives, use the same trick to feed power back into the overhead wires as they slow down.

The first electric age

Electric cars were popular more than a century ago. Around 1900, according to the US Department of Energy, about a third of the cars on American roads ran on batteries. They were quiet, clean and easy to start, unlike petrol cars, which had to be cranked by hand.

Then, in 1908, Henry Ford launched the cheap petrol-powered Model T. Texas oil made petrol cheap, and by 1912 an electric starter removed the need for hand cranking. Electric cars faded away. They came back in the 2000s and 2010s, once lithium-ion batteries, the kind in phones and laptops, became powerful and cheap enough.

Electric India

In India, the electric revolution has started with smaller vehicles. Battery e-rickshaws now carry passengers in towns across the country, and electric scooters are sold by the lakh. Delhi, Bengaluru and other cities run electric buses.

How clean an electric car really is depends on where its electricity comes from. In places where most power comes from coal, the benefit is smaller. As countries build more solar panels and wind farms, electric cars get cleaner too. Making the batteries needs metals like lithium, nickel and cobalt, so engineers are working on ways to recycle old batteries and on new kinds that need fewer rare materials. That could matter a lot in the fight against climate change.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Electric cars cause no pollution at all.

Truth: They have no exhaust, so they keep city air cleaner. But making their batteries takes energy and mining, and the electricity that charges them may come from coal. Most studies find that over its whole life an electric car usually produces less carbon dioxide than a petrol car, and the gap grows as power stations get cleaner.

Try this

Find a pull-back toy car. Roll it backwards along the floor and feel it resist, then let go and watch it zoom forward. Pulling it back winds up a spring that stores energy, a bit like charging a battery. An electric car does something similar every time it brakes, saving energy to use again.
