What were Triceratops's horns and frill for?

The first Triceratops horns ever found were mistaken for the horns of a giant bison.

The short answer

Triceratops means "three-horned face". It had two long horns above its eyes, a short one on its nose, and a wide bony frill at the back of its head. It was a plant-eater as long as a school bus and heavier than an elephant.

Its horns and frill probably did several jobs. Healed wounds show that Triceratops fought each other, locking horns like deer. The horns could also have been used against hungry T. rex. The frill was big, bold and easy to see, so it may have helped Triceratops recognise each other and show off.

Surprising but true

Young Triceratops had horns that curved backwards. They turned to point forwards as the animal grew up.

The bison mistake

In 1887, a pair of large horn cores was found near Denver, in the USA. The famous fossil hunter Othniel Marsh thought they came from an enormous ancient bison and named it as one. Two years later, more complete skulls turned up in Wyoming. Marsh realised his mistake and named the animal Triceratops in 1889.

Since then, hundreds of Triceratops fossils have been dug up, many in Montana and the Dakotas. It is one of the most common big dinosaurs from the end of the dinosaur age. It shared those floodplains with T. rex.

Horn to horn

Many animals with horns today, such as antelope and wild sheep, use them mainly on each other, to win mates or space. In 2009, scientists studied dozens of Triceratops skulls and counted the healed injuries. Most were on the sides of the frill and on the cheekbones.

That is where a rival's horns would hit if two animals locked heads and twisted. A cousin called Centrosaurus, with one big nose horn and tiny brow horns, had far fewer injuries there. The wounds had healed, so these animals lived through their fights.

Facing T. rex

Triceratops did meet T. rex. Some Triceratops bones carry T. rex tooth marks. One Triceratops skull has a brow horn that was bitten off and then healed, which means the Triceratops survived the attack. That is good evidence that some Triceratops stood their ground and lived.

Still, horns that were useful against a rival would also help against a predator. One use does not rule out the other. Rhinos today use their horns both ways, and a rhino's horn is made of keratin, the same material as your hair.

A frill that changed with age

Baby Triceratops had short horns that curved backwards and a small frill with a wavy edge. As they grew, the horns turned to point forwards and the frill became broader. Grown-ups looked quite different from youngsters, a bit like the way a stag grows antlers that a fawn does not have.

The frill itself was solid bone, which is unusual. Many of its relatives had frills with two large holes in them, covered only by skin. A frill full of holes would make a poor shield, so in those animals display seems the likelier job. The surface of the bone is covered in grooves where blood vessels ran, and some scientists once suggested the frill helped shed heat. Few think that was its main job now.

The way heads changed with age is one more reason scientists think the horns and frill were signals. An adult could tell at a glance who was young, who was grown and who was a rival. Like a peacock's tail, a showy head may have mattered as much as a sharp one.

Triceratops is now the state fossil of South Dakota and the state dinosaur of Wyoming. Not bad for an animal once mistaken for a cow's cousin.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Scientists decided Triceratops never existed and renamed it.

Truth: That story spread online in 2010 and it is wrong. Some scientists suggested that Torosaurus might be a grown-up Triceratops. If so, the older name, Triceratops, would be kept.
