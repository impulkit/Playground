What is the Hajj?

Each year, nearly two million people in plain white cloth walk the same steps on the same few days in Mecca.

The short answer

The Hajj is a pilgrimage, a religious journey, to the city of Mecca in Saudi Arabia. It is one of the five pillars of Islam, the basic duties of a Muslim. Every adult Muslim who is healthy enough and can afford it is expected to go at least once in their life.

The Hajj takes place over five or six days each year, in the Islamic month of Dhul Hijjah. Pilgrims follow a set of rituals in and around Mecca that remember the Prophet Ibrahim (Abraham), his wife Hajar and their son Ismail. In 2024 about 1.8 million people took part, from almost every country on Earth. Only Muslims are allowed to enter Mecca.

Surprising but true

The Hajj follows the Islamic lunar calendar, so it comes about 11 days earlier each year and slowly moves through every season, summer to winter and back, in about 33 years.

The Kaaba

At the heart of Mecca's Great Mosque stands the Kaaba, a cube-shaped stone building about 15 metres high, draped in black cloth embroidered with gold verses from the Quran. Muslims believe Ibrahim and Ismail built it as a house for the worship of one God.

Five times a day, Muslims all over the world turn towards the Kaaba to pray. In India, that means facing roughly west. In the USA it means facing north-east. On arriving, pilgrims walk around the Kaaba seven times, anticlockwise, in a ritual called tawaf. Seen from above, the crowd moves like a slow white whirlpool.

Following in footsteps

Next, pilgrims walk seven times between two small hills, Safa and Marwah, now inside the mosque. This remembers Hajar running between them searching for water for her baby, Ismail. According to tradition, water then sprang from the ground, and the Zamzam well still flows today. Pilgrims drink from it and often carry bottles home.

On the most important day, pilgrims gather on the plain of Arafat, praying and asking forgiveness from noon until sunset. They spend the night under the open sky at Muzdalifah, collecting small pebbles. Over the next days, at Mina, they throw the pebbles at three stone pillars, representing the rejection of evil.

Eid al-Adha

The Hajj ends with Eid al-Adha, the festival of sacrifice. It remembers the story of Ibrahim's readiness to sacrifice his son at God's command, and the ram sent in his place. Families who can afford it share meat from an animal with relatives, neighbours and people in need. Muslims around the world, including those who are not on Hajj, celebrate the same Eid, which Indians often call Bakrid.

Because the Islamic calendar follows the Moon, its year is about 354 days long. That is why Eid and the Hajj come about 11 days earlier each year in the usual calendar.

Pilgrims through history

People have travelled to Mecca for centuries, by camel caravan, ship and on foot. In 1325, a 21-year-old from Morocco named Ibn Battuta set off for the Hajj. He kept going for nearly 30 years and reached India, China and East Africa.

A year earlier, in 1324, Mansa Musa, the ruler of the Mali Empire in West Africa, crossed the Sahara on his way to Mecca with a vast caravan. Writers of the time said he handed out so much gold in Cairo that its value there dropped for years.

Today, most pilgrims fly. India sends one of the largest groups, more than a lakh of pilgrims in recent years. Saudi Arabia has built huge tented camps at Mina, cooling sprays and wide walkways, since moving so many people safely through summer heat that can pass 45°C is an enormous task.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Muslims worship the Kaaba.

Truth: Muslims worship God alone. The Kaaba is the direction Muslims face when they pray, wherever they are, and a focus that unites them. They do not pray to the building itself.
