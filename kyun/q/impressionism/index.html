What is Impressionism?

The name Impressionism began as an insult from a newspaper critic, and the painters decided to keep it.

The short answer

Impressionism is a style of painting that began in France in the 1860s and 1870s. Impressionist painters wanted to capture a moment: sunlight glittering on water, steam from a train, a breeze in a field of poppies. They painted quickly, often outdoors, with short, visible brushstrokes and bright, unmixed colours.

Up close, an Impressionist painting can look like a mess of dabs. Step back, and the dabs come together into shimmering light. At first, critics hated it. Today Impressionist paintings are among the most popular in museums from Paris to Chicago.

Surprising but true

Painting outdoors became much easier after an American painter invented the squeezable metal paint tube in 1841.

An insult that stuck

In the 1800s, French artists who wanted to succeed had to get into the official Salon, a giant yearly exhibition. Its judges liked smooth, polished paintings of history, myths and serious portraits. They kept rejecting a group of young painters whose work looked rough and unfinished.

So in 1874 the group organised their own show in Paris. One painting, by Claude Monet, showed a harbour at dawn, with an orange sun and a few quick strokes of blue. He called it Impression, Sunrise. A critic named Louis Leroy wrote a mocking review calling the artists "Impressionists", as if they could only manage rough first impressions. The painters liked the name and used it themselves.

Painting outdoors

Before this, most artists sketched outdoors but painted in their studios. The Impressionists took their easels outside, a practice called painting en plein air, French for "in the open air".

Two inventions helped. In 1841 an American painter, John Goffe Rand, invented the squeezable metal paint tube, so artists could carry ready-made colours anywhere. And new railway lines let painters from Paris reach the countryside and seaside in a few hours. Trains themselves became a favourite subject, puffing clouds of steam into the light.

How was photography invented?The oldest surviving photograph from a camera needed hours, perhaps days, of sunlight to appear. It shows a blurry view of rooftops from a window in France.

Catching light

The Impressionists noticed that shadows are not just grey or black. Shadows on snow look blue, and sunlight can turn a white wall pink or gold. They painted those colours boldly.

Instead of mixing every colour on a palette, they often placed small strokes of different colours side by side. Your eyes blend them from a distance, and the result often looks brighter than mixed paint. This is one reason Impressionist paintings seem to glow.

The painters

Claude Monet is the most famous Impressionist. In his garden at Giverny he dug a pond and spent his last decades painting water lilies, some on canvases several metres wide. Pierre-Auguste Renoir painted crowds dancing and chatting in dappled sunlight. Edgar Degas painted ballet dancers backstage and at practice.

Women were part of the group too. Berthe Morisot painted gardens and family life. Mary Cassatt, an American who settled in Paris, painted mothers and children and helped Impressionism become popular in the United States, where collectors bought many of the paintings.

A camera in the room

Photography had been announced to the world in 1839. Cameras could now record exactly how things looked, so painters began asking what painting could do that a camera could not. The Impressionists answered by painting light, movement and feeling.

Their paintings also borrowed from photographs: figures cut off at the edge of the picture, or scenes seen from unusual angles. Painters who came after, like Vincent van Gogh, built on these ideas and went further, and the road to modern art had begun.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Impressionist paintings look blurry because the painters were careless or had poor eyesight.

Truth: The loose brushstrokes were a deliberate choice. The painters worked fast to catch changing light, and they wanted the viewer's eye to blend the dabs of colour from a distance.

Try this

Pick something you can see from a window, like a tree or a building. Sketch it quickly with coloured pencils in the morning, at midday and in the evening, spending only five minutes each time. Lay the three drawings side by side and compare the colours of the light and shadows.
