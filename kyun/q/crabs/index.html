How do crabs grow?

To grow, a crab must climb out of its own skeleton, legs, eyes and all, and then hide for days while its new body hardens.

The short answer

A crab's skeleton is on the outside, a hard shell called an exoskeleton. It protects the crab well, but it can't stretch. So to grow, a crab has to moult.

First it builds a new, soft, wrinkled shell underneath the old one. Then the old shell cracks open along the back, and the crab slowly backs out of it. It swallows water to puff up its soft body, so the new shell will set larger than the last. For a few days, the crab is soft and helpless and stays hidden. As the new shell hardens, the crab grows into the extra space. Then the whole cycle starts again.

Surprising but true

When a crab moults, it sheds more than its outer shell. It also leaves behind the thin lining of its gills, the covering of its eyes and the hard teeth inside its stomach.

Climbing out of yourself

Moulting is risky and exhausting. Weeks before, the crab starts taking some calcium back out of its old shell to save it. A new soft skin forms beneath.

When it's time, the crab pumps itself up with water until the old shell splits at the back. It then pulls out every part of its body, including each leg, each claw and the stalks of its eyes. The thin lining of its gills and the hard grinding teeth inside its stomach come away too. The whole thing can take minutes or hours, and a crab that gets stuck may die.

Afterwards, many crabs eat their old shell to get back the minerals in it. Nothing useful goes to waste.

Soft and in danger

A freshly moulted crab is like a knight without armour. Fish, birds, octopuses and even other crabs will eat it. So it hides under rocks, in burrows or among mangrove roots until its new shell is hard, usually a few days for small crabs and longer for big ones.

People catch crabs at exactly this moment. In the USA, blue crabs from Chesapeake Bay are sold as "soft-shell crabs" and fried whole, shell and all.

Fast growth, slow growth

Crabs start life as tiny drifting larvae that look nothing like crabs. They float in the sea as part of the plankton and moult again and again, changing shape each time. Eventually they settle on the seabed as miniature crabs.

Young crabs may moult every few weeks. Older ones slow down, moulting once a year or less, and some stop entirely. A crab that loses a leg in a fight, or drops it on purpose to escape a predator, can regrow it, a little bigger with each moult.

Size varies enormously. The Japanese spider crab has legs that can span about 3.7 m, as wide as a small car is long. The coconut crab, which lives on islands in the Indian and Pacific oceans, including the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, can weigh about 4 kg and crack coconuts with its claws.

Crabs that borrow homes

Hermit crabs never grow a full hard shell. Their soft, curly back end needs protection, so they live in empty snail shells. When a hermit crab outgrows one, it looks for a bigger one. Sometimes several crabs line up from largest to smallest by a new shell, and each moves up one size in turn.

On beaches in Goa, Odisha and Chennai, pale ghost crabs dart sideways at high speed and vanish into holes. In mudflats and lagoons like Chilika, male fiddler crabs wave one giant claw to attract mates. And on Christmas Island, an Australian territory in the Indian Ocean, tens of millions of red crabs march from the forest to the sea each year to breed. Roads close and bridges are built just for them, one of the great animal migrations on land.

Myth or fact?

Myth: An empty crab shell on the beach means a crab has died.

Truth: Many empty shells are moults, the outgrown skins of living crabs. A moult looks complete, but it is light, often split along the back edge, and has nothing inside.

Try this

Make a paper "crab shell" by wrapping a small soft toy tightly in paper. Now try to let the toy get bigger without tearing the wrapping. You can't. Then cut a slit along the back and slide the toy out, which is exactly how a crab escapes its old shell.
