Why is Shakespeare still famous?

About half of Shakespeare's plays might have vanished forever if two actor friends hadn't collected them into one big book after he died.

The short answer

William Shakespeare was an English playwright and poet who lived from 1564 to 1616. He wrote about 37 plays, including Romeo and Juliet, Hamlet, Macbeth and A Midsummer Night's Dream, and 154 short poems called sonnets.

He is still famous for three main reasons. His stories are gripping, with murders, ghosts, twins, shipwrecks and young lovers. His characters feel like real people with mixed-up feelings. And his language was so memorable that many of his phrases are still in everyday English, used by people who have never read a word of him.

Surprising but true

In Shakespeare's theatre, all the women's parts, including Juliet and Lady Macbeth, were played by boys.

A glover's son from a market town

Shakespeare grew up in Stratford-upon-Avon, a market town in central England. His father made gloves. At 18 he married Anne Hathaway, and they had three children. By the early 1590s he was in London, working as an actor and writer.

He became a partner in an acting company and helped build a playhouse called the Globe in 1599. It was round, open to the sky, and held up to about 3,000 people. Poorer people stood in the yard in front of the stage for a penny. In 1613 a cannon fired during a play set the thatched roof alight, and the Globe burned down. A careful copy, Shakespeare's Globe, opened near the old site in 1997.

The book that saved his plays

Plays in Shakespeare's time were working scripts for actors, not books for readers. About half of his plays were never printed while he was alive.

In 1623, seven years after his death, two of his fellow actors, John Heminges and Henry Condell, gathered 36 plays into one large volume, now called the First Folio. Without it, plays such as Macbeth, Twelfth Night and The Tempest would probably have been lost. Around 235 copies are known to survive today, and they are among the most valuable books in the world. It shows how much the printing press mattered for keeping words alive.

Words we still use

Phrases like "wild-goose chase" and "break the ice" became popular through his plays, though some were already around before him. Many of his lines have become common sayings: "to be, or not to be" and "all the world's a stage".

He wrote in verse a lot of the time, using a rhythm called iambic pentameter, which goes da-DUM five times in a line. Try saying "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks" and you can hear the beat. Kings and lovers often speak in verse, while servants and clowns often speak in plain prose. That was one way Shakespeare showed who a character was.

Shakespeare in India

Shakespeare reached India with the British, and Indian theatre groups were performing him in English, Bengali, Marathi, Urdu and other languages by the 1800s. Over time he was adapted so fully that his stories now feel at home here.

The Hindi film Angoor (1982) is a comedy based on The Comedy of Errors, with two pairs of twins causing chaos. Director Vishal Bhardwaj set Macbeth among Mumbai gangsters in Maqbool (2003), Othello in rural Uttar Pradesh in Omkara (2006), and Hamlet in Kashmir in Haider (2014). In the United States, Romeo and Juliet became the musical West Side Story in 1957. Shakespeare's plots work because jealousy, ambition and first love have not changed since 1600, and Bollywood knows it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Shakespeare invented thousands of English words.

Truth: He is the first known writer to use many words, such as "bedroom" and "eyeball". But as researchers search more old books, earlier uses keep appearing. He coined some words, and popularised many more.

Try this

Pick a short Shakespeare insult or line, like "Thou art a boil" from King Lear, and say it three ways, angrily, sadly and as a joke. Notice how the same words can mean very different things depending on how an actor speaks them.
