What are the Great Lakes?

Five lakes on the US–Canada border hold about a fifth of all the fresh water on the Earth's surface.

The short answer

The Great Lakes are five huge freshwater lakes in North America: Superior, Michigan, Huron, Erie and Ontario. The border between the USA and Canada runs through four of them. Lake Michigan is the only one entirely inside the USA.

Together they hold about 21 percent of all the fresh water sitting on the surface of the planet. Water flows from one lake to the next, from Superior down to Ontario, then out along the St Lawrence River to the Atlantic Ocean. Standing on the shore of Lake Superior, you cannot see the other side. The waves, beaches and lighthouses make it feel like the sea, until you taste the water and find no salt.

Surprising but true

If the water in the Great Lakes were spread evenly over the 48 joined-up US states, it would cover them almost 3 metres deep.

Dug out by ice

During the last ice age, a sheet of ice more than a kilometre thick covered this part of North America. It flowed slowly south, scraping soft rock out of old river valleys and making them deeper and wider.

When the climate warmed, the ice melted back. Meltwater filled the scooped-out basins, and the lakes took roughly their modern shape between about 14,000 and 10,000 years ago. The land is still slowly rising in places, springing back now that the weight of the ice has gone.

Five lakes, five personalities

Lake Superior is the biggest by area of any freshwater lake, about 82,000 square kilometres, larger than the whole of Punjab. It is also the deepest and coldest of the five. Lake Erie is the shallowest, so it warms up fastest in summer and freezes over most easily in winter.

Between Erie and Ontario, the water drops over Niagara Falls. Ships can't go over a waterfall, so the Welland Canal next to it lifts and lowers them through a staircase of locks. Big cities line the shores: Chicago, Detroit, Cleveland, Milwaukee and Toronto. More than 30 million people in the two countries get their drinking water from the lakes, according to the US Environmental Protection Agency.

Storms and shipwrecks

The lakes can be wild. In autumn, cold winds from Canada whip up waves taller than a two-storey house. On 10 November 1975, the cargo ship Edmund Fitzgerald sank in a storm on Lake Superior. All 29 crew were lost, and a famous folk song by Gordon Lightfoot still tells the story.

Thousands of shipwrecks lie on the lake beds. In cold fresh water there are no shipworms and little rot, so some wooden ships from the 1800s still have their masts standing.

In winter, the lakes make weather of their own. Icy air picks up moisture as it crosses the warmer open water, then dumps it as heavy "lake-effect" snow on cities such as Buffalo.

Keeping the water clean

By the 1960s, factories and towns were pouring waste into the lakes. Lake Erie was so polluted that newspapers called it dead. In 1969 a river flowing into it, the Cuyahoga in Cleveland, caught fire because of the oil floating on it.

The fire shocked people. In 1972 the USA passed a major clean water law, and the USA and Canada signed an agreement to clean up the lakes together. The water became much cleaner, though new problems keep arriving. Zebra mussels, small striped shellfish from Europe, came in the ballast water of ships in the 1980s. These invasive species now carpet rocks and pipes across the lakes, filtering out the tiny food that native fish need.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Lake Michigan and Lake Huron are two separate lakes.

Truth: They are joined by the Straits of Mackinac and sit at the same water level. In terms of water, they behave as one huge lake with two names.
