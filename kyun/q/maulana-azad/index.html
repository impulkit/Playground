Who was Maulana Azad?

India celebrates National Education Day every 11 November, the birthday of Maulana Abul Kalam Azad, the country's first education minister.

The short answer

Maulana Abul Kalam Azad was a scholar, journalist and freedom fighter who became independent India's first education minister. He wrote powerful articles against British rule, led the Indian National Congress during some of its hardest years, and spent about ten years of his life in British prisons.

Azad believed passionately in Hindu-Muslim unity and fought hard against the Partition of India. After independence, he spent more than ten years shaping the country's schools, universities and arts. His birthday, 11 November, is celebrated as National Education Day. "Maulana" is a title of respect for a learned Muslim scholar.

Surprising but true

Azad became president of the Indian National Congress in 1923 at the age of 35, the youngest person ever to lead it.

A scholar from a young age

Azad was born in 1888 in Mecca, where his father, an Indian religious scholar, was living. The family moved to Calcutta, now Kolkata, when he was a small child. He didn't go to an ordinary school. His father and private teachers taught him Arabic, Persian, Urdu, philosophy and mathematics, and he taught himself English by reading.

He began writing and editing magazines while still a teenager. He chose the pen name "Azad", meaning free, which matched his independent mind.

A newspaper that worried the British

In 1912, aged 23, Azad launched Al-Hilal, an Urdu weekly. It had bold headlines and pictures, unusual for an Urdu paper then, and it urged Indian Muslims to join the freedom movement alongside Hindus. Its sales soared.

The British didn't like it. They seized its printing press in 1914. Azad started another paper, and in 1916 he was forced to leave Bengal and kept under watch in Ranchi until 1920. When he came out, he joined Gandhi's Non-Cooperation Movement. In 1923, at 35, he became the youngest president the Congress has ever had.

Prison letters

Azad led the Congress again from 1940 to 1946, an unusually long stretch, because war and prison stopped the party from holding new elections. When the Quit India movement began in August 1942, the British arrested the whole Congress leadership. Azad, Nehru, Sardar Patel and others were locked up in Ahmednagar Fort in Maharashtra for nearly three years.

There, Azad wrote letters to a friend that couldn't be sent. They later became a book, Ghubar-e-Khatir. While he was in prison, his wife Zulaikha fell ill and died in 1943, and he was not allowed to see her.

How did Urdu develop?Urdu was born in the markets and army camps of north India, where Persian, Arabic and Turkish words met the everyday speech of Delhi.

Against partition

As independence came closer, the Muslim League demanded a separate country for Muslims. Azad disagreed strongly. He believed Indian Muslims belonged to India just as much as anyone else, and that dividing the country would bring suffering to everyone.

Partition went ahead in 1947, with terrible violence. In October that year, Azad stood at the Jama Masjid in Delhi and urged frightened Muslims not to leave. India was their home, he said, and they should stay and help build it. Many remember that speech as one of the most moving of the time.

Building India's schools

As education minister from 1947 until his death in 1958, Azad had a huge task. Most Indians could not read. He pushed for free and compulsory schooling for children, and for adult education.

He also helped build institutions that still matter today. The first Indian Institute of Technology opened at Kharagpur in 1951. The University Grants Commission, which looks after universities, was set up under him. So were national academies for literature, music and dance, and the visual arts. Like modern school systems around the world, his plan joined science with the arts.

Remembered

Azad died in Delhi on 22 February 1958. He was buried near the Jama Masjid, where he had once spoken. He was awarded the Bharat Ratna, India's highest civilian honour, in 1992. He had reportedly refused it while alive, because he felt people on the selection committee should not receive it. National Education Day has been marked on his birthday since 2008.

Myth or fact?

Myth: All Muslim leaders supported the partition of India.

Truth: Many did not. Maulana Azad was one of the strongest opponents of partition. He argued for a united India where Hindus, Muslims and others would live together as equal citizens.
