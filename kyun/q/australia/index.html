Why does Australia have such unusual animals?

When British scientists first saw a platypus, some thought someone had stitched a duck's beak onto a furry animal as a joke.

The short answer

Australia has been an island for tens of millions of years. Long ago it was part of a giant southern landmass called Gondwana, joined to Antarctica, South America and India. Over time those lands drifted apart, and Australia finally broke away from Antarctica and floated north on its own.

Cut off from the rest of the world, its animals went their own way. Elsewhere, mammals whose babies grow inside the mother for a long time took over. In Australia, marsupials, whose tiny babies finish growing in a pouch, filled almost every job instead: grazing, climbing, burrowing and hunting.

Surprising but true

The platypus and the echidna are mammals that lay eggs, and both live only in Australia and New Guinea.

A continent on a raft

When India broke away from Gondwana and began its race north, as you can read in India's drift, Australia stayed attached to Antarctica for much longer. When it did split off, it carried its passengers with it like a raft.

At that time, marsupials and egg-laying mammals were already living there. Very few other mammals ever reached the island, except bats, which could fly, and rodents, which probably arrived on floating logs or across short stretches of sea much later. So kangaroos became Australia's grazers, playing the part that deer and antelope play in India or the United States.

Pouches, eggs and gum leaves

A newborn kangaroo, called a joey, is about the size of a jelly bean. It crawls through its mother's fur into her pouch and stays there for months. Koalas, wombats and Tasmanian devils are marsupials too.

Koalas eat almost nothing but eucalyptus (gum) leaves, which are tough and mildly poisonous to most animals. Their slow digestion and low-energy diet are why koalas sleep 18 hours a day or more. Then there are the monotremes, the platypus and the echidna, which lay eggs. The platypus also hunts with its bill, sensing tiny electrical signals from shrimps and worms in muddy water with its eyes and ears shut.

Deadly reputation, real facts

Australia is famous for venomous animals. The inland taipan has the most powerful venom of any land snake tested in the lab. There are box jellyfish in its northern seas and funnel-web spiders in Sydney's gardens.

The real risk is smaller than the reputation. Good antivenoms, quick hospitals and careful habits mean deaths are rare.

The first Australians

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples have lived in Australia for at least 50,000 years, one of the longest continuous cultures on Earth. To reach it, their ancestors had to cross open sea from Southeast Asia, among the earliest long sea journeys we know of.

They knew the land intimately: which plants could be eaten, where water hid in the desert and how to manage the bush with careful burning. Uluru, the giant red rock in the middle of the country, is a sacred site for its traditional owners, the Anangu. Since 2019, visitors have no longer been allowed to climb it.

Shared loves with India

Australia and India are on opposite sides of the Indian Ocean but share some big passions. Cricket is the most obvious. India and Australia play some of the fiercest Test series in the sport, and both countries have won the men's World Cup several times.

Many Indians now live, study and work in Australia, especially in Melbourne and Sydney. Hindi and Punjabi are among the fastest-growing languages spoken at home there. Off its north-east coast lies the Great Barrier Reef, the largest coral reef system on Earth, which stretches for about 2,300 km.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Sydney is the capital of Australia.

Truth: The capital is Canberra. It was built from scratch as a compromise in the early 1900s, because Sydney and Melbourne each wanted the honour and neither would let the other have it.
