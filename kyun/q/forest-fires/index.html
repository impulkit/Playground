Why do forest fires happen?

Some pine cones stay shut tight for years, glued closed with resin, and only open to drop their seeds after a fire has swept through.

The short answer

A forest fire needs three things: fuel, oxygen and heat. Forests are full of fuel, from dry leaves and grass on the ground to dead branches and resin-rich pines. Air supplies the oxygen. All that is needed is a spark.

Sometimes nature provides it, usually with lightning. Far more often, people do: an unattended campfire, burning crop waste, a cigarette, a spark from a machine or a fallen power line. Once a fire starts, hot, dry and windy weather decides how fast it spreads. Long droughts and heatwaves, which are becoming more common as the planet warms, make forests easier to burn.

Surprising but true

In the USA, about 85% of wildfires are started by people, through campfires, burning rubbish, sparks from machines, cigarettes and power lines.

How fires spread

Most forest fires begin on the ground, burning grass, needles and fallen leaves. These ground fires can move slowly and leave big trees scarred but alive. If flames climb up shrubs and low branches into the treetops, they become crown fires. Crown fires race through the canopy and can leap across roads and rivers.

Wind is a fire's best friend. It pushes flames forward, brings fresh oxygen and carries glowing embers, sometimes more than a kilometre ahead, starting new spot fires. Fires also run uphill faster, because the flames lean towards the slope and preheat the fuel above. A big fire can create its own wind and even its own thunderclouds.

India's fire season

In India, most forest fires happen between about February and June, before the monsoon, when leaves have dropped and the ground is dry. The Forest Survey of India tracks fires with satellites and sends alerts to forest officers.

The hills of Uttarakhand often see bad fire seasons. Chir pine forests there drop thick carpets of needles that burn easily. Many Indian forest fires are started on purpose to clear land, encourage fresh grass for cattle or collect forest products, and some of them get out of control. Central Indian states such as Odisha, Madhya Pradesh and Chhattisgarh also report many fires each year.

Wildfires in the American West

California, Oregon and other western US states have long, dry summers and forests full of fuel. Strong autumn winds can turn a small fire into a fast-moving disaster. More people now live at the edge of forests, so more homes are in the way. In 2018 the Camp Fire destroyed the town of Paradise, California, and killed 85 people, one of the deadliest US wildfires in a century.

For decades, the US Forest Service tried to stop every fire. Its mascot, Smokey Bear, first appeared in 1944 with the message that only you can prevent forest fires. Preventing careless fires was a good idea. But stopping natural fires too let dead wood build up.

Forests that need fire

Some ecosystems depend on burning. Lodgepole pines in Yellowstone National Park have cones sealed with resin that open in the heat of a fire, scattering seeds onto the freshly cleared ground. After huge fires burned about a third of Yellowstone in 1988, young pines sprang up in their millions.

Indigenous peoples in Australia, North America and parts of India have long set small, careful fires to clear undergrowth and keep larger fires away. Many forest managers now use similar planned burns, lit in cool, damp weather, to reduce fuel.

A warming world

Scientists find that hotter, drier conditions linked to climate change are making fire seasons longer in many places. Australia's 2019–20 bushfires burned more than 17 million hectares, an area about half the size of Rajasthan, and killed or displaced vast numbers of animals. Smoke from Canadian wildfires in 2023 turned the sky orange over New York City, more than 1,000 km away, and added to air pollution for millions of people.

Myth or fact?

Myth: All forest fires are disasters that should be put out immediately.

Truth: Many forests evolved with fire. Small, regular fires clear dead wood and help some seeds sprout. Putting out every fire for decades can let fuel pile up, making later fires bigger and harder to control.

Try this

Look at a tree stump or a wooden log cut across, in a park or a furniture shop, with an adult. Count the rings and look for dark scars or gaps. Scientists read fire scars like these in old trees to work out when forests burned hundreds of years ago.
