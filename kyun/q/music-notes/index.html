How is music written down?

The names "do, re, mi" come from the first syllables of a thousand-year-old hymn.

The short answer

Most music in the West is written on a staff: five horizontal lines. Each note is a small oval placed on a line or in a space. The higher it sits, the higher the note. The shape of the note shows how long it lasts, and extra signs show how loud, how fast and how smoothly to play.

Other traditions use other systems. In India, musicians write notes with the syllables Sa, Re, Ga, Ma, Pa, Dha and Ni, often in Devanagari or other scripts. But for centuries, much Indian music was not written at all. It passed from teacher to student by ear, one phrase at a time.

Surprising but true

The oldest song ever found written down was carved on clay tablets in Syria about 3,400 years ago.

Songs in clay

The oldest written melody yet found comes from the ancient city of Ugarit, in today's Syria. It was pressed into clay tablets about 3,400 years ago, using the wedge-shaped writing of Mesopotamia. Scholars still argue about exactly how it should sound.

The ancient Greeks wrote music with letters above the words. One short Greek song, carved on a tombstone about 2,000 years ago, survives complete with its notes. Its words tell the listener to enjoy life while it lasts.

A monk and his staff

In medieval Europe, monks sang long chants in church. At first, they wrote small marks above the words that only hinted at whether the tune went up or down. Singers had to learn each chant by heart, which took years.

Around 1000 CE, an Italian monk named Guido of Arezzo had a better idea. He drew horizontal lines and placed notes on and between them, so the exact pitch was clear. He also used a hymn whose lines began on rising notes, with the syllables ut, re, mi, fa, sol, la. Singers could learn new songs far faster. Later, "ut" became "do", and "ti" was added.

Printing music

Copying music by hand was slow and full of mistakes. In 1501, in Venice, a printer named Ottaviano Petrucci published a book of songs using movable type, about fifty years after the printing press appeared in Europe.

Printed music let composers share their work widely. A piece written in Vienna could be played in London a few months later. Sheet music became a big business, and by the 1800s, many middle-class homes in Europe and America had a piano and a stack of printed songs.

Notes on paper in India

Indian music has an ancient idea of notes. The Vedic chants of the Samaveda were sung with marked rises and falls in pitch. But classical music relied mostly on the bond between guru and student, rather than on written pages.

In the early 1900s, the musicologist Vishnu Narayan Bhatkhande travelled across India collecting songs and created a notation system for Hindustani music. Carnatic music also developed its own written forms. Still, as explained in Carnatic and Hindustani music, most musicians say paper is only a reminder. The real music lives in listening.

Reading the rhythm

On a staff, the note shapes tell you how long to hold each sound. A hollow oval lasts a long time. Fill it in and add a stem, and it lasts half as long, or less. Flags and beams make notes shorter still.

Numbers at the start, called a time signature, tell you how many beats are in each bar. A big orchestra relies on all of this, so that a hundred musicians reading separate pages still land on the same beat. You can explore beats more in rhythm.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Western staff notation can write down any music exactly.

Truth: It works well for many kinds of music, but it struggles with things like the slides and shakes between notes in Indian classical music, or the loose "swing" of jazz. Musicians learn those by listening.

Try this

Invent your own way to write a clapping rhythm. Use symbols, such as a dot for a short clap, a line for a long clap and a gap for a rest. Write a rhythm of eight claps, then hand it to a friend and see if they can clap it back exactly.
