Are sharks really dangerous?

In 2025, sharks killed 9 people in unprovoked attacks around the world. People killed somewhere in the region of 80 million sharks in a single year.

The short answer

Very rarely. There are more than 500 kinds of shark, and only a handful have ever been known to bite people without being provoked. In 2025, scientists recorded 65 such bites worldwide, and 9 of them were deadly. More people die each year from lightning or bee stings than from sharks.

Meanwhile, people kill an enormous number of sharks, roughly 80 million a year, for their fins, meat and oil, or by catching them by accident. For sharks, people are far more dangerous than the other way round.

Surprising but true

Sharks were swimming in the oceans before there were any trees on land.

Where the fear came from

Many people's fear of sharks grew after a 1975 film called Jaws, about a giant great white that attacks swimmers. It was a huge hit. The writer of the novel it was based on, Peter Benchley, later said he regretted how it made people see sharks. He spent much of his life afterwards working to protect them.

Our brains are good at remembering scary stories and bad at judging risk. One shark bite on the news feels more real than thousands of safe swims. That's part of why fear of sharks is so much bigger than the danger.

Who bites, and why

Most bites come from three species: the great white, the tiger shark and the bull shark. Bull sharks can live in rivers as well as the sea, and have been found far up the Mississippi and the Amazon.

Scientists think most bites are mistakes. A shark may take a test bite of a surfer paddling on a board, which from below looks a bit like a seal. Many bites happen in murky water near the shore, and the shark often lets go and swims off. Florida usually has the most bites of anywhere, simply because so many people surf and swim there.

Staying safe in the sea

Experts at the International Shark Attack File suggest a few simple habits. Swim close to the shore, near lifeguards, and with other people. Avoid the water at dawn and dusk, when many sharks feed, and stay out of murky water or places where people are fishing. Shiny jewellery can flash like fish scales, so leave it on the sand.

Shark bites are very rare on India's beaches. The bigger dangers there are strong currents and rough monsoon waves, which is why swimming is banned on many Indian beaches during the monsoon.

Super senses

Sharks have an excellent sense of smell. Some can notice blood in the water at about one part in a million, roughly a drop in a large tub of water. The old claim that they can smell a single drop from a kilometre away is an exaggeration.

They also have senses we lack. Tiny jelly-filled pores on their snouts detect the weak electric signals given off by living animals, even ones hiding in sand. A line of sensors down each side feels movement in the water. A hammerhead's wide head spreads these sensors out, like a metal detector sweeping the seabed.

Why sharks need us more than we fear them

Sharks are ancient. Their ancestors appeared more than 400 million years ago, before trees grew on land, and they have survived several mass extinctions. As hunters at the top of the food chain, they help keep reefs and seas in balance by eating sick and weak animals.

Today, many shark species are in trouble. They grow slowly and have few young, so heavy fishing hits them hard. India made the whale shark, the biggest fish in the sea at up to about 18 metres long, a protected animal in 2001. Fishers in Gujarat, who once caught them, now often cut their nets to free any that get tangled. Gujarat's government pays them for the damaged nets, and hundreds of whale sharks have been set free this way.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Sharks must keep swimming all the time or they will die.

Truth: Some sharks, such as great whites, do need to keep moving so water flows over their gills. But many others, like nurse sharks, can rest on the seabed and pump water over their gills while lying still.
