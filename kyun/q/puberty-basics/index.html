Why do bodies change as children grow up?

Somewhere between about 8 and 14, a tiny part of your brain sends a signal that slowly turns a child's body into an adult's.

The short answer

Puberty is the time when a child's body turns into an adult's. It usually starts between about 8 and 13 in girls and 9 and 14 in boys, and lasts a few years. Everyone's timing is different, and early or late are both usually normal.

It begins in the brain. A small part called the hypothalamus sends a signal to the pituitary gland just below it. The pituitary then releases hormones into the blood. These tell the ovaries in girls to make more oestrogen, and the testes in boys to make more testosterone. These hormones travel around the body and set off the changes of puberty, from a growth spurt to a deeper voice.

Surprising but true

Your brain keeps rebuilding itself all through your teens and isn't fully grown until your mid-twenties.

Changes everyone goes through

Some changes happen to almost everyone. You grow taller, often very fast for a year or two. Hair starts to grow under your arms and in other new places. The sweat glands in your armpits wake up, so sweat starts to smell, and washing every day becomes more important.

Your skin makes more oil, which can block tiny pores and cause spots. Feet often grow before the rest of the body catches up, so for a while shoes get too small very fast. Many people feel clumsy during a growth spurt, because their brain is still getting used to longer arms and legs.

Changes that differ

Girls' bodies usually start changing first. Breasts begin to develop, hips widen and, a year or two later, periods begin. A period is when a small amount of blood leaves the body through the vagina, about once a month. It's part of a monthly cycle that prepares the body to be able to have a baby one day. Periods are a normal, healthy part of growing up, even though in many families, in India and elsewhere, people have felt shy talking about them.

Boys usually start a little later. Their shoulders broaden, muscles grow, and hair appears on the face. The voice box grows bigger, and its vocal cords get longer and thicker. For a while, the voice may crack or jump between high and low before it settles deeper. This is sometimes called the voice breaking, though nothing is actually broken.

How do singers hit high notes?Your voice comes from two small folds of tissue in your throat, only one or two centimetres long, buzzing hundreds of times a second.

Earlier than it used to be

Puberty seems to start earlier now than it did long ago. In Europe in the 1800s, many girls had their first period at around 16 or 17. Today, in India, the USA and many other countries, the average is closer to 12 or 13. Doctors think better food and health play a big part, because the body needs enough energy stores before puberty can begin. Scientists are also studying whether other things, such as body weight and chemicals in the environment, play a part.

A brain under construction

Puberty isn't only about the body. The brain is changing too. Through the teenage years it trims away connections it doesn't use and strengthens the ones it does, like a gardener pruning a hedge. The front part of the brain, which helps with planning and self-control, is among the last to finish, in the mid-twenties.

Hormones and a busy, rebuilding brain can make feelings stronger and moods change faster. Teenagers also tend to feel sleepy later at night. All of this is normal. Bodies change at different speeds, and comparing yourself with friends can be confusing. Talking to a parent, a trusted adult or a doctor helps. Every adult you know has been through it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Spots and acne come from eating chocolate or having a dirty face.

Truth: Acne mainly comes from hormones that make the skin's oil glands more active during puberty. Washing gently helps, but acne isn't a sign of being dirty, and the evidence linking it to particular foods is weak.

Try this

Make a list of questions you have about growing up, even ones that feel awkward. Pick an adult you trust, such as a parent, an older cousin, a school nurse or a doctor, and choose one question to ask them. Most adults are glad to be asked, and they went through it too.
