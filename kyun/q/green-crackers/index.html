What makes a firecracker "green"?

A green cracker is not green in colour, and it is not free of pollution. So what makes it "green", and why did India's Supreme Court allow only these in Delhi for Diwali?

The short answer

A green cracker is a firecracker made with a cleaner recipe. It uses smaller shells, less of the harmful metal barium, fewer other toxic chemicals, and sometimes adds water-releasing ingredients that trap dust. The aim is to cut smoke and dust by at least 30%.

"Green" means "less harmful to the environment", not green in colour, and not pollution-free. Green crackers were developed by Indian scientists after the Supreme Court asked for safer options.

Surprising but true

The famous Supreme Court case that led to green crackers was filed in 2015 on behalf of three babies, all under two years old, whose parents said Delhi's smoky air was harming their lungs.

How it works

An ordinary cracker is a small packet of chemistry. It has three main parts:

  • 1. An oxidiser, such as potassium nitrate, which supplies oxygen.
  • 2. A fuel, such as charcoal, sulphur or aluminium powder, which burns fast.
  • 3. Colour makers, such as metal salts. Barium gives green, strontium red and copper blue, as the story of how fireworks get their colours explains.

When lit, the fuel burns in a flash, releasing heat, light, noise and gas. This combustion also throws out tiny particles of soot and metal, and gases like sulphur dioxide. This pollution is what turns festival skies hazy. The smallest particles, called PM2.5, are thinner than a hair and can go deep into the lungs.

Green crackers change the recipe:

  • - SWAS crackers release water vapour when they burst, which helps hold down dust.
  • - STAR crackers drop potassium nitrate and sulphur to cut harmful gases.
  • - SAFAL crackers use much less aluminium.

All of them avoid barium salts or use far less, and they are made smaller. Barium matters because its compounds are poisonous. Breathing in barium dust can irritate the lungs and harm the heart and muscles, and the dust settles on soil and water too. Removing it is one of the biggest changes in a green cracker. Think of switching from deep-frying to air-frying. You still cook, and there is still some smoke, but much less oil splashes everywhere.

Where it came from

Fireworks began in China more than a thousand years ago, after people discovered gunpowder. They spread across Asia and Europe, and became part of celebrations in India too.

India's cracker industry grew up around Sivakasi in Tamil Nadu from the 1920s, helped by its hot, dry weather, which is good for drying gunpowder. Today Sivakasi makes most of India's crackers, and lakhs of people work in the trade.

In 2015, a case called Arjun Gopal and others versus Union of India was filed in the Supreme Court on behalf of three babies in Delhi. Their families said cracker smoke made Delhi's winter air dangerous. In 2018, the court allowed only "green" and lower-pollution crackers in many places, and asked scientists to make them.

India's story

Government scientists at CSIR-NEERI, a lab in Nagpur, worked on new recipes. They launched green crackers in 2019 and gave licences to manufacturers who followed the formulas.

The court also banned joined crackers, the long strings that go off one after another for minutes, because they make a huge amount of smoke and noise in one go. Online sales of crackers were not allowed under the rules, so buyers had to go to licensed shops, where officials could check the boxes. Makers of approved crackers print the green cracker logo and their licence details on each box, and their names appear on NEERI's official list.

But Delhi's air remained a big problem every winter. Farm fires, traffic smoke, dust and cold, still air trap pollution over the city, and the high humidity of winter nights helps tiny particles hang in the air. In some years, Delhi banned all crackers. Critics said total bans just pushed people to buy illegal crackers, which are often more polluting.

On 2 November 2026, the Supreme Court allowed certified green crackers in Delhi-NCR on 7 and 8 November, but only for three hours a day, and banned them again from 9 November. After Diwali, an assessment by the Indian Institute of Tropical Meteorology found that crackers caused about 71% of the fine PM2.5 dust in Delhi's air in the early hours after Diwali night, showing how much difference the choice of crackers makes.

What does the AQI number mean?On a smoggy day, the news says the AQI is 400. That one number squeezes a whole cocktail of invisible pollution into something you can read at a glance.

The numbers that matter

  • - At least 30%: how much less dust green crackers are designed to release than ordinary crackers of the same type.
  • - 2.5 micrometres: the size of PM2.5 particles, about 30 times thinner than a human hair.
  • - 3 hours a day: the time allowed for bursting crackers in Delhi-NCR on 7 and 8 November 2026.
  • - About 71%: the share of Delhi's PM2.5 that came from crackers between 1 am and 9 am on 9 November 2026, the night after Diwali, according to the Indian Institute of Tropical Meteorology.

What people argue about

Many doctors and parents want strict limits, because smoke harms children, older people and anyone with asthma, and because Delhi's air is already among the worst in the world in winter. Pets and birds also suffer from the noise.

Others say crackers are part of the joy of Diwali and other festivals, and that lakhs of workers in Sivakasi depend on them. They argue that crackers burn for only a few days, while traffic and farm fires pollute for months. The Supreme Court has tried to balance both sides with green crackers, time slots and bans on the worst chemicals.

Myths and mix-ups

"Green crackers are pollution-free." They pollute less, not zero.

"A green-coloured flame means a green cracker." Ironically, the green colour in ordinary crackers often comes from barium, which green crackers avoid.

"One night of crackers cannot matter." On still winter nights, smoke can hang over a city for many hours, turning into thick smog.

What it means for you

If your family bursts crackers, choose approved green crackers from a licensed shop, use them only at allowed times, keep a bucket of water nearby, and stay away from hospitals and animals. Loud bangs can hurt ears too, so keep a safe distance and help little children cover theirs. Many families now choose diyas, lights and community celebrations instead.

Watching the AQI number before and after a festival is a simple way to see how our choices change the air we all breathe. Learning what makes air dirty helps you understand why courts step in.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Green crackers do not cause any pollution.

Truth: Green crackers are made to give off at least 30% less dust and fewer harmful gases than ordinary ones. They still release smoke and noise, which is why courts limit when and where they can be used.

Try this

Light a small incense stick and a candle next to each other in a still room, with an adult. Hold a white paper plate high above each for a few seconds. Which leaves more black soot? Smoke is made of tiny particles, and cleaner burning makes fewer of them.
