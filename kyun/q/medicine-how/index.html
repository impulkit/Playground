How do medicines know where to go in your body?

A headache pill doesn't travel to your head. It spreads through your whole body and only works where it finds the right target.

The short answer

Medicines don't actually know where to go. When you swallow a tablet, it dissolves, gets absorbed into your blood and is carried all around your body. It only has an effect in places where it meets the right target.

Most targets are special molecules on or inside cells, such as receptors or enzymes. A medicine's shape lets it fit onto its target like a key into a lock. It might block a lock to stop a signal, like a painkiller blocking a pain chemical, or turn a lock to start one. Because the same targets can exist in many parts of the body, medicines sometimes have side effects in places you didn't intend.

Surprising but true

People chewed willow bark to ease pain for thousands of years. Its active ingredient became the model for aspirin, first sold in 1899, and scientists only worked out how aspirin works in the 1970s.

The journey of a pill

A tablet first breaks apart in your stomach. Most medicines are absorbed through the walls of the small intestine into the blood. That blood flows first to the liver, which breaks down some of the medicine before it reaches the rest of the body. Doctors take this into account when they choose a dose.

From there, the heart pumps the medicine everywhere. Over hours, the liver and kidneys gradually remove it, which is why many medicines must be taken several times a day. Some medicines skip this route. Injections go straight into the blood or muscle, inhalers go into the lungs, and creams work on the skin.

From willow bark to aspirin

For thousands of years, people in many cultures chewed willow bark or drank tea made from it to ease pain and fever. In the 1800s, chemists found the active substance, salicin. In 1897, a chemist at the German company Bayer, Felix Hoffmann, made a gentler version, acetylsalicylic acid. Bayer sold it as aspirin from 1899.

For decades, nobody knew how it worked. In 1971, the British scientist John Vane showed that aspirin blocks an enzyme the body uses to make chemicals that cause pain, swelling and fever. He shared the 1982 Nobel Prize. Many medicines, from penicillin to some heart drugs, began with substances found in nature.

Testing what works

Doctors need proof that a medicine really works. In 1747, the Scottish naval doctor James Lind tested six treatments on 12 sailors with scurvy. Only the pair given oranges and lemons recovered quickly. His fair comparison is often called one of the first controlled trials.

Today, new medicines go through years of testing. They're tried in the lab, then in small groups of volunteers, then in larger trials where some patients get the real medicine and others get a dummy pill. Neither patients nor doctors know who got which, so their hopes can't affect the results. The placebo effect, where people feel better from a dummy pill, is surprisingly strong, which is exactly why these checks matter.

Making medicines for the world

Once a medicine's patent expires, other companies can make cheaper copies called generics. India is one of the world's biggest makers of generic medicines and vaccines. It supplies many countries in Africa, Asia and Latin America, and a large share of the generic tablets used in the USA. This has helped bring the price of many important medicines, including HIV treatments, down sharply.

Using medicines safely

Medicines are powerful, which is why they need care. Never take medicine on your own or share someone else's prescription. The 16th-century Swiss doctor Paracelsus is remembered for a famous idea: the dose makes the poison. Even water is harmful in huge amounts, and the right medicine at the wrong dose can be dangerous. Always let a trusted adult or doctor decide what you take and how much.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If a little medicine helps, taking more will help faster.

Truth: Every medicine has a safe dose. Taking more doesn't usually work faster and can be dangerous, even with common medicines like paracetamol. Only take medicine given by a parent, doctor or other trusted adult, exactly as directed.

Try this

Look at the box or leaflet of any medicine in your home with an adult (don't open or taste anything). Find the name of the active ingredient, the dose, the expiry date and the warnings. Notice that the brand name and the ingredient name are often different.
