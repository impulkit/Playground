Where can you see dinosaurs in museums?

The most famous T. rex in the world is named Sue, lives in Chicago, and was sold at auction for over 8 million US dollars.

The short answer

You can see dinosaurs in natural history museums across the world. Some of the most famous are in the United States, which has a huge number of dinosaur fossils. The American Museum of Natural History in New York, the Field Museum in Chicago and the Smithsonian's National Museum of Natural History in Washington, DC all have giant halls of skeletons.

India has dinosaur fossils too. In Kolkata, the Indian Statistical Institute displays a skeleton of Barapasaurus, a long-necked plant-eater found in Telangana. In Gujarat and Madhya Pradesh, fossil parks let you see dinosaur eggs and bones where they were found.

Surprising but true

Many dinosaur skeletons you see in museums are casts, exact copies made of lightweight materials. The real bones are often too heavy, too fragile or too valuable to hang up.

How museums build a dinosaur

Dinosaur fossils are rarely found as complete skeletons. Usually scientists find scattered bones, often broken, and spend years cleaning them out of rock. Then they work out how the bones fit, using other skeletons and living animals such as birds and crocodiles as guides.

Because real fossil bones are made of rock, they can be very heavy. So museums often show casts. A mould is taken from each real bone, then filled with lightweight resin or plaster. Missing bones are sculpted from similar animals. Steel frames, often hidden inside, hold the skeleton up.

The first dinosaur skeleton mounted for the public was Hadrosaurus, a duck-billed dinosaur shown at the Academy of Natural Sciences in Philadelphia in 1868. Crowds flocked to see it.

Why do we have museums?About 2,500 years ago, a princess in the city of Ur collected objects that were already ancient and labelled them. She may have made the first museum.

Famous dinosaurs in America

Sue, at Chicago's Field Museum, is one of the largest and most complete T. rex skeletons ever found. It was dug up in South Dakota in 1990 and named after the fossil hunter Sue Hendrickson. In 1997, the museum bought it at auction for over 8 million US dollars. Nobody knows if Sue was male or female.

The American Museum of Natural History displays a cast of Patagotitan, a giant plant-eater from Argentina. It is about 37 m long, so long that its head pokes out of the room it stands in. In Washington, DC, the Smithsonian reopened its dinosaur hall in 2019 with a real T. rex skeleton on loan from the US government, posed eating a Triceratops.

Dinosaurs in India

India's dinosaur story is less famous but rich. The skeleton of Barapasaurus at the Indian Statistical Institute in Kolkata was assembled from bones dug up from the late 1950s onwards in the Pranhita-Godavari valley, in what is now Telangana.

At Raiyoli near Balasinor in Gujarat, a fossil park protects the place where hundreds of dinosaur eggs and the bones of Rajasaurus, a meat-eater with a small horn, were found. In Dhar district in Madhya Pradesh, another fossil park shows nests of titanosaur eggs. Seeing fossils where they lay gives a sense of how long ago, and how near, those animals were.

Beyond the bones

Modern museums do more than display skeletons. Many have glass-walled labs where you can watch scientists clean fossils with tiny drills and brushes. Others use screens to show how dinosaurs moved and what colours they may have been. Some let visitors touch a real fossil, smoothed by thousands of hands, so you can feel bone that turned to stone millions of years ago.

In London, the Natural History Museum's famous Diplodocus cast, "Dippy", toured Britain after 2017, while a real blue whale skeleton took its place in the main hall. Whether in London, Chicago or Kolkata, a museum is the closest any of us will get to standing beside a living dinosaur.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The dinosaur skeletons in museums are always made of the real bones.

Truth: Many are casts, or a mix of real bones and casts to fill missing parts. Museums do this to protect the originals, to make skeletons light enough to mount, and so that several museums can display the same famous dinosaur.

Try this

Press a shell, a leaf or a small toy into a flat piece of modelling clay, then lift it out carefully. You have made a mould. Fill it with a little wet plaster of Paris, with a grown-up's help, and let it set. When you peel it out, you have a cast, the same way museums copy dinosaur bones.
