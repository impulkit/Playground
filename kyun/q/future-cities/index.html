What will cities look like in 2050?

In 2025 the UN counted 33 cities with more than 10 million people each, and parts of the biggest, Jakarta, are sinking into the ground.

The short answer

Most of the cities of 2050 already exist, so they will look partly like today's. But they will be bigger, hotter and, if planners succeed, greener. The UN's 2025 report found that about 45% of the world's 8.2 billion people live in cities, and that roughly two-thirds of population growth until 2050 will happen in them.

Many cities will add more metro lines, electric buses, trees and shade, and use computers to run traffic, water and power. Some may try out self-driving taxis, farms inside buildings and roads that soak up rain. But the hardest problems are older ones: heat, floods, water, housing and traffic. How well cities handle those will matter more than any gadget.

Surprising but true

In 2025 the UN named Jakarta the world's most populous city, with about 42 million people in its urban area, overtaking Tokyo.

Bigger than ever

In 2025, the UN counted 33 megacities, cities of more than 10 million people. Jakarta came top with about 42 million in its urban area, followed by Dhaka and Tokyo. Several Indian cities, including Delhi, are on the list. A megacity of that size has more people than many countries.

The fastest growth is expected in Asia and Africa. Places that are towns today may be big cities by 2050. That means millions of new homes, schools, pipes and bus routes, many of which haven't been built yet.

Hotter streets

Concrete and roads soak up heat by day and release it at night. This makes cities several degrees warmer than the countryside around them. Climate change adds even more heat, and heatwaves are expected to get worse.

Cities are trying fixes. In 2013, Ahmedabad created one of South Asia's first heat action plans, with early warnings and cooler public spaces. Cities from Ahmedabad to Los Angeles paint some roofs or roads white or light grey to reflect sunlight. Planting trees helps too. Shade from a single big tree can make the pavement below much cooler to stand on.

Cities and water

Many big cities sit on coasts or rivers. Rising seas and heavier rain make floods more common. Jakarta has a second problem: parts of it are sinking, because water is pumped out of the ground. Partly because of crowding and sinking, Indonesia has started building a new capital, Nusantara, on the island of Borneo.

China has a "sponge city" programme, started in 2015, that uses parks, ponds and special pavements to soak up rainwater instead of letting it flood the streets. Chennai and Bengaluru are restoring old lakes for the same reason.

Why are some cities sinking?Parts of Mexico City are sinking more than 2 cm a month, so fast that a satellite built by NASA and ISRO can watch it happen from space.

Smarter, maybe

In 2015, India launched its Smart Cities Mission for 100 cities, with control rooms watching traffic cameras, water supply and streetlights. Sensors can spot leaking pipes or full rubbish bins. Apps can tell you exactly when the next bus will come. Yet the hardest part of becoming a smart city is often plain good roads and drains.

Self-driving taxis already carry passengers in a few US cities, like San Francisco and Phoenix. Some people think they will be common by 2050. Others think buses and trains will matter far more, because they move many people in little space.

The 15-minute city

Some planners want cities where you can reach school, shops, a doctor and a park within 15 minutes on foot or by bicycle. Paris has made this a goal. It means mixing homes, offices and shops instead of keeping them far apart.

Tall towers made of strong engineered wood are going up in Norway and the USA, because wood stores carbon instead of releasing it. And the strangest part? Some of the ideas that feel futuristic are very old: the Indus cities had planned streets and covered drains about 4,500 years ago.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Future cities will be full of flying cars.

Truth: Flying taxis are being tested, but they are noisy, costly and carry few people. Most experts expect metros, buses, bicycles and walking to move far more city people in 2050, because they move crowds using little space and energy.
