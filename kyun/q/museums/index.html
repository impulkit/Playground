Why do we have museums?

About 2,500 years ago, a princess in the city of Ur collected objects that were already ancient and labelled them. She may have made the first museum.

The short answer

Museums exist to keep things that matter, so that people long after us can still see them. A museum collects objects, looks after them, studies them and puts them on show. A fossil, a sword, a sari, a painting or a spacecraft can each teach something that a book can only describe.

The first museums were private collections owned by kings, priests and rich families. Over the last 300 years, they slowly opened their doors to everyone. Today there are tens of thousands of museums around the world, from giant national museums to a single room in a village.

Museums also argue about hard questions, such as who really owns the treasures they hold.

Surprising but true

The Smithsonian, America's biggest museum group, was paid for by a British scientist who never visited the United States.

A princess and her labels

In about 530 BCE, in the ancient city of Ur in what is now Iraq, a princess named Ennigaldi-Nanna kept a collection of objects that were already centuries old. When the archaeologist Leonard Woolley dug there in the 1920s, he found the objects together, along with a clay label describing some of them in three languages. Some historians call it the world's first museum.

The word "museum" comes from Ancient Greek and means "place of the Muses", the goddesses of the arts and learning. In Alexandria, in Egypt, the Mouseion was a great centre for scholars, and it included the famous library that collected scrolls from all over the known world.

From private rooms to public halls

For centuries, collections stayed private. Wealthy Europeans in the 1500s and 1600s filled "cabinets of curiosities" with shells, stuffed animals, coins and carvings to impress their guests. In 1683, the Ashmolean Museum in Oxford opened as one of the first museums the public could visit.

The British Museum in London was founded in 1753, built around the collection of a doctor named Hans Sloane. It promised free entry to "all studious and curious persons". After the French Revolution, the royal palace of the Louvre in Paris became a public museum in 1793. Today it holds the Mona Lisa and welcomes millions of visitors a year.

India's museums

India's oldest museum is the Indian Museum in Kolkata, which grew out of the collection of the Asiatic Society in 1814. It holds fossils, an Egyptian mummy, coins, and carved railings from an ancient Buddhist stupa at Bharhut. Mumbai's Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj Vastu Sangrahalaya, once called the Prince of Wales Museum, opened in 1922. The National Museum in New Delhi was set up after independence, in 1949.

Many smaller museums tell more local stories. Ahmedabad has a museum of kites, Delhi has a National Rail Museum full of old steam engines, and Bhopal has a museum of tribal life built with the help of Adivasi artists.

A gift from a stranger

The British scientist James Smithson died in 1829. In his will, he left his fortune to the United States "to found at Washington, under the name of the Smithsonian Institution, an establishment for the increase and diffusion of knowledge". He had never set foot in America, and nobody knows exactly why he did it.

The Smithsonian was founded in 1846. It now runs more than twenty museums and a zoo, most of them free to visit. Its National Air and Space Museum displays the Wright brothers' 1903 flyer, the first powered aeroplane to carry a pilot.

Who owns the past?

Many great museums in Europe and North America hold objects that were taken from other countries during wars or colonial rule. Some, like the Benin Bronzes from what is now Nigeria, were seized by soldiers in 1897. Others were bought, given or dug up under rules that would not be allowed today.

Some people argue these objects should go home to the communities they came from. Others say big museums let millions of people see the art of many cultures in one place. In recent years, several museums and governments have returned objects. In 2022, Germany handed some of its Benin Bronzes back to Nigeria. The debate continues, one object at a time.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Everything a museum owns is on display.

Truth: Most big museums show only a small part of their collections, often less than a tenth. The rest is kept safely in storerooms, where researchers study it and curators rotate pieces onto display.

Try this

Make a shoebox museum. Choose five objects from home that tell a story, like an old coin, a seashell, a photo of a grandparent's village or a festival decoration. Write a small label for each saying what it is, where it came from, how old it is and why it matters. Then invite your family on a tour.
