Who was Brahmagupta?

In 628 CE, a 30-year-old astronomer in Rajasthan wrote down the first known rules for doing sums with zero and with numbers less than nothing.

The short answer

Brahmagupta was an Indian mathematician and astronomer who lived in the 600s CE. In 628 CE he wrote a huge book in Sanskrit verse called the Brahmasphutasiddhanta. In it, he treated zero as a number in its own right and gave rules for adding, subtracting and multiplying with it. He also gave rules for negative numbers, which he called debts.

He solved difficult equations, found a formula for the area of four-sided shapes drawn inside a circle and calculated the movements of planets. His work travelled to Baghdad, where it was translated into Arabic. Through that path, Indian numbers and ideas reached the wider world.

Surprising but true

Brahmagupta thought zero divided by zero equals zero. Today mathematicians say it has no answer, which shows how hard the idea was even for a genius.

A scholar in Rajasthan

Brahmagupta was born in 598 CE. He lived and worked in Bhillamala, now the town of Bhinmal in southern Rajasthan. It was then an important city and a centre of learning. Later writers called him the teacher from Bhillamala.

He tells us he wrote his main book when he was 30, in 628 CE. Its title means something like "the correctly established doctrine of Brahma". Most of its two dozen or so chapters are about astronomy (scholars count 24 or 25). Two are about mathematics. Like other Indian scholars of his time, he wrote in verse, which made his work easier to memorise and recite.

Rules for nothing

Before Brahmagupta, people used a dot or an empty space as a placeholder in numbers, to show that a place was empty. Brahmagupta went further and treated zero as a number you could calculate with. He wrote that a number plus zero stays the same, a number minus zero stays the same, and a number times zero is zero.

He also wrote rules for fortunes and debts. A debt minus zero is a debt. A fortune times a debt is a debt. A debt times a debt is a fortune. These are the rules for negative numbers you learn at school today.

He struggled with dividing by zero. He said zero divided by zero is zero, which we now know does not work, and was vague about other numbers divided by zero. Mathematicians took centuries to settle the question.

Equations and shapes

Brahmagupta was a skilled problem-solver. He gave a general method for solving quadratic equations, the kind with a squared unknown. He also tackled harder puzzles, such as finding whole numbers that fit equations like 92x² + 1 = y². He wrote that anyone who could solve it within a year was a real mathematician. European mathematicians worked on the same type of equation a thousand years later.

In geometry, he found a formula for the area of a cyclic quadrilateral, a four-sided shape whose corners all touch a circle. It is still called Brahmagupta's formula.

From Bhinmal to Baghdad

Brahmagupta did not always agree with earlier astronomers. He criticised parts of Aryabhata's work, including his idea that the Earth spins.

In 665 CE, at the age of 67, he wrote a second, more practical astronomy book called the Khandakhadyaka. Around 770 CE, scholars visiting the court of the Abbasid caliph al-Mansur in Baghdad brought Indian astronomical texts with them. Brahmagupta's work was translated into Arabic and studied there. Later Baghdad became famous for its House of Wisdom, one of the great libraries of history. Ideas moved along the same trade routes as silk, spices and horses. From the Arab world, Indian numerals and the rules for zero travelled on to Europe, and to every school notebook today.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Any number divided by zero is zero.

Truth: Dividing by zero has no answer. Brahmagupta himself said 0 ÷ 0 = 0, but later mathematicians showed this causes contradictions. Today we say division by zero is undefined, which is why calculators show an error.
