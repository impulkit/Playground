How did black pepper change the world?

Pepper from Kerala was once so valuable, people called it black gold.

The short answer

Spices like pepper, cardamom and cinnamon were among the most valuable goods in the ancient world. Black pepper grows on vines in the forests of the Western Ghats, in Kerala, and for centuries India was its main source.

The monsoon winds made long sea voyages possible, connecting India with Africa, Arabia, Rome and China. Traders who controlled the spice routes grew rich, and later, European countries sailed around the world trying to reach India's spices directly.

Ideas travelled with the spices: numbers, foods, stories, religions and inventions. The search for pepper helped shape empires, and even led Europeans to the Americas by accident.

Surprising but true

Roman gold coins have been found buried in South India, payment for pepper 2,000 years ago.

Riding the monsoon

More than 2,000 years ago, sailors learned to ride the monsoon winds across the Arabian Sea. From about June, the wind blows from the south-west, pushing ships towards India. From about November, it turns and blows from the north-east, carrying them home.

A Greek guidebook for traders, written in the first century CE and called the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, describes ports along India's coast and what each one sold. Pepper, pearls, ivory, cotton cloth and precious stones all went west.

Rome's gold

Roman traders paid with gold and silver coins, and thousands have been found in South India. The port of Muziris, on the Kerala coast, was one of the busiest. Archaeologists digging at Pattanam, near Kochi, have found Roman pottery and glass that may mark where it stood.

The Roman writer Pliny the Elder complained that India, China and Arabia were draining the empire of its money. Rome bought so much pepper that it built special warehouses for it.

What was the Silk Road?Silk from China reached Rome, and Buddhism reached China, along roads that almost nobody travelled from end to end.

Ideas on board

Ships and caravans carried more than goods. Indian numbers, including the idea of zero, travelled west through Arab traders and later reached Europe. Buddhism spread along land and sea routes to Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia and China. The game of chess, which probably began in India, travelled to Persia and on to Europe.

Overland, the Silk Road linked China, India and the Mediterranean, and many spices went that way too.

The race for the source

For centuries, Arab and Venetian merchants controlled the spice trade into Europe, and prices there were very high. European rulers wanted to cut them out.

In 1492, Christopher Columbus sailed west hoping to reach Asia, and landed in the Americas instead. In 1498, the Portuguese explorer Vasco da Gama sailed around Africa and landed near Calicut, in Kerala. Soon Portuguese, Dutch, English and French ships were fighting to control the trade.

From spices to empire

The English East India Company was founded in 1600 to trade in Asian spices. Over the next 250 years, it moved from trading to ruling, and took control of much of India.

The Dutch focused on the "Spice Islands" of Indonesia, where nutmeg and cloves grew. They used great violence to keep control, including against the people of the Banda Islands.

Spices that came to India

Trade worked both ways. Portuguese ships brought chillies, potatoes and tomatoes from the Americas to India. Within a few centuries, chilli was so common in Indian cooking that it is hard to picture a curry without it. Pepper, once worth its weight in silver, now sits on almost every dining table in the world.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In the Middle Ages, Europeans bought spices mainly to hide the taste of rotten meat.

Truth: Historians say this is unlikely. Spices cost far more than meat, so people rich enough to buy pepper could also afford fresh food. Spices were prized for flavour, medicine and showing off wealth.

Try this

Collect five spices from your kitchen, such as black pepper, cardamom, cinnamon, cloves and dried chilli. Smell each one, then find out where it first grew. Mark the places on a world map. You will find that chilli, now everywhere in Indian food, came from the Americas only about 500 years ago.
