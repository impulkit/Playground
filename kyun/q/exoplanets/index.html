Are there planets around other stars?

Thirty-five years ago, nobody had found a single planet around another star. Now we know of more than 6,000.

The short answer

Yes, lots of them. Planets that circle stars other than our Sun are called exoplanets. Astronomers have confirmed more than 6,000 so far, and thousands more are waiting to be checked. Studies suggest that most stars in our Milky Way galaxy have at least one planet, which means there are probably hundreds of billions of planets in our galaxy alone.

Some are giant balls of gas, some are rocky like Earth, and many are unlike anything in our solar system. Since the first discoveries in the 1990s, finding them has become one of the busiest parts of astronomy.

Surprising but true

Almost every picture you have seen of a planet around another star is an artist's painting. Nearly all exoplanets are found without ever being seen directly.

The first discoveries

For centuries people guessed that other stars had planets, but nobody could prove it. Stars are so far away, and so much brighter than any planet, that spotting one is like trying to see a firefly next to a searchlight from thousands of kilometres away.

The first confirmed exoplanets, found in 1992, were odd. They circle a pulsar, the spinning leftover core of a star that exploded. Then, in 1995, two Swiss astronomers, Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz, found a planet around an ordinary star like the Sun, called 51 Pegasi. The planet was half as heavy as Jupiter but circled its star in just four days, far closer than Mercury is to our Sun. They won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2019.

Wobbles and shadows

There are two main ways to find exoplanets without seeing them.

The first is the wobble method. As a planet orbits, its gravity tugs its star back and forth very slightly. That wobble shifts the colour of the starlight a tiny bit, which careful instruments can measure. This is how 51 Pegasi b was found.

The second is the transit method. If a planet passes in front of its star, as seen from Earth, it blocks a little of the starlight. Astronomers plot the star's brightness on a graph, and a regular small dip reveals the planet. The size of the dip shows how big the planet is, and the time between dips shows how long its year lasts.

NASA's Kepler space telescope used transits to find more than 2,600 planets between 2009 and 2018, by staring at about 150,000 stars at once. Its successor, TESS, is surveying nearly the whole sky.

Strange new worlds

Exoplanets turned out to be far more varied than expected. There are "hot Jupiters", giant gas planets roasting very close to their stars. There are "super-Earths", bigger than Earth but smaller than Neptune, a size our own solar system doesn't have even though such planets seem common elsewhere.

In 2017, astronomers announced seven Earth-sized planets around a small, cool star called TRAPPIST-1, about 40 light-years away. Several of them may be at the right distance for liquid water. The nearest known exoplanet, Proxima Centauri b, orbits the closest star to the Sun, about 4.2 light-years away.

Reading a planet's air

When a planet passes in front of its star, a little starlight shines through the planet's atmosphere on its way to us. Different gases soak up different colours of light, leaving tell-tale gaps. By studying that light, astronomers can work out what the air is made of.

The James Webb Space Telescope has already detected water vapour, carbon dioxide and other gases in the atmospheres of several distant planets. One day, a telescope like this might spot a mix of gases on a rocky planet that is hard to explain without life. Nobody has found that yet, but for the first time in history we have a way to look.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The colourful pictures of exoplanets in the news are photos taken by telescopes.

Truth: Nearly all of them are artists' illustrations, based on what scientists have measured. Only a few dozen exoplanets have ever been photographed directly, and they appear as small dots of light.

Try this

Shine a torch at a pale wall in a dark room. Ask a friend to pass a marble or a small ball slowly between the torch and the wall while you watch the bright spot. You should see a small shadow and a slight dimming. Now try a bigger ball. Bigger planets make bigger dips.
