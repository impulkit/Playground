What is the Maths Olympiad?

Once a year, about 600 teenagers from over 100 countries sit two exams of just three questions each. Some spend all four and a half hours on one problem.

The short answer

The International Mathematical Olympiad, or IMO, is the world's top maths competition for school students. Each year, every country can send a team of up to six students who are younger than 20 and not yet at university.

The contest takes two days. Each day students get three problems and four and a half hours to solve them. The questions need no advanced maths beyond school, but they are extremely tricky, and answers must be written as full proofs, step-by-step arguments that leave no gaps. Each problem is worth 7 points, so a perfect score is 42.

Surprising but true

Terence Tao won an International Mathematical Olympiad gold medal when he was just 13. He later won the Fields Medal, the highest honour in maths.

How it began

The first IMO was held in Romania in 1959, with seven countries from Eastern Europe. It grew slowly at first, then quickly. Today more than 100 countries take part, and a different country hosts it each year.

India first sent a team in 1989. Seven years later, in 1996, India hosted the IMO in Mumbai. The United States has taken part since 1974 and has topped the medal table several times.

The road to the team

In India, the olympiad programme is run by the Homi Bhabha Centre for Science Education in Mumbai, part of the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research. Students sit a qualifying exam, then a regional olympiad, then the Indian National Mathematical Olympiad. The best few dozen go to a training camp, and the final team of six is chosen there.

American students climb a similar ladder. It starts with the AMC contests, which tens of thousands take in schools, and passes through harder exams to the USA Mathematical Olympiad. The top students then join a summer training programme.

How old are the Olympic Games?They began in ancient Greece about 2,800 years ago, and came back in 1896.

What the problems are like

Olympiad problems are closer to logic puzzles than to homework. A problem might ask you to prove that some pattern always happens, or to find every whole number that fits a strange rule. Most fall into four areas: algebra, number theory (the study of whole numbers), geometry and combinatorics (clever counting).

A strong student might spend two hours staring at one problem before spotting the key idea. Then the solution can fit on half a page.

A taste of the very first contest

The first problem at the 1959 IMO asked students to prove that the fraction (21n + 4) ÷ (14n + 3) can never be simplified, whatever whole number n is. Trying examples shows it works for n = 1, 2 and 3, but examples are not a proof.

The trick is short. Three lots of the bottom number minus two lots of the top number always equals exactly 1. Any number that divided both top and bottom would also have to divide 1. So nothing bigger than 1 can, and the fraction never simplifies. That one neat idea is the whole solution, which is typical of olympiad maths.

Maths is not the only subject with olympiads. There are international contests in physics, chemistry, biology, astronomy and computer programming too, and India sends teams to all of them.

Medals, and what comes after

Medals go to no more than half of all contestants. They are split roughly in the ratio of one gold to two silvers to three bronzes. Students who don't win a medal but solve one problem perfectly get an honourable mention.

Many medallists become research mathematicians. Terence Tao, who grew up in Australia, won gold at 13. Maryam Mirzakhani of Iran won golds in 1994 and 1995 and later became the first woman to win the Fields Medal, in 2014. Her story is one of many in the history of women in maths. Plenty of other contestants go on to work in computing, science, engineering or medicine.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You have to be a born genius to do well at maths olympiads.

Truth: Most medallists spend years practising problems, often in school clubs or camps. Olympiad problems need clever ideas more than hard sums, and those ideas can be learned.

Try this

Try a classic olympiad-style puzzle. Can you cover an 8 × 8 chessboard with 2 × 1 dominoes if two opposite corner squares are cut off? Draw it or use a real board and paper strips. The answer comes from noticing what colours each domino always covers.
