What happens to things we recycle?

A drinks can you recycle today could be back on a shop shelf as a new can in about two months.

The short answer

Recycling turns old things into raw materials for new things. First, used items are collected, from bins, from homes, or from the street. Then they are sorted by material: paper, cardboard, glass, metals and different kinds of plastic. Sorting is the most important step, because mixed materials are hard to use.

Next, each material is cleaned and broken down. Paper is soaked into a mushy pulp. Glass is crushed into small pieces. Metals are shredded and melted. Plastic is chopped into flakes and melted into small pellets. Factories then use these materials to make new products, from fresh cardboard boxes to cans, bottles, park benches and even clothes.

Recycling saves raw materials and usually a lot of energy, and it keeps rubbish out of landfills and the sea.

Surprising but true

Making a can from recycled aluminium uses about 95% less energy than making one from fresh metal dug out of the ground.

Sorting machines and sharp eyes

In many American and European cities, mixed recycling goes to big sorting plants. Conveyor belts carry it past a series of machines. Spinning screens let small things fall through and carry flat paper away. Magnets lift out steel cans. A spinning magnetic field flings aluminium cans off the belt. Light sensors spot different plastics, and puffs of air blow each one into its own bin.

People still check the belts by hand, pulling out things that shouldn't be there, like plastic bags that tangle the machines. Dirty items cause trouble too. A jar still full of pickle or a greasy pizza box can spoil a whole bale of clean paper, which is why rinsing things before recycling them helps.

India's recycling army

In India, much recycling happens through people rather than machines. The kabadiwala visits homes to buy old newspapers, bottles and metal by weight. Lakhs of waste pickers collect and sort materials from streets and dumps, then sell them to dealers who pass them on to factories. Their work keeps huge amounts of material out of landfills.

Many cities, including Pune and Bengaluru, have helped waste pickers form groups, so they can work more safely and earn fairer pay for this hard and often overlooked job.

Not all materials are equal

Aluminium is the star of recycling. Remelting it uses about 95% less energy than making new aluminium from rock, and it can be recycled endlessly. Steel and glass can also be recycled over and over without wearing out.

Paper is different. Each time it is pulped, its fibres get shorter, so after about five to seven rounds they are too short to hold together. New fibres from wood must be mixed in. Very short fibres often end up in egg cartons or newsprint, which don't need to be strong.

Plastic is the hardest. There are many kinds, and they can't easily be mixed. Plastic usually gets weaker each time it is melted, so a bottle often comes back as something less demanding, such as carpet, fleece jackets or stuffing for pillows. Researchers estimated in 2017 that only about 9% of all plastic ever made had been recycled.

Reduce and reuse first

Recycling still uses energy, water and trucks. That's why many people say "reduce, reuse, recycle", in that order. The best rubbish is the rubbish you never make.

Carrying a cloth bag, refilling a steel water bottle and repairing broken things often do more good than recycling. A steel tiffin carried to school for years saves hundreds of plastic boxes. Recycling is the last step, for things that can't be avoided or reused. Many Indian homes already do this without calling it anything special: glass jars become pickle jars, old saris become quilts, and newspapers end up at the kabadiwala.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If something has the recycling triangle on it, it will be recycled.

Truth: The triangle with a number on plastics only tells you what kind of plastic it is. Many types, especially thin films and mixed materials, are rarely recycled because there is no one to buy them. Whether something is recycled depends on local collectors and factories.

Try this

For one week, sort your family's rubbish into piles. Use one for paper, one for plastic, one for metal and glass, one for food scraps, and one for everything else. Look for the small number inside a triangle on plastic items and make a list. At the end of the week, see which pile was biggest and talk about what could be reused or avoided.
