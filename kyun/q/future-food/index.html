What will we eat in the future?

In 2020, a restaurant in Singapore served chicken that had been grown in a steel tank, with no chicken killed to make it.

The short answer

In 30 years, your plate may look a lot like today's: rice, roti, dal, vegetables, maybe a burger. But some of that food may be made in new ways. Meat might be grown from cells in steel tanks. Lettuce might come from a warehouse with no windows. Protein might come from crickets, seaweed or microbes.

The reason is simple maths. The UN expects about 9.7 billion people on Earth by 2050, nearly 1.5 billion more than today. Farming already uses about half of the planet's habitable land. To feed everyone without cutting down more forests, food will need to be grown using less land, less water and less energy.

Surprising but true

A Finnish company makes a protein powder from bacteria fed on air, water and electricity, with no farm field involved.

Meat without the animal

In 2020, Singapore became the first country to let shops and restaurants sell meat grown from cells. A few cells were taken from a chicken and fed with nutrients inside a tank called a bioreactor, a bit like a giant pressure cooker. They multiplied into chicken meat. In 2023, the US allowed two companies to sell lab-grown chicken too.

It is still far more expensive than farm meat, and only small amounts have been sold. Some countries and US states have banned it. Whether it will ever be cheap enough for everyday meals is one of the big open questions.

Crunchy protein

Crickets and mealworms are packed with protein. They need far less feed and water than cattle, and they can be farmed in stacked boxes. In Thailand, fried insects are a street snack. In parts of Odisha, Chhattisgarh and Jharkhand, red ant chutney called chaprah or kai has been eaten for generations.

Many people in the US and India still find the idea strange. So some companies grind insects into flour and hide them in pasta, crisps and protein bars.

Farms that grow upwards

A vertical farm grows plants in tall racks indoors, under pink and blue LED lights. The roots sit in water mixed with nutrients, not in soil. A building the size of a school gym can grow as much lettuce as many acres of fields, using far less water.

The catch is electricity. Lights and air conditioning cost a lot, and several big vertical farming companies have shut down since 2022. For now, they work best for leafy greens and herbs that grow fast and spoil quickly.

The sea is another farm we have barely used. Seaweed grows fast without fresh water, fertiliser or land. Fishing families in Tamil Nadu already grow seaweed on bamboo rafts in the Gulf of Mannar. The tiny alga spirulina is sold as a green powder all over India.

Food from air

A Finnish company called Solar Foods grows bacteria that feed on hydrogen, carbon dioxide and a few minerals. The bacteria are dried into a yellow powder called Solein, which is mostly protein. It doesn't need sunlight, soil or rain. Space agencies have looked at the idea for long missions, because astronauts can't take a farm to Mars.

Other teams use fermentation, the same process that turns milk into curd, to make milk proteins and egg whites without cows or hens.

The old future

Some future food is very old. India asked the UN to make 2023 the International Year of Millets. Grains like ragi, jowar and bajra grow on dry land, need little water and survive heat better than rice or wheat. Your grandparents' grandparents ate them every day.

The cheapest way to feed more people may simply be wasting less. About a third of all food grown is lost or thrown away, according to the FAO, and a whole branch of future-food science is about stopping that.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Lab-grown meat is made from chemicals and isn't real meat.

Truth: Cultivated meat is grown from real animal cells, fed with nutrients in a tank. It is made of the same kind of muscle and fat cells as meat from an animal.

Try this

Grow a tray of microgreens on a windowsill. Soak a handful of mustard or methi seeds overnight, spread them on wet cotton wool or tissue in a shallow dish, and keep them damp. In about a week you will have a tiny indoor farm you can sprinkle on your food.
