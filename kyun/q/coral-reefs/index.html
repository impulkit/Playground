Is coral a plant, a rock or an animal?

It looks like a plant. It feels like a rock.

The short answer

Coral is an animal, though it does a good impression of the other two. A coral is a colony of thousands of tiny animals called polyps, most only a few millimetres across. Each polyp is a soft tube with a mouth at the top, ringed by stinging tentacles. Polyps are cousins of jellyfish and sea anemones.

Each polyp builds a hard cup of limestone underneath itself. When it dies, new polyps grow on top. Over thousands of years, those cups pile up into reefs.

Tiny algae live inside the polyps. They make food from sunlight by photosynthesis and share it with their host. They also give coral its colours.

Surprising but true

India's Lakshadweep islands are built on top of coral reefs.

Rainforests of the sea

Reefs cover less than 1% of the seabed, but about a quarter of all sea species depend on them at some point in their lives. Fish hide in the branches, octopuses lurk in the cracks, and turtles graze nearby.

Parrotfish scrape algae off coral with beak-like teeth, swallowing bits of limestone too. They grind it up and poop it out as fine white sand. Much of the soft sand on tropical beaches started out this way.

The biggest reef system of all, the Great Barrier Reef off Australia, stretches for about 2,300 km. That is roughly the straight-line distance from Delhi to Kanyakumari.

Reefs grow only in warm, clear, shallow water, where sunlight can reach the algae. In the USA, the biggest reefs lie off the Florida Keys and around Hawaii. India has four main reef areas: the Gulf of Kutch in Gujarat, the Gulf of Mannar between Tamil Nadu and Sri Lanka, Lakshadweep, and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands. Most reef-building corals grow slowly, often just a centimetre or so a year. Your fingernails grow faster.

Islands made by animals

In 1836, a young Charles Darwin sailed past coral islands in the Indian Ocean and wondered how they formed. He guessed that reefs grow around volcanic islands. As the volcano slowly sinks, the coral keeps building upward, until only a ring of reef is left. This ring is called an atoll.

More than a hundred years later, drills sank deep into an atoll in the Pacific and hit volcanic rock far below the coral, just as Darwin had predicted.

India's Lakshadweep islands are atolls like these. So the ground under the houses, coconut palms and beaches there was built by polyps.

When the sea gets too hot

Corals are fussy about temperature. If the water stays even a degree or two warmer than usual for weeks, the polyps push out their algae. Without them, the coral turns ghostly white. This is called bleaching.

A bleached coral is not dead yet. If the water cools soon, the algae can return. If the heat lasts, the coral starves. Long heatwaves in the sea have caused bleaching across the Great Barrier Reef, the Florida Keys and the Lakshadweep and Andaman reefs. In 2024, scientists announced that a worldwide bleaching event was under way, only the fourth ever recorded.

A night of snowstorms

On a few nights each year, many corals on the same reef release eggs and sperm at the same time, often a few days after a full moon. The water fills with drifting pink and white specks, like an upside-down snowstorm.

Divers come from around the world to see it. Scientists are now collecting those eggs, growing baby corals in tanks and planting them back on damaged reefs. Off India's coasts, from the Andaman Islands to the Gulf of Mannar, researchers are testing ways to help reefs recover.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Coral reefs are rocks that sea creatures simply live on.

Truth: A reef is built by living animals. Each coral polyp lays down a stony cup beneath itself, and millions of them over thousands of years pile up into a reef.

Try this

Hold up one hand with your fingers spread. Your fingers are a polyp's tentacles and your palm is its mouth. Now look closely at a seashell or a stick of chalk, with a magnifying glass if you have one. Both are made of calcium carbonate, the same stony stuff a polyp uses to build its cup.
