How was television invented?

One of television's inventors got his big idea at 14, while ploughing a potato field in Idaho in neat back-and-forth rows.

The short answer

Television works by breaking a moving scene into a grid of tiny spots and turning each spot's brightness into an electrical signal. The signal is sent, and the picture is rebuilt at the other end, many times a second. Inventors spent decades working out how to do that.

The first person to show recognisable moving faces on a screen was John Logie Baird, a Scottish inventor, in London in 1926. His system used a spinning disc full of holes. Around the same time, Philo Farnsworth in the United States built a television with no moving parts, using beams of electrons inside a glass tube. Electronic television, developed by Farnsworth, Vladimir Zworykin and others, was the kind that took over the world.

Surprising but true

The first television picture Philo Farnsworth sent with his electronic system, in 1927, was just a single straight line.

Baird's spinning disc

Baird worked with very little money. His early machines were patched together from things like a hatbox, a tea chest and bicycle lamps. The key part was a disc with a spiral of holes, based on an idea patented in Germany by Paul Nipkow in 1884. As the disc spun, each hole swept one line across the scene.

On 26 January 1926, Baird showed moving pictures to scientists from the Royal Institution, in a small attic room in Soho, London. The image was tiny, flickering and orange, made of only about 30 lines. But you could see a face, and see it move.

A farm boy's idea

Philo Farnsworth grew up on a farm in Idaho, in the United States. He later said that at 14, while ploughing a field in straight rows, he had a sudden thought. A picture could be scanned the same way, line by line, using a beam of electrons instead of a spinning disc. He sketched his idea for his science teacher.

On 7 September 1927, in a small laboratory in San Francisco, Farnsworth sent his first electronic image. It was a single straight line. Soon he could show moving images clearly. A long patent battle followed with the giant company RCA, where Vladimir Zworykin was building his own electronic camera. In 1939, RCA agreed to pay Farnsworth for using his patents.

Lines, dots and colour

Electronic television was much sharper than spinning discs. In 1936, the BBC began the world's first regular high-definition TV service from Alexandra Palace in London. In the United States, RCA showed television to crowds at the 1939 New York World's Fair.

Old television sets drew the picture with a beam of electrons that swept across the screen in lines, lighting up a glowing coating. Colour television arrived in the 1950s in the United States. Today's flat screens are completely different inside. They use millions of pixels, but they still rebuild the picture many times every second.

Television comes to India

India's first television broadcast came from Delhi on 15 September 1959. It was a small experiment, reaching a few hundred sets for an hour or so a few days a week. The service later became Doordarshan.

In 1975–76, India borrowed a NASA satellite for a project called SITE. It beamed educational programmes on farming, health and schooling to about 2,400 villages, where many people saw television for the first time. Colour came in 1982, in time for the Asian Games in Delhi. When the serial Ramayan aired on Sunday mornings in 1987, streets across India emptied as families gathered around their sets. That story is part of TV in India.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Television was invented by one person.

Truth: Many inventors in Britain, the United States, Germany, Russia and Japan worked on television at the same time. Mechanical systems came first, and fully electronic systems, which became the standard, were developed by several people.

Try this

Make a flip book. Take a small notebook and draw a simple stick figure on the last page. On the page before, draw it again, moving its arm slightly. Do this on 15 to 20 pages, then flip quickly with your thumb. Television, like a flip book, shows many still pictures one after another so fast that your brain sees movement.
