What happened to the dinosaurs?

66 million years ago, an ordinary day ended with a light in the sky.

The short answer

Scientists are confident a giant asteroid hit Earth 66 million years ago. About three out of every four kinds of living things went extinct.

The crash itself wasn't the biggest problem. What came after was: darkness and cold that broke the food chain.

Huge volcanoes in India were erupting around the same time. Scientists still argue about how much they added to the trouble.

Surprising but true

Not all dinosaurs died. Birds are dinosaurs, the ones that made it.

The day it happened

The asteroid struck the shallow sea near what is now the Yucatán coast of Mexico. It was about 10 km wide, roughly the height of Mount Everest above sea level, and travelling many times faster than a bullet. How big was it?

Rock was blasted into space and fell back as glowing beads. The heat from this falling debris may have started wildfires far from the crater. Huge waves swept the coasts of the Gulf of Mexico, and earthquakes shook the ground. Anything close by died within minutes. Rocks along the Brazos River in Texas, and in Haiti, still hold jumbled layers of sand, shells and glassy beads churned up by the waves and fallout. Today, space agencies track asteroids near Earth, partly so that this never happens to us.

The long dark

The real killer came next. Soot and dust spread around the world and dimmed the Sun for months, maybe years. Plants struggled, and the food chain fell apart from the bottom up.

In the oceans, tiny plankton that feed everything else crashed. Ammonites, which had lived for over 300 million years, died out. So did the great sea reptiles, the mosasaurs and plesiosaurs. On land, no animal much bigger than a medium-sized dog is thought to have survived.

How we know

In 1980, the father-and-son team Luis and Walter Alvarez studied a thin clay layer in rocks near Gubbio, in Italy. It was full of iridium, a metal rare on Earth's surface but common in asteroids. They proposed that a huge asteroid had hit the planet.

The same iridium layer was soon found all over the world. Around 1990, scientists showed that a buried crater about 180 km wide at Chicxulub, in Mexico, was exactly the right age. Oil company geologists had first spotted the buried ring years earlier, but it took clues in the clay to connect it to the dinosaurs.

Who made it

Small animals that could hide, eat seeds or scavenge did best: early mammals, crocodiles, turtles, frogs, and some birds. Animals that lived in fresh water often survived well, perhaps because rivers and lakes had food washing in from dead plants. Meet the survivors, and see how mammals took over.

Still being argued

At the same time, enormous volcanoes in India were pouring out lava. Some scientists think this weakened life first, by changing the climate before the asteroid struck. Others think the volcanoes made little difference and the asteroid did almost all the damage. The Deccan story is still being debated.

A world that started again

The gap left by the dinosaurs gave other animals a chance. Within a few million years, mammals had grown larger and spread into new ways of life. Without that asteroid, mammals might have stayed small, and humans might never have appeared. In the end, birds carried the dinosaur family into the present, which is why the dinosaurs never fully left.

Myth or fact?

Myth: All the dinosaurs died on the day the asteroid hit.

Truth: Animals near the crater died quickly, but most died in the months and years afterwards, as the cold and dark broke the food chain. Some dinosaurs, the ancestors of today's birds, survived.

Try this

Shine a torch onto a potted plant leaf, then hold a sheet of paper between them. Now try two sheets, then three. See how quickly the light fades. Dust and soot high in the sky after the asteroid did the same thing to sunlight for months.
