Can sport bring peace?

In 1971, a game of table tennis helped the USA and China start talking after more than 20 years of silence.

The short answer

Sport can't end a war by itself. But again and again, a match has given enemies a safe, friendly reason to meet when nothing else could.

The ancient Greeks had an Olympic truce, a promise that athletes and fans could travel to Olympia safely. In 1971, table tennis players helped the USA and China start talking. In 1995, a rugby final helped bring South Africans together after years of racial division.

Sport has also stirred up anger between rivals. So the honest answer is that it can help peace along, when people are ready to use it that way.

Surprising but true

At the 2018 Winter Olympics, North and South Korea marched into the stadium together and played as one women's ice hockey team.

The ping-pong surprise

In 1971, at the World Table Tennis Championships in Japan, an American player named Glenn Cowan climbed onto the Chinese team's bus by mistake. China's star, Zhuang Zedong, shook his hand and gave him a silk painting. Photographers caught the moment.

Days later, China invited the American team to visit. The two countries had not had friendly contact for more than 20 years, during the Cold War. In February 1972, President Richard Nixon flew to Beijing. Newspapers called it "ping-pong diplomacy".

A jersey that meant something

South Africa had a system called apartheid, which kept people apart by race. Rugby was loved mostly by white South Africans, and many Black South Africans saw the national team as a symbol of that system.

In 1995, a year after becoming president, Nelson Mandela walked onto the field at the Rugby World Cup final wearing the team's green jersey. The crowd, mostly white, chanted his name. South Africa won, and many people still call it a turning point.

Who was Nelson Mandela?He spent 27 years in prison for fighting unfair laws, walked out at 71, and four years later became his country's president.

A truce from ancient Greece

The ancient Olympic Games began at Olympia, in southern Greece, nearly 2,800 years ago. Greece was split into many city-states that often fought each other. So, before each Games, messengers travelled around announcing a truce, called ekecheiria.

The truce let athletes, trainers and fans travel to Olympia and home again without being attacked. Anyone who broke it could be fined or banned from the Games.

In 1993, the United Nations revived the idea. Before every Olympics, it now asks countries to stop fighting during the Games. Many wars have carried on anyway, but the request is still made, and some ceasefires have been tied to it.

Cricket across a border

India and Pakistan have played tense cricket since 1952. The games have also been a way for leaders to meet. In 1987, Pakistan's President Zia-ul-Haq came to watch a Test match in Jaipur. In 2011, the prime ministers of both countries sat together at the World Cup semi-final in Mohali.

In 2004, Indian fans crossed the border for a "friendship series" and found Pakistani families offering them meals. A cricket match lasts a day or five. The memories can last much longer.

Teams with no country

At the 2016 Olympics in Rio de Janeiro, ten athletes marched under the Olympic flag as the first Refugee Olympic Team. They had fled war and danger in places like South Sudan and Syria.

When sport goes wrong

Sport doesn't always calm things down. In 1980 and 1984, rival countries boycotted each other's Olympics. In 1969, fierce World Cup qualifying matches between El Salvador and Honduras came just before a short war between them, though the war had much deeper causes. A ball can bring people to the same field, but what they do there is up to them.

Myth or fact?

Myth: During the ancient Olympics, all wars in Greece stopped.

Truth: The truce protected athletes and fans travelling to and from Olympia, and banned fighting in the host region. Wars elsewhere in Greece often carried on. It was a safe passage, not a pause for the whole country.
