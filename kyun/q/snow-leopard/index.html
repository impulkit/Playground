How does the snow leopard survive the Himalayas?

It lives where the air is thin, the nights drop far below freezing, and dinner is a wild goat on a cliff.

The short answer

The snow leopard lives high in the Himalayas and the mountains of Central Asia, usually between about 3,000 and 5,000 m. Up there, winter nights can fall far below freezing, the air holds little oxygen, and the ground is rock, snow and steep cliffs.

Its whole body is built for this. It has thick, smoky-grey fur, wide paws that work like snowshoes, a big chest for breathing thin air and a tail almost as long as its body. It hunts wild sheep and goats on slopes so steep that a person would need ropes. Despite the name, its closest relative is the tiger.

It is so hard to spot that people in the mountains call it the "ghost of the mountains". India's first full survey, published in 2024, counted 718.

Surprising but true

Despite its name, the snow leopard's closest relative is the tiger, not the leopard.

A coat for the cold

A snow leopard's fur is long and dense, especially on its belly, which is often pressed against snow and rock. Its ears are small and rounded, so they lose less heat than big ears would. Its nose has a large space inside that warms icy air before it reaches the lungs.

The famous tail is thick and furry from end to end. When the snow leopard curls up to sleep, it wraps the tail around its face like a woolly scarf. On a narrow ledge, the tail helps it balance, the way a tightrope walker uses a pole.

Its grey coat with dark rosettes blends perfectly into rock and snow. This camouflage lets it creep close to its prey.

Hunting on the cliffs

Snow leopards mainly hunt bharal, also called blue sheep, and Himalayan ibex, a wild goat with long curved horns. Both are expert climbers. The snow leopard stalks them from above, then charges downhill, using its powerful back legs to leap across gaps between rocks.

It can bring down animals heavier than itself. After a big kill, it may stay near the carcass for several days, feeding slowly. In summer it also catches marmots, big ground squirrels that whistle a warning when they spot danger.

The ghost of the mountains

For a long time, almost nobody had seen a wild snow leopard. In 1970, the American biologist George Schaller took some of the first photographs of one in the wild, in the mountains of Pakistan.

A few years later, the writer Peter Matthiessen trekked across Nepal with Schaller, hoping to glimpse one. He never did. His book about the journey, The Snow Leopard, became famous anyway, because it was about the search as much as the animal.

Today, scientists use camera traps, small cameras that click when an animal walks past. Each snow leopard has its own pattern of spots, so the photos let researchers tell individuals apart and count them.

Snow leopards in India

Most of India's snow leopards live in Ladakh, with others in Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Sikkim, Arunachal Pradesh and Jammu and Kashmir. Hemis National Park in Ladakh is one of the best places in the world to see one, usually in winter, when they come down to lower valleys.

In Ladakhi villages, snow leopards sometimes kill sheep and goats in their pens, which can make herders angry. Conservation groups have helped families build pens with mesh roofs that snow leopards can't get into. Many villages also run homestays, where visitors pay to stay with local families while searching for the cats. That way, a living snow leopard brings money to the village.

A changing home

Snow leopards are listed as vulnerable. Scientists worry that warmer temperatures could push forests higher up the mountains and squeeze the open, rocky land they need. Roads and mines are reaching further into their range, which stretches across 12 countries.

They are the state animal of both Himachal Pradesh and Ladakh. The mountains they live in, the Himalayas, are still rising by a few millimetres a year, and the ghost is still out there, watching from the rocks.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The snow leopard is a kind of leopard that lives in the snow.

Truth: DNA studies show that the snow leopard's closest living relative is the tiger. It got its name because it is spotted like a leopard, long before scientists could compare their genes.

Try this

Fill a tray with flour or dry sand and smooth it flat. Press one fingertip into it, then press your flat hand down with the same force. The fingertip sinks deep, but the hand barely leaves a mark. A snow leopard's wide, furry paws spread its weight like your hand, so it doesn't sink into soft snow.
