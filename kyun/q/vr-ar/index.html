What are virtual reality and augmented reality?

In 1968 one of the first VR headsets was so heavy it had to hang from the ceiling. People nicknamed it the Sword of Damocles.

The short answer

Virtual reality, or VR, puts you inside a world made by a computer. You wear a headset that covers your eyes, and wherever you turn your head, the picture changes as if you were really there. The real room disappears.

Augmented reality, or AR, keeps the real world in view and adds computer images on top. A phone game that shows a cartoon creature sitting on your real table is AR. So is a filter that puts puppy ears on your head in a video. Augmented means added to.

Surprising but true

Pokémon Go, an augmented reality game, was downloaded more than 100 million times within about a month of its release in July 2016.

How VR fools your eyes

You have two eyes a few centimetres apart, so each sees the world from a slightly different angle. Your brain combines the two views to judge depth. 3D glasses at the cinema use this trick, and so does a VR headset.

Inside the headset are two small screens, or one split in half, with a lens in front of each eye. Each eye gets its own picture, drawn from a slightly different position. The lenses make the screens look large and far away.

Following your head

Sensors in the headset track how you move. Tiny devices called gyroscopes and accelerometers, like the ones that rotate your phone screen, feel every tilt and turn. Cameras on the headset watch the room to track where you step.

The computer must redraw the world very fast, often around 90 times a second. If the picture lags even slightly behind your head, the world seems to swim, and many people feel sick. That queasy feeling comes from a mismatch: your eyes see movement, but the balance sensors in your inner ear don't feel any.

How do 3D glasses work?A 3D film is really two films shown at once. The glasses make sure each of your eyes sees only one of them.

Old ideas, new screens

People have loved 3D pictures for a long time. In 1838 the British scientist Charles Wheatstone described the stereoscope, a viewer that showed each eye a slightly different drawing. In 1939 the View-Master toy put 3D photos on cardboard reels, and children have collected them ever since.

In 1968 the American computer scientist Ivan Sutherland built a headset that drew simple wire-frame shapes over the real room. It was so heavy it hung from a mechanical arm attached to the ceiling. Cheap, light headsets only arrived in the 2010s, when phone screens and sensors became small and powerful.

AR in your pocket

Most people first meet AR on a phone. The camera shows the room, and software spots flat surfaces like floors and tables, then places 3D objects on them so they stay put as you walk around. In July 2016 Pokémon Go sent millions of people outdoors to catch creatures that appeared on their screens in parks and streets.

Shops use AR to let you see how a sofa would look in your living room. Some cars show directions on the windscreen. Museums use it to bring old ruins back to life on a screen.

Uses and limits

Surgeons practise operations in VR before touching a patient. Pilots have trained in simulators for decades, and VR makes that cheaper. Students can "visit" the inside of a cell or the surface of Mars.

Headsets can still be heavy, hot and tiring after a while. Many makers set a minimum age of 10 to 13 for their headsets and suggest regular breaks, because young eyes and brains are still developing. It's a good idea to check the age guidance with a parent before trying one.

Myth or fact?

Myth: VR and AR are just new kinds of video games.

Truth: Games are a big part of it, but doctors use VR to practise surgery, pilots and astronauts train in it, and engineers use AR to see how machines fit together before building them.

Try this

Hold one finger up at arm's length and look at it, closing one eye, then the other. Your finger seems to jump sideways. Each eye sees a slightly different view, and your brain combines them into depth. A VR headset uses exactly this trick, showing each eye its own picture.
