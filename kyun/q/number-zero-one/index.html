Why are zero and one special?

Every photo, song and video game on your phone is stored using only two numbers, 0 and 1.

The short answer

Zero and one behave in ways no other numbers do. Add 0 to any number, and the number stays the same. Multiply any number by 1, and it stays the same too. Multiply anything by 0, and you get 0. Mathematicians call 0 and 1 "identities", because they leave other numbers unchanged.

They are special for practical reasons as well. Zero is what lets us write big numbers with only ten digits, by holding empty places. And 0 and 1 together are enough to store anything a computer knows, from a selfie to a whole film.

Surprising but true

For a long time, many mathematicians counted 1 as a prime number. It was taken off the list mostly to keep other rules of maths simple.

The number that holds a place

Many ancient peoples counted without zero. Roman numerals have no symbol for it. The Babylonians left a gap or used a mark to show an empty place, but didn't treat it as a number.

Indian mathematicians went further. In 628 CE, Brahmagupta wrote rules for zero as a number in its own right: a number minus itself is zero, and zero times anything is zero. At the Chaturbhuj temple in Gwalior, an inscription from 876 CE writes the number 270 with a small circle for zero, one of the oldest such zeros still on a wall. The Bakhshali manuscript, now in Oxford, uses a dot for zero, and some of its pages may be even older. These Indian numerals travelled through the Arab world to Europe.

The trouble with zero

Zero breaks one rule you learned early: you can always divide. What is 6 ÷ 0? If it had an answer, say x, then x × 0 would equal 6. But anything times 0 is 0. So there is no answer, and mathematicians say division by zero is "undefined". Calculators show an error.

People sometimes ask whether zero is even or odd. It's even, because it can be split into two equal groups of nothing, and it sits between the odd numbers −1 and 1.

Zero causes trouble outside maths too. Calendars jump from 1 BCE straight to 1 CE, with no year zero, because the system was set up in Europe before zero was in use there. In cricket, a batter out for 0 is said to be out for a "duck", because the 0 on the scoreboard looks like a duck's egg.

Is one a prime?

A prime number is a whole number with exactly two factors: 1 and itself. So 2, 3, 5 and 7 are prime. The number 1 has only one factor, itself, so it isn't prime.

This wasn't always agreed. For centuries many mathematicians did list 1 as a prime. Eventually most dropped it because of a beautiful rule: every whole number above 1 can be built by multiplying primes in exactly one way. 12 is 2 × 2 × 3, and nothing else. If 1 were prime, you could add as many 1s as you liked, and "exactly one way" would stop being true.

How does a computer think?A computer only understands two things, on and off. Yet with billions of tiny switches, it can play chess, show films and land rockets.

Two numbers that run the world

Computers are built from billions of tiny switches that can be either off or on. Off is written 0 and on is written 1. A system that uses only these two digits is called binary.

In binary, each place is worth double the one to its right: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and so on. So 1011 means 8 + 0 + 2 + 1, which is 11. String eight of these digits together and you can store any number from 0 to 255. Colours on a screen, letters in a message and notes in a song are all turned into long strings of 0s and 1s.

The German thinker Gottfried Leibniz wrote about binary in 1703. Centuries earlier, the Indian scholar Pingala had used patterns of short and long syllables in poetry that work in a similar way. Neither of them could have guessed that their two-symbol counting would one day carry video calls across the planet.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Zero means nothing, so it doesn't really matter.

Truth: Zero holds places in numbers. Without it, 5, 50 and 500 would look the same. It also has its own rules. Adding it changes nothing, multiplying by it gives zero, and dividing by it has no answer.

Try this

Count to 31 on one hand in binary. Your thumb is worth 1, your first finger 2, your middle finger 4, your ring finger 8 and your little finger 16. A finger that is down means 0 and up means 1. Show 5 with your thumb and middle finger, then try 10. With both hands you can count to 1,023.
