How do the Dutch live below sea level?

Planes at Amsterdam's main airport land on the bottom of an old lake, about 3 m below the level of the sea next door.

The short answer

About a quarter of the Netherlands lies below sea level, and much more would flood if rivers burst their banks. The Dutch live there safely because, for about a thousand years, they have built walls of earth called dikes to hold back the sea and rivers. Then they pump the water out from behind them.

Land won back from water like this is called a polder. Pumps run day and night to keep polders dry. On top of that, giant storm barriers can shut the sea out during storms. It is a constant job, and the Dutch have whole local governments devoted to it.

Surprising but true

The famous story of the Dutch boy who saved his town by plugging a leaking dike with his finger isn't an old Dutch legend. It first appeared in French and English magazines around 1850, and an American novel made it famous in 1865.

Building land from water

The western Netherlands began as marshes, peat bogs and river mouths. Around a thousand years ago, farmers started digging ditches to drain the bogs. But drained peat shrinks and sinks. The more they drained, the lower the land became, and the more they needed dikes.

So the Dutch got very good at moving water. Today the lowest point, near Rotterdam, is about 6.7 m below sea level. Standing there, the sea would be higher than a two-storey house.

Windmills that were pumps

Many of the famous Dutch windmills were not for grinding grain. They were pumps. Their sails turned paddle wheels or big screws that lifted water out of ditches into higher canals. Several windmills in a row could raise water step by step.

At Kinderdijk, near Rotterdam, 19 windmills built mostly around 1740 still stand in neat lines. They are a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Today electric pumping stations do the hard work, but the old mills still spin. Their great-grandchildren are the wind turbines now spinning off the Dutch coast.

A lake becomes an airport

In the 1800s, a big lake called the Haarlemmermeer kept growing in storms and eating up land near Amsterdam. Between 1849 and 1852, steam engines pumped it dry.

On that old lake bed now sits Schiphol, Amsterdam's main airport. Its runways lie about 3 m below sea level. In fact, "Schiphol" is often said to mean "ship hole", because ships were wrecked in storms on the old lake.

The night the sea came in

On the night of 31 January 1953, a huge storm pushed the North Sea against the southwest of the country. Dikes broke in many places. About 1,800 people in the Netherlands died, and tens of thousands of animals drowned.

The country vowed it would never happen again. It built the Delta Works, a chain of dams, sluices and storm barriers that took decades to finish. One of them, the Maeslantkering near Rotterdam, has two floating arms, each about 210 m long, about as long as two football fields. In a big storm they swing shut across the river to block the sea.

A whole province from the sea

In 1932, the Dutch finished the Afsluitdijk, a sea dam about 32 km long. It turned a stormy saltwater bay into a calm freshwater lake. Then they built polders inside it. One of them became the province of Flevoland, where more than 400,000 people now live on land that was seabed a hundred years ago.

India's below-sea-level farms

India has its own polder country. In Kerala, much of the Kuttanad region lies between about 1 and 3 m below sea level. Farmers there have grown rice behind mud walls for centuries, pumping water out of their fields. The United Nations food agency has recognised it as a globally important farming system.

Both places now face the same worry. Warmer oceans and melting ice are slowly raising the seas, as you can see in sea level rise. The Dutch are now giving some land back to rivers, so floodwater has room to spread safely.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The story of the little Dutch boy with his finger in the dike is an old Dutch legend.

Truth: It first appeared in a French children's story in 1848 and then in English magazines. It became world famous through Hans Brinker, or the Silver Skates, a novel by the American writer Mary Mapes Dodge published in 1865. The Dutch didn't know the tale until tourists began asking about it. A statue of the boy was later put up to please visitors.

Try this

Build a mini polder in a baking tray. Make a wall of modelling clay across the middle and pour water into one side. Put a little soil on the dry side. Then use a spoon or a straw to move water that leaks through back over the wall, as Dutch pumps do every day.
