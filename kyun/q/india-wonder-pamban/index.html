What is special about the Pamban Bridge?

In the middle of the sea near Rameswaram, a 72-metre piece of railway bridge rises straight up into the air like a lift, so ships can sail underneath.

The short answer

The Pamban Bridge carries trains across the sea from mainland Tamil Nadu to Rameswaram, a holy island town. It is special twice over. The old bridge, opened in 1914, was India's first sea bridge, and it worked for more than a hundred years in salty, stormy water. The new bridge, opened beside it on 6 April 2025, is India's first vertical-lift sea bridge.

In the middle of the new bridge, a 72-metre section of track can rise straight up between two towers, like a giant lift. That lets ships and fishing boats pass through the channel underneath. When the ships have gone, the span comes down and the trains roll across again.

Surprising but true

The old Pamban Bridge, opened in 1914, had a middle section that workers lifted by hand with levers, taking about 45 minutes to open each half.

A railway into the sea

Rameswaram sits on Pamban Island, in the narrow sea between India and Sri Lanka. It is one of Hinduism's most important pilgrimage places, with the Ramanathaswamy Temple at its heart. Before 1914, pilgrims had to cross by boat.

The British built the railway bridge from 1911 to 1914. It was about 2.07 km long and stood on more than 140 piers in the sea. For decades it also linked to a ferry from Dhanushkodi, at the tip of the island, to Sri Lanka. Until a road bridge opened in 1988, the train was the only way to reach the island over land.

Jaws that opened

The old bridge had a clever middle. Two steel leaves, each weighing about 415 tonnes, tilted up to open a gap for ships. It was called a Scherzer rolling lift. Workers opened it by hand, with levers, and each leaf took about 45 minutes.

The sea was hard on the bridge. Salt spray causes rust, and the Pamban area is one of the most corrosive places for steel in India. Engineers painted, patched and replaced parts for a century. In December 2022, the old lifting span was found too weak, and trains stopped for good.

The night of the cyclone

In December 1964, a powerful cyclone hit the coast. Huge waves wrecked much of the bridge and destroyed the town of Dhanushkodi. A passenger train on the Dhanushkodi line was swept away, and well over 100 people on board died, though counts vary.

Engineers expected repairs to take months. A young railway engineer, E. Sreedharan, led the team that got trains back on the bridge in just 46 days. He later became famous for building the Konkan Railway and the Delhi Metro.

The new bridge

The new bridge runs right beside the old one. It is about 2.07 km long, with 99 short spans and one long lifting span. The piers go deep into the seabed, and the steel is protected with special coatings meant to resist salt for decades.

The deck sits about 3 metres higher than the old one. When the lift span is fully raised, ships up to about 22 metres tall can pass, roughly the height of a seven-storey building. The bridge was built by Rail Vikas Nigam Limited, a government railway company, at a cost of about ₹500 crore. It was designed for electric trains and for two tracks in future.

Between two bridges

For now, the old bridge still stands next to the new one, rusting quietly, its jaws frozen in place. It may be kept as a heritage monument. On a calm day, you can see both from the road bridge: a century of engineering side by side in turquoise water. India's bridges across the sea have grown longer since, from the Bandra-Worli Sea Link in Mumbai to the 21.8 km Atal Setu, but Pamban was the first to try.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The new Pamban Bridge replaced the road to Rameswaram.

Truth: The Pamban rail bridge is for trains only. A separate road bridge, opened in 1988, runs beside it and carries cars and buses to the island.

Try this

Make a model of a vertical-lift bridge. Stand two tall cereal boxes a short distance apart as towers. Lay a ruler between them as the track, tie a string to each end of the ruler, loop the strings over the tops of the boxes and pull gently to lift it. Float a paper boat underneath to test the gap.
