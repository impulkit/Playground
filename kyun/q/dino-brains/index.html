How smart were dinosaurs?

Stegosaurus weighed as much as an elephant and had a brain often compared to a walnut. Some of its relatives were much brainier.

The short answer

Most dinosaurs had small brains for their size, and probably were not very clever by human standards. But "dinosaur" covers thousands of kinds of animal over more than 160 million years. Big plant-eaters like Stegosaurus had tiny brains. Some small meat-eaters, like Troodon, had brains as big for their bodies as some living birds.

And some dinosaurs are clever by any standard. Crows and parrots are dinosaurs, and they can use tools, remember faces and solve puzzles.

Surprising but true

Crows and parrots, which are living dinosaurs, can solve puzzles that challenge young children.

Reading an empty skull

Brains are soft and rot quickly, so they almost never fossilise. Instead, scientists study the hollow inside the skull. In many dinosaurs the brain did not fill the space completely, so this gives an upper limit on brain size.

In the past, scientists poured liquid rubber into a skull to make a cast. Today they use CT scanners, a kind of X-ray machine, to map the space without harming the fossil. The scan shows which parts were large. Big smelling areas, called olfactory bulbs, mean a keen nose. Big areas at the back can mean sharp eyesight or good balance.

The walnut problem

In the late 1800s, the fossil hunter Othniel Marsh studied a Stegosaurus skull and found a brain space famously compared to a walnut, in an animal of about 5 tonnes. He also noticed a much bigger space in its hip bones. Newspapers loved the idea of a dinosaur with a second brain in its bottom.

There was no second brain. Birds have a similar space in their hips, filled with nerves and a jelly-like tissue that stores energy. Stegosaurus probably had the same. A small brain was enough for a slow plant-eater that mostly had to find leaves and stay out of trouble.

Clever hunters

Troodon, a slim hunter from North America, had large eyes and one of the biggest brains for its body size of any non-bird dinosaur. In 1982, a Canadian scientist called Dale Russell playfully asked what Troodon might have become if it had kept evolving. He made a model of a big-headed, upright "dinosauroid". It was a thought experiment, not a real animal.

Scans show that brains grew larger, for their bodies, in the feathered dinosaurs closest to birds. Some had brains as big as early birds like Archaeopteryx, with large areas for sight and balance. So the "bird brain" was partly built before birds took to the air.

T. rex had a big brain for a reptile, and its smelling areas were very large. It probably had a strong sense of smell, good for finding prey or bodies from far away.

The T. rex argument

In 2023, a scientist estimated the number of nerve cells in T. rex's brain. She suggested it had about as many as a baboon, and might have been as smart as a monkey. Many other experts disagreed. In 2024, a larger team argued that T. rex's brain did not fill its skull, and that its nerve cell count was probably closer to a crocodile's.

That argument shows how hard the question is. Even in living animals, brain size is a rough guide. A crow's brain is tiny, yet its nerve cells are packed tightly, much more than in a mammal brain of the same size. Your own brain is clever because of how its parts connect, as well as how big it is. For dinosaurs we have the box, but not what was inside it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Stegosaurus had a second brain in its hips to control its back legs and tail.

Truth: This idea came from a large space in its hip bones, found in the 1800s. Today scientists think the space held nerves and a store of energy-rich tissue, as in birds. Stegosaurus had only one brain.
