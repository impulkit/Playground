How does electricity get to your home?

The power in your fan may have been made a few thousandths of a second ago, in a power station hundreds of kilometres away.

The short answer

Electricity is made in a power station, usually by spinning a generator. Its voltage is raised very high so it can travel a long way without losing much energy. Tall steel towers carry it across the country. Near towns, substations lower the voltage step by step. A transformer near your street lowers it one last time, and a cable brings it through your meter and into your switchboard.

All of this happens almost instantly. When you switch on a light, a power station somewhere is already turning a little harder to supply it. Control rooms in each region watch the demand every minute. On a hot evening, when millions of air conditioners and fans come on together, they ask more power stations to join in.

Surprising but true

Electricity can't easily be stored on the grid, so power stations across India must make almost exactly as much as everyone is using, every second of the day.

Making the power

Most power stations spin a turbine connected to a generator. The turbine might be pushed by steam from burning coal, by falling water at a dam, or by wind. Solar panels are different: they turn sunlight into electricity directly, with nothing spinning.

In India, roughly 70% of electricity still comes from coal (2024). Solar and wind are growing fast. By 2025, about half of India's power-plant capacity was from sources that don't burn fossil fuels, such as solar, wind, hydro and nuclear. In the USA, natural gas is the biggest source.

Up to 765,000 volts

Sending power far away has a problem. Wires resist the flow of electricity and warm up, so some energy is lost as heat. The fix is to raise the voltage. At high voltage, the same amount of power moves with a smaller current, and much less is wasted. Think of water in a pipe: pushing a thin, fast jet at high pressure can deliver as much as a wide, lazy flow, but with far less rubbing against the pipe.

So right outside a power station, giant transformers boost the voltage. India's biggest AC lines run at 765,000 volts, and a few special direct-current links go even higher. They hang from towers taller than a ten-storey building, high enough that no one can come near. In 2013, India linked all its regional grids into one, so power made in Odisha can light a home in Gujarat.

What was the war of the currents?In the 1880s, two kinds of electricity fought to light America's cities. The loser's champion was Thomas Edison, the most famous inventor alive.

Stepping it down

As the power gets close to where people live, substations lower the voltage, first to tens of thousands of volts, then to about 11,000. These are the lines you see on concrete or wooden poles along roads.

The last step is the transformer, a grey metal box on a pole or on the ground near your street. It lowers the voltage to 230 volts for Indian homes. In the USA, homes get 120 volts, which is why some foreign gadgets need an adapter. The current also flips direction back and forth: 50 times a second in India, 60 in America.

Inside your walls

The cable from the street goes first to your meter, which counts how many units you use. A unit is one kilowatt-hour: enough to run a ceiling fan for about 12 hours. Then the cable reaches a board of fuses or circuit breakers. Each one guards a different circuit, such as the kitchen or the bedrooms, and cuts the power if something goes wrong.

From there, copper wires hidden in the walls run to every socket and switch. Grown-ups should handle anything inside them. A switch simply opens or closes a gap in the wire. When you press it, the circuit is complete, and the whole chain, all the way back to that distant turbine, is connected to your lamp.

An early start in Karnataka

Public power supply was still new then. In New York, Thomas Edison's Pearl Street Station had started selling electricity in 1882. In 1902, a hydroelectric station at Shivanasamudra Falls on the Kaveri began sending power about 150 km to the Kolar Gold Fields. It was one of the first big hydro plants in Asia. In 1905, the same plant helped Bengaluru light its streets with electric lamps, years before many other cities in India.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Electricity is stored in the wires, like water in a pipe, until you switch something on.

Truth: The grid stores very little. Power stations make electricity at almost the same moment it is used. Grid controllers watch demand all day and tell stations to make more or less.
