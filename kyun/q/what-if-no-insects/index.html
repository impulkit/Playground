What would happen if all insects disappeared?

If every insect vanished tomorrow, you'd still have rice and wheat, but within a few years you might struggle to find a mango or a bar of chocolate.

The short answer

If all insects disappeared, the world wouldn't end at once, but it would slowly unravel. Many flowering plants would stop making fruit and seeds, because insects carry their pollen. Birds, frogs, lizards, bats and fish that eat insects would go hungry. Dead plants, animals and dung would pile up much more slowly, because insects help break them down.

People wouldn't starve straight away. Our biggest crops, rice, wheat and maize, are pollinated by the wind. But many fruits, vegetables, nuts, coffee and chocolate would become rare or vanish. Scientists can't say exactly how fast it would happen, but nearly all agree it would be a disaster for life on land.

Surprising but true

Australia once imported dung beetles from overseas because its own beetles couldn't cope with cow dung, and the pats were lying around for months.

The great pollen crash

Insects have been carrying pollen for more than 100 million years. The UN's food agency estimates that about three out of four kinds of leading food crops do better with animal pollinators, mostly insects.

Some depend on them almost completely. Mango flowers are visited by flies, bees and wasps. Cacao trees, which give us chocolate, are pollinated by tiny midges. Cotton, mustard and many pulses grow better with insect visits too. Wild flowers would suffer even more, and with them the animals that eat their seeds and fruits.

Empty plates for wildlife

Insects are food for an enormous number of animals. Most songbirds feed insects to their chicks, even species that eat seeds as adults. Frogs, geckos, many bats, spiders and freshwater fish live largely on insects or their young.

Take insects away, and these animals would decline quickly. Then the animals that eat them, like snakes, owls and foxes, would follow. This kind of knock-on effect is called a trophic cascade, a chain of losses running up a food web.

Who cleans up?

Insects are some of nature's best recyclers. Termites break down dead wood. Beetle and fly larvae eat dead animals. Dung beetles bury droppings, returning nutrients to the soil.

Australia learned this the hard way. Its native dung beetles evolved to handle the dry pellets of kangaroos, not the large wet pats of cattle brought by European settlers. Cow pats lay on the fields for months, smothering grass and breeding flies. From the 1960s, scientists brought in dung beetles from Africa and Europe to clear them up.

Things we'd lose at home

Insects give people more than pollination. Silk comes from the cocoons of silkworm moths, and India is the world's second-biggest silk producer. Honey comes from bees. Lac, a sticky resin made by tiny lac insects on trees in Jharkhand and nearby states, is used for bangles, wood polish and the shiny coating on some sweets and fruit.

In many countries, insects are food. More than 2 billion people eat them as part of a normal diet, from fried grasshoppers in Mexico to red ant chutney in Odisha and Chhattisgarh. Some fish farms and poultry farms now feed their animals insect meal too.

Even medicine would notice. Some doctors still use clean, specially raised maggots to tidy up wounds that won't heal, because the maggots eat only dead tissue.

This isn't only a thought experiment

Insects aren't about to vanish, but many are in trouble. A 2017 study in Germany found that the weight of flying insects caught in nature reserves had fallen by more than 75 percent over 27 years. Habitat loss, pesticides and climate change are all suspects, though the causes differ from place to place.

There would be a few winners, perhaps wind-pollinated grasses. And there's one insect plenty of people think they could happily live without. Whether the world would really be better off is the puzzle at the heart of a world without mosquitoes.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Bees are the only insects that pollinate our food.

Truth: Bees do a huge share, but flies, beetles, moths, butterflies, wasps and even tiny midges pollinate many crops. Cacao, for example, depends on midges no bigger than a pinhead.

Try this

Spend ten minutes watching one flowering plant in a garden, park or balcony pot. Count every insect visitor and draw what each looks like. Then try the same plant at a different time of day. You'll probably spot visitors you'd never noticed before.
