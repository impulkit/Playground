What is the Hornbill Festival?

Every December, Nagaland's tribes gather on a hillside near Kohima for ten days of dances, drums, wrestling and a contest to eat one of the hottest chillies on Earth.

The short answer

The Hornbill Festival is a ten-day celebration held every year from 1 to 10 December in Nagaland, in northeast India. It takes place at the Kisama Heritage Village, about 12 km from the state capital, Kohima, and brings together the many Naga tribes to share their dances, songs, food, crafts and games.

The Nagaland government started it in 2000 to celebrate Naga culture, encourage tribes to meet each other and attract tourists. It has grown into one of India's most famous festivals. In 2025, the 26th edition drew visitors and performers from across India and several other countries.

Surprising but true

The festival is named after a bird. The great hornbill appears in many Naga folk songs and stories, and its feathers have long been used in traditional headgear.

Named after a bird

The festival takes its name from the great hornbill, a huge black-and-white bird with a casque, a hollow helmet-like bump, on top of its bill. Hornbills appear in many Naga folk songs and stories, and the bird is linked with courage and good fortune.

Traditionally, hornbill tail feathers decorated headdresses worn by warriors and dancers. Today, many groups use replica feathers instead, because the birds are protected. The great hornbill is the state bird of Kerala and Arunachal Pradesh, not Nagaland, but Nagaland made it the face of its biggest festival.

A village of villages

Kisama Heritage Village is like a living museum. Each major Naga tribe, including the Angami, Ao, Konyak, Lotha, Sumi and others, has built its own morung there. A morung is a traditional house where young people once lived together and learned their community's history, songs and skills.

Walking through Kisama, you can see how the tribes differ. Each has its own style of house, carving, weaving and dress. Shawls show patterns that tell which tribe a person belongs to and, in some groups, what honours their family has earned.

Dances, drums and wrestling

The days are packed. Groups in full traditional dress perform dances, many with spears, shields and long rows of dancers singing together. Huge log drums, carved from single tree trunks, boom across the hills.

There are sports too: Naga wrestling, archery and traditional games. Craft markets sell bamboo baskets, beadwork and handwoven shawls. Visitors can try traditional games, watch contests in old skills such as making fire without matches, and taste food cooked in bamboo tubes. At night, the Hornbill International Rock Contest turns Kohima into a music festival, because Nagaland has a strong love of rock music and many young bands.

Chillies and food

Naga food is famous for smoked pork, bamboo shoots, fermented soybeans and fiery chillies. One festival event is a chilli-eating contest featuring the Naga king chilli, also called bhut jolokia or ghost pepper.

In 2007, Guinness World Records named the bhut jolokia the world's hottest chilli. It has since been overtaken by peppers bred in the USA, such as the Carolina Reaper and Pepper X. Contestants still need plenty of courage and plenty of rice. Food stalls offer dishes from every tribe, and the festival is a feast of northeast Indian food.

Why it matters

Nagaland has 16 or more officially recognised tribes and dozens of languages and dialects. In the past, many villages were isolated by steep hills and forests. The festival gives tribes a shared stage, while keeping their differences. Many Nagas speak Nagamese, a mixed language, or English to understand each other across tribes.

It also brings money into a region far from big cities. Hotels, homestays and taxi drivers depend on the festival crowds. At the same time, Naga elders and young people use it to pass on songs and skills that might otherwise fade, part of the wider story of India's tribal communities.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Hornbill Festival is an ancient tribal festival that has been held for centuries.

Truth: It was started by the Nagaland government in 2000. It brings together the separate traditional festivals of many tribes, which are much older, into one shared celebration.
