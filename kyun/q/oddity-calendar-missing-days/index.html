Why did Britain skip 11 days in 1752?

In 1752, people across Britain and its colonies went to bed on Wednesday 2 September and woke up on Thursday 14 September.

The short answer

Britain skipped 11 days in 1752 because its calendar had slowly drifted away from the seasons. For centuries it had used the Julian calendar, which counted a year as exactly 365¼ days. The real year is about 11 minutes shorter.

Eleven minutes doesn't sound much, but it adds up to about one whole day every 128 years. By 1752 the calendar was 11 days out of step with the Sun. So Parliament ordered a fix. In Britain and its colonies, including those in America and parts of India, Wednesday 2 September 1752 was followed by Thursday 14 September.

Surprising but true

The UK tax year still starts on 6 April because of the 1752 calendar change, after officials shifted the old date forward to avoid losing tax.

A calendar from Julius Caesar

The Julian calendar was set up by the Roman leader Julius Caesar in 45 BCE. It had 365 days, with an extra day every fourth year. That made the average year 365.25 days long.

Earth actually takes about 365.2422 days to go round the Sun and bring the seasons back. The Julian year was a little too long, so dates crept later and later compared with the seasons. The spring equinox, when day and night are about equal, was slowly sliding earlier in the calendar. That mattered to churches, because the date of Easter depends on it.

The Pope's fix

In 1582 Pope Gregory XIII introduced a new calendar, now called the Gregorian calendar. To catch up, 10 days were dropped: in Italy, Spain and other Catholic countries, Thursday 4 October 1582 was followed by Friday 15 October.

He also changed the rule for leap years. Years divisible by 100 would not be leap years unless they were also divisible by 400. So 1900 was not a leap year, but 2000 was. This keeps the calendar accurate to about one day in more than 3,000 years.

Britain holds out

Britain, a Protestant country, was in no hurry to follow the Pope. For 170 years, crossing from France to England meant travelling back in time by about ten days. By 1752, after an extra Julian leap day in 1700, the gap had grown to 11 days.

Parliament finally passed the Calendar Act. As well as skipping 11 days, it moved New Year's Day. Until then, the year in England officially began on 25 March. From then on, it began on 1 January.

Birthdays, rents and taxes

The change caused small puzzles. George Washington was born on 11 February 1731 by the old calendar. Under the new one, his birthday became 22 February 1732, the date Americans use today. The year changed too, because his birth had fallen before 25 March.

Officials collecting rent and taxes didn't want to lose 11 days of money. So the tax year, which had begun on 25 March, was pushed forward to 5 April. Later, another small adjustment moved it to 6 April. In the UK, the tax year still begins on that date.

The rest of the world

Other countries switched at different times. Russia kept the old calendar until 1918. That is why its October Revolution of 1917 is remembered in November by today's calendar. Greece changed in 1923.

Even now, the Julian calendar survives in some churches. Orthodox Christians in Russia and Serbia celebrate Christmas on 7 January, which is 25 December by the old calendar.

India's many calendars

India has used many calendars for thousands of years, based on the Moon, the Sun or both. Festivals like Diwali and Eid move around the Gregorian calendar because they follow the Moon. In 1957 India adopted an official national calendar, the Saka calendar, alongside the Gregorian one. How so many calendars work side by side is explained in India's calendars, and why the year has seasons at all comes down to the tilt of the Earth.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Angry crowds rioted in the streets in 1752, shouting "Give us our eleven days".

Truth: Historians have found little or no evidence of such riots. The idea comes mostly from a 1755 painting by William Hogarth, which shows that slogan on a banner during an election. Many people grumbled, but the change went off quietly.
