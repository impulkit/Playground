What are Rajput paintings?

Some Rajput paintings are smaller than a school notebook, yet painters used brushes of a few squirrel hairs to show every pearl in a princess's necklace.

The short answer

Rajput paintings are small, brightly coloured pictures made for the kings and nobles of Rajput kingdoms in what is now Rajasthan, and in the hill states of the western Himalaya. Most were painted between the 16th and 19th centuries, on paper, and kept in albums or bundles rather than hung on walls.

They show Krishna and Radha, scenes from the Ramayana, kings out hunting, ladies on terraces, and even pieces of music. Many are called miniatures, because they are small enough to hold in your hands. Viewers looked at them up close, slowly, passing them from hand to hand and talking about the story or the music they showed.

Surprising but true

Painters in Rajasthan made pictures of musical ragas, so that a tune sung at dawn or during the monsoon could also be seen as a scene.

Two big families

Art historians split Rajput painting into two main groups. Rajasthani painting came from courts on the plains, such as Mewar (Udaipur), Bundi, Kota, Kishangarh, Jaipur and Bikaner. Pahari painting, meaning "of the hills", came from small kingdoms in the Himalayan foothills, like Basohli, Guler and Kangra.

Each court had its own style. Mewar paintings are bold, with flat red and yellow backgrounds. Kota artists loved wild hunting scenes in thick jungle. Basohli paintings use fierce colours and even pieces of shiny beetle wing to look like emeralds. Kangra paintings are softer and more delicate, with pale greens and gentle faces.

Painting a raga

One of the most unusual kinds is the Ragamala, or "garland of ragas". In Indian classical music, a raga is a set of notes with its own mood, and many belong to a time of day or season. Painters gave each raga a picture.

A monsoon raga might show lovers under dark clouds, with peacocks dancing and lightning cracking the sky. A gentle morning raga might show a woman feeding birds. A full Ragamala set could have 36 or more paintings. In this way, music became something you could hold, and a painting became something you could almost hear.

How they were made

Painters began with handmade paper, often several sheets pasted together for strength. They polished it with a smooth stone, sketched the outline in pale red, then filled in colours layer by layer.

Paints were ground from minerals, plants and other natural materials: red from cinnabar, blue from indigo or lapis lazuli, white from shells. Real gold and silver were beaten into powder for crowns and jewels. The finest brushes used a few hairs from a squirrel's tail. A single painting could take weeks, and several artists often worked on one, each doing the part they did best.

Rajput and Mughal painters

The Mughal emperors ran large painting workshops, and many Rajput kings spent time at their court. Ideas travelled both ways. Rajput paintings borrowed some Mughal habits, like shading faces and drawing royal portraits. Mughal painters picked up Rajput colours and themes.

Still, the two styles feel different. Mughal paintings often look like windows onto real scenes. Rajput paintings feel more like poems: hills shaped like cushions, rivers as patterned ribbons, and a sky that may be bright orange for no reason except that it looks wonderful.

A famous face

At the court of Kishangarh, around 1750, the painter Nihal Chand painted a woman known as Bani Thani, with a long, curved eye and a sharp nose. The image became so famous that India Post put it on a stamp in 1973. People sometimes call it India's Mona Lisa.

You can see Rajput paintings in museums in Udaipur, Jaipur and New Delhi, and in big collections in London, New York and Los Angeles. Many palaces in Rajasthan's forts also have walls painted in the same style.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Rajput paintings are just copies of Mughal paintings.

Truth: Rajput and Mughal painters influenced each other, and some artists worked for both. But Rajput painting has its own roots, with flatter spaces, bolder colours and a love of Krishna stories and music that sets it apart.

Try this

Draw a small scene, no bigger than a postcard, with a thick border all round it. Fill the border with a repeating pattern of flowers or leaves. Then paint the sky as a flat band of one bright colour, the way many Rajasthani painters did, and see how it changes the mood.
