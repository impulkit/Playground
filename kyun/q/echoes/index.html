What makes an echo?

In the dome of the Gol Gumbaz in Karnataka, a single clap comes back again and again, bouncing round one of the biggest old domes in the world.

The short answer

An echo is sound that has bounced off something and come back to you. Sound travels through air as a wave, a ripple of squeezed and stretched air spreading out from its source. When that wave hits a large, hard surface, like a cliff, a wall or the inside of a dome, much of it bounces back, the way a ball bounces off a wall.

If the surface is far enough away, the bounced sound arrives a moment after the original, and you hear it as a separate copy of your voice. Soft surfaces like curtains, grass and snow soak up sound instead of reflecting it, so they make poor echoes.

Surprising but true

For years, people repeated that a duck's quack doesn't echo. Scientists at a British university tested it in 2003, and it does.

How far is far enough?

Sound in air travels about 343 metres every second, roughly the length of three football fields. That sounds fast, but your brain can notice gaps of about a tenth of a second. Anything closer together blends into one sound.

In a tenth of a second, sound goes about 34 metres. Since an echo has to travel there and back, you need to be at least about 17 metres from a wall to hear a clear, separate echo. Closer than that, the reflection arrives too soon. It still happens, but it mixes with the original and just makes the sound richer, like singing in a bathroom. That blurred, lingering sound is called reverberation.

The dome that answers back

In Vijayapura, once called Bijapur, in Karnataka, stands the Gol Gumbaz, the tomb of Sultan Muhammad Adil Shah, completed in the mid-1600s. Its dome is about 44 metres across on the inside, one of the largest in the world from that time.

Running round the base of the dome is a narrow balcony called the whispering gallery. A whisper on one side can be heard clearly on the other, as the sound creeps round the curved wall. A clap or a shout bounces around the dome and comes back several times. Visitors' voices fill the space with echoes, so it is rarely quiet. St Paul's Cathedral in London has a famous whispering gallery that works the same way.

Seeing with sound

Some animals use echoes to find their way. Bats send out high-pitched calls, usually too high for us to hear, and listen to the echoes. From how long each echo takes and how it sounds, a bat can tell where a moth is, how big it is and which way it's flying. This is called echolocation.

Dolphins and some whales do the same underwater with clicks. A few birds that live in dark caves, such as oilbirds in South America and some swiftlets in Asia, use simpler clicks to avoid hitting walls.

Echoes put to work

People copied the animals. Ships use sonar, sending pulses of sound down into the sea and timing the echoes to measure depth. This is how scientists mapped the ocean floor, finding underwater mountain ranges longer than any on land. Doctors use ultrasound, very high-pitched sound, to see babies growing inside their mothers and to check hearts and kidneys.

Concert halls are designed with echoes in mind. Architects shape walls and ceilings, and add soft panels, so that music sounds full but not muddy. Too many echoes, and every note smears into the next. Too few, and a hall sounds dead, as in a soundproofed studio.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A duck's quack doesn't echo.

Truth: It does. Researchers at the University of Salford in the UK recorded a duck in an echo chamber in 2003. The quack's echo is hard to notice because the quack fades slowly and the echo blends in with it.

Try this

Find a large, flat wall outside your school, with an adult, and stand far away from it in an open space. Clap sharply and listen. Move closer and further until you hear a clear echo. Then count your paces from the wall. You may find you need to stand at least about 17 m away.
