What are impossible shapes?

You can draw a triangle made of three wooden beams that all meet at right angles. You just can't build it, because it could never exist.

The short answer

An impossible shape is a drawing that looks like a solid 3D object but could never exist in real life. Each small part of it makes sense. Put the parts together, though, and they contradict each other.

The most famous is the Penrose triangle. It seems to be made of three square beams joined at right angles. Follow the beams around and you end up where you started, which can't happen with three right angles in real space. Our brains are built to read flat pictures as solid objects, and they check one corner at a time. That is why the trick works so well.

Surprising but true

In the city of Perth in Australia, there is a giant metal sculpture of an impossible triangle. From one special spot it looks perfect, but walk around it and you see it is really three separate beams.

The triangle with three right angles

In a normal triangle on paper, the three corners add up to 180 degrees. In the Penrose triangle, each corner looks like 90 degrees, adding up to 270. Only on a curved surface like a globe can a triangle's angles go above 180, and the Penrose triangle is drawn as straight beams in flat space.

The Swedish artist Oscar Reutersvärd drew the first version in 1934, made of cubes. In 1958, the British scientist Lionel Penrose and his son Roger, later a Nobel Prize winner in physics, published it as an "impossible object". They also invented the endless Penrose stairs.

Stairs that never end

The Penrose stairs make a square loop. Walk up them clockwise and you climb forever, yet always return to the start. Go the other way and you walk down forever.

Each flight of steps looks normal. The trick lies in how the picture squeezes depth, so that the top step and the bottom step seem to be on the same level. Your brain accepts each bit and never notices that the whole loop is impossible.

Why do optical illusions fool us?Your eyes don't send your brain a photograph. They send clues, and your brain guesses the rest. Illusions catch it guessing wrong.

Escher's impossible worlds

The Dutch artist M. C. Escher loved these ideas. In 1960 he drew Ascending and Descending, in which monks march round the endless stairs on a rooftop. In 1961 came Waterfall. Water flows down a channel, along a zigzag and somehow ends up higher than it started, then pours over a wheel and back to the start. It looks like a machine that runs forever.

Escher returned to strange geometry again and again, from lizards that tile a page to ants marching round a Möbius strip. His maths art still turns up in maths classrooms all over the world.

The devil's fork

Another favourite is the devil's fork, or blivet. At one end, it has three round prongs. At the other, they become two square bars. Look at either end on its own and it's fine. Look at the whole thing and your eyes keep sliding back and forth, trying to make it fit.

It spread widely in the 1960s, when it appeared on magazine covers as a puzzle for readers.

Why our brains get fooled

Your eyes receive a flat image, like a photo. Your brain turns it into a 3D world using clues: lines that meet at corners, shading, and things in front hiding things behind. This is the same skill that lets artists use perspective to make paintings look deep.

These shortcuts are usually right, so the brain trusts them. Impossible shapes break the rules in a way that each shortcut alone can't catch.

Building the impossible

Some artists build real objects that look impossible from one spot. In Perth, Australia, a giant aluminium impossible triangle stands in a park. From the right angle it looks closed. Walk a few steps and it falls apart into three beams.

The Japanese mathematician Kokichi Sugihara makes small models where balls seem to roll uphill towards a peak. They won prizes in the Best Illusion of the Year contest. Seen from another side, the "uphill" slopes actually point down. These are all optical illusions, proof that seeing isn't always believing.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Impossible shapes are just bad drawings with mistakes in them.

Truth: Each corner of an impossible shape is drawn correctly. The trick is that the corners can't all be true together. Our brains check small parts of a picture at a time, so we don't notice the whole thing breaks the rules of space.

Try this

Draw a Penrose triangle. Start with an equilateral triangle, then draw a smaller one inside it, and connect the corners with short lines so each side looks like a thick bar. Shade each bar a different colour. Now try to build it with three pencils and see why you can't close the last corner.
