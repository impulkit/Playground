Why were Roman roads so straight?

Some Roman roads run dead straight for kilometres, over hills and through forests, and cars still drive along a few of them 2,000 years later.

The short answer

Roman roads were straight mainly because a straight line is the shortest way between two places, and Rome needed to move soldiers fast. A legion marching on a straight road reached trouble sooner, and supply carts followed more easily.

Straight lines were also easier to survey. Roman engineers sighted from one high point to the next using simple tools, laying out long straight sections between them. Where a hill or river got in the way, they changed direction and set off straight again. So a Roman road is often a series of straight lines joined by bends.

Surprising but true

The Roman mile was measured in footsteps. "Mille passus" meant a thousand paces, each pace counted as two steps, making a Roman mile about 1.48 km.

The queen of roads

The most famous Roman road is the Via Appia, or Appian Way. It was begun in 312 BCE by an official named Appius Claudius, who gave it his name. It ran south from Rome to Capua and was later extended to Brindisi, on the heel of Italy, about 560 km in all. Romans called it the "queen of long roads".

Parts of it can still be walked today, with the original grey paving stones worn smooth by carts and feet. Tombs and monuments line its edges, because Romans were not allowed to bury their dead inside the city.

Building in layers

A good Roman road was built like a layered sandwich. Workers dug a trench down to firm ground. They filled it with large stones, then smaller stones and gravel, then sand or crushed stone set with lime. On top, they fitted big flat paving stones tightly together.

The surface curved slightly, higher in the middle and lower at the edges, so rainwater ran off into ditches on each side. Many roads were raised on a bank above the land around them, which kept them dry and gave soldiers a better view. Modern roads are built on the same idea of firm layers and good drainage.

Tools for straight lines

Roman surveyors used a tool called a groma. It was a wooden cross on a pole, with a weighted string hanging from each arm. When the strings hung straight down, the cross was level. By lining up the strings with markers far away, a surveyor could set out long straight lines and right angles.

Signal fires or smoke on hilltops may also have helped teams line up stretches many kilometres long. In England, a road called the Fosse Way runs for about 370 km. Along nearly 300 km of it, from Lincoln to Ilchester, it is never more than about 10 km from a straight line.

Milestones and messengers

Along major roads stood stone pillars called milestones, one every Roman mile. They showed the distance to the next town or to Rome, and the name of the emperor who built or repaired the road. In Rome's forum stood a golden milestone, which is part of why people said that all roads lead to Rome.

Official messengers changed horses at stations along the way, and could carry news across the empire in days rather than weeks. Traders, pilgrims and ordinary travellers used the roads too, spreading goods and ideas.

Roads in India

India had great roads too. The Uttarapatha, an ancient route across the north, linked the Gangetic plains to the north-west. Emperor Ashoka wrote that he had planted banyan trees for shade and dug wells along roads for travellers and animals. In the 1500s, Sher Shah Suri rebuilt a long highway with rest houses, which later became part of the Grand Trunk Road, running from Bengal towards the north-west.

Still on the map

Many modern roads in Europe follow Roman routes, because the Romans had already chosen the best line. The Roman Empire fell long ago, but its road network shaped where towns grew and how people still travel. In Italy, some main roads still carry the names of the Roman roads they follow, such as the Via Appia and the Via Flaminia.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Roman roads were always perfectly straight.

Truth: They were straight where the land allowed. Around steep hills, rivers and marshes, Roman engineers curved and zigzagged their roads like anyone else.
