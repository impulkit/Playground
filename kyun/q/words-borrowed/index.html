Which English words came from India?

Every time you put on pyjamas, wash with shampoo or talk about a jungle, you are borrowing from Indian languages.

The short answer

Hundreds of English words come from Indian languages. Some you may use every day: shampoo, pyjamas, bungalow, jungle, cot, loot and bandana. Others belong to food and ideas: chutney, curry, karma, guru and avatar.

Most arrived during the 300 or so years when British traders, soldiers and officials lived in India. They picked up words from Hindi, Urdu, Bengali, Tamil, Persian and Sanskrit and carried them home. Some words changed meaning on the way. An avatar, in Hindu belief, is a god taking earthly form. Today it is also the little character that represents you in a video game.

Surprising but true

Shampoo comes from the Hindi champo, meaning "press" or "massage". In English it first meant a massage, not soap.

Words for clothes and homes

Pyjamas comes from Persian and Urdu pai jama, "leg garment", loose trousers tied at the waist. British men in India adopted them for sleeping, and the habit travelled home. Bandana comes from the Hindi bandhna, "to tie", after a style of tie-dyed cloth. Khaki, from an Urdu word meaning "dusty", became the colour of army uniforms after British soldiers in India began dyeing their white clothes in the 1840s to hide the dirt.

A bungalow was originally a house "in the Bengal style", a single storey with a wide verandah. The word spread with the British across their empire and beyond, so you can now find bungalows in Bengaluru, London and Los Angeles. A cot comes from khat, a light wooden bed strung with rope.

A massage that became a soap

In 1814, Sake Dean Mahomed, an entrepreneur from Patna, opened a bathhouse in Brighton, England. He offered "shampooing", an Indian-style massage with steam and oils, and claimed it could ease aches and pains. It became so fashionable that he was appointed "shampooing surgeon" to King George IV.

The word came from Hindi champo, "press". Only later in the 1800s did "shampoo" come to mean washing the hair, and later still the liquid you wash it with. Dean Mahomed has another claim to fame. In 1794, he published his travels, making him one of the first Indians to write a book in English.

How was soap invented?The first soap was made more than 4,000 years ago from fat and wood ash, and it was often used to clean wool and cloth rather than people.

Jungles, juggernauts and thugs

Jungle comes from the Hindi jangal, which grew from the Sanskrit jangala, meaning dry, rough, uncultivated land. In English it came to mean almost the opposite: thick, wet forest. Loot is from Hindi lut, "plunder", and thug from thag, a cheat or robber.

Juggernaut comes from Jagannath, the deity of the great temple at Puri in Odisha. Each year, during the Rath Yatra festival, huge wooden chariots carrying the deity are pulled through the streets by vast crowds of devotees. European visitors wrote about these giant chariots, and in English "juggernaut" came to mean any huge, unstoppable force.

Words still arriving

In 1886, two British scholars, Henry Yule and Arthur Burnell, published a dictionary of Indian words used in English, with the odd title Hobson-Jobson. It listed well over a thousand words and is still fun to browse.

The flow hasn't stopped. Dictionaries such as the Oxford English Dictionary keep adding Indian English words. In 2017 it added jugaad, a clever, low-cost fix. Food words travel fastest, as Indian restaurants spread around the world. A Mumbai dabbawala or a plate of biryani can now appear in a London newspaper without any explanation. That is how English has always grown, one borrowed word at a time.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Curry is the name of one traditional Indian dish.

Truth: Curry is an English word for many different spiced dishes with sauce. It probably comes from a South Indian word, such as the Tamil kari, via Portuguese. Indian cooks usually call each dish by its own name.
