How do spinning tops and yo-yos work?

A wooden lattu and a plastic yo-yo use the same trick to stay upright: spin fast enough and gravity can't easily knock you over.

The short answer

A spinning top stays upright because of its spin. Anything that spins has momentum going round in a circle, and it resists being tilted. When gravity tries to tip a fast top over, the top doesn't fall. It leans and slowly swings its tilt around in a circle instead.

A yo-yo uses spin as well. As it drops down its string, falling turns into spinning. At the bottom, the spinning yo-yo winds itself back up the string, or "sleeps" there, still whirling. Both toys stop for the same reason: friction slowly steals their spin.

Surprising but true

A painted Greek drinking cup from nearly 2,500 years ago shows a boy playing with what looks like a yo-yo.

Why spinning keeps things upright

Try balancing a top on its point without spinning it. It falls over at once. Spin it fast, and it stands on that tiny point for a minute or more.

Each bit of the top is moving in a circle. When gravity pulls the top sideways, those moving bits carry the push around with them. So instead of tipping, the top wobbles in a slow circle. Scientists call that wobble precession. A moving bicycle gets some help from its spinning wheels in a similar way, though steering does most of the work.

The lattu and its cousins

In India, a classic lattu is a pear-shaped wooden top with an iron nail for a tip. You wind a string tightly around it, then fling it down and pull. The string unwinds and sets the top spinning on the ground. Expert players can scoop a spinning lattu onto a palm, still turning.

In Tamil Nadu the top is called a bambaram, and every region has its own name and games. One common game has players try to hit an opponent's top out of a chalk circle. Craftspeople in Channapatna, Karnataka, make brightly lacquered wooden tops as well.

How a yo-yo comes back

A yo-yo is two discs joined by a short axle, with a string looped around it. When you let go, it falls and spins faster and faster. At the bottom, all the energy of falling has turned into spin.

On a simple yo-yo, the string is tied tight to the axle. The spinning yo-yo winds itself back up, almost to your hand. Each trip loses a little energy to friction, so you give a small tug to help.

Modern yo-yos have a loose loop around a smooth ball bearing. At the bottom they "sleep", spinning in place. A sharp tug makes the string grip again, and up it comes.

Where the energy goes

Hold a yo-yo at your hand and it has stored energy, because gravity can pull it down. As it falls, that stored energy turns into two kinds of motion: dropping and spinning. A heavy rim helps, which is why many yo-yos put most of their weight near the edge.

A top gets its energy from your throw and the pull of the string. From then on it only loses energy. Some escapes as a faint scratch of sound, some as warmth at the tip, and some pushing through the air. A top with a smooth, hard point on a polished floor can spin for minutes. On rough cement it gives up far sooner.

An old toy that keeps coming back

A painted Greek cup from about 440 BCE shows a boy with a disc on a string. In the 1920s, Pedro Flores, a Filipino immigrant in California, started a company making yo-yos. A businessman named Donald Duncan bought it, and yo-yo contests spread across the USA.

Tops are probably even older, and children on every continent have spun them. Spin a ball, a coin, or a plate on a stick, and you meet the same idea that kept the first lattu steady.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A spinning top stays up because spinning cancels out gravity.

Truth: Gravity pulls on a spinning top just as hard as on a still one. The spin turns that pull into a slow sideways wobble, called precession, instead of a fall. Once the spin fades, the top tips over.

Try this

Spin a coin on its edge on a smooth table, then on a cloth. Count how many seconds it spins each time. The cloth has more friction, so the coin should give up much sooner.
