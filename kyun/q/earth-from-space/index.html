What does Earth look like from space?

Astronauts on the International Space Station see about 16 sunrises and 16 sunsets every day, one roughly every 90 minutes.

The short answer

From space, Earth looks like a brilliant blue ball swirled with white clouds, brown and green land and bright white ice at the poles. Oceans cover about 71% of the surface, so blue is the colour you notice first. A thin, glowing layer of air hugs the edge of the planet like a halo.

At night, the view changes completely. Cities glow orange and gold, linked by strings of light along highways and rivers. Lightning flickers inside storms, and green auroras ripple near the poles. Astronauts often say the view is the best part of their job, and many describe feeling protective of the planet after seeing it.

Surprising but true

From space, Earth's air looks like a thin, glowing blue line hugging the edge of the planet, thinner compared with Earth than the skin of an apple.

From the space station

The International Space Station orbits about 400 kilometres above the ground, roughly the straight-line distance from Delhi to Lucknow, but straight up. It zooms around Earth in about 90 minutes. So astronauts see a sunrise or sunset roughly every 45 minutes, about 16 of each a day.

From that height, they can make out rivers, mountain ranges, deserts and big cities. The Himalaya look like a crumpled white sheet. The Thar Desert shows as a sandy patch next to the green fields of Punjab. Big dams, airports and even large ships' trails in the water are visible. Hurricanes and cyclones look like giant spinning pinwheels.

Photos that changed minds

On 24 December 1968, the crew of Apollo 8 became the first people to see Earth rise over the Moon's horizon. Astronaut Bill Anders grabbed a camera and took the photo now called Earthrise. It showed our planet small, blue and alone in black space. Many people say Earthrise helped inspire the environmental movement and the first Earth Day in 1970.

Four years later, on 7 December 1972, the Apollo 17 crew photographed the whole Earth lit by the Sun, with Africa and Antarctica clearly visible. This image, The Blue Marble, became one of the most shared photos in history.

How far have the Voyager probes gone?Launched in 1977, Voyager 1 is so far away that a radio message from Earth takes almost a whole day to reach it.

A pale blue dot

In 1990, the Voyager 1 spacecraft was about 6 billion kilometres away, beyond Neptune's orbit. At the suggestion of astronomer Carl Sagan, NASA turned its camera back towards home. In the photo, Earth is a tiny speck, less than a single pixel, caught in a beam of scattered sunlight.

Sagan called it the Pale Blue Dot. He pointed out that every person who has ever lived spent their life on that speck. The photo is still shown in classrooms to give a sense of how small and precious our world is.

Indians who saw it

In 1984, Rakesh Sharma became the first Indian in space, flying on a Soviet mission. Prime Minister Indira Gandhi spoke to him by video link and asked how India looked from space. He replied with a line from a famous song: "Saare Jahan Se Achha", better than all the world.

Kalpana Chawla, born in Karnal, flew on two NASA space shuttle missions. In 2025, Shubhanshu Shukla became the first Indian astronaut to visit the space station. Their photos and stories have brought the view home to millions of Indian children. Sunita Williams, an American astronaut whose father came from Gujarat, has spent hundreds of days on the space station too.

A planet without borders

One thing astronauts notice is what they can't see: borders between countries. Rivers, coastlines and mountains are clear, but the lines on a map are invisible. The writer Frank White called the feeling of seeing Earth this way the overview effect.

Satellites now photograph every part of Earth every few days. Indian satellites track crops, forests, floods and fishing zones. The view from space isn't only beautiful. It helps farmers, scientists and rescue teams on the ground.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Great Wall of China is the only human-made thing you can see from space.

Truth: From the space station, astronauts can see many human-made things, like cities, airports, dams and big farms, especially at night when city lights shine. The Great Wall is actually one of the hardest to spot, because it is so narrow.
