Why do Bengalis love fish and sweets?

There's an old Bengali saying, "machhe bhate Bangali", which means fish and rice make a Bengali.

The short answer

Bengal sits on the world's biggest river delta, where the Ganga and Brahmaputra spread into hundreds of channels before reaching the sea. The land is wet and flat, full of rivers, ponds and paddy fields. Rice and fish are everywhere, so they became the heart of Bengali food.

Sweets come from milk. Bengal's sweet-makers became experts at turning milk into soft cheese, called chhena, and then into rosogolla, sandesh and dozens of other sweets. A Bengali meal without fish is unusual, and one without something sweet at the end is almost unthinkable.

Surprising but true

The hilsa, Bengal's most prized fish, lives in the sea but swims up rivers to lay its eggs, a bit like a salmon.

A land of water and fish

In much of West Bengal and Bangladesh, almost every village has a pond. Families raise fish such as rohu and katla in them. Rivers bring prawns, and the coast and the mangrove forests of the Sundarbans add crabs and many other fish.

The most prized fish is the hilsa, or ilish. It lives in the Bay of Bengal but swims up rivers like the Padma and Hooghly during the monsoon to lay eggs. Bengalis cook it in many ways, but a favourite is ilish bhapa, steamed in a paste of mustard seeds.

Bengalis argue cheerfully about whose hilsa is best, from the Padma in Bangladesh or the Ganga in India.

Mustard and five spices

Mustard is the signature flavour. Food is cooked in sharp mustard oil, and many fish dishes use a paste of ground mustard seeds that tingles in the nose.

Another key flavour is panch phoron, which means "five spices". It's a mix of whole fenugreek, nigella, cumin, black mustard and fennel seeds. They are fried in hot oil at the start of cooking until they pop, flavouring the whole dish.

A meal in order

A traditional Bengali meal follows an order of tastes. It often starts with something bitter, like shukto, made with bitter gourd and other vegetables. Then come dal, fried vegetables, fish, and sometimes meat. A sweet-sour chutney follows, often made with tomato or raw mango. Finally come sweets and mishti doi, a sweet curd set in clay pots.

The sweet-makers

Bengal's sweet shops, run by families called moira, are famous across India. Most of their sweets begin with chhena: milk split with an acid like lemon juice into soft curds, a little like fresh paneer.

The rosogolla is a ball of chhena boiled in sugar syrup until it becomes spongy. Bengal credits a Kolkata sweet-maker, Nobin Chandra Das, with creating it in 1868. Odisha says a similar sweet was offered at the Jagannath Temple in Puri long before. After a long dispute, both states received official tags for their own versions.

Sandesh is drier, made by cooking chhena with sugar and pressing it into moulds. In winter, sweet-makers use nolen gur, a jaggery made from date palm sap.

Festivals and feasts

Food is central to festivals. During Durga Puja, Kolkata's streets fill with food stalls selling rolls, biryani and sweets. Some families serve bhog, a meal offered at the puja and then shared, with khichuri, vegetables and payesh, a rice pudding.

On Poila Boishakh, the Bengali New Year, families visit sweet shops and eat special lunches. Bengalis in Bangladesh, India and around the world, from London to New Jersey, celebrate with fish, rice and sweets, keeping the old saying alive.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Everyone agrees that the rosogolla was invented in Kolkata.

Truth: Bengal credits the Kolkata sweet-maker Nobin Chandra Das in 1868. Odisha says a version called rasagola was offered at the Jagannath Temple in Puri long before. Both states received official Geographical Indication tags for their own versions.

Try this

With an adult, try making chhena, the soft cheese used in Bengali sweets. Warm some milk, add a little lemon juice, and watch it split into white curds and greenish water. Strain the curds through a clean cloth and taste them with a pinch of sugar.
