How are stars born, and how do they die?

Stars live, grow old and die. And you are made from the leftovers.

The short answer

Stars are born in giant clouds of gas and dust called nebulas. Gravity slowly pulls clumps of the cloud together. As a clump shrinks, its centre gets hotter and more squashed, until it reaches about 10 million °C. Then hydrogen atoms begin to fuse into helium, releasing enormous energy. A star switches on.

A star dies when its fuel runs low. A star like the Sun swells up, puffs off its outer layers and shrinks into a small, hot leftover called a white dwarf. Much heavier stars explode.

Surprising but true

The calcium in your bones and the iron in your blood were made inside stars.

Star nurseries

You can see a star nursery with your own eyes. Below the three stars of Orion's belt hangs a fuzzy patch called the Orion Nebula, about 1,300 light-years away. Through binoculars it looks like a faint glowing cloud. Inside it, thousands of young stars are forming right now.

In 1995, the Hubble telescope photographed towering columns of gas in the Eagle Nebula, nicknamed the Pillars of Creation. In 2022, the Webb telescope looked again in infrared light, which passes through dust. It revealed newborn stars glowing inside the pillars like lamps in fog.

A long middle age

Once a star switches on, it spends most of its life steadily turning hydrogen into helium. Gravity squeezes inward, and the heat from fusion pushes outward. The two balance.

How long this lasts depends on the star's mass. The Sun is about halfway through a life of roughly 10 billion years. Bigger stars burn through their fuel much faster and live only a few million years. Small, cool red dwarfs can last far longer than the universe has existed so far.

Colour gives away a star's temperature. Blue-white stars are the hottest. Orange and red stars are cooler.

Quiet endings

When a Sun-like star runs low on hydrogen in its core, it swells into a red giant, hundreds of times wider than before. In about 5 billion years, the Sun will do this, and may swallow Mercury and Venus. Then it will gently push off its outer layers into a glowing shell of gas. What's left is a white dwarf, about as big as Earth but as heavy as a star, slowly cooling for billions of years.

Explosive endings

A star more than about eight times the Sun's mass ends far more violently. Its core collapses in less than a second, and the star blows apart in a supernova. For a few weeks, one exploding star can outshine a whole galaxy.

In the year 1054, Chinese astronomers recorded a new star bright enough to see in daylight. Today, telescopes pointed at that spot see the Crab Nebula, the expanding wreck of that explosion. At its centre spins a tiny, superdense neutron star. If the leftover core is heavy enough, it becomes a black hole.

The Indian scientist Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar worked out the limit for how heavy a white dwarf can be before it must collapse. He won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1983.

Made of stars

The first stars had almost nothing to work with but hydrogen and helium. Over billions of years, stars built heavier elements, like carbon, oxygen, calcium and iron, and scattered them into space when they died. New stars and planets formed from that enriched dust, including the Sun and Earth.

So almost every atom in your body, apart from hydrogen, was made inside a star. The astronomer Carl Sagan put it simply: we are made of star-stuff.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Stars are giant balls of fire.

Truth: Nothing is burning in a star the way wood burns. Stars shine because of nuclear fusion, where light atoms are squeezed together to make heavier ones, releasing huge amounts of energy.

Try this

On a clear winter evening, between December and February, find Orion with an adult. Look for the three stars in a row that make his belt. Compare the orange-red star at one shoulder, Betelgeuse, with the blue-white star at the opposite knee, Rigel. The colour tells you the temperature. Blue-white is hotter than orange-red.
