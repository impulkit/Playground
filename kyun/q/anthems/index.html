Why do countries have national anthems?

Rabindranath Tagore wrote the national anthems of two countries, India and Bangladesh.

The short answer

A national anthem is a song that stands for a country. It is played at official ceremonies, sports matches, school assemblies and when leaders visit other nations. When people sing it together, it reminds them that they belong to the same country, whatever language they speak at home.

Most anthems are short and easy to sing in a crowd. They usually praise the land, remember a struggle for freedom, or ask for blessings on the nation. Many became anthems long after they were written, because people already loved them. A few were written to order, for a competition or a new constitution, and some countries have swapped one anthem for another.

Surprising but true

Spain's national anthem has no official words, so Spanish football players stand silently while it plays.

India's anthem

Jana Gana Mana was written by Rabindranath Tagore in Bengali, in a style rich in Sanskrit words, so it can be understood across much of India. It was first sung on 27 December 1911 at a session of the Indian National Congress in Calcutta. The song names regions, rivers and mountains from across the country, from Punjab and Sindh to Bengal, and from the Himalaya to the seas.

The Constituent Assembly adopted its first stanza as the national anthem on 24 January 1950, two days before the Constitution came into force. The full version takes about 52 seconds.

Who was Rabindranath Tagore?He wrote the national anthems of two countries, won Asia's first Nobel Prize, and only started painting seriously in his sixties.

Tagore also wrote Amar Shonar Bangla, which became the national anthem of Bangladesh in 1971. Very few people in history have written the national anthems of two countries.

America's anthem

The Star-Spangled Banner began as a poem by Francis Scott Key. In 1814, during a war with Britain, he watched British ships bombard Fort McHenry in Baltimore through the night. At dawn, he saw a huge American flag still flying, and wrote about it. His words were set to a popular British tune. The song became the official US anthem only in 1931.

It is famously hard to sing. Its melody spans about an octave and a half, a wide range for most voices, and singers at big games sometimes crack on the high notes. The flag Key saw still exists and hangs in the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History.

Anthems around the world

The Netherlands' anthem, Het Wilhelmus, has a tune and words from the 1500s, which makes it one of the oldest. Japan's anthem, Kimigayo, has words from a poem more than a thousand years old, and it is one of the shortest anywhere. Britain's God Save the King changes to "Queen" when a queen is on the throne. Spain's Marcha Real has no official words at all.

Some countries have changed their anthems after big changes in history. South Africa's anthem, adopted after the end of apartheid, mixes five of the country's languages in a single song.

Standing and singing

In India, people stand to attention when the anthem is played. In 1986, the Supreme Court considered the case of three schoolchildren in Kerala who had been expelled for not singing it because of their religious beliefs. They had stood respectfully. The Court ruled that they could not be punished for staying silent while standing.

In 2016, the Supreme Court ordered cinemas to play the anthem before films. In 2018, it changed that order, making it optional. Anthems carry strong feelings, which is why rules about them are argued over so often. Like flags, they are simple things that can mean a great deal.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Jana Gana Mana was written to praise King George V.

Truth: This rumour began because the song was first sung in December 1911, during the King's visit to India. Tagore himself denied it in a letter years later, saying the "dispenser of India's destiny" in the song was not any king.
