How did Polynesians find islands across the Pacific?

Without compasses, maps or metal tools, Polynesian sailors found almost every habitable island in an ocean bigger than all the world's land put together.

The short answer

Polynesian sailors found their way across the Pacific by reading nature. They steered by the rising and setting points of stars and felt the long ocean swells rolling under their canoes. They watched which way birds flew at dusk, and noticed clouds piling up over hidden islands.

They sailed in big double-hulled canoes, two hulls joined by a deck, with sails woven from pandanus leaves. These canoes could carry families, pigs, chickens, dogs and plants to start a new life. Food for the voyage included breadfruit, taro, coconuts and dried fish.

Over about 2,000 years, they spread across a huge triangle of ocean, from Hawaiʻi in the north to Aotearoa New Zealand in the south-west and Rapa Nui in the east. They reached almost every island where people could live.

Surprising but true

In 1976, the double canoe Hōkūleʻa sailed about 4,000 km from Hawaiʻi to Tahiti using only traditional navigation, guided by a master navigator from a tiny Micronesian island.

Starting in the west

The voyagers' ancestors came originally from Taiwan and the islands of South-East Asia. By about 3,000 years ago, a people known to archaeologists by their pottery, called Lapita, were sailing east from islands near New Guinea to Fiji, Tonga and Samoa.

There they stayed for centuries, and Polynesian culture took shape. Then, around 1,000 years ago, a burst of long voyages carried people to the far corners of the triangle: Hawaiʻi, Rapa Nui and finally Aotearoa, settled around 1250 to 1300 CE.

A star compass in the head

Navigators trained from childhood. They memorised where dozens of stars rose and set on the horizon. Each star's rising point was like a house on a giant circle, a "star compass" kept in memory.

As one star climbed too high to use, the navigator switched to the next one rising at the same spot. By day, they steered by the Sun and by the swells. A swell is a long wave made by a far-off storm, which can keep a steady direction for days. Some navigators lay down in the hull to feel the waves crossing beneath them.

Signs of land helped close the gap. Clouds often pile up over islands, and lagoons can tint their undersides green. Floating plants, and seabirds heading home at sunset, also showed the way.

The voyage that proved it

By the 20th century, many outsiders doubted Polynesians had sailed on purpose. Some thought they had simply drifted. In 1947, the Norwegian explorer Thor Heyerdahl even argued they had come from South America, and drifted west on a raft called the Kon-Tiki to show it was possible.

In 1976, a group in Hawaiʻi built a replica double canoe called Hōkūleʻa. No Hawaiian navigator still knew the old methods, so they asked Mau Piailug, from the tiny Micronesian island of Satawal. Using no instruments, he guided the canoe about 4,000 km to Tahiti in about a month. Huge crowds met them on the beach.

Mau went on to teach Hawaiian navigators, and from 2014 to 2017 Hōkūleʻa sailed right around the world.

The sweet potato puzzle

Polynesians grew the sweet potato, a plant from South America, long before Europeans arrived. And a DNA study published in 2020 suggested that some Polynesians and Native South Americans met around 800 years ago.

Who travelled to whom is still debated. Many experts think Polynesian sailors reached South America and came back, but the evidence isn't settled.

Wayfinding today

Across the Pacific, people are reviving traditional voyaging. Young navigators in Hawaiʻi, Aotearoa and Micronesia learn the star compass and sail long trips. The skills that once filled an ocean with people have become a source of pride. They are also a reminder that careful watching can find the way across a sea with no roads at all.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Pacific islands were settled by accident, by people blown off course on drifting canoes.

Truth: Computer studies of winds and currents show drifting canoes would almost never reach distant islands like Hawaiʻi. Voyages like Hōkūleʻa's showed that Polynesians could sail there and back on purpose, using stars, waves and birds.

Try this

On a clear night, with an adult, pick one bright star low on the eastern horizon and note what's under it, like a tree or a roof. Check again an hour later. The star will have risen and moved west. Polynesian navigators memorised where hundreds of stars rose and set, and used them like a compass.
