Why is Sachin Tendulkar called the Little Master?

He was only about 1.65 metres tall, played for India at 16 and finished with 100 international centuries, a record nobody has matched.

The short answer

Sachin Tendulkar is called the Little Master because he was short, about 1.65 metres (5 feet 5 inches), and because he mastered batting like almost nobody before him. The nickname had been used earlier for another great Mumbai batter, Sunil Gavaskar, and passed to Sachin as he broke Gavaskar's records.

He played international cricket for 24 years, from 1989 to 2013. He scored more runs in Tests and one-day internationals than anyone else, and is the only player with 100 international centuries.

Surprising but true

As a boy, Sachin wanted to be a fast bowler. At a pace academy in Chennai, the Australian bowling great Dennis Lillee told him to concentrate on batting instead.

A boy from Shivaji Park

Sachin grew up in Bandra, in Mumbai. He was a mischievous child, and his elder brother Ajit took him to Ramakant Achrekar, a coach who trained boys at Shivaji Park. Sachin practised for hours every day, often playing several matches across the city in a single afternoon, travelling between them on his coach's scooter.

In 1988, aged 14, he and his friend Vinod Kambli put on 664 runs together in a school match, a partnership that was then a world record at any level.

Sixteen and facing Pakistan

In November 1989, aged 16, Sachin made his Test debut against Pakistan in Karachi. Pakistan had some of the fastest bowlers in the world. In a later match in that series, a bouncer from Waqar Younis hit him on the nose. Bleeding, he refused to leave the field and kept batting.

His first Test century came in England in 1990. In 1992, at Perth in Australia, he made a hundred on one of the fastest, bounciest pitches in the world, aged 18.

The records

Over his career Sachin scored 15,921 Test runs and 18,426 one-day runs, both world records. He hit 51 Test centuries and 49 one-day centuries. In 2010, against South Africa in Gwalior, he became the first man to score 200 in a one-day international.

He played 200 Test matches, more than anyone else. His Test batting average was 53.78, which means he scored that many runs on average every time he was out. His batting was neat and balanced, using a heavy bat of about 1.5 kg, heavier than most players used. He was a handy bowler too, sending down leg-spin, off-spin and medium pace, and took more than 200 international wickets.

Six World Cups

Sachin played in six World Cups, from 1992 to 2011. In 2003 he was player of the tournament, but India lost the final to Australia.

In 2011 India won at last, in his home city. After the final at the Wankhede Stadium, his teammates carried him on their shoulders. Virat Kohli, then 22, said Sachin had carried the burden of the nation for 21 years, so it was their turn to carry him.

Why he mattered

For Indian fans in the 1990s, India often seemed to depend on Sachin alone. Many people say they switched off the TV when he got out. He batted in an age of great fast bowlers, before helmets were as safe as today, relying on quick reflexes and footwork.

He retired after his 200th Test, at the Wankhede in November 2013. That same day the government announced he would receive the Bharat Ratna, India's highest civilian award. He was 40, its youngest recipient, and the first sportsperson to receive it. He later served six years as a member of the Rajya Sabha, Parliament's upper house.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Sachin was a natural genius who never needed to practise much.

Truth: He was famous for practising for hours, even late in his career. He often prepared for particular bowlers by practising on special pitches to copy the conditions he would face.
