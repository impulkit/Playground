How do puppets come alive?

In Japanese Bunraku theatre, it takes three puppeteers working together to move a single puppet, and the best can train for decades.

The short answer

A puppet is a figure that a person moves to tell a story. It comes alive through movement, voice and the audience's imagination. Puppeteers practise for years to make a figure of wood, cloth or leather seem to breathe, think and feel.

There are four main kinds. String puppets, or marionettes, hang from strings tied to a control. Glove puppets fit over the hand. Rod puppets are moved by sticks from below or behind. Shadow puppets are flat figures held between a lamp and a screen, so the audience sees only their shadows. India has all four, and so do many other countries.

Surprising but true

Some shadow puppets from Andhra Pradesh are made of leather and can be taller than an adult, with jointed arms and legs that move against a glowing screen.

Small moves, big feelings

The secret of a good puppet show is that small movements suggest a lot. A slight tilt of the head looks like curiosity. A slump of the shoulders looks like sadness. Our brains are very good at reading feelings from body language, even in a block of wood.

Puppeteers also make sure the puppet seems to look at whatever it is talking to, and pauses before it speaks, as if it is thinking. Together with a good voice, this makes the audience forget, for a while, that someone is holding the strings.

Kathputli of Rajasthan

In Rajasthan, kathputli puppeteers have performed for centuries. Kath means wood and putli means doll. The puppets have carved wooden heads, big painted eyes and bright clothes, with long skirts instead of legs.

The puppeteer holds strings tied to the head and hands, and makes the figures dance, fight on horseback and bow to kings. Often a musician plays a drum and sings while the puppeteer speaks for the characters, sometimes using a small reed whistle held in the mouth to make a squeaky puppet voice. Many shows tell tales of Rajput heroes like Amar Singh Rathore.

What is the Ramayana?A prince, a princess, a demon king and an army of monkeys. The Ramayana has been retold for more than 2,000 years, from Indian villages to Thai palaces.

Shadows on a screen

Shadow puppetry is found from India to Indonesia and China. In Andhra Pradesh, tholu bommalata puppets are made from animal hide, scraped so thin it lets light through, then dyed in bright colours. Some are life-sized. Behind a white screen, an oil lamp or bulb makes them glow.

Shows often tell stories from the Ramayana and Mahabharata and can last all night. Karnataka has a similar tradition called togalu gombeyata, and Kerala has tolpava koothu, performed in temples. In Indonesia, wayang kulit shadow puppetry is listed by UNESCO as world cultural heritage.

Three people, one puppet

In Japan, Bunraku puppet theatre grew up in Osaka more than 300 years ago. Its puppets are more than a metre tall. One senior puppeteer moves the head and right arm, a second moves the left arm, and a third moves the feet. Junior puppeteers often train for years on the feet alone before they are allowed to move a head.

The puppeteers stand on stage in full view, often dressed in black. Yet after a few minutes, audiences stop seeing them and watch only the puppet. A narrator chants all the voices, accompanied by a three-stringed instrument called the shamisen. It is a form of theatre for adults, full of love stories and tragedy.

Puppets on screen

In the United States, Jim Henson created the Muppets, starting with a TV show in 1955. In 1969 his characters joined Sesame Street, which taught letters and numbers to millions of children around the world. India had its own version, Galli Galli Sim Sim, from 2006.

Puppets also live on in films through stop-motion, where puppets are moved a tiny bit between each photograph, as in animation. Many traditional Indian puppeteers struggle to make a living today, but festivals and schools are working to keep their skills alive.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Puppets are just toys for small children.

Truth: For most of history, puppet shows were for everyone, telling epics, religious stories, news and jokes for adults. In many places they still are, and some puppet traditions are recognised by UNESCO as world heritage.

Try this

Cut a character from card and tape it to a straw or a stick. Hang a white bedsheet in a doorway and shine a torch from behind. Move the puppet between the torch and the sheet and see how the shadow grows when it's near the light and shrinks when it's near the sheet.
