Why are bird eggs different colours?

Birds paint their eggs from the inside, using just two colour chemicals, one reddish-brown and one blue-green.

The short answer

Bird eggs get their colours from just two pigments. One, called protoporphyrin, makes reddish-brown colours and speckles. The other, biliverdin, makes blue and green. A bird adds them in a part of its body called the shell gland, in the last hours before the egg is laid. Mixing different amounts, and dabbing them in spots or streaks, gives everything from snowy white to deep blue to speckled brown.

Why each bird has its own colours is a harder question. Hiding eggs from hungry animals is a big reason for many birds. Others may be protecting eggs from sunlight, signalling health, or helping parents recognise their own eggs.

Surprising but true

Coloured eggs did not start with birds. Some dinosaurs, like Oviraptor, laid blue-green eggs too.

Painted from the inside

An egg forms inside a female bird over about a day. The shell builds up around it, made of calcium carbonate, the same material as chalk. Near the end, the shell gland adds colour. Blue-green pigment often goes right through the shell. Brown spots are usually added at the very end, on the surface.

Some brown-speckled eggs show which way the egg was turning as the colour went on. The spots can be smeared into streaks, like paint on a spinning wheel.

Hiding in plain sight

Birds that nest on open ground have the biggest reason to hide their eggs. In India, the red-wattled lapwing, the noisy bird that seems to shout "did-he-do-it", lays its eggs in a shallow scrape on bare earth or gravel. The eggs are olive-brown with dark blotches, almost impossible to spot until you nearly step on them. This kind of disguise is called camouflage, and it works for eggs just as stripes work for a tiger.

Birds that nest in dark holes, like kingfishers, owls and woodpeckers, often lay plain white eggs. Nothing can see them in there, so camouflage doesn't matter.

Copycat eggs

The common cuckoo lays its eggs in other birds' nests and lets them raise its chicks. Different groups of cuckoos specialise in different host birds, and their eggs often match the host's eggs closely in colour and pattern. Host birds that spot an odd egg may throw it out, so a good match helps the cuckoo.

In India, the koel lays its eggs in crows' nests. Its eggs look quite like crows' eggs, which may help them escape notice.

Blue skies, blue eggs

The American robin's eggs are such a clean sky-blue that "robin's egg blue" became the name of a colour. Many thrushes and starlings, including India's common myna, lay blue eggs too.

Why blue? Scientists have several ideas. Blue-green pigment may help shield the growing chick from strong sunlight. Bright blue eggs may also show that the mother is healthy, because making the pigment uses up resources. The evidence is mixed, and the reason may differ from bird to bird.

Dinosaurs did it first

For a long time, scientists thought coloured eggs were a bird invention. Then, in 2018, researchers tested fossil eggshells and found traces of both pigments in the eggs of some dinosaurs. Oviraptor, a feathered dinosaur, laid blue-green eggs.

That fits with what fossils show more and more clearly: birds are living dinosaurs. Egg sizes vary wildly too. An ostrich egg is about 15 cm long and weighs about as much as two dozen hen's eggs, while some hummingbird eggs are no bigger than a pea. Whatever their colour, every one of them is built the same way, as you can read in what is inside an egg.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Brown chicken eggs are healthier than white ones.

Truth: Egg colour depends on the breed of hen, not on how healthy the egg is. Brown and white eggs have the same nutrition. What the hen eats affects the yolk, not the shell's colour.

Try this

Make ten paper "eggs". Colour five with brown and grey speckles and leave five plain white. Ask a friend to look away while you scatter them on a patch of soil, gravel or dry grass. Time how long it takes them to find each kind. Which ones hide better?
