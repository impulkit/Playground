How was the Grand Canyon made?

A river carved a canyon more than 1.6 km deep, and the rocks at the bottom are almost 2 billion years old.

The short answer

The Grand Canyon in Arizona, USA, was carved by the Colorado River. Over millions of years, the river cut down through the rock while the land around it was slowly pushed upward. Sand and pebbles carried by the water worked like sandpaper.

Most geologists think the canyon took its shape in the last 5 to 6 million years, though parts may be older.

Surprising but true

Walking down to the bottom of the Grand Canyon is like walking back through about 1.8 billion years of Earth's history, one rock layer at a time.

A book made of rock

The canyon walls are made of layers, like pages in a book. Each layer is a different ancient world: a shallow sea, a sandy desert, a muddy coast. The sediment piled up and hardened into rock.

Near the rim is the Kaibab Limestone, about 270 million years old, full of fossil sea sponges and shells. Below it lies the pale Coconino Sandstone, made from desert dunes, where fossil footprints of small four-legged animals still march across the stone. Each layer lets geologists read an ancient world.

The rocks at the bottom are about 1.8 billion years old, about 40% of Earth's age. In some places, younger rocks sit right on top of much older ones with a gap of over a billion years between them, as if pages were torn out of the book. Geologists call it the Great Unconformity.

A river with sandpaper

On its own, water is too soft to cut rock quickly. But the Colorado River once carried huge loads of sand, gravel and boulders, especially during spring floods from melting snow in the Rocky Mountains. Those rolled and scraped along the riverbed, grinding it down.

At the same time, the whole region, called the Colorado Plateau, was being pushed upward by forces deep inside Earth. As the land rose, the river kept cutting, like a saw held still while the wood is pushed up into it. Rain, frost and side streams then widened the canyon, which is up to about 29 km across.

Today the Glen Canyon Dam, upstream, traps much of that sand in a reservoir. The river below runs clearer and colder than it used to. Dams bring water and electricity, but they change rivers too.

How old is the canyon?

Here geologists disagree. Many think the Colorado River found its present route only 5 to 6 million years ago and carved most of the canyon since. Others argue that parts of it were cut by older rivers as far back as 70 million years ago, and the modern river joined them together. Clues from minerals that record when rocks cooled near the surface point both ways, so the debate goes on.

People and the canyon

Native American peoples have lived in and around the canyon for thousands of years. The Havasupai still live deep inside a side canyon, and their village of Supai is so remote that mail arrives by mule.

In 1869, a one-armed Civil War veteran named John Wesley Powell led the first known expedition down the river through the canyon, in wooden boats. The canyon became a US national park in 1919, and millions of people visit every year.

Rivers that cut mountains

The same thing happens in India. As the Himalayas rise, rivers like the Ganga and its sisters cut deep gorges through them.

India has a smaller canyon of its own at Gandikota in Andhra Pradesh. There, the Penna River has carved a steep red gorge beside an old stone fort. Visitors often call it the Grand Canyon of India.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Grand Canyon is the deepest canyon in the world.

Truth: It is one of the most famous, but not the deepest. The Yarlung Tsangpo canyon in Tibet, cut by the river that becomes the Brahmaputra, is several kilometres deeper. So is the Kali Gandaki gorge in Nepal.

Try this

Make a sand river. Pile damp sand into a slope at one end of a baking tray. Pour water slowly from a cup at the top and watch it cut a channel. Now lift the top end of the tray a little at a time while you keep pouring. The channel cuts deeper, just as the Colorado did while the land rose.
