Why does some cheese have holes?

The holes in Swiss cheese are made by bacteria breathing out gas, and in 2015 scientists found that tiny bits of hay help them form.

The short answer

The holes in cheeses like Emmental are made by bacteria. While the cheese ripens, a type of bacteria called Propionibacterium feeds on a substance in the young cheese and gives off carbon dioxide gas. The gas collects in small pockets. As more gas builds up, the pockets swell into round holes.

Cheese makers call the holes "eyes". In a big wheel of Emmental they can be as small as a cherry or as large as a walnut. The same bacteria also help give the cheese its sweet, nutty taste.

Most cheeses don't have holes at all. Cheddar is packed tight, and paneer is made in a completely different way.

Surprising but true

Cheese makers call the holes "eyes", and a cheese that should have holes but doesn't is called "blind".

A cheese that breathes

Cheese starts as milk. A cheese maker adds helpful microbes and an enzyme called rennet, which turns the milk into soft curds and watery whey. The curds are pressed into a big wheel and soaked in salty water.

For Emmental, the wheel is then moved to a warm room, often a little above 20°C, for several weeks. This is when the hole-making bacteria wake up. They eat lactic acid, made earlier by other bacteria, and turn it into a few new substances, including carbon dioxide.

This is a kind of fermentation, the same family of changes that turns milk into curd. The rind of the cheese is fairly airtight, so the gas can't escape easily. Instead it gathers in weak spots and pushes outward, making round holes.

The hay mystery

For a long time, people believed the gas formed holes wherever it happened to collect. Then cheese makers in Switzerland noticed something odd. Over the past few decades, their cheeses had fewer and fewer holes.

In 2015, scientists at Agroscope, a Swiss government research centre, found the answer. In the past, cows were milked by hand into open buckets in barns, and tiny flecks of hay fell into the milk. Those specks gave the gas a starting point, like a seed for each hole.

Modern milking machines are closed and spotless. With almost no hay in the milk, the gas had nowhere to begin. When the researchers added tiny amounts of hay dust back into milk, the holes came back.

Big wheels

Emmental comes from the Emme valley in central Switzerland. Its wheels are among the largest cheeses in the world, often weighing around 75 to 120 kg, as heavy as a grown man or more. Moving one takes two people or a machine.

The holes affect more than looks. Too many holes, or holes in the wrong shape, can make cheese crack. A cheese with no holes at all, when it should have them, is called "blind". A Swiss expert can tap a wheel and listen to tell roughly how the eyes are forming inside, a bit like tapping a watermelon.

Other holey cheeses include Jarlsberg from Norway and Gruyère, which has only a few small eyes.

Cheese without bubbles

Indian cheeses are mostly made without long ripening, so they don't grow holes. Paneer is made by curdling hot milk with lemon juice or vinegar, then pressing out the whey. It is ready in hours, not months.

India does have a few older aged cheeses. Bandel cheese, from a town near Kolkata, was introduced by Portuguese settlers centuries ago. It comes in small smoked or plain rounds. In Jammu and Kashmir, a stretchy cheese called kalari is fried until crisp outside and gooey inside.

And the strangest part? The bubbles inside Emmental are almost perfectly round for the same reason soap bubbles are, which you can explore in why bubbles are round.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Mice love cheese more than any other food.

Truth: That idea comes mostly from cartoons and stories. Mice will nibble cheese, but they usually prefer grains, seeds and sweet foods.

Try this

Pour a fizzy drink into a smooth glass and watch where the bubbles form. They usually stream up from tiny scratches or specks on the glass. Cheese holes start in a similar way, around tiny specks inside the cheese.
