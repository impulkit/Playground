How did Indian wrestlers win Olympic medals?

In 1952 a wrestler from a Maharashtra village could not afford the trip to the Olympics. His neighbours chipped in, and he came home with India's first individual medal.

The short answer

Indian wrestlers have won eight Olympic medals. The story began in 1952, when K. D. Jadhav won bronze in Helsinki, independent India's first individual Olympic medal. The next medal took 56 years, but since Sushil Kumar's bronze in 2008, Indian wrestlers have won medals at every Summer Olympics up to 2024.

The medals grew out of India's long tradition of kushti, wrestling in mud pits called akharas. They also came from the hard work of coaches, families and village communities, especially in Haryana and Maharashtra.

Surprising but true

Indian wrestlers have won Olympic medals at every Summer Games from 2008 to 2024.

The akhara

Kushti, also called pehlwani, is centuries old. Wrestlers, called pehlwans, train in an akhara, a pit of soft earth under a guru. They wake before dawn, do hundreds of squats and push-ups, swing heavy wooden clubs and wrestle for hours.

Their diet is famous: milk, ghee, almonds and plenty of roti. In the early 1900s a pehlwan known as the Great Gama became world famous. He was never defeated in a long career, and in 1910 he went to London and won a big international contest.

K. D. Jadhav's bronze

Khashaba Dadasaheb Jadhav came from Goleshwar, a village in Satara district, Maharashtra. He was small, quick and very fit. In 1952 he qualified for the Helsinki Olympics, but needed money for the journey. People in his village and region raised funds, and the principal of his old college reportedly mortgaged his house to help.

In Helsinki he won the bronze medal in bantamweight freestyle wrestling. For 44 years he was India's only individual Olympic medallist, until the tennis player Leander Paes won bronze in 1996. Jadhav never received a major national sports award in his lifetime. In 2001, long after his death, he was given the Arjuna Award.

The Chhatrasal generation

From the 1980s, Delhi's Chhatrasal Stadium, under the coach Satpal Singh, trained many top wrestlers. One was Sushil Kumar, who won bronze in Beijing in 2008 and silver in London in 2012. He was the first Indian since independence to win two individual Olympic medals.

In London, Yogeshwar Dutt also won bronze. At Rio in 2016, Sakshi Malik became the first Indian woman to win an Olympic wrestling medal. At Tokyo, Ravi Kumar Dahiya won silver and Bajrang Punia bronze. In Paris in 2024, 21-year-old Aman Sehrawat won bronze.

Daughters on the mat

Women's wrestling met resistance in many Haryana villages, where girls were expected to stay at home. Mahavir Singh Phogat trained his daughters anyway. Geeta Phogat won gold at the 2010 Commonwealth Games, the first Indian woman wrestler to do so. The Bollywood film Dangal (2016) told their story and was a hit in India and China.

His niece Vinesh Phogat became one of India's best wrestlers. At Paris 2024 she reached the final, but was disqualified on the morning of the final for being 100 grams over the weight limit. It was a heartbreaking moment for Indian fans.

Weight, mats and rules

Olympic wrestling has weight classes, so wrestlers must "make weight" by stepping on the scales. Many cut weight before a competition by sweating and eating less, which can be hard on the body. Experts warn young athletes to do this only with proper supervision.

The sport has moved from mud to mats, and from local dangals, village wrestling contests, to world championships. Yet many Olympic wrestlers still start in an akhara, where the earth is soft and the day begins before sunrise.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Wrestling is only about being the biggest and strongest.

Truth: Olympic wrestlers compete in weight classes, so they face opponents of similar size. Balance, speed, technique and stamina often matter more than raw strength.

Try this

Try a wrestler's warm-up called the "bethak", a deep squat. Stand with feet apart, squat down slowly with your back straight, then stand. Do ten, rest, and do ten more. Traditional wrestlers are said to do hundreds a day, but ten done well is a good start.
