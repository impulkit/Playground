How does a song become a hit?

When scientists let thousands of people choose songs in a test, the same song could be a hit in one group and a flop in another.

The short answer

A song becomes a hit when lots of people listen to it, again and again. Music charts keep score. They count streams, downloads, sales and radio plays every week and rank songs from the top down. The best-known chart in the USA, the Billboard Hot 100, has done this since 1958.

What makes people press play is harder to pin down. A catchy tune, a strong beat and a famous singer all help. So does being heard on the radio, in a film or in thousands of short videos. Researchers have found that luck plays a bigger part than most people think.

Surprising but true

On most streaming services, a play counts only after about 30 seconds, so many pop songs now get to the catchy part very quickly.

Keeping score

Early charts counted records sold in shops and how often songs were played on the radio or on jukeboxes. Shop staff filled in forms, and some of the counting was guesswork. In India, the radio show Binaca Geetmala, which began in 1952, ranked film songs using listener votes and record sales, as radio shows describes.

Today the counting is much more exact. Streaming services know every time a song is played. Charts now combine streams, downloads and sales using fixed rules, so a stream and a bought copy are worth different amounts.

The 30-second rule

On many streaming services, a play is counted only after about 30 seconds. If a listener skips sooner, it doesn't count.

This rule has changed how songs are written. Long, slow introductions have become rarer, and many pop songs now open with the chorus or a hook, a short catchy bit that grabs attention. Songs have also become shorter on average, because a short song that is replayed twice earns two plays.

Why some tunes stick

Our brains enjoy patterns we can almost predict. A hit song often repeats a simple melody several times, with small changes that keep it fresh. Hearing a song again and again tends to make people like it more, which is why radio stations play hits so often.

Some songs get stuck in your head for hours. Scientists call these earworms. They tend to have simple, repeating tunes with an unusual jump or rhythm somewhere. Humming along, or finishing the song in your head, often helps them fade.

Luck and the snowball

In 2006, researchers Matthew Salganik, Peter Dodds and Duncan Watts ran an online experiment with about 14,000 people. Participants listened to songs by unknown bands and downloaded the ones they liked. In some groups, people could see how many others had downloaded each song.

In those groups, early favourites quickly pulled ahead, like a snowball rolling downhill. Very good songs rarely did badly and weak songs rarely won, but which song came first was hard to predict. This is one reason music companies can never be sure what will become a hit.

Hits in India today

In India, many of the biggest hits are still film songs. A film's songs are often released weeks before the film, and they build excitement for it. Independent artists and Punjabi pop singers have also climbed charts on streaming services.

Short videos have become a powerful launch pad too. A few seconds of a song used in thousands of dance clips can push it up the charts in many countries at once. Sometimes the songs that spread this way are years old, rediscovered by listeners who were not born when they first came out.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The best song always becomes the biggest hit.

Truth: Quality helps, but research shows that early luck, timing and seeing what others like play a big part. A strong song can be missed, and a fairly ordinary one can take off.
