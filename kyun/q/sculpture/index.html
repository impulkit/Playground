How are statues made?

Sculptors make statues in four basic ways. They can take material away, add it on, pour it into a mould, or build it up from pieces.

The short answer

A statue, or any sculpture, is art you can walk around. Sculptors make them in four main ways.

Carving means cutting material away from a block of stone or wood until the shape is left. Modelling means building up a soft material, like clay or wax, with your hands. Casting means making a mould and pouring in liquid metal, which hardens into the shape. Assembling means joining pieces together, such as welding steel or fitting parts.

Big statues often use several methods at once. A sculptor might model a small clay version first, then scale it up for carving or casting.

Surprising but true

About 90 percent of the rock removed to carve Mount Rushmore was blasted away with dynamite before workers finished the faces with drills and hammers.

Carving: taking away

Carving is the oldest method we know of. People have carved small figures from bone, ivory and stone for tens of thousands of years. Carving is hard and slow. You cannot put material back, so one wrong strike can ruin months of work.

Stone carvers use chisels and hammers. Soft stones like sandstone and marble are easier to shape, while hard granite takes much longer. That's why the kind of rock matters so much.

In Shravanabelagola, Karnataka, the statue of Gommateshwara (also called Bahubali), a figure honoured by Jains, stands about 17 metres tall. It was carved from a single granite rock on a hilltop more than a thousand years ago. Every twelve years, devotees pour milk, turmeric and flowers over it in a ceremony.

Polishing like glass

Some of India's most famous early sculpture comes from the time of Emperor Ashoka, more than 2,200 years ago. His craftsmen carved tall stone pillars topped with animals, polished so smooth they still shine.

The best known is the Lion Capital found at Sarnath, near Varanasi: four lions standing back to back. Today it is the national emblem of India, printed on banknotes, passports and government papers.

Modelling and casting: adding on

Clay can be pushed, pinched and smoothed, so it's perfect for trying ideas. Many sculptors model a version in clay or wax before making the final work.

Clay can be fired into terracotta, or the model can be used to make a mould for metal. Casting in bronze, a metal made mostly of copper, lets sculptors make strong figures with fine details and thin parts that stone could not support. Chola artists in south India used this method to make dancing gods, as you can read in How did Chola artists cast bronze gods?.

Giants made of metal

Very large statues are usually built more like buildings. The Statue of Liberty in New York has thin copper sheets hammered into shape and fixed to an iron framework inside.

The Statue of Unity in Gujarat, honouring Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, is the tallest statue in the world at 182 metres. That's about twice the height of the Statue of Liberty including its base. It has a frame of steel and concrete, covered with bronze panels.

Carving a mountain

At Mount Rushmore in South Dakota, USA, four faces of American presidents were carved into a granite mountain between 1927 and 1941. Each is about 18 metres (60 feet) tall. Workers hung from the cliff in harnesses. They used dynamite to remove most of the rock, then drilled honeycomb patterns of holes and chipped away the rest by hand.

Not everyone celebrates it. The mountain is in the Black Hills, land sacred to the Lakota people, who say it was taken from them. Like many monuments, it means different things to different people.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ancient Greek and Roman marble statues were always pure white.

Truth: Many were painted in bright colours. Scientists have found tiny traces of paint on them, but most of it wore away over the centuries, leaving the bare stone we see in museums.

Try this

Take a bar of soap and a blunt butter knife or an old spoon, with an adult nearby. Draw a simple shape on the soap, such as a fish or a turtle, then slowly scrape away everything that isn't the shape. You are carving, just like a stone sculptor, only much softer.
