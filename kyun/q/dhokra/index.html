What is Dhokra metal casting?

The bronze Dancing Girl of Mohenjo-daro, about 4,500 years old, was made with the lost-wax method. Dhokra artists in India still use the same idea today.

The short answer

Dhokra, also spelled Dokra, is a way of making metal figures using the lost-wax method. The artist builds a model in wax over a clay core, covers it with more clay, then heats it so the wax melts and runs out. Molten brass is poured into the empty space the wax left behind. When it cools, the clay is broken off, and a metal figure appears.

It's one of the oldest metal-casting methods in the world. In India, it's practised mainly by tribal and rural communities in Chhattisgarh, West Bengal, Odisha, Jharkhand and nearby states.

Dhokra pieces are easy to spot: horses, elephants, owls, tribal deities, lamps and dancers, often covered in fine raised lines that look like twisted thread.

Surprising but true

Every Dhokra mould is broken open to get the figure out, so no two Dhokra pieces are ever exactly the same.

As old as the Indus cities

In 1926, archaeologists at Mohenjo-daro, one of the great Indus cities, found a small bronze figure of a girl, about 10 cm tall. She stands with one hand on her hip, and her arm is covered in bangles. She is about 4,500 years old and was made by lost-wax casting.

Today she's known as the Dancing Girl and is one of the treasures of the National Museum in New Delhi. Dhokra artists use a method that works in much the same way, more than 4,000 years later.

The name Dhokra is often linked to the Dhokra Damar, travelling metalsmiths of eastern India.

Step by step

First, the artist shapes a rough core from clay mixed with rice husk and lets it dry. Then comes the wax, usually beeswax from local hives mixed with tree resin. It's softened, pressed through a sieve-like tool into thin threads and wound around the core. Patterns, eyes and jewellery are added in wax too.

Next, the wax model is covered in layers of fine clay, then coarser clay, leaving a small channel open. Once dry, the mould goes into a fire. The wax melts and drains out, which is why it's called "lost wax".

Meanwhile, brass is melted in a clay crucible. It's poured into the hot mould through the channel and fills every thread and curl the wax left. After it cools, the clay is cracked open and the piece is cleaned and polished.

Brass from old pots

Most Dhokra today is made from brass, an alloy of copper and zinc. Artists often melt down old brass pots, broken lamps and scrap metal. In that sense, Dhokra has always been a kind of recycling.

The beeswax is reused too. When it drains out of the mould, some can be collected and used again.

Villages of metal workers

In Bastar, Chhattisgarh, the village of Kondagaon is a famous Dhokra centre. In West Bengal, artists in Bikna near Bankura and Dariyapur in Bardhaman are known for the craft. Dhokra is also made in Odisha, Jharkhand and Madhya Pradesh.

Bastar Dhokra and Bengal Dokra both have Geographical Indication, or GI, tags. A GI tag is a kind of official label that says a product really comes from a particular place.

Old craft, new buyers

For centuries Dhokra makers made lamps, measuring bowls, anklets and figures of village gods for local use. Now their work sells in craft fairs, city shops and abroad. Some artists make modern designs like pen stands and wall hangings, alongside traditional horses and elephants.

The work is hot, slow and hard, and young people in some villages are choosing other jobs. Craft groups and government schemes try to help artists find buyers and fair prices, so that each new piece, made once and never again, keeps a 4,500-year-old idea alive.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Dhokra figures are made in reusable moulds, so they all come out the same.

Truth: Each Dhokra mould is made around a single wax model and broken open after casting. A new model has to be made for every piece, so each one is slightly different.

Try this

Roll thin "snakes" of modelling clay or plasticine and coil them over a small ball of clay to make an animal, like an elephant or a tortoise. Add eyes and patterns with more coils. Dhokra artists build their wax models in exactly this way, thread by thread.
