Who is Kailash Satyarthi?

An engineer from Madhya Pradesh gave up his job to free children from factories, and won the Nobel Peace Prize for it.

The short answer

Kailash Satyarthi is an Indian campaigner who has spent more than 40 years fighting child labour, which means children being made to work instead of going to school. In 1980 he founded Bachpan Bachao Andolan, the "Save the Childhood Movement".

His teams have raided factories, brick kilns and workshops where children were kept working, often with police, and brought the children out. His organisation says it has freed more than a lakh children.

In 2014, he shared the Nobel Peace Prize with Malala Yousafzai. The prize honoured both for their struggle for every child's right to education.

Surprising but true

In 1998, Satyarthi led the Global March Against Child Labour, which travelled about 80,000 km across more than 100 countries.

A boy at the school gate

Satyarthi was born in 1954 in Vidisha, a town in Madhya Pradesh. He has often told a story from his first day of school. Outside the gate sat a boy his own age, polishing shoes with his father.

Young Kailash asked his teacher why that boy wasn't in class. The answer was that poor children often had to work. The question stayed with him.

He grew up, trained as an electrical engineer and began teaching. Then, at 26, he gave it up to work against child labour full time.

Freeing children

Bachpan Bachao Andolan began by rescuing children trapped as bonded labourers. That means working to pay off a family debt that never seems to shrink. Many were in carpet workshops, quarries and brick kilns.

Rescue was only the start. Children needed schooling, food and safe homes. Free school meals, like India's mid-day meal scheme, later helped keep many of them in class.

What is the mid-day meal scheme?Every school day, India serves a hot lunch to more than 10 crore children, the largest school meal programme in the world.

A march around the world

In 1998, Satyarthi helped organise the Global March Against Child Labour. Children and adults marched, rode and flew about 80,000 km through more than 100 countries, ending in Geneva, Switzerland.

The next year, the International Labour Organization agreed a new treaty against the worst kinds of child labour. Many people credit the march with helping to push it through.

He also helped start a label, now called GoodWeave, that certifies carpets made without children. India's long tradition of weaving and textiles is world famous, and the label lets buyers choose rugs made fairly.

Danger on the job

Freeing children angers people who profit from their work. Satyarthi and his colleagues have been threatened and attacked during rescue raids, and two of his co-workers were killed over the years. He has said he never thought of stopping.

He also argued that the problem was bigger than a few cruel factory owners. Poverty pushes families to send children to work. Buyers want cheap goods. Schools are sometimes too far away or too poor. So his work moved beyond raids to campaigning for laws, better schools and fair shopping.

When he won the Nobel Prize in 2014, many Indians had never heard his name. Overnight, child labour was front-page news across the country.

Laws and what remains

India has made strong laws over the years. The Right to Education Act of 2009 promises free schooling to every child aged 6 to 14, and a 2016 law bans employing children under 14 in almost all work.

Child labour has fallen worldwide since 2000, but millions of children still work. Satyarthi often says a child freed is a child who gets to be a child again.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Child labour is a thing of the past, or only happens in a few far-off places.

Truth: The ILO and UNICEF estimated that about 16 crore (160 million) children were in child labour in 2020, roughly 1 in 10 children worldwide. It happens in poorer and richer countries alike, on farms, in workshops and in homes.

Try this

Look at the labels on clothes, shoes, rugs or footballs at home and write down where each was made. Then look for labels such as Fairtrade or GoodWeave, which promise that no children were used to make the product. Talk with an adult about why such a label might matter.
