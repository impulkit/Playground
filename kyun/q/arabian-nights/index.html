What is the Arabian Nights?

The most famous story in the Arabian Nights, Aladdin, was never in the old Arabic books at all.

The short answer

The Arabian Nights, also called One Thousand and One Nights, is a huge collection of stories from the Middle East, Persia and India. It is held together by one clever frame. A young woman called Shahrazad tells a king a new story every night, always stopping at the most exciting moment, so he must keep her alive to hear the ending.

Inside her stories are adventures of sailors, thieves, jinn, talking animals, merchants and kings. There are love stories, mysteries and jokes. Some stories have smaller stories tucked inside them, like boxes inside boxes.

Surprising but true

Aladdin and Ali Baba were added in the 1700s by a French translator, who heard them from a Syrian storyteller called Hanna Diyab.

The frame story

In the opening tale, King Shahryar learns that his wife has betrayed him. Angry and bitter, he decides that no woman can be trusted, and he begins to treat each new wife cruelly. Shahrazad, the clever daughter of his chief minister, offers to marry him anyway, with a plan.

Each night she begins a story and leaves it hanging at dawn. The king, curious, lets her live another day. After a thousand and one nights, in most versions, he has changed his mind, and they live together in peace. Her weapons are words and suspense.

A story that holds other stories is called a frame story. India's Panchatantra works the same way, and it reached the Arab world in translation centuries before the Nights took shape.

Where the stories came from

Scholars think the collection began with a Persian book called Hazar Afsan, "A Thousand Tales", which itself may have drawn on Indian stories. Writers in Baghdad in the 900s mention it. That Persian book is lost.

The oldest surviving piece of the Nights is a scrap of paper from the 800s, with the title and a few opening lines. Paper was then a fairly new material in the Arab world, brought along trade routes from China. Over the next centuries, storytellers in Baghdad, Cairo and Damascus added new tales. Some feature Harun al-Rashid, a real ruler of Baghdad from 786 to 809, wandering the city in disguise at night.

Merchants on the Silk Road and sea routes carried stories along with goods. That is one reason the Nights feels at home in so many places.

What was the Silk Road?Silk from China reached Rome, and Buddhism reached China, along roads that almost nobody travelled from end to end.

Sinbad, Aladdin and a French surprise

The voyages of Sinbad the Sailor follow a merchant who sails from Basra. He meets a giant bird called the roc, an island that turns out to be a whale, and a valley of diamonds.

Aladdin and Ali Baba came later. A French scholar called Antoine Galland translated the Nights into French between 1704 and 1717. His Arabic manuscript ran out, and his publisher wanted more. In 1709, a young Syrian traveller called Hanna Diyab told Galland several stories in Paris. Galland wrote them up, and two of them became Aladdin and Ali Baba and the Forty Thieves.

Scholars call these "orphan tales" because no older Arabic manuscript of the Nights contains them. Hanna Diyab later wrote his own travel memoir, which was published in full only in the 2010s.

A book that never stops changing

Galland's French books were a huge success, and they were soon translated into English and many other languages. Each translator chose different stories and told them differently. There is no single "complete" Arabian Nights.

The tales have since become films, plays and animated movies. Disney's Aladdin, released in 1992, turned a genie into one of the best-known cartoon characters in the world. Shahrazad's trick of stopping at the best moment is still used by every television series that ends an episode on a cliffhanger.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Aladdin and Ali Baba are among the oldest Arabian Nights stories.

Truth: Neither appears in any known Arabic manuscript of the Nights made before Galland's translation. He added them from tales told to him in Paris in 1709.
