Why do winds blow in the same directions?

In 1492 Columbus sailed west on one belt of wind and came home on another blowing the opposite way, and those belts still blow today.

The short answer

Wind is air moving from where pressure is high to where it is low. On a world scale, the Sun does the pushing. It heats the equator far more than the poles. Warm air rises near the equator, flows towards the poles high up, cools, sinks and flows back along the ground.

If Earth stood still, that would make simple north–south winds. But Earth spins once a day, and that turns the air sideways. The result is a set of wind belts that wrap around the planet like stripes on a ball. In each belt, the wind usually blows from the same direction, year after year.

From the equator outwards, the main belts are the trade winds, the westerlies and the polar easterlies.

Surprising but true

Because Earth spins, winds don't blow straight from high pressure to low. They curve to the right in the northern half of the world and to the left in the southern half.

Giant loops of air

The first big loop starts at the equator. Hot, damp air rises, building tall rain clouds, and spreads out high in the sky. At around 30° north and south it sinks again. This loop is called a Hadley cell, after the English lawyer George Hadley, who described it in 1735.

The sinking air is dry, which is one reason so many deserts, from the Sahara to the Thar, lie near 30° north. Some of it flows back towards the equator along the ground. Those are the trade winds.

Two more loops sit further out, between about 30° and 60°, and between 60° and the poles. Each makes its own surface wind belt.

Why the winds curve

Stand on the equator and you are whizzing east at about 1,670 km an hour as Earth turns. Stand near the North Pole and you are hardly moving. Air flowing across those different speeds gets bent off course.

In the northern half of the world it swings to the right, and in the southern half to the left. This is the Coriolis effect, named after the French scientist Gaspard-Gustave de Coriolis, who worked out the maths in 1835.

So the trade winds don't blow straight towards the equator. North of it, they come from the north-east. South of it, from the south-east. And the westerlies, as the name says, blow from the west.

How can a sailboat sail into the wind?A sailboat can't sail straight into the wind, but it can zigzag towards it, because its sail works like an aeroplane wing turned on its side.

Roads in the sky for sailors

Before engines, these winds decided where ships went. In 1492, Columbus sailed south from Spain to the Canary Islands, picked up the north-east trades and rode them across the Atlantic. To get home, he sailed north into the westerlies. Spanish and Portuguese ships used the same route for centuries.

Near the equator lies a belt of light winds called the doldrums, where sailing ships could drift for days. Around 30° there's another calm patch, known in English as the horse latitudes.

India's own wind

India adds a twist. The monsoon is a wind that changes direction with the seasons. In summer, the land heats up faster than the sea, and moist winds blow in from the south-west, bringing rain. In winter the pattern flips and dry winds blow out from the north-east.

Arab and Indian sailors knew this rhythm well. For around 2,000 years, traders timed voyages across the Arabian Sea to the monsoon winds, reaching India in summer and sailing home in winter.

High above the clouds

Far above the surface winds, about 10 km up, narrow rivers of air called jet streams race from west to east. Pilots use them. A flight from New York to London can be an hour or so faster than the trip back. Down at sea level, those same west winds help push the great ocean currents in slow circles around each ocean.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Because of Earth's spin, water always swirls down a plughole one way in the north and the other way in the south.

Truth: The Coriolis effect is real but far too weak to steer water in a sink or toilet. The shape of the basin and how the water was poured matter much more. It only shows up clearly in huge systems like cyclones.

Try this

Spin a lazy Susan or a round tray slowly, and ask a friend to draw a straight line from the centre to the edge with a marker while it turns. The line comes out curved. That's how Earth's spin bends the winds, even though the air itself is moving straight.
