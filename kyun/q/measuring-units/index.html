Why are there metres and feet?

In 1999, a NASA spacecraft was lost at Mars because one team used pounds and another used metric units.

The short answer

The first units came from things people always had with them: their bodies. A foot was the length of a foot. A cubit, used in ancient Egypt and India, was the length of a forearm. A pace was a step. These were handy, but everyone's body is different, so different towns and kingdoms ended up with different units.

Over centuries, rulers tried to fix standard sizes so that trade would be fair. Britain's system of inches, feet, yards and miles spread with its empire. In the 1790s, France invented the metric system, based on tens and on the size of the Earth. Most of the world has since switched to metric. The United States is the biggest country that still mainly uses the older units in daily life.

Surprising but true

Since 1959, the inch has been defined using the metric system. An inch is exactly 2.54 centimetres.

Measuring with your body

A hand is about the width of a palm, roughly 10 cm. It is still used to measure the height of horses. A yard was said to be the distance from an English king's nose to his outstretched thumb, though that story may be a legend.

India had its own body units. An angula was the width of a finger. A hasta, or hath, was the length from elbow to fingertip, like a cubit. Builders of temples used these units, and old texts on architecture describe buildings in them.

The problem with bodies is obvious. A tall trader buying cloth by the arm's length would get more than a short one.

Weights for fair trade

People needed fair weights as much as fair lengths. In the Indus Valley cities, about 4,500 years ago, traders used carefully cut cubes of stone. Archaeologists have found that their sizes follow a regular pattern, with each weight a set multiple of a smaller one. A shopkeeper in Harappa and one in Lothal could trust each other's scales.

Later, India used units like the tola, seer and maund. The tola, about 11.7 grams, is still used by many jewellers when they sell gold.

One country, many units

By the 1700s, France alone had hundreds of different units, and the same name could mean different sizes in different towns. After the French Revolution, scientists created the metric system. Everything was based on tens, so you could change centimetres to metres just by moving a decimal point.

India adopted the metric system by a law passed in 1956, and switched over during the following years. The rupee had already become decimal, with 100 paise instead of 16 annas. Kilograms replaced seers in markets, and kilometres replaced miles on road signs.

The spacecraft that was lost

In September 1999, NASA's Mars Climate Orbiter reached Mars after a ten-month journey. Then it vanished. An investigation found that one engineering team's software reported the push of its small thrusters in pound units. Another team's software expected newton units, which are metric.

Each small mistake added up. The spacecraft flew much too close to Mars and was destroyed or skipped off into space. It was an expensive lesson in checking units. Today's Mars rovers are planned entirely in metric.

Living with both

In the US, rulers often show inches on one edge and centimetres on the other. Since 1959, an inch has been defined as exactly 2.54 centimetres, so the old units are now officially measured in metric. In India, people may still talk about a room in square feet or their height in feet and inches, while buying vegetables in kilograms. The two systems still live side by side.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The United States doesn't use the metric system at all.

Truth: Americans use miles, pounds and feet in daily life, but science, medicine and the military in the US use metric units. Soft drinks are sold in 2-litre bottles, and even the inch is legally defined in centimetres.

Try this

Measure the length of a table using your hand span, then ask a grown-up to measure it with theirs. You'll get different answers. Now measure it with a ruler. This is the problem that standard units were invented to solve.
