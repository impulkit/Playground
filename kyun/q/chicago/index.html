How did Chicago become a city of skyscrapers?

In 1871, a fire burned the middle of Chicago to the ground. Rebuilding it, architects invented a new kind of building, and the skyscraper was born.

The short answer

In October 1871, the Great Chicago Fire destroyed the centre of the city. It burned for about two days, killed around 300 people and left about 100,000 homeless. Most buildings had been made of wood, and a long dry summer and strong winds helped the flames spread.

Chicago rebuilt fast, and land in the centre became very expensive. To fit more offices on each plot, architects built upwards. In 1885, the Home Insurance Building used a frame of iron and steel to hold up its floors, instead of thick stone walls. That idea made tall buildings possible, and many people call it the first skyscraper.

Surprising but true

Engineers made the Chicago River flow backwards in 1900, away from Lake Michigan, to keep the city's drinking water clean.

Out of the ashes

Before the fire, Chicago was a young city that had grown very quickly. It sat where railways from the east met the farmland of the west, and where Lake Michigan connected to rivers leading to the Mississippi. Grain, timber and cattle all passed through.

After the fire, the city passed rules against wooden buildings in the centre. Architects and engineers rushed in from across the country. They had a blank space, rich clients and new materials such as steel. The fire had cleared the way for a new kind of city.

A skeleton of steel

Older tall buildings needed very thick walls at the bottom to carry the weight above. The higher they went, the thicker the walls had to be. William Le Baron Jenney's Home Insurance Building changed that. A metal frame, like a skeleton, carried the weight. The walls just hung on it like skin.

That meant thinner walls and bigger windows. Add safe elevators, and buildings could rise 10, 20, then 100 floors. Architects such as Louis Sullivan designed tall buildings that looked light and modern. This group became known as the Chicago School.

Reaching higher

In 1973, the Sears Tower, now called the Willis Tower, was finished. At about 442 m, it was the tallest building in the world for 25 years. It is made of nine square tubes bundled together, a design by the engineer Fazlur Rahman Khan, who was born in what is now Bangladesh. His tube designs made very tall skyscrapers cheaper to build, and many later towers used them.

On a glass ledge called the Skydeck, you can stand on the 103rd floor and look straight down through the floor.

The river that flows backwards

In the 1800s, Chicago dumped its sewage into the Chicago River, which flowed into Lake Michigan, where the city got its drinking water. People got sick from water-borne diseases. So in 1900, engineers opened a canal that reversed the river, sending it away from the lake. Every year on St Patrick's Day, the river is dyed bright green for a few hours.

A world stage

In 1893, Chicago hosted the World's Columbian Exposition, a giant fair. It showed off the world's first Ferris wheel, about 80 m tall. At the Parliament of the World's Religions, held alongside it, Swami Vivekananda gave a famous speech that began "Sisters and Brothers of America". It introduced many Americans to Hindu ideas and Indian thought about world religions.

Chicago is also known for blues and jazz, for its "deep-dish" pizza, baked in a high-sided pan, and for icy winds blowing off the lake in winter.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Great Chicago Fire started when Mrs O'Leary's cow kicked over a lantern.

Truth: A newspaper reporter later admitted he made up the cow story. The fire did begin near the O'Learys' barn, but its exact cause is unknown. In 1997 the Chicago City Council officially cleared Catherine O'Leary and her cow.
