Is there a planet made of diamonds?

In 2012, astronomers suggested that a scorching planet 41 light-years away might be one-third diamond. Then the arguments began.

The short answer

Maybe, but nobody has proved it. In 2012, a team led by Nikku Madhusudhan, an Indian-born astronomer then at Yale University, studied a planet called 55 Cancri e. Its star seemed rich in carbon, and the planet's size and weight fitted a world made largely of carbon. Under deep pressure, they suggested, as much as a third of it might be diamond.

Headlines called it the "diamond planet". But later studies found the star may not be as carbon-rich as thought, and in 2024 the James Webb Space Telescope detected hints of an atmosphere on the planet. Today, many astronomers think its interior is more like Earth's rock than a diamond. That's how science often works: a bold idea gets tested, and the evidence decides.

Surprising but true

Scientists think it may "rain" diamonds deep inside Neptune and Uranus, where crushing pressure squeezes carbon into crystals.

A world with an 18-hour year

55 Cancri e circles its star so closely that a full year there lasts about 18 hours. You'd have a birthday every day, with time left over. It's about twice as wide as Earth and about eight times heavier, so it's called a super-Earth.

It's also blisteringly hot. Its dayside reaches over 2,000 °C, hot enough to melt many rocks, so its surface may be partly covered in a lava ocean. The planet is probably tidally locked, with one side always facing its star, just as the Moon always shows the same face to Earth.

Its star, 55 Cancri, is about 41 light-years away, in the constellation Cancer. On a very dark night far from city lights, it's just barely visible without a telescope.

Diamond rain on Neptune

Diamonds may form much closer to home. Deep inside Neptune and Uranus, the pressure is millions of times greater than on Earth's surface. Their atmospheres contain methane, a gas made of carbon and hydrogen.

Scientists think the pressure there may strip the carbon out and squeeze it into tiny diamonds, which sink like hailstones towards the planet's core. In 2017, researchers at SLAC in California blasted plastic with powerful lasers to recreate those conditions, and saw nanodiamonds form for a split second. Nobody can visit Neptune to check, so this is still an educated guess. If it's right, the diamonds may melt deeper down and form a layer of liquid carbon.

Why does Neptune have the fastest winds?The planet farthest from the Sun gets the least sunlight, yet its winds blow faster than the speed of sound on Earth.

A star like a giant gem

Astronomers have found a dead star that might be even more sparkly. When stars like the Sun die, they shrink into white dwarfs, about the size of Earth. As they cool over billions of years, the carbon and oxygen inside can freeze into crystals.

One white dwarf, BPM 37093, nicknamed "Lucy", has a core thought to be largely crystallised. It's sometimes compared to a diamond as wide as a planet, though its crystals are packed differently from diamonds on Earth. Our own Sun may end this way in about 5 billion years, part of the life cycle of stars.

Diamonds are common in space

On Earth, natural diamonds form about 150 km underground and are carried up by volcanic eruptions. India was the world's only known source of diamonds for many centuries, from the Golconda mines that produced the famous Kohinoor.

In space, carbon is the fourth most common element. Tiny nanodiamonds have been found inside meteorites, older than the Sun itself. So diamond worlds aren't impossible, just unproven. With better exoplanet studies, astronomers may yet find one that really sparkles.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Astronomers have found a planet that is solid diamond.

Truth: No planet has been shown to be made of diamond. A few have been suggested to contain lots of carbon or diamond, but the evidence is uncertain and newer studies disagree.
