Where does chocolate come from?

From seeds inside a pod that grows straight out of a tree trunk.

The short answer

Chocolate comes from the seeds of the cacao tree. Its pods grow right out of the trunk and branches. Inside each pod are about 30 to 50 seeds, cocoa beans, wrapped in sweet white pulp.

Farmers let the beans ferment, dry them in the sun, then roast and grind them. Add sugar and milk, and you have chocolate.

The tree's scientific name, Theobroma, means "food of the gods" in Greek. It was given by the Swedish scientist Carl Linnaeus in the 1700s.

Surprising but true

The Aztecs used cacao beans as money. A turkey might cost about 100 beans.

A tree that flowers on its trunk

Cacao is a small tree that grows in the shade of taller rainforest trees. Unlike most trees, it grows its tiny white and pink flowers straight from the trunk and thick branches. A tree can produce thousands of flowers a year, but only a few out of every hundred become pods.

The pods are about the size of a papaya and turn yellow, orange, red or purple when ripe. Farmers cut them down with long-handled knives, crack them open and scoop out the slimy seeds.

A drink for kings

People in Central America were drinking cacao thousands of years ago. The Maya and Aztecs made a bitter, spicy, frothy drink. Cacao beans were so valuable the Aztecs used them as money.

The tree's story may begin even earlier, further south. In 2018, scientists found traces of cacao in pots about 5,300 years old in Ecuador, in the upper Amazon. The Maya later painted cacao pods on their pottery and carved them on temples. They poured the drink from one cup to another, held high, to build up a thick foam.

In the 1500s, Spanish travellers brought cacao to Europe. There people added sugar and drank it hot. Solid chocolate bars came much later, in the 1800s.

How is chocolate made in a factory?Chocolate starts as bitter, purple seeds wrapped in sweet white pulp, and they must rot a little before they taste of chocolate at all.

From bean to bar

The fresh seeds are piled in heaps or boxes and covered with banana leaves for about a week. Yeasts and bacteria feed on the sweet pulp, warming the heap and starting the fermentation that turns the seeds brown and creates the first hints of chocolate flavour. Then the beans are spread out to dry in the sun.

Factories roast and crack the beans and grind the inside into a thick paste. In 1875, Daniel Peter in Switzerland mixed it with condensed milk to make the first milk chocolate. In 1900, Milton Hershey began selling chocolate bars in the USA, and a whole town in Pennsylvania grew up around his factory.

Tiny helpers

Cacao flowers are pollinated by tiny flies called midges. Like figs and their wasps, the tree depends on a very small partner. Midges breed in rotting leaves and damp places on the forest floor, so farms with a bit of mess and shade often grow more pods than very tidy ones.

Where it grows

Cacao only grows in warm, wet places near the equator. Most of the world's cocoa now comes from West Africa. Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana together grow more than half of it, mostly on small family farms. Investigations have found children doing hard and risky work on some of these farms, and chocolate companies and governments are under pressure to stop it.

India grows it too, mostly in Kerala, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu. There, cacao is often planted in the shade of coconut and areca palms, as a second crop for farmers.

One warning for pet owners: chocolate contains a chemical called theobromine, which humans handle easily but dogs can't. Even a small amount can make a dog seriously ill.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Chocolate grows as brown beans that just need to be melted down.

Truth: Fresh cacao seeds are pale and bitter, wrapped in sweet white pulp. Chocolate flavour only develops after the seeds are fermented, dried, roasted and ground. Most of chocolate's sweetness comes from added sugar.

Try this

Do a chocolate taste test. With an adult, collect three kinds of chocolate, such as milk, dark and very dark (70% cocoa or more). Read the labels and note the cocoa percentage. Let a small piece of each melt slowly on your tongue and describe the taste. Is the darkest one more bitter? Does it taste fruity, nutty or earthy?
