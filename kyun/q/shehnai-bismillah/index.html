Who was Bismillah Khan?

When India became free in 1947, a shehnai played by a man from Varanasi rang out at the Red Fort in Delhi.

The short answer

Ustad Bismillah Khan was a master of the shehnai, a wind instrument that sounds a little like a bright, singing oboe. He was born in 1916 in Dumraon, in Bihar, into a family of court musicians. As a boy he moved to Varanasi to learn from his uncle, Ali Bux, who played at the Kashi Vishwanath temple.

Before him, the shehnai was mostly heard at weddings and temple gates. Bismillah Khan took it onto the concert stage and made it a classical instrument. In 2001 he was given the Bharat Ratna, India's highest civilian award.

Surprising but true

Bismillah Khan was a devout Muslim who played every morning at a Hindu temple in Varanasi, and he saw no clash between the two.

A small pipe with a big voice

A shehnai is a wooden tube about the length of a school ruler or a bit more, with finger holes and a flared metal bell at the end. The player blows through a double reed: two thin strips of leaf or cane that buzz against each other.

It is a hard instrument to play softly or smoothly. Players must control their breath very carefully, and many use a trick called circular breathing, breathing in through the nose while pushing air out of the mouth. Bismillah Khan could make it bend and slide between notes almost like a human voice singing a raga.

Shehnai players often perform with a partner who holds one steady note on a second shehnai, called the sur. This drone gives the main player a fixed point to weave around. It works rather like the tanpura behind a singer in Hindustani music, the classical style of North India.

The sound of freedom

On the night of 14–15 August 1947, India gained independence. At the Red Fort in Delhi, Bismillah Khan was invited to play the shehnai as the new nation began. He played there again for the first Republic Day in 1950.

For decades after, his shehnai was broadcast on Doordarshan on Independence Day. To many Indians, its sound became tied to national celebrations, the way fireworks are to the Fourth of July in the United States.

A musician of the ghats

Bismillah Khan practised for hours on the ghats, the stone steps leading down to the Ganga. He was a devout Shia Muslim who prayed five times a day. He also played at the Kashi Vishwanath temple and spoke with love about Saraswati, the Hindu goddess of music and learning. For him, music was a kind of prayer that belonged to everyone.

He travelled the world, playing at festivals in Europe, North America and beyond. Yet he refused to move away from Varanasi. He lived simply, in an old house in a narrow lane, surrounded by a large family he supported. He often said that nowhere else had the Ganga, and that his music came from the city.

He also played for a few films. His shehnai can be heard in the Hindi film Goonj Uthi Shehnai (1959), and he played for the Kannada film Sanaadi Appanna (1977). The documentary maker Goutam Ghose later made a film about his life.

What he left behind

Bismillah Khan died in 2006, at the age of 90. He was buried in Varanasi with a shehnai beside him. Today young players learn from his recordings, and his name is given to a national award for young artists from the Sangeet Natak Akademi.

The shehnai still plays at countless weddings. But thanks to him, it also has a place in serious concert halls, where a single pipe of wood and metal can hold an audience completely still.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The shehnai is only a wedding instrument, not real classical music.

Truth: That was a common view before Bismillah Khan. He played full ragas on it at major concerts and festivals around the world, and became one of only a few musicians ever given the Bharat Ratna.

Try this

Flatten the end of a paper straw by pressing it hard between your teeth or fingers, then snip the corners into a point. Put the flat end in your mouth and blow firmly. The two flaps buzz against each other, a simple double reed like the one in a shehnai.
