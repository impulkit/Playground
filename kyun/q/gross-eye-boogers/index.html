What is the gunk in your eyes in the morning?

Every night, your eyes quietly pack the day's dust, dead cells and oil into little crusts, and leave them in the corners for you to find.

The short answer

The crusty or sticky stuff in the corners of your eyes in the morning is called rheum, though most people call it "sleep". It is a mix of mucus, oil, dead skin cells and dust. Your eyes make these things all day to keep themselves clean and wet.

When you are awake, every blink sweeps the mess towards the inner corner, where a tiny drain carries tears away. When you sleep, the blinking stops. The gunk collects in the corners and dries out, and by morning it is waiting for you.

Surprising but true

Your eyelids hold dozens of tiny oil glands, and the oil they make stops your tears from drying out too fast. Some of that oil ends up in morning eye gunk.

Your eye's three-layer coat

The surface of your eye is covered by a thin film of tears, only a few thousandths of a millimetre thick. It has three layers. The inner one is sticky mucus, which helps the film cling to the eye. The middle one is mostly salty water. The outer one is oil.

That oil comes from glands along the edges of your eyelids, several dozen of them, lined up like the teeth of a comb. Without the oil, your tears would dry out in seconds, the way a puddle shrinks on a hot pavement.

A cleaning crew that never stops

Your eye is always under attack, from dust, pollen, smoke and germs. Mucus traps these bits like flypaper. Dead cells from the surface of the eye are shed and replaced too.

Awake, you blink about 15 to 20 times a minute, more than 10,000 times a day. Each blink acts like a car's windscreen wiper. It spreads fresh tears, then pushes the dirty ones towards the inner corner. There, two small holes, one on each lid, drain the tears into a tube that runs to your nose. That is why your nose runs when you cry.

Why it dries into crusts

At night, the wipers stop. Mucus and oil drift to the corners and sit there for hours. The water in them evaporates, and what is left hardens into yellowish crusts or stays as soft, sticky blobs. It is the same reason snot dries into crusts inside your nose.

Babies often have extra gunk, because the tiny drain from the eye to the nose can be partly blocked for the first months of life. It usually opens up on its own.

Some mornings bring more than others. A dusty room, a cold, hay fever or a long cry before bed can all mean extra gunk.

When to tell an adult

A little gunk every morning is normal and healthy. But sometimes an eye turns red, itchy or sore, with thick green or yellow gunk, or lids glued shut in the morning. That can be conjunctivitis, an infection or irritation of the thin skin over the eye. It is common in children and spreads easily. Tell a parent or doctor, wash your hands often, and don't share towels.

The best way to clean gunk is with clean fingers or a damp cloth, wiping gently from the inner corner outwards. Rubbing hard can scratch the eye.

The Sandman's sand

For hundreds of years, European parents told children that a figure called the Sandman sprinkled sand in their eyes to make them sleepy. The gritty crusts in the morning were said to be his leftover sand. The story appears in tales by the German writer E. T. A. Hoffmann and the Danish writer Hans Christian Andersen.

Many animals get eye gunk too. Dogs and cats often have a little at the corners after a nap. And while your eyes tidy themselves overnight, the rest of you is busy too: sleep is when your body repairs itself and your brain files away the day's memories.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Eye gunk is just dried-up tears.

Truth: Tears are mostly water, which evaporates. What stays behind is mostly mucus and oil, mixed with dead skin cells and dust that your eyes cleaned off during the day.

Try this

Sit with a friend and count how many times they blink in one minute while chatting. Then count again while they read a book or watch a screen. Most people blink far less when they concentrate, which is one reason eyes feel dry after a long time on a screen.
