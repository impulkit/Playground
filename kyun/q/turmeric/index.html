Why is turmeric yellow?

Turmeric's yellow can turn bright red in a second, if you add a little soap.

The short answer

Turmeric is yellow because of a chemical called curcumin. Molecules of curcumin soak up blue light from sunlight and bounce back the rest, which our eyes see as a deep golden yellow.

Curcumin is made in the plant's underground stem, a knobbly rhizome that looks like ginger but is bright orange inside. Farmers boil the rhizomes, dry them in the sun for weeks, and grind them into the powder you know. A tiny pinch is enough to colour a whole pot of dal.

Surprising but true

In the 1990s, India won back the right to turmeric's wound-healing use after a US patent office agreed it was ancient Indian knowledge, not a new invention.

A plant from India

Turmeric has been grown in India for thousands of years. The plant hardly ever makes seeds, so farmers plant pieces of rhizome, which sprout into new plants. It needs warmth and plenty of rain, which India has.

India grows about three quarters of the world's turmeric, and uses most of it at home. Erode in Tamil Nadu, Sangli in Maharashtra and Nizamabad in Telangana have huge turmeric markets. Erode turmeric has a GI tag, a label that protects a product linked to one place, the way Darjeeling tea has one.

The colour that changes

Curcumin is an indicator: it changes colour depending on whether it is in acid or alkali. In lemon juice it stays yellow. In soap or baking soda it turns red.

This has a famous use. In many Indian homes, a turmeric stain on a white shirt turns red in the wash. The same chemistry is behind traditional kumkum, the red powder used for a tilak or bindi, which was made by mixing turmeric with lime (the chalky kind, not the fruit). Chemistry teachers around the world use turmeric paper to test for alkalis.

Yellow for celebrations

Yellow is a lucky, happy colour in many Indian traditions. Before a Hindu wedding, friends and family smear turmeric paste on the bride and groom in a haldi ceremony, full of laughter and songs.

Why are Indian weddings so big?Some Indian weddings last five days, feed a thousand guests and need a brass band, a horse and a priest who watches the clock.

Turmeric was also one of India's oldest dyes. It gave cotton and silk a bright yellow, though the colour faded quickly in the sun. Dyers often mixed it with other plant colours to make greens and oranges, a craft you can explore in block printing.

Health claims and a patent fight

Turmeric has long been used in Ayurveda, the traditional Indian system of medicine, and in home remedies, like warm turmeric milk for a cough. In the USA, "golden milk" became a trendy café drink in the 2010s.

In 1995, a US patent was granted for using turmeric to heal wounds. India's Council of Scientific and Industrial Research challenged it, showing old Sanskrit texts and earlier Indian medical writing. In 1997, the US Patent Office cancelled the patent because the use was already known. It became a famous case about protecting traditional knowledge.

Scientists keep studying curcumin, but so far there is little strong proof that it treats any disease in people. Researchers say the body absorbs very little of it. Doctors say it is safe in the amounts used in food, and that anyone who is ill should see a doctor, not rely on spice.

Gold that is everywhere

Look closely and you'll find curcumin in many foods. It is used to colour some mustards, cheeses, butters and packet snacks. On food labels in Europe it appears as E100, the very first number on the list of permitted colours.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Eating lots of turmeric can cure serious diseases.

Truth: There is no good evidence that turmeric cures any illness. Curcumin is poorly absorbed by the body, and most exciting results come from lab dishes, not people. It's a lovely spice, but medicine is a question for a doctor.

Try this

Mix a pinch of turmeric in a little water and paint it on white paper. Let it dry. Then dab on some water mixed with baking soda, or a bit of soapy water, using a cotton bud. Watch the yellow turn red where the paint meets the soap.
