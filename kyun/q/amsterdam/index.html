Why does the Netherlands have so many canals?

About a quarter of the Netherlands lies below sea level, so its people have spent centuries pumping to stay dry.

The short answer

The Netherlands is a flat, low country on the North Sea, where several big rivers, including the Rhine, reach the sea. About a quarter of it lies below sea level, and much more would flood without protection. For centuries, the Dutch have built dykes, which are walls of earth, to keep the sea and rivers out, and dug canals to carry water away.

Canals do several jobs at once. They drain water from fields and streets. They act as roads for boats. In cities like Amsterdam, they also once carried goods right to merchants' front doors. Some canals are old rivers tamed and straightened. Others were dug by hand to turn swamps and lakes into dry land.

Surprising but true

Amsterdam's Royal Palace stands on 13,659 wooden posts driven deep into the soggy ground.

Land made by people

The Dutch have a saying: God created the world, but the Dutch created the Netherlands. Starting in the Middle Ages, they built a dyke around a marsh or a shallow lake, then pumped the water out. The dry land left behind is called a polder.

For centuries the pumping was done by windmills, which turned scoops or screws to lift water into canals at a higher level. Some windmills still stand in rows at Kinderdijk, near Rotterdam. Today electric pumps do the job, but the principle is the same. If the pumps stopped, large parts of the country would slowly fill with water.

Amsterdam's canal ring

Amsterdam began as a fishing village by a dam on the river Amstel, which gave the city its name. In the 1600s, during what the Dutch call their Golden Age, it became one of the richest trading cities in the world. Planners dug a ring of new canals in half-circles around the old centre, with tall merchants' houses along them.

Today Amsterdam has about 100 kilometres of canals and well over a thousand bridges. It is sometimes called the Venice of the North, though Venice was built on islands in a lagoon rather than on drained marsh. In 2010, the canal ring became a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The house where Anne Frank hid during the Second World War stands beside one of these canals, the Prinsengracht.

Houses on stilts

Below Amsterdam lies wet peat and clay, too soft to hold up a heavy building. So builders drove long wooden posts, called piles, down through the mud until they reached a firmer layer of sand. As long as the wood stays under water, with no air, it does not rot. The Royal Palace on Dam Square rests on 13,659 of these piles.

Over time, some piles shift, and that is one reason old canal houses lean at odd angles. Many are also very narrow, with steep stairs, so furniture is hauled up the outside with a hook and rope.

Fighting the sea

On a stormy night in 1953, the North Sea broke through dykes in the south-west of the country. Around 1,800 people died. The Dutch responded with the Delta Works, a giant system of dams, barriers and gates finished over the following decades. Some of the gates stay open so tides can flow, and close only when a storm is coming.

With sea level rise, the Dutch are building higher dykes, but also making "room for the river": fields and parks that are allowed to flood safely. Meanwhile, the flat land is perfect for cycling. The Netherlands has more bicycles than people, and thousands are fished out of Amsterdam's canals every year.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Holland and the Netherlands are the same thing.

Truth: North Holland and South Holland are just two of the twelve provinces of the Netherlands. Amsterdam is in North Holland, which is why people often use "Holland" for the whole country.

Try this

Make a polder. Put a small dish upside down in a large tray and build a ring of play dough around its edge, like a dyke. Pour water into the tray so it rises around the ring. Then use a spoon or a syringe to "pump" any water that leaks into your ring back out over the dyke.
