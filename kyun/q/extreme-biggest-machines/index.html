What are the biggest machines ever built?

A digging machine in Germany weighs as much as about 2,000 elephants and can scoop out enough earth in one day to fill nearly 100 Olympic swimming pools.

The short answer

The biggest machine people have ever built is the Large Hadron Collider, or LHC, a scientific instrument in a 27 km ring under France and Switzerland. The heaviest machine that can move on land is a German coal digger called Bagger 293. It weighs about 14,200 tonnes, roughly as much as 2,000 elephants.

Other giants include the longest ship ever made, the heaviest aeroplane and the slow, mighty vehicles that carry rockets to their launch pads. Each one was built because a job was simply too big for anything smaller.

Surprising but true

The biggest machine of all, the Large Hadron Collider, is a ring 27 km round, buried about 100 m under the border between France and Switzerland.

The monster digger

Bagger 293 is a bucket-wheel excavator. That means it has a giant spinning wheel at the front with 20 buckets around its edge, each big enough to hold a small car. The wheel is more than 21 m across, as tall as a seven-storey building.

The whole machine is about 96 m tall and 225 m long, longer than two football fields. It works in open-pit coal mines in Germany, scraping away earth and coal. In one day it can move about 2,40,000 cubic metres of material. That would fill nearly 100 Olympic swimming pools. Yet it needs only a handful of workers to run it.

It moves on caterpillar tracks at well under 1 km/h. A snail would lose the race, but not by much.

A ring under two countries

The LHC lies about 100 m underground near Geneva. Inside its ring, tiny particles race in opposite directions at nearly the speed of light and smash into each other. Scientists study the pieces.

To steer the particles, the LHC uses more than 1,200 giant magnets, cooled to about −271°C. That is colder than outer space. In 2012, experiments there discovered a long-predicted particle called the Higgs boson.

Giants of the sea

The longest ship ever built was an oil tanker named Seawise Giant, later called Knock Nevis. It was about 458 m long, longer than the Empire State Building is tall. Fully loaded, it was too big to pass through the Suez Canal or the English Channel. It was taken apart for scrap in 2010, at Alang in Gujarat, one of the world's biggest shipbreaking yards.

Today's biggest ships include giant container ships about 400 m long, carrying more than 20,000 containers each. Stacked end to end, those boxes would stretch more than 120 km.

The heaviest plane

The Antonov An-225 Mriya, from Ukraine, was the heaviest aeroplane ever built. It had six engines and a wingspan of about 88 m. It could take off weighing about 640 tonnes, and it once carried a single piece of cargo weighing nearly 190 tonnes.

Only one was ever completed. It was destroyed in 2022 at an airfield near Kyiv during the war in Ukraine.

Moving rockets

NASA's two crawler-transporters were built in the 1960s to carry Saturn V moon rockets. Each weighs about 2,700 tonnes and is about the size of a baseball diamond. They still work today, carrying NASA's newest rockets to the launch pad in Florida.

With a rocket on top, a crawler moves at about 1.6 km/h. The ride to the pad takes hours. Its tracks are made of steel shoes, each weighing about a tonne.

Big machines in India

India builds giants too. Indian Railways' WAG-12B electric locomotives, made at Madhepura in Bihar, are among the most powerful freight engines in the world, at 12,000 horsepower. Each can haul a train of about 6,000 tonnes.

Some of the most surprising giant machines work out of sight, deep underground, chewing through mountains to make the world's longest tunnels.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The biggest machines are always the most powerful ones.

Truth: Size and power are different. The Bagger 293 is huge but crawls at well under 1 km/h. A rocket engine much smaller than a house can produce far more power for a few minutes.

Try this

Feel how gears help big machines. Hold the pedal of a bicycle on its stand and turn it slowly, then watch how fast the back wheel spins. Now count: how many times does the wheel go round for one turn of the pedal? Giant machines use gears in the opposite way, trading speed for huge pushing power.
