How does a phone carry your voice?

Your voice never actually travels down a phone line. What travels is a copy of it, made of electricity or radio waves.

The short answer

A phone does two jobs, one at each end. When you speak, your voice makes the air shake. Inside the phone, a microphone has a thin part that shakes along with it. Those shakes are turned into a changing electrical signal, a pattern that matches your voice.

That signal travels to the other phone, along wires or as radio waves. There, a tiny loudspeaker does the reverse. It uses the signal to shake a thin sheet back and forth, which shakes the air, which reaches your friend's ear as your voice. The whole trip can cross continents in a fraction of a second.

Surprising but true

India's first mobile phone call was made on 31 July 1995, from Kolkata to Delhi.

Turning sound into electricity

Sound is just air being pushed and pulled very quickly. A deep voice shakes the air slowly, a squeaky one shakes it fast. A microphone has a light sheet, called a diaphragm, that moves with these pushes. In many microphones, the moving diaphragm is attached to a coil of wire near a magnet, and moving wire near a magnet makes a small electric current.

The current rises and falls in exactly the same pattern as your voice. The loudspeaker at the other end works the same way backwards: current flows through a coil, the magnet pushes it, and the coil shakes a paper or plastic cone.

"Mr Watson, come here"

On 10 March 1876, in Boston, Alexander Graham Bell spoke into his new device and his assistant Thomas Watson heard him in another room. Bell had received his patent a few days earlier. He was not alone, though. Elisha Gray in the USA and Antonio Meucci, an Italian living in New York, had been working on similar ideas. Arguments over who came first went on for years, and some still go on. You can follow that story in the history of the telephone. Bell himself kept experimenting all his life, with flying machines, hydrofoil boats and even a way to send sound on a beam of light.

How did the telegraph send messages across oceans?In 1858, Queen Victoria sent a 98-word message to the US president through a cable on the floor of the Atlantic. It took about 16 hours to get through.

Wires, exchanges and towers

Early phones were joined by wires to an exchange, where operators plugged cables into a board to connect one caller to another. Within a few years, exchanges were opening around the world, including in Calcutta, Bombay and Madras in the 1880s. Later, machines did the connecting automatically. Landline calls still travelled along copper wires for most of the twentieth century. Today many go as flashes of light through thin glass threads called optical fibres, which can carry thousands of calls at once.

A mobile phone has no wire at all. It turns your voice into numbers, a stream of 0s and 1s, and sends them as radio waves to the nearest tower. The tower passes them into a network of cables, some lying on the ocean floor, and eventually to another tower near your friend.

The first mobile calls

On 3 April 1973, a Motorola engineer named Martin Cooper stood on a street in New York and made the first call from a handheld mobile phone. It weighed about a kilogram, roughly as much as a bag of sugar, and its battery lasted around half an hour of talking. Charging it took about ten hours.

In India, the first mobile call came on 31 July 1995, when the Chief Minister of West Bengal, Jyoti Basu, called the Union Telecom Minister, Sukh Ram, from Kolkata to Delhi. Calls were expensive then. Today India has more than a billion mobile connections, carried by mobile networks that reach high into the Himalaya.

Myth or fact?

Myth: When you talk on the phone, your actual voice travels down the wire.

Truth: Sound can't travel far along a wire. The phone turns your voice into an electrical or radio copy, which zips along almost at the speed of light. The phone at the other end turns that copy back into sound.

Try this

Make a string telephone. Poke a small hole in the bottom of two paper cups with a pencil, thread a long piece of string through, and tie a knot inside each cup. Pull the string tight and take turns talking and listening. Your voice shakes the cup, the cup shakes the string, and the string shakes the other cup.
