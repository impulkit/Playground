What makes Goa different?

Goa became part of India in 1961, fourteen years after independence. Before that, Portugal ruled it for about 450 years, and you can still see it in the churches, houses and food.

The short answer

Goa is India's smallest state, a strip of coast on the Arabian Sea between Maharashtra and Karnataka. It feels different because its history went a different way. While the British ruled most of India, Portugal ruled Goa for about 450 years, from 1510 to 1961. That long rule left whitewashed churches, red-tiled houses, Portuguese words in Konkani, and a cooking style found nowhere else.

Goa has also kept its own older traditions. Hindu temples, Konkani songs, rice fields and fishing villages are as much a part of it as the churches. About 15 lakh people live there, and millions of visitors come each year for its beaches.

Surprising but true

Cashew nuts, now one of Goa's best-known crops, were brought to India from Brazil by the Portuguese.

The Portuguese arrive

In 1510, the Portuguese commander Afonso de Albuquerque captured Goa from the Sultan of Bijapur. Portugal made it the capital of its empire in Asia, controlling a sea trade in spices that stretched from Africa to Japan. Old Goa, on the Mandovi River, grew into a large city of churches and palaces.

Missionaries spread Christianity, and today about a quarter of Goa's people are Christian. The Basilica of Bom Jesus, finished in 1605, holds the remains of St Francis Xavier, a Spanish missionary. It is part of the UNESCO World Heritage Site called the Churches and Convents of Goa.

Freedom came later

When India became independent in 1947, Portugal refused to give up Goa, Daman and Diu. Goans held protests, and many freedom fighters were jailed. In December 1961, the Indian Army moved in, in an operation called Operation Vijay. Portuguese rule ended on 19 December 1961, which Goa now celebrates as Liberation Day. Goa became a full state in 1987.

Food from both sides of the world

Goan food mixes Konkan cooking with Portuguese influence. Fish curry with rice is the everyday meal for many families. Vindaloo comes from a Portuguese dish of meat in wine and garlic, which Goan cooks changed with vinegar and spices. Bebinca is a layered dessert made from coconut milk, eggs and sugar, baked layer by layer.

The Portuguese also brought plants from the Americas, among them cashews, pineapples and chillies. Today Goa's cashew nuts are famous, and the cashew fruit is used to make a drink called feni.

Festivals and music

Goa's calendar is full. Christmas is a huge festival, with stars hung outside homes and midnight Mass. Before Lent, the Carnival fills the streets with floats and music. In spring, Hindu families celebrate Shigmo, with folk dances and colourful parades. Ganesh Chaturthi is one of the biggest festivals of all.

Many Goans are fine musicians, and Goan players were known for bringing Western music and jazz into Bollywood film songs. Fado, a sad style of Portuguese song, is still sung in a few Goan homes, while Konkani songs called mando mix Indian and European tunes. In Fontainhas, an old quarter of Panaji, houses are painted yellow, blue and green, with tiled roofs and wooden balconies. Walking its lanes can feel like being in a small town in Portugal.

Beaches, forests and turtles

Goa's coast is about 105 km long, with dozens of beaches. Inland rise the forested Western Ghats, where Dudhsagar Falls plunges about 310 m. Its name means "sea of milk", from the white foam. On a few quiet northern and southern beaches, olive ridley sea turtles come ashore to lay their eggs in winter, and local groups guard the nests until the babies hatch.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Goa became part of India in 1947, along with the rest of the country.

Truth: In 1947, Goa was still ruled by Portugal, which refused to leave. Indian troops entered in December 1961 and Portuguese rule ended on 19 December, now celebrated as Goa Liberation Day.
