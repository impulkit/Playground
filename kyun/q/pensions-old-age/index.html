How do societies care for old people?

The first person to get a monthly US Social Security cheque, in 1940, was a retired legal secretary named Ida May Fuller. She lived to 100 and collected it for 35 years.

The short answer

For most of history, old people were cared for by their children. They lived in the same house, shared the farm or the shop, and the younger generation took over the work. In much of India, that is still common.

But as people moved to cities and lived longer, many countries added other kinds of support. A pension is a regular payment to someone who has stopped working because of age. It might come from the government, from an employer or from savings built up over a working life. Societies also care for elders through health schemes, special housing, discounts on trains, and laws that protect them.

Surprising but true

In India, a 2007 law makes it a legal duty for adult children to look after their parents if the parents cannot support themselves.

Bismarck again

In 1889, Germany's chancellor Otto von Bismarck set up one of the first national old-age pension systems. Workers and employers paid in, and the state added money. The payments began at 70, at a time when few labourers lived that long.

Other countries followed. In the USA, the Great Depression of the 1930s left millions of older people with no savings and no work. In 1935 President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed the Social Security Act. The first monthly cheque went to Ida May Fuller of Vermont in January 1940, for $22.54. She had paid in only a few years of contributions, but she lived to 100.

What was the Great Depression?In October 1929 share prices in New York crashed. Within three years, about one in four American workers had no job, and the trouble had spread around the globe.

Saving while you work

There are two main ways to fund pensions. In one, today's workers pay into a fund that is used to pay today's retirees. Social Security works mostly like this. In the other, each worker saves for their own old age, and the money grows over time through interest and investments.

India's Employees' Provident Fund, started in 1952, works the second way. Each month, part of a worker's salary, plus a matching amount from the employer, goes into an account. Many government workers once received a guaranteed pension, but since 2004 most new central government staff join a savings-based system instead. Most Indian workers, though, have informal jobs with no employer pension at all. Since 2015, a scheme called Atal Pension Yojana has let them save small monthly amounts towards a fixed pension.

Many families, many changes

Large joint families have long been a safety net in India. Grandparents help raise children, and children care for grandparents as they age. Many families still live this way.

But more young Indians now move to other cities or countries for work. In 2007, Parliament passed a law that allows parents who cannot support themselves to claim maintenance from their adult children. The government also pays small monthly pensions to poor elderly people through the National Social Assistance Programme, started in 1995. Many states top this up.

Getting older together

Around the world, people are living longer and having fewer children. Japan has one of the oldest populations: almost three in ten people there are 65 or older. The country even has a public holiday, Respect for the Aged Day, every September.

India is younger, but that is changing. About one Indian in ten is now 60 or older, and that is expected to double to about one in five by 2050. A growing population of elders means more need for doctors who specialise in old age, homes with ramps and handrails, and pensions that keep pace with rising prices. How a country treats its oldest citizens tells you a lot about it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In India, old people are always looked after by their families, so pensions don't matter much.

Truth: Many elderly Indians live alone or with just a spouse, especially when children move to cities or abroad. Surveys show that many older people, particularly widowed women, have little income of their own, which is why pensions and support schemes matter.

Try this

Sit with a grandparent or an older neighbour and ask how they plan to pay for things now that they work less, or how their own parents managed in old age. Then ask what help they would want from younger people. Write down one thing you could do for them this week.
