Why was the United Nations created?

After two world wars in thirty years, the world's countries agreed to try something new, a place where every nation could talk before it fought.

The short answer

The United Nations, or UN, is an organisation that nearly every country in the world belongs to. It was set up in 1945, at the end of the Second World War. Its job is to help countries settle arguments without fighting, protect human rights and work together on problems like hunger and disease.

Its founders had just lived through two world wars in thirty years. They wanted a permanent place where countries could meet, talk, and act together. Today 193 countries are members. The UN has not ended wars, but it has helped stop some, fed millions of people and vaccinated millions of children.

Surprising but true

Hansa Mehta, an Indian delegate, is credited with changing the Universal Declaration of Human Rights from "all men are born free and equal" to "all human beings are born free and equal".

A second try

After the First World War, countries created the League of Nations to keep the peace. It failed. The USA never joined, and the League could not stop Germany, Italy or Japan from invading other countries.

During the Second World War, the Allies planned something stronger. In 1945, delegates from 50 countries met in San Francisco and signed the UN Charter, the organisation's rulebook. Poland signed soon after, making 51 founding members. India was one of them, two years before it became independent.

The Charter came into force on 24 October 1945. That date is now celebrated as United Nations Day.

How it works

Every member country has a seat and one vote in the General Assembly, whether it is as big as India or as small as Tuvalu. The Assembly meets in New York each September, and world leaders give speeches there.

The Security Council has 15 members and deals with threats to peace. Five of them are permanent: China, France, Russia, the UK and the USA, the main winners of the war in 1945. Each has a veto. India and several other countries argue the Council should change to reflect today's world, and this debate continues.

The Secretary-General runs the UN's day-to-day work, from the headquarters beside the East River in New York.

More than meetings

Much of the UN's work happens through its agencies. UNICEF helps children, the World Health Organization fights disease, and the World Food Programme delivers food in emergencies. UNESCO protects schools, science and culture, and keeps the list of World Heritage Sites, which includes the Taj Mahal and the Statue of Liberty.

In 1989 the UN adopted the Convention on the Rights of the Child, now accepted by almost every country.

India's voice

On 10 December 1948, the UN adopted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, a list of rights belonging to every person. The Indian delegate Hansa Mehta argued that saying "all men" would leave women out, and the wording was changed to "all human beings".

In 1953, Vijaya Lakshmi Pandit of India became the first woman to lead the General Assembly. Over the decades, India has also sent more than two lakh soldiers and police to serve as UN peacekeepers, among the most of any country. Peacekeepers wear blue helmets so they can be recognised.

Does it work?

Critics say the UN is slow, expensive and often blocked by the veto. It failed to prevent terrible events in places such as Rwanda in 1994. Supporters reply that it has helped end several conflicts, wiped out smallpox through the WHO, and given small countries a voice they would not otherwise have.

The UN and its agencies have won the Nobel Peace Prize several times. Outside the headquarters, the flags of all member countries fly in a long line, in alphabetical order.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The United Nations is a world government that can make laws for every country.

Truth: The UN has no power to make laws inside countries. It works mostly through agreement. Only the Security Council can make decisions that members are required to follow, and even those are often hard to enforce.

Try this

Hold a mini UN in your class or family. Give each person a country, pick a problem such as a shared playground, and give everyone one vote. Then try it again where two people have a veto. Talk about which way felt fairer and which got things done.
