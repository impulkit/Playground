What is kalaripayattu?

In Kerala, fighters train in a sunken mud pit with a sword so bendy that it can be worn rolled up like a belt.

The short answer

Kalaripayattu is a martial art from Kerala, on India's south-west coast. It mixes high kicks, leaps, deep crouching stances, weapons and wrestling. The name joins two Malayalam words: kalari, the training ground, and payattu, practice or fighting.

It is very old, though nobody knows quite how old. Legends say the warrior-sage Parashurama taught it. Historians link its present form to the warrior families of medieval Kerala, from around the 12th century or later. Today, children and adults train in it for fitness, discipline and sport, and it has appeared in national games and films.

Surprising but true

The urumi, a kalaripayattu sword, is a strip of springy steel that can be longer than a grown man is tall.

A gym dug into the ground

A traditional kalari is a pit, often about a metre deep and about 13 metres long, a little longer than a city bus. The mud walls block wind and keep the space cool in Kerala's heat. A small shrine sits in one corner, and students touch the ground and the shrine before training.

Fighters usually train in oil-rubbed skin and a simple loincloth or cotton clothes. The oil keeps muscles warm and supple. Some kalaris also give special oil massages, using knowledge of the body that is close to ayurveda.

Moves borrowed from animals

The first stage is meipayattu, which means body exercise. Students learn eight main stances named after animals: the elephant, lion, horse, boar, snake, cat, rooster and fish. Each has its own balance and power. The cat stance is low and springy. The horse stance is stable and grounded.

Then come leaps that turn into kicks above head height, splits and spins. A skilled fighter can jump, twist and land facing the other way. Teachers say a student's body should become "all eyes", sensing danger from every side. That needs lightning-fast reflexes. Traditionally, much training happened in the cool monsoon months, when bodies were thought to be most supple. A session often ends with the student bowing to the teacher, called the gurukkal, and touching the floor of the kalari.

Sticks, daggers and the bendy sword

After years of body training, students move to weapons. They start with wooden sticks of different lengths, then the curved wooden otta, shaped a bit like an elephant's tusk. Later come daggers, spears, swords and shields.

The most famous weapon is the urumi. It is a long, thin band of springy steel, sometimes 1.5 metres or more, and some have several blades. When it isn't in use, it can be coiled around the waist like a belt. Swinging one is dangerous, so only advanced students learn it. Last of all comes verumkai, fighting with bare hands, including ways to stop an armed attacker.

Hard times and a comeback

Under British rule, carrying weapons was restricted, and kalaripayattu faded in many places. A revival started in the 1920s, when teachers in northern Kerala began demonstrating it in public again.

Today there are kalaris across Kerala and beyond. Meenakshi Amma of Vadakara, who began training as a small girl, was still teaching in her seventies when she received the Padma Shri, one of India's top civilian honours, in 2017. In 2021, kalaripayattu was added to the Khelo India Youth Games.

From the battlefield to the stage

Kalaripayattu has shaped other arts. Kathakali dancers have long done kalari-style training to build strong legs and control. Film fight scenes in Malayalam, Tamil and Hindi cinema have borrowed its leaps.

Old ballads from northern Kerala, the Vadakkan Pattukal, sing of kalari heroes, including a woman fighter named Unniyarcha. And the strangest part? India has another ancient sport where athletes twist and balance on top of a wooden pole, and mallakhamb can look even harder than kalari.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Kalaripayattu was carried to China by the monk Bodhidharma and became kung fu.

Truth: This is a popular story, but there is no good historical evidence for it. Chinese martial arts have their own long history, and the Bodhidharma link only appears in much later legends.

Try this

Try the "elephant" stance. Stand with your feet wide apart, bend your knees deeply and keep your back straight, arms forward like a trunk. See how long you can hold it while a friend counts. Do it on a soft mat or grass, and stop if anything hurts.
