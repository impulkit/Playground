How does Pixar make films?

Toy Story, released in 1995, was the first feature film made entirely on computers, and some of its frames took hours each to draw.

The short answer

Pixar makes films by building whole worlds inside computers. Artists first design characters and sets on paper. Then modellers build them as 3D shapes on screen, and animators move them pose by pose, like puppets with thousands of strings. Finally, powerful computers work out how light falls on every surface and turn each moment into an image.

A film has 24 images, or frames, for every second. A 90-minute film needs about 130,000 frames. Each one is calculated by computers, and complex frames can take hours. Before any of that, though, a Pixar film spends years on its story.

Surprising but true

Pixar began as a computer graphics team inside Lucasfilm, the company that made Star Wars, before Steve Jobs bought it in 1986.

Born in a Star Wars company

In the late 1970s, the film-maker George Lucas set up a computer graphics team inside his company, Lucasfilm. In 1986, Steve Jobs, a co-founder of Apple, bought that team, and it became Pixar.

That year, Pixar made a short film called Luxo Jr., about two desk lamps playing with a ball. The little lamp became the company's mascot and still hops across the screen at the start of every Pixar film.

In 1995, Pixar released Toy Story, the first feature film made entirely with computer animation. Disney, which had worked with Pixar on its films, bought the company in 2006.

Story first

Pixar's films start as ideas pitched by its directors. A small team writes and draws the story as thousands of sketches called storyboards. These are filmed in order with rough voices, making a crude version of the film called a story reel.

Directors then show these reels to a group of the studio's senior film-makers, who give honest notes on what isn't working. Pixar's co-founder Ed Catmull called this group the Braintrust. Films are often rebuilt many times. Inside Out, about the emotions inside a girl's head, went through years of changes before its release in 2015.

Building the world

Once the story works, modellers build every character and object as a 3D shape made of thousands of tiny flat surfaces. Riggers add hidden "bones" and controls, so animators can bend an elbow or lift an eyebrow.

Animators then pose the characters for each shot. Studying real movement is part of the job. For Finding Nemo, the team learned how fish swim and how light ripples under water.

Other teams handle things that are hard to animate by hand, like hair, cloth, water and crowds. In Monsters, Inc. (2001), the furry monster Sulley had more than 2 million individual hairs, each moving on its own.

Turning maths into pictures

The final step is rendering. The computer works out the colour of every pixel in every frame, based on the shapes, the materials and where the lights are. A single frame can take hours.

What is a pixel?Every photo on your phone is really a grid of tiny coloured squares. Zoom in far enough and you can count them.

To get it done, Pixar uses a "render farm" of thousands of computers working together. Pixar also wrote its own software, called RenderMan, which many other studios use for visual effects.

Stories from everywhere

Pixar's films travel widely, from a Mexican boy who loves music in Coco to a Chinese-Canadian girl in Turning Red. In 2015, Pixar released Sanjay's Super Team, a short film by the animator Sanjay Patel. It was based on his own childhood: a boy bored by his father's prayers who imagines Hindu gods as superheroes. It was nominated for an Oscar.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In computer animation, the computer does all the work.

Truth: Computers do the heavy maths of drawing and lighting each frame, but people design every character, write the story, and decide every movement. A Pixar film takes hundreds of artists and engineers several years.

Try this

Make a flipbook. On the corner of 20 pages of an old notebook, draw a ball, moving it a little lower on each page, and squash it slightly when it hits the "ground". Flip the pages fast. Pixar animators practise with exercises like this bouncing ball.
