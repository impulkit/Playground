Who was Satyajit Ray?

He made his first film on weekends with borrowed money and a crew who had never made a film. It changed how the world saw Indian cinema.

The short answer

Satyajit Ray was a film director, writer and artist from Kolkata. His first film, Pather Panchali (1955), told the story of a poor village family through the eyes of a small boy named Apu. It won a prize at the Cannes Film Festival in 1956 and made many people around the world take notice of Indian films.

Ray went on to make about 30 feature films, many of them in Bengali. He wrote the scripts, drew the posters and, later, composed the music himself. Children in Bengal also know him as the author of the Feluda detective stories.

Surprising but true

Satyajit Ray received his honorary Oscar in 1992 lying in a Kolkata hospital bed. The award was presented to him by video, and he died less than a month later.

A family of storytellers

Ray grew up surrounded by books and pictures. His grandfather, Upendrakishore Ray Chowdhury, started a children's magazine called Sandesh in 1913. His father, Sukumar Ray, wrote funny nonsense poems that Bengali children still recite. Sukumar died when Satyajit was only two.

As a young man, Satyajit studied art at Santiniketan, the university founded by Rabindranath Tagore. Then he worked in an advertising company in Calcutta, designing book covers. One of the books he illustrated was a children's version of Pather Panchali. He began to picture it as a film.

The film made on weekends

In 1950, on a work trip to London, Ray watched about a hundred films in a few months. One Italian film, Bicycle Thieves, showed him that a great movie could be made with real streets and actors who were not stars.

Back home, he started filming Pather Panchali with a camera operator who had never shot a film and many actors who had never been in a film. He kept his day job and filmed at weekends. The money ran out, and shooting stopped for many months, until the state government stepped in. Ray began the famous scene of Apu and his sister running through white kash flowers to see a train. When he came back to finish it, cattle had eaten the flowers, so he waited a year for them to bloom again.

Apu, Feluda and Shonku

Pather Panchali became the first part of the Apu Trilogy, followed by Aparajito (1956) and Apur Sansar (1959). The trilogy follows Apu from childhood to adulthood. Many film schools still use it to teach how to tell a story with pictures.

In 1961 Ray revived his grandfather's Sandesh magazine. To fill its pages, he invented Feluda, a sharp-eyed detective from Kolkata, and Professor Shonku, a scientist whose inventions take him across the world. He also made two Feluda films. Praise and criticism

The Japanese director Akira Kurosawa said that not to have seen Ray's films was like never having seen the Sun or the Moon. Hollywood directors, including Martin Scorsese, have named him as an influence.

Not everyone agreed. Some Indian critics found his films slow. In 1980, the actor and politician Nargis Dutt complained that Ray's films showed India's poverty to foreign audiences. Ray's admirers answered that his films showed ordinary people with dignity, and that honesty was not an insult.

The last award

In 1992 the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences gave Ray an honorary Oscar for his life's work. He was too ill to travel, so the award was filmed at his bedside in Kolkata. The same year, India gave him the Bharat Ratna, its highest civilian honour. He died on 23 April 1992, and his films are still restored and shown around the world.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Satyajit Ray's films are only about poverty.

Truth: Ray made films about villages, cities, royal families, scientists and ghosts. He also made musical fantasies for children, such as Goopy Gyne Bagha Byne (1969), and detective films.
