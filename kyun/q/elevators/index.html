How does a lift go up and down?

A lift is really a giant see-saw. A heavy block on the other end of the cables does most of the work.

The short answer

Most lifts in tall buildings hang from strong steel cables. The cables run up to the top of the lift shaft, loop over a big grooved wheel, and come down the other side to a heavy block called a counterweight. An electric motor turns the wheel. Turn it one way and the car rises while the counterweight sinks. Turn it the other way and the car goes down.

The counterweight weighs about as much as the car plus roughly half a full load of passengers. So the motor never has to lift the whole car on its own. It only works against the difference, like pushing one end of a balanced see-saw.

Shorter buildings often use a different kind of lift, pushed up from below by a piston filled with oil. These are called hydraulic lifts.

Surprising but true

In 1854, Elisha Otis stood on a raised platform in New York and ordered the only rope holding it to be cut. His new safety brake caught it, and people began to trust lifts.

The man who cut the rope

People had used platforms on ropes for centuries, to lift goods in mines and warehouses. But nobody liked riding them. If the rope broke, the platform crashed down.

In 1852, an American mechanic named Elisha Otis invented a safety brake. In 1854, at an exhibition in New York, he rode a platform high above the crowd and had the rope cut. The platform dropped only a short way before metal teeth sprang out and locked into the guide rails on either side. Otis is said to have called out, "All safe, gentlemen." The first passenger lift with his brake opened in a New York shop in 1857.

How lifts made cities taller

Before lifts, few people wanted to climb more than five or six floors, so buildings stayed short. The top floors were often the cheapest, because nobody wanted to walk that far. Safe lifts flipped that. Suddenly the top floors, with the best views and the cleanest air, became the most expensive.

Steel frames and lifts arrived around the same time, and together they made the first skyscrapers possible. Today the Burj Khalifa in Dubai has dozens of lifts, and some of its cars travel about 500 metres in a single trip.

Why your stomach feels funny

When a lift starts going up, the floor pushes on you a little harder than usual. For a moment, you feel heavier. When it slows down near the top, you feel lighter. Going down, it's the other way round. While the lift moves at a steady speed, you feel completely normal.

This is the same gravity and motion that astronauts feel when a rocket launches. The fastest lifts in the world, in towers in China, travel at about 20 metres a second. That is over 70 km/h, faster than a car on most city roads. They speed up gently and are pressurised so passengers' ears don't hurt too much.

Clever lifts

Modern lifts are run by computers. In some tall offices, you tap your floor on a screen in the lobby and it tells you which lift to take. The computer groups people going to nearby floors, so lifts make fewer stops.

Some lifts also save energy. When a heavy car comes down, or a light one goes up, the motor can work as a generator and send electricity back into the building. Very tall towers sometimes use two cars stacked on top of each other in one shaft, stopping at two floors at once.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If a lift's cable snaps, the lift falls all the way to the bottom.

Truth: Most lifts hang from several steel cables, and each one alone could hold the car. If the car ever moves too fast, a governor sets off safety brakes that grip the rails. Lifts that fall freely are extremely rare.

Try this

Make a model lift. Tie a paper cup to one end of a long string and a small bag of coins to the other. Loop the string over a broom handle resting between two chairs. Put a toy in the cup and add or remove coins until the cup rises with just a gentle tug. You've just found a counterweight.
