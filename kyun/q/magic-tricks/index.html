How do magicians fool us?

In one famous experiment, about half the people watching a short video failed to notice a person in a gorilla suit walking right through the middle of it.

The short answer

Magicians fool us by playing tricks on our attention and memory. Your brain cannot take in everything your eyes see, so it picks out what seems important and ignores the rest. A good magician decides what you will think is important.

The key skill is misdirection, guiding your attention away from the secret move. Magicians also use sleight of hand, fast and smooth finger skills practised for years, and hidden props such as false bottoms and secret pockets. Your brain fills in the gaps, and the trick seems impossible.

Surprising but true

The famous Indian rope trick, in which a boy climbs a rope into the sky, was most likely spread by a made-up newspaper story in 1890. No one has ever performed it as described.

The gorilla in the room

In 1999, psychologists Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris at Harvard showed people a short video of students passing basketballs. Viewers had to count the passes. Halfway through, a person in a gorilla suit walked into the middle, thumped their chest and walked out.

About half the viewers did not see the gorilla at all. Scientists call this inattentional blindness. When we focus hard on one task, we can miss something obvious. Magicians use the same weakness, giving you something to watch while the real work happens elsewhere.

How do your eyes see?The picture at the back of your eye is upside down. Your brain flips it the right way up without you ever noticing.

Where you look

Magicians know where your eyes will go. People look where the magician looks, so a performer can glance at one hand while the other does the secret move. A sudden noise, a joke or a question also grabs attention for a moment.

Researchers have found that a hand moving in a smooth curve draws the eyes better than a hand moving in a straight line. Magicians discovered this long before scientists did, simply by practising in front of audiences.

Tricks of memory

Some tricks work after they are over. Magicians describe what happened in a slightly wrong way, and people remember the description instead of what they saw. A shuffled deck is called "your deck", and a card the magician actually chose becomes "the card you picked".

Our memory is not a recording. It is rebuilt every time we remember, and small suggestions can change it. Many people leave a show sure they saw something that never happened.

Famous magicians

The Frenchman Jean-Eugène Robert-Houdin, who lived in the 1800s, is often called the father of modern magic. He wore ordinary evening clothes instead of wizard robes and performed in a theatre like a gentleman scientist. The American escape artist Harry Houdini took his stage name from him.

In India, P. C. Sorcar became world famous in the mid-1900s for grand stage shows, including making a person appear to be sawn in half. Street magicians across India have long performed tricks with cups and balls, a routine so old that versions of it appear in writings from ancient Rome.

The rope trick that never was

For more than a century, people around the world have heard of the Indian rope trick. A magician throws a rope into the air, it stays standing, and a boy climbs up and vanishes. The historian Peter Lamont traced the story and found it took off after an 1890 article in the Chicago Tribune.

The newspaper later admitted the article was invented. Since then, many people have offered prizes for anyone who could perform the trick in the open air. No one has claimed them. The rope trick shows how a good story can fool people as well as any magician.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Magicians in India once performed the rope trick, making a boy climb a rope into thin air and vanish.

Truth: Historians have found no reliable eyewitness accounts. The story spread widely after an 1890 article in the Chicago Tribune, which the newspaper later admitted was a hoax.

Try this

Learn the classic French drop with a coin. Hold the coin in one hand, pretend to take it with the other, but let it drop secretly into your palm. Then watch the empty hand closely yourself as you "open" it. Practise in a mirror until your eyes follow the wrong hand too.
