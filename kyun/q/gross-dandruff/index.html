What is dandruff?

Living on almost everyone's scalp is a tiny yeast that eats the oil from your hair. In some people, its leftovers make the skin flake off like snow.

The short answer

Dandruff is flakes of dead skin falling from the scalp. Everyone sheds skin, all over the body, every day. Usually the bits are too tiny to notice. With dandruff, the scalp sheds faster, and the dead cells clump into white or yellowish flakes that land on shoulders and collars.

The main cause is a yeast called Malassezia, a microscopic fungus that lives on nearly everyone's skin. It feeds on the oil in hair follicles. As it eats, it leaves behind substances that irritate some people's skin. Their scalp responds by making new skin cells too fast, and flakes pile up.

Surprising but true

Young children rarely get dandruff. It usually starts around puberty, when the scalp begins making much more oil for the yeast to feed on.

A yeast that likes oil

Malassezia is a distant relative of the yeast used in making bread. It is so small that thousands could sit on the head of a pin. It has lived on human skin for so long that it can no longer make some of the fats it needs. It has to get them from us.

So it heads for places with plenty of oil: the scalp, the face, the chest and behind the ears. It breaks down the oil into smaller pieces. One of them, oleic acid, can irritate the skin.

In most people, the scalp doesn't mind. In people with dandruff, it reacts with mild redness and itching, and the skin renews in a rush.

Skin on fast-forward

Your skin is always replacing itself. New cells form at the bottom, move up over about a month, then die and drop off. You shed tens of thousands of cells every minute. Some of the dust in your home is old skin.

When the scalp is irritated, it speeds this up a lot. Cells reach the surface before they have dried out and separated properly. Instead of falling as fine powder, they stick together with oil into visible flakes.

Why it starts around puberty

Young children rarely have dandruff, because their scalps make little oil. During puberty, hormones switch on the oil glands. The yeast suddenly has a feast, and dandruff often appears for the first time.

Babies sometimes get a crusty, yellowish scalp called cradle cap, which is linked to the same yeast. It is harmless and usually clears up on its own.

Things that make it worse

Dandruff often gets worse in cold, dry winters and better in summer sunshine. Stress, tiredness and illness can make it flare up. Not washing hair for a long time lets oil and flakes build up, though washing more doesn't always cure it.

Some people notice more flakes after using hair gels or sprays, which can leave a crust on the scalp that looks similar.

It is not contagious. You can't catch dandruff by sharing a comb or sitting next to someone, because almost everyone already carries the yeast.

How people treat it

Special anti-dandruff shampoos contain ingredients that slow down the yeast or calm the skin. Common ones are zinc pyrithione, ketoconazole and selenium sulfide. They usually work best if left on the scalp for a few minutes before rinsing.

In India, many families also use traditional remedies such as coconut oil, neem or curd. Some people find them soothing, but there is less scientific evidence that they work. If the scalp gets very red, painful or itchy, or flakes spread to the face, it is worth talking to a parent or a doctor.

Flakes everywhere

Dandruff is only one way your body loses old skin. Over a year, those tiny flakes add up to a surprising pile. Much of it ends up in household dust. There it becomes dinner for tiny creatures you never see: the dust mites living in your pillow.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Dandruff happens because you don't wash your hair, or because your scalp is too dry.

Truth: Dandruff is mainly caused by a yeast that feeds on scalp oil, not by being dirty. Washing helps keep flakes under control, but clean people get dandruff too. A dry, flaky scalp in winter is a different, milder problem.

Try this

See how yeast loves food. With a grown-up, put a spoon of dried yeast in two cups of warm water, adding a spoon of sugar to one cup only. After 15 minutes, compare the foam. The scalp yeast that causes dandruff works the same way, but its favourite food is the oil from your skin.
