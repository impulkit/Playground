What are feelings, and where do they come from?

A feeling is your brain and body teaming up in a split second to tell you whether something matters, and what to do about it.

The short answer

A feeling, or emotion, is your brain's quick judgement about what is happening and what it means for you. When something matters, your brain sends signals through your body. Your heart may race, your face may change, your stomach may flutter. At the same time, you notice an inner sense of fear, joy, anger or sadness.

Feelings help you act quickly. Fear gets you ready to escape. Disgust keeps you away from rotten food. Joy makes you want to do good things again. Sadness can slow you down and bring others to comfort you. Scientists still argue about exactly how the brain makes feelings, but they agree that emotions involve the brain and body working together.

Surprising but true

Roughly 2,000 years ago, the Indian text called the Natya Shastra listed eight main emotions for actors to show. Dancers still train in them today as the navarasas, the nine flavours of feeling.

Inside the brain

There's no single feelings centre in the brain. Many parts work together. Deep inside sit two almond-shaped bundles called the amygdala. They help spot danger very quickly, sometimes before you're even aware of it. That's why you can jump at a rope on the ground before you realise it isn't a snake.

The front of the brain, behind your forehead, helps you understand your feelings and decide what to do with them. In children and teenagers, this part is still growing. Strong feelings can arrive quickly, while the ability to calm them develops over years.

Faces around the world

In 1872, Charles Darwin wrote a book about how people and animals show emotions. He noticed that a frightened dog and a frightened person both widen their eyes, and he wondered whether expressions were inborn.

In the 1960s, American psychologist Paul Ekman travelled to Papua New Guinea to test this. He suggested six basic emotions found everywhere: happiness, sadness, fear, anger, surprise and disgust. Other scientists, such as Lisa Feldman Barrett, argue that emotions are more flexible. They say the brain builds each feeling from body signals and past experience, and that cultures label and show emotions differently. Both sides have evidence, and the debate is very much alive.

The flavours of feeling

People thought hard about emotions long before modern science. The Natya Shastra, an ancient Indian text on drama usually credited to the sage Bharata, describes rasas, the flavours of feeling an audience tastes during a performance. It listed eight, and later writers added a ninth, peace. Together they're called the navarasa.

Dancers in Indian classical dance forms like Bharatanatyam and Kathakali still train to show each one with the eyes, eyebrows and mouth. In 2015, the Pixar film Inside Out turned five emotions into cartoon characters. Paul Ekman and another scientist, Dacher Keltner, advised the animation studio on the science.

All feelings have a job

It's easy to think some feelings are good and others are bad. But every emotion does something useful. Anger can tell you something is unfair. Fear keeps you careful near traffic. Sadness shows you what you care about. Feelings become a problem mainly when they're very strong for a long time, or when they push us to hurt ourselves or others.

Putting a feeling into words can help it settle. Talking to a friend, a parent or a teacher about how you feel isn't a sign of weakness. It's one of the best ways people have found to understand their own minds.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Feelings come from the heart.

Truth: Your heart speeds up or slows down with your feelings, which is why people have long linked the two. But emotions are created by the brain, working closely with signals from the whole body, including the heart and stomach.

Try this

Keep a feelings weather report for a week. Three times a day, write one word for how you feel and where you notice it in your body, such as a tight tummy or warm cheeks. Try to find new words beyond happy and sad, like proud, nervous, lonely or calm. At the end of the week, look for patterns.
