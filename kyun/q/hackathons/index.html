What is a hackathon?

At a hackathon, teams get a day or two to build a working app from nothing, often fuelled by pizza, samosas and very little sleep.

The short answer

A hackathon is an event where people team up to build something new with technology in a short time, usually 24 to 48 hours. The name joins "hack", which programmers use to mean a clever, quick piece of work, with "marathon".

Teams pick a problem, sketch an idea, write code and show a working demo at the end. Judges choose winners, but many people go just to learn, make friends and see what they can build when the clock is ticking.

Surprising but true

Despite the name, hackathons have nothing to do with breaking into computers. "Hack" here means building something clever, fast.

How a hackathon works

Most hackathons start with a theme. It might be healthier cities, cleaner rivers or helping older people use phones. Teams of two to five people then brainstorm, split the work and start building.

One person might design the screens. Another writes the code that makes buttons work. Someone else prepares the presentation. Organisers provide desks, Wi-Fi, power sockets, food and mentors who help when teams get stuck. At the end, each team gets a few minutes to show judges what they made.

Where the word came from

The word appeared in 1999. That year, a group of programmers in Canada met to work on free software called OpenBSD, and the company Sun Microsystems ran a similar coding challenge in the United States. Both used the word.

Programmers had been doing late-night building sessions for years. But hackathons really spread in the 2000s and 2010s, as companies, universities and even governments started running them. Several big tech companies hold in-house hackathons, and some features of well-known apps began as weekend experiments.

Hackathons in India and beyond

India runs one of the world's biggest. The Smart India Hackathon, organised by the central government since 2017, asks college students to solve problems set by ministries and companies, such as tracking water use or making railway services smoother. Hundreds of thousands of students have taken part over the years.

NASA runs the Space Apps Challenge every year, with events in cities on every inhabited continent. In its first year, 2012, it even had a location in Antarctica. Teams use real NASA data to build tools, such as maps of wildfires or apps that show where the International Space Station is. Students in Bengaluru, Pune, Texas and Kenya might be working on the same challenge on the same weekend.

Not just for coders

You don't need to be an expert programmer to join. Teams need people who can draw, explain ideas, test apps and think about who will use them. Some hackathons are for school students and use friendly block-coding tools like Scratch, where you snap commands together like puzzle pieces. A ten-year-old can build a simple game in an afternoon that way, and many school hackathons give prizes for the best idea, not the fanciest code.

Others focus on hardware, where teams build gadgets with small computer boards, sensors and motors. Some are about art, music or games. Many teams reuse free code from open-source projects, so they don't have to start every piece from scratch.

What happens after

Most hackathon projects are rough demos that never go further. That's fine, because the point is to try ideas fast and learn. But some do grow into real products, and a few teams have gone on to start companies, which is how many startups begin.

Long events can mean staying up all night. Good organisers now set up quiet rooms and remind people to rest, because tired brains make more mistakes. Scientists who study sleep say it's when your brain sorts and stores what it learned that day, which matters even when you're racing a deadline. Making an app is easier with a clear head.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Hackathons are contests for people who break into computer systems.

Truth: Hackathons are about making things, such as apps, websites or gadgets. Some security events do test systems, but only with permission and under strict rules.

Try this

Run a mini "paper hackathon" with friends. Pick a small problem, such as forgetting your water bottle, and give yourselves 30 minutes to design an app or gadget that solves it. Draw the screens on paper and present your idea in one minute.
