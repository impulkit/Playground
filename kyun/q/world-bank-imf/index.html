What do the World Bank and IMF do?

In 1991, India was so short of foreign money that it flew tonnes of its gold to London as security for loans. The IMF helped it through the crisis.

The short answer

The World Bank and the International Monetary Fund, or IMF, are two global institutions set up after the Second World War. They are owned by their member countries, about 190 of them, including India and the USA.

The World Bank lends money and gives advice to developing countries for long-term projects, such as roads, dams, schools, hospitals and clean water. The IMF has a different job. It watches over the world's money system and lends to countries that run into a sudden crisis, for example when they can't pay for imports or repay foreign debts. You could think of the World Bank as a builder and the IMF as a firefighter.

Surprising but true

The World Bank's very first loan, in 1947, went to France, to help rebuild after the Second World War.

Born in a hotel in the mountains

In July 1944, while the Second World War was still raging, delegates from 44 countries met at a hotel in Bretton Woods, in the mountains of New Hampshire, USA. India sent a delegation too, though it was still under British rule.

They wanted to avoid the mistakes of the 1930s, when countries had raised trade barriers and let their currencies crash, deepening the Great Depression. The British economist John Maynard Keynes and the American official Harry Dexter White led the planning. Out of three weeks of talks came both the IMF and the bank that grew into the World Bank Group.

What was the Second World War?The largest war in history involved more than 100 million soldiers, and it was fought on land, at sea, in the air and in secret code rooms.

The builder

The World Bank's first loan, in 1947, went to France for rebuilding after the war. Soon it turned to poorer countries. In 1949, India received its first World Bank loan, for its railways. Since then, India has been one of the bank's biggest borrowers, for projects from power plants to rural roads and school programmes.

The bank raises money by selling bonds and from its member countries. Its poorest borrowers get loans at very low or no interest, with decades to repay, and sometimes grants that don't need paying back.

The firefighter, and India in 1991

By 1991, India was in deep trouble. Oil prices had jumped, and it had foreign money left for only a few weeks of imports. To borrow, the government sent tonnes of the country's gold to the Bank of England as security.

India also borrowed from the IMF. Some of the changes that followed were conditions of the loans, and many were ones the new government wanted anyway. The government cut many controls on business, lowered taxes on imports and opened up to foreign companies. These reforms, led by Finance Minister Manmohan Singh, helped India's economy grow much faster over the following decades.

Why they are debated

IMF loans usually come with conditions, such as cutting government spending, raising taxes or selling state companies. Supporters say these steps fix the problems that caused the crisis. Critics argue that, in some countries, they led to job losses and cuts to health and education, hurting the poorest people.

There are also arguments about power. Votes in both institutions depend largely on how much money a country puts in, so the USA and Europe have the most say. By tradition, an American leads the World Bank and a European leads the IMF. Many developing countries, including India, want a bigger voice. Over the years, both bodies have shifted voting shares slightly. They now also talk more about reducing poverty and tackling climate change, alongside their old worries about debt and inflation.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The World Bank is a bank where ordinary people can open an account.

Truth: The World Bank lends to governments, not to individuals. It raises money from member countries and by selling bonds, then lends it for things like roads, schools, clean water and power.
