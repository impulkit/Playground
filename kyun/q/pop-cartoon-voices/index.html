Who makes cartoon voices?

One man gave voices to Bugs Bunny, Daffy Duck, Porky Pig and Tweety, and his gravestone reads "That's All Folks".

The short answer

Cartoon voices are made by voice actors, people trained to bring characters to life using only their voices. They stand in soundproof rooms in front of a microphone and perform lines again and again until the director is happy.

Many voice actors play several characters in the same show. Some keep a role for decades. A good one can sound like a squeaky mouse in one moment and a booming dragon the next, without any computer tricks. They have to act with everything except their face and body, since nobody will see them.

Surprising but true

Pikachu is voiced by the same Japanese actor, Ikue Ōtani, in almost every language, because the character only ever says its own name.

The man of a thousand voices

The most famous voice actor of all may be Mel Blanc. Starting in the 1930s, he voiced Bugs Bunny, Daffy Duck, Porky Pig, Tweety, Sylvester and many more for the Looney Tunes cartoons. He was nicknamed "The Man of a Thousand Voices".

For Porky Pig's stutter and Daffy's spluttering lisp, Blanc built each voice from small habits of the mouth and throat. When he died in 1989, his family had his gravestone carved with Porky Pig's famous last line, "That's All Folks".

Even Mickey Mouse began this way. In 1928, Walt Disney himself squeaked Mickey's first words, and he kept the role for almost 20 years.

Inside the recording booth

In most American animation, actors record first. Animators then listen and draw mouths, eyes and gestures to fit. Voice actors often make faces and wave their arms in the booth, because it changes how they sound. Some films set up cameras to film them, so animators can borrow their expressions.

Actors usually record alone, reading only their own lines. They may not hear the other characters until the film comes out. Japanese anime often flips the order. Actors record together, watching the scenes play on a screen and fitting their words to mouths that are already drawn.

How does your voice work?Every word you speak starts as two small folds of tissue in your throat, slapping together hundreds of times a second.

Bending one throat into many characters

Every voice starts in the larynx, the voice box in your throat, where two small folds vibrate. Voice actors change the speed of those vibrations to go higher or lower. They also shape the sound with the tongue, lips and nose. Squeezing sound through the nose makes a whiny voice. Opening the throat wide makes a deep one.

Long sessions can strain the voice, especially for screaming monsters or gravelly villains. So actors warm up like singers, drink lots of water and save the loudest scenes for the end of the day. A cartoon series can run for hundreds of episodes, so an actor must be able to find exactly the same voice years later.

Voices that cross languages

When cartoons travel to India, a new team of actors records the characters in Hindi, Tamil, Telugu and other languages. This is dubbing. The new actors must match the original mouth movements and timing, and often add local jokes. For the 2019 Hindi version of The Lion King, film star Shah Rukh Khan voiced Mufasa, and his son Aryan voiced the grown-up Simba.

Some voices never change. Pikachu says only its own name, in different tones and moods, so the Japanese actor Ikue Ōtani's voice is used around the world. Homer Simpson has sounded the same since 1987, because Dan Castellaneta has voiced him the whole time, along with dozens of other characters in the same show.

Indian studios now make their own hits, with Indian actors voicing heroes like Chhota Bheem from the very start. And voices are only half the soundtrack. The crunch of a punch in a cartoon is often made by someone snapping celery or slapping a wet towel. That is a whole hidden job, explored in sound effects.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Cartoon voices are made by speeding up or changing normal voices with a computer.

Truth: A few are, but most are real actors using their own throats, mouths and breath to create each voice. Many can switch between dozens of characters in a single session.

Try this

Choose one simple line, like "Where did I leave my sandwich?" Record yourself saying it on a phone as a tiny mouse, a grumpy giant, a robot, a nervous duck and a royal queen. Play them back and notice how you changed your pitch, speed and mouth shape.
