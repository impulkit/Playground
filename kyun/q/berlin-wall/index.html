Why was the Berlin Wall built and torn down?

One August night in 1961, Berliners woke up to find their city cut in half by barbed wire. It stayed split for 28 years.

The short answer

The Berlin Wall was a guarded concrete barrier that cut the German city of Berlin in two from 1961 to 1989. East Germany's communist government built it to stop its own people escaping to the West. It became the best-known symbol of the Cold War.

After the Second World War, Germany and its capital Berlin were divided between the winners. The eastern part came under Soviet control and became communist East Germany. Millions of East Germans fled west for freedom and better jobs, many of them through Berlin. The wall stopped them. In 1989, after huge peaceful protests, the gates opened, and crowds began chipping it apart. A year later, Germany became one country again.

Surprising but true

The Berlin Wall opened in 1989 partly because an official announced new travel rules by mistake, saying at a press conference that they took effect "immediately".

A city cut into four

In 1945, the Allies split defeated Germany into four zones, run by the USA, Britain, France and the Soviet Union. Berlin, inside the Soviet zone, was also split four ways.

In 1949 the three western zones became West Germany, and the Soviet zone became East Germany. West Berlin stayed linked to the West, like an island surrounded by East German land. When the Soviet Union blocked roads to it in 1948, American and British planes flew in food and coal for almost a year, landing every few minutes at the busiest times.

Building the wall

East Germany was losing its people fast. Between 1949 and 1961, around 25 lakh East Germans left, many of them young doctors, engineers and teachers. Crossing in Berlin was as easy as taking the train.

In the early hours of 13 August 1961, soldiers rolled out barbed wire along the border through the city. Families were split overnight. Over the following years, the wire became a concrete wall, then two walls with a wide "death strip" between them, full of guard towers, lights and patrols.

Life beside the wall

Some streets ran right along the wall. In the first days, people jumped from windows of buildings on the border into the West, until the windows were bricked up.

People kept trying to escape, by tunnelling, hiding in cars and even, in the countryside, flying a balloon. Around 5,000 people escaped across the Berlin Wall. At least 140 people died trying, many shot by guards.

US presidents went to the wall to make speeches. In 1987 Ronald Reagan called out: "Mr. Gorbachev, tear down this wall."

The night it opened

By 1989, the Soviet Union under Mikhail Gorbachev was no longer willing to use force to keep eastern Europe in line. In East Germany, hundreds of thousands of people marched peacefully each week, demanding change.

On the evening of 9 November, a government spokesman, Günter Schabowski, read out new rules making travel easier. Asked when they started, he looked at his notes and said, "immediately, without delay". In fact they were meant to start the next day. Thousands of East Berliners rushed to the crossings. Guards, with no orders, finally opened the gates. People climbed onto the wall and danced.

What's left

Germany was reunited on 3 October 1990, and most of the wall was knocked down. A memorial at Bernauer Strasse keeps a section with its death strip, to show what it was like.

The longest surviving stretch, about 1.3 km, is the East Side Gallery. After 1990, artists from many countries covered it with murals, turning a symbol of division into a giant outdoor canvas of street art.

Is graffiti art?People have been scribbling on walls for thousands of years. In the ruins of Pompeii, you can still read jokes, election slogans and names scratched nearly 2,000 years ago.

Pieces of the wall now stand in many countries, from museums in the USA to cities across Asia and Europe. They are reminders of how a city once lived with a border running down its streets.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Berlin Wall ran between East Germany and West Germany.

Truth: Berlin lay deep inside East Germany, about 160 km from the western border. The Wall surrounded West Berlin, an island of the West, cutting it off from East Berlin and the countryside around it.
