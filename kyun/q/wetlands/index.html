Why are wetlands important?

In Kolkata, a marsh quietly cleans a big share of a giant city's dirty water, and grows fish while it does it.

The short answer

A wetland is land that is soaked with water for at least part of the year. Marshes, swamps, bogs, mangrove forests, the edges of lakes and the flat land beside rivers all count.

Wetlands work like a giant sponge and a giant filter at the same time. They soak up floodwater and release it slowly. Their plants and mud trap dirt and break down pollution, so water leaves them cleaner than it came in. They are also nurseries: young fish, frogs, insects and birds grow up in their shallow, food-rich water. Freshwater wetlands cover only a small slice of the planet, yet a big share of all animal species depend on them.

Surprising but true

Peat bogs cover only about 3% of the world's land, yet they hold roughly twice as much carbon as all the world's forests put together.

A city's secret kidney

On the eastern edge of Kolkata lies a maze of ponds, fields and channels called the East Kolkata Wetlands. Every day, a large share of the city's sewage flows into them. Sunlight, bacteria and tiny algae break the waste down in the shallow ponds. Fish farmers then raise fish in the cleaner water, and vegetables grow on its banks.

Local people worked out this system over many decades, long before engineers studied it. In 2002 the wetlands were named a Ramsar site, a wetland of international importance. Building a treatment plant to do the same job would cost a fortune. The marsh does it with sunshine, patience and fish. Some of that fish ends up in the city's markets, so in a sense Kolkata eats what its wetlands have cleaned.

Rest stops for travelling birds

Each winter, birds fly thousands of kilometres from Central Asia, Siberia and beyond to India's lakes and marshes. Chilika Lake in Odisha, a huge salty lagoon by the sea, can host around a million of them in a good year. Flocks of flamingos, ducks and waders feed in its shallows, and rare Irrawaddy dolphins swim in its channels. Fishing families in hundreds of villages around the lagoon depend on its prawns, crabs and fish.

Keoladeo National Park in Bharatpur, Rajasthan, began as a duck-hunting reserve for a maharaja. Today it is a protected bird sanctuary and a UNESCO World Heritage Site. It was once famous for Siberian cranes, but the last ones were seen there around 2002, after their numbers crashed along their long route.

River of grass

In Florida, USA, the Everglades spread across a vast, slow-moving sheet of shallow water and sawgrass. In the early 1900s people dug canals to drain much of it for farms and towns. In 1947 the writer Marjory Stoneman Douglas published The Everglades: River of Grass and helped persuade Americans that the swamp was worth saving. The same year, part of it became a national park, and work to restore its water flow still goes on.

Disappearing fast

The Ramsar Convention, a world agreement to protect wetlands, was signed in 1971 in the town of Ramsar in Iran. India now has more than 100 Ramsar sites (103 in 2026), from Loktak Lake in Manipur, with its floating islands of plants, to the mangroves of the Sundarbans.

Even so, about a third of the world's natural wetlands have vanished since 1970, faster than forests. Many were drained for farms or filled with rubbish and buildings. When a city loses its marshes, the monsoon often answers with floods in places that never used to flood.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Wetlands are useless swamps that are better off drained and built on.

Truth: For centuries many people thought so, and huge areas were drained. We now know wetlands soak up floods, clean water, store carbon and feed fish and birds. Many countries now spend money restoring the wetlands they once destroyed.

Try this

Make a wetland in a tray. Press a strip of damp sponge across the middle of a baking tray to be your marsh, with a pile of soil on one side as the hills. Slowly pour a cup of water onto the hills and watch how the sponge holds it back. Then try again without the sponge and see how fast the water rushes across.
