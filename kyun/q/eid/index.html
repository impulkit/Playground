How is Eid celebrated?

Each year, Eid arrives about 11 days earlier than the year before, so over a lifetime it moves through every season.

The short answer

Eid is the name of two main festivals celebrated by Muslims around the world. Eid al-Fitr, the "festival of breaking the fast", comes at the end of Ramadan, a month when adults who are able go without food and drink from dawn until sunset. Eid al-Adha, the "festival of sacrifice", comes about two months later.

On both Eids, families wake early, wash and put on new or best clothes, and go to special morning prayers. Then come visits to relatives, big meals, sweets and gifts. In India, many people greet each other with "Eid Mubarak" and a hug, three times.

Surprising but true

Because Eid depends on seeing the new crescent Moon, it can fall on different days in different countries, and families sometimes wait up late to hear whether the Moon has been spotted.

Waiting for the Moon

The Islamic calendar follows the phases of the Moon. Each month starts when the thin new crescent is first seen after sunset. Ramadan ends, and Eid al-Fitr begins, when the crescent of the next month, Shawwal, appears.

Because clouds or dust can hide the Moon, people don't always know until the evening before whether Eid is tomorrow. Committees of religious scholars watch and announce it. Some countries now use astronomical calculations. This is why Eid can fall on one day in Saudi Arabia and the next day in India.

Eid al-Fitr

After a month of fasting, Eid al-Fitr is a joyful day. Before the Eid prayer, every Muslim family who can afford it gives a small amount of food or money, called zakat al-fitr, to people in need. The idea is that everyone should be able to celebrate.

In India, it's often called Meethi Eid, "sweet Eid", because of sheer khurma, a dish of fine vermicelli cooked in milk with dates and nuts. Children receive Eidi, gifts of money from older relatives. Markets stay open late on the last nights of Ramadan, full of bangles, clothes and food.

Why does the Moon change shape?It doesn't really change. We just see it from different angles.

Eid al-Adha

Eid al-Adha remembers a story shared by Muslims, Jews and Christians. Muslims believe the prophet Ibrahim, known in the Bible as Abraham, was willing to sacrifice his son because God commanded it. God then provided a ram to sacrifice instead.

On this Eid, families who can afford it sacrifice a goat, sheep or other animal, following rules about how it is done. The meat is usually shared in three parts: for the family, for relatives and friends, and for people in need. It falls during the Hajj, the pilgrimage to Mecca that Muslims aim to make once in their lives if they are able.

Prayers in the open

Eid prayers are often held in large open grounds or big mosques, because so many people attend at once. At Delhi's Jama Masjid, built by the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan in the 1650s, tens of thousands gather in the courtyard and spill into the streets.

In the USA, Eid prayers fill mosques, parks and convention centres. New York City's public schools have closed for both Eids since 2015, so Muslim students do not have to choose between school and the festival.

A moving festival

The Islamic year is about 354 days long, around 11 days shorter than the solar year. So Eid moves earlier on the ordinary calendar every year. Someone who celebrates Eid in summer as a child will celebrate it in winter some years later.

This also changes how hard Ramadan feels. When it falls in summer, the days are long and hot, so fasting lasts longer. In winter, the fasts are shorter. Either way, the first sweet date eaten at sunset, and then the Eid morning that follows the month, are moments many families look forward to all year.

Myth or fact?

Myth: There is only one Eid.

Truth: Muslims celebrate two main Eids each year. Eid al-Fitr comes at the end of Ramadan, and Eid al-Adha comes about two months and ten days later, during the season of the Hajj pilgrimage to Mecca.
