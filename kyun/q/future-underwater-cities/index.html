Could people live under the sea?

In January 2025, a German engineer finished 120 days living in a steel capsule under the Caribbean Sea off Panama.

The short answer

People can live under the sea, but only in small numbers and not for long. For more than 60 years, scientists have stayed in underwater bases for days or weeks at a time. Some people have lasted months. But a full city under the waves, with schools, shops and parks, has never been built.

The reasons are simple. Water is heavy, and it pushes hard on everything below it. Sea water is cold, salty and dark further down. Every breath of air, every drop of fresh water and every light bulb has to be supplied from somewhere.

Engineers still dream about it, though, and some plans are moving forward.

Surprising but true

People who live in a deep sea base breathe air at such high pressure that they need about 17 hours of slow decompression before they can safely return to the surface.

The squeeze of the sea

At sea level, the air above you presses on every part of your body. You don't notice because you are used to it. Go 10 metres underwater and the weight of the water doubles that pressure. At 20 metres it triples.

This is why divers' ears hurt as they go down. It is also why deep-sea submarines have thick, round hulls. Humans can cope with a few atmospheres if they breathe air at the same pressure. But their bodies soak up extra gas, as you can read in what diving does to your body.

That causes a problem when you come back up. If the pressure drops too fast, gas bubbles form in the blood, a dangerous condition called the bends. People who live in a deep habitat must decompress slowly, sometimes for most of a day, before they can safely return.

Homes on the sea floor

In 1963, the French explorer Jacques Cousteau set up Conshelf II in the Red Sea. Five men lived there for a month, with a garage for a small submarine. Around the same time, the US Navy tested its own Sealab habitats.

Today, the best-known base is Aquarius, about 19 metres deep off the coast of Florida. Scientists and astronauts-in-training live there for missions of about ten days. They study coral reefs, and NASA has used it to practise for life in space. When a mission ends, the crew spend about 17 hours decompressing before they swim back up.

In 2023, an American researcher lived for 100 days in a small underwater lodge in Florida. In January 2025, a German engineer named Rüdiger Koch broke that record by spending 120 days in a capsule off the coast of Panama.

What is India's Samudrayaan mission?India is building a small submarine called Matsya-6000 to carry three people 6 km down into the Indian Ocean, as deep as about 80 Qutub Minars stacked one on top of another.

What would a city need?

A real underwater city would face huge challenges. Walls and windows must hold back the sea for decades without leaking. Salt water eats away at metal. Air must be cleaned of carbon dioxide all the time, and the damp inside encourages mould.

Power and food are harder still. Ideas include using ocean currents or waves for electricity, and growing seaweed and fish around the base. Fresh water could come from removing salt from sea water, which already happens in many dry countries.

Living without sunlight for months can also affect sleep and mood. Astronauts face the same problem, which is why the challenges are similar to building colonies on Mars. At least under the sea, help is a few hours away rather than months.

Glimpses of the future

You can already eat lunch underwater. Ithaa, a restaurant in the Maldives, sits about 5 metres below the surface inside a curved glass tunnel. A restaurant in Norway, opened in 2019, has a giant window looking onto the sea.

Engineers have drawn up bigger designs. A Japanese company has proposed a spiral city plunging deep into the ocean, and a new research base called Proteus is planned off the Caribbean island of Curaçao. None of these are built yet.

Some people see the ocean as a place to escape crowded land or rising seas. Others say it would always be easier to build on land. And the strangest part? We have better maps of the surface of Mars than of most of our own sea floor.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Water pressure would crush a person just a few metres below the surface.

Truth: Your body is mostly water, which hardly squashes, so it copes well at shallow depths. What you feel are the air spaces, like your ears. Divers regularly work 30 metres down, and people have lived in habitats at about 20 metres for weeks.

Try this

Put your hand inside a thin plastic bag and slowly lower it into a bucket or deep sink full of water. Feel how the bag presses tighter against your skin the deeper your hand goes. That squeeze is water pressure, and it keeps growing with depth.
