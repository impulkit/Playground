What was the biggest dinosaur ever?

A farmer in Argentina thought he had found a log of stone. It was one backbone of perhaps the heaviest animal ever to walk on land.

The short answer

Nobody knows for sure, but the best guess is a long-necked plant-eater called Argentinosaurus. It lived in what is now Argentina about 95 million years ago. It was perhaps 30–35 m long, about as long as three school buses, and may have weighed 65–80 tonnes. That is as much as a dozen African elephants.

Argentinosaurus was a titanosaur, one of the last and largest groups of sauropods. Several other titanosaurs come close in size. Because the biggest ones are known from only a handful of bones, the title of "biggest dinosaur" could change with the next dig.

Surprising but true

A single backbone of Argentinosaurus is about 1.6 m tall, taller than most 10-year-olds.

A log that wasn't

In 1987, a rancher named Guillermo Heredia found a huge bone on his land in Neuquén province, in Patagonia. He thought it was a piece of petrified wood. It turned out to be part of a leg bone. Scientists who came to dig found backbones, ribs and pieces of hip.

They named the animal Argentinosaurus in 1993. One backbone from its middle is about 1.6 m tall. Put it beside a ten-year-old and it would look down on them.

That is still not much of an animal. No skull has been found, and almost none of the neck or tail. Every picture and model of Argentinosaurus fills the gaps using its better-known cousins, so the long neck and small head you see in books are educated guesses.

How do you weigh a fossil?

You cannot put a dinosaur on a scale, so scientists use clues. One method uses living animals. Heavier animals need thicker leg bones, so the thickness of the thigh and upper-arm bones gives a rough weight. Another method builds a 3D model of the body around the skeleton and works out its volume.

Both methods give a range, not one number. For Argentinosaurus, which has only a broken leg bone, the guesses are especially wide. This kind of careful estimation is common in science. You give your best figure and say how sure you are.

Rivals for the crown

In 2017, scientists named Patagotitan, another Argentine titanosaur known from several skeletons. Their best estimate for it was around 57–69 tonnes. A cast of it is so long that, at the American Museum of Natural History in New York, its head pokes out into the next room. Another cast stands in the Field Museum in Chicago.

India has a mysterious contender too. In 1989, scientists described Bruhathkayosaurus, meaning "huge-bodied lizard", from rocks in Tamil Nadu. Its leg bones, as described, would make it a rival to Argentinosaurus. But the fossils fell apart and were never properly studied again, so most experts do not count it. A giant backbone found in Colorado during the Bone Wars of the 1870s had the same fate. It was drawn and measured, and then it disappeared.

How did they get so big?

Sauropods had a few tricks that let them grow huge. Their long necks let them feed over a wide area without walking much. Their bones were full of air spaces, linked to a breathing system like a bird's, which made the skeleton lighter. And they did not chew. They swallowed leaves whole and let their gut do the work.

They also grew very fast. A baby hatched from an egg smaller than a football and may have reached full size in a few decades. Even so, no dinosaur matched the blue whale, which still holds the record for the heaviest animal known.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Brachiosaurus was the biggest dinosaur.

Truth: Brachiosaurus was very tall and very big, and for a long time it was the most famous giant. But titanosaurs found later in South America were much heavier.
