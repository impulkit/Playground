Why is the Narmada called a lifeline?

The Narmada runs west across the middle of India for about 1,312 km, the opposite way to almost every other big river in the peninsula.

The short answer

The Narmada is one of the longest rivers of central India. It rises in the forests of Amarkantak in Madhya Pradesh, runs about 1,312 km west and pours into the Arabian Sea near Bharuch in Gujarat.

People call it a lifeline because so much depends on it. Its water fills taps in cities such as Indore, waters farms across Gujarat and turns turbines that make electricity. For millions of Hindus it is also a holy river, often called Reva or simply Ma Narmada, "Mother Narmada".

Along the way it passes through three states, thick teak forests, marble gorges and wide fields of wheat and cotton. More than 40 smaller rivers join it before it reaches the sea.

Surprising but true

The Narmada flows along a long crack in the Earth's crust, a rift valley squeezed between two mountain ranges, which is why it runs west when most peninsular rivers run east.

A river in a crack

Look at a map of India and most peninsular rivers, like the Godavari and the Krishna, slope east toward the Bay of Bengal. The Narmada goes the other way. It flows through a rift valley, a long trough where blocks of the Earth's crust slipped down millions of years ago. The Vindhya hills rise to the north and the Satpura hills to the south, and the river is trapped between them.

Near Jabalpur, it has cut a gorge through soft white marble. At Bhedaghat the rock walls rise straight out of the water, and boats drift between them at night. A little upstream, the river crashes over Dhuandhar Falls in a cloud of spray, which is where the name, "smoke stream", comes from.

Holy water

Many Hindus believe that a dip in the Narmada washes away wrongs, and temples line its banks. The island town of Omkareshwar holds one of the twelve jyotirlingas, the most sacred shrines of Shiva. Smooth, egg-shaped stones from the riverbed are kept in homes and temples as Shiva lingas.

Some devotees walk the Narmada parikrama. They set off from the source, walk down one bank to the sea, cross over and walk all the way back up the other side. The full walk covers thousands of kilometres and can take months. Pilgrims sleep in ashrams and villages, and are fed by strangers along the way.

Dams and a long argument

From the 1960s, governments planned about 30 large dams on the Narmada and its branches. The biggest is Sardar Sarovar in Gujarat, about 163 m tall at its highest point. Its canals carry water hundreds of kilometres into dry parts of Gujarat and Rajasthan, and it generates electricity for several states. The Statue of Unity stands just downstream.

The dams also flooded forests and villages. Tens of thousands of families, many of them Adivasi farmers, had to move. From the 1980s a protest movement called the Narmada Bachao Andolan, "Save the Narmada", argued that people were not being treated fairly. Supporters of the dams pointed to the water and power they bring to drought-hit regions. Courts spent years weighing both sides, and people still disagree about whether the cost was worth it.

Where the river meets the sea

By the time the Narmada reaches Bharuch, it is wide and slow. It drops its fine sediment into the Gulf of Khambhat, where the tides are among the strongest on India's coast. Fishermen here catch hilsa, the same silvery fish loved in Bengal.

Old bones turn up along the river too. In 1982, a geologist found part of an ancient human skull in its banks at Hathnora, one of the oldest human fossils known from India. Scientists still argue about its exact age, but most place it somewhere in the last few hundred thousand years. The same riverbanks have given up bones of giant elephants, hippos and wild cattle. The Narmada has been feeding people, and animals, for a very long time.

Myth or fact?

Myth: All the big rivers of India start in the Himalaya.

Truth: The Ganga, Yamuna and Brahmaputra do, but many great rivers start on the peninsula. The Narmada begins at Amarkantak, a plateau in central India about 1,000 m high.

Try this

Fill a long baking tray with damp sand and press a ruler down the middle to make a groove. Tilt the tray slightly and pour water slowly at the high end. Watch how the water follows the groove, just as the Narmada follows its valley, and see where the sand it carries piles up.
