Why does Greece have so many islands?

Greece has thousands of islands because it is a mountain range that has partly sunk into the sea, with volcanoes poking up for good measure.

The short answer

Greece has thousands of islands and islets scattered across the Aegean and Ionian Seas, about 200 of them home to people. There are so many because the land here is broken, stretched and partly drowned.

Millions of years ago, the movement of Earth's plates crumpled this region into mountains and then pulled parts of it apart. Some land sank. When sea levels rose at the end of the last Ice Age, the sea flooded the valleys, and only the mountaintops were left showing. On top of that, volcanoes built a few islands of their own. The result is a sea full of rocky peaks, an archipelago that has shaped Greek life for thousands of years.

Surprising but true

The famous cliffs of Santorini are the inside wall of a volcano that collapsed in a huge eruption about 3,600 years ago.

Drowned mountains

The Aegean Sea sits where the African plate pushes slowly north under the plate that carries Greece and Turkey. This squeezing and stretching cracked the land into blocks. Some rose as mountains, and others sank as basins.

During the last Ice Age, so much water was locked up in ice sheets that the sea was about 120 m lower than today. Many of today's islands were then hills on dry land. As the ice melted, the sea crept in, and those hills became islands. Divers have found ancient shorelines far below the waves.

Islands born from fire

Some Greek islands are volcanoes. Santorini is the most famous. Around 3,600 years ago, it blew apart in one of the largest eruptions of the last few thousand years. The middle of the island collapsed into a caldera, a huge bowl that the sea filled.

Today white houses line the top of the cliffs, and the bay below is about 400 m deep in places. The volcano is not dead. Small islands in the middle of the bay were built by later eruptions, the most recent in 1950. Ash from the great eruption buried a Bronze Age town called Akrotiri, preserving houses with painted walls, rather like Pompeii.

A people of the sea

With so many islands and so little flat farmland, ancient Greeks took to the sea. They fished, traded olive oil and wine, and founded towns all around the Mediterranean. Sailing between islands, you were rarely out of sight of land.

The island of Crete, the largest, was home to the Minoans, one of Europe's earliest civilisations, with a great palace at Knossos. Greek stories are full of the sea, from Odysseus's ten-year voyage home to Poseidon, god of the waves. You can meet them in Greek myths. The sea also linked the Greek city-states of ancient Greece, even while they quarrelled.

Island life today

Ferries are to Greek islanders what buses and local trains are to people in Mumbai or New York. Children on small islands may take a boat to secondary school. In summer, tourists arrive in huge numbers, and some tiny islands see their population grow many times over.

Islands are grouped into chains. The Cyclades circle the holy island of Delos, said to be the birthplace of the god Apollo. The Dodecanese lie near Turkey, and the green Ionian Islands sit on the western side, facing Italy.

Wind, water and old marble

Island life has always been shaped by the weather. Strong summer winds called the meltemi blow from the north, cooling the islands and making ferry trips bumpy. Many islands are dry and get little rain, so some depend on rainwater tanks, desalination plants or water brought by boat.

Marble from the islands of Paros and Naxos was prized in ancient times. Some of the most famous Greek statues were carved from it, and quarries there still cut stone today.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Atlantis was a real place that historians have found.

Truth: Atlantis first appears in the writings of the philosopher Plato, around 360 BCE, probably as a story to make a point. Some people link it to Santorini's eruption, but there is no proof that Atlantis ever existed.

Try this

Crumple a sheet of paper into a lumpy ball, then open it out and lay it in a tray so it has hills and valleys. Slowly pour in water. Watch the low valleys flood first, leaving only the peaks sticking out as "islands". This is roughly what happened in the Aegean Sea.
