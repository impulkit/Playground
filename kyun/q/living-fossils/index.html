What are living fossils?

In 1938, a museum curator in South Africa spotted a strange blue fish in a fisherman's catch. Scientists had thought its kind died out with the dinosaurs.

The short answer

A "living fossil" is a plant or animal alive today that looks very much like relatives known only from very old fossils. Its family has been around for tens or hundreds of millions of years without changing its basic body plan very much.

Famous examples include the coelacanth, a deep-sea fish, the horseshoe crab, the ginkgo tree and the nautilus, a shelled cousin of the octopus. Charles Darwin first used the phrase in 1859. Many scientists today use it with care, because these creatures have kept evolving. They have just changed slowly on the outside.

Surprising but true

India has its own living fossil, the purple frog of the Western Ghats. It spends most of its life underground, and its closest living relatives are tiny frogs in the Seychelles, more than 2,500 km away.

The fish that came back

In December 1938, Marjorie Courtenay-Latimer, a young museum curator in the town of East London, South Africa, checked a fishing boat's catch. Among the sharks and fish she saw a large, steel-blue fish with fleshy fins that looked almost like stubby legs. She sketched it and sent the drawing to a fish expert, J. L. B. Smith.

Smith recognised it as a coelacanth, a fish known from fossils but thought to have died out about 66 million years ago. He named it Latimeria after her. In the late 1990s, a second species was found in Indonesia, when a scientist on honeymoon spotted one in a fish market.

Old shapes, new lives

Horseshoe crabs are not true crabs. They are more closely related to spiders and scorpions. Fossils of their relatives go back about 450 million years, long before the first dinosaurs. Each year, they still crawl onto beaches in the USA and Asia to lay eggs, including in Odisha and West Bengal. Their blue blood is even used in medicine, to test whether vaccines and injections are free of harmful germs.

The ginkgo tree has fan-shaped leaves that look almost exactly like fossil leaves more than 200 million years old. Wild ginkgos nearly vanished, but monks in China planted them in temple gardens for centuries. Now they line streets in New York, Tokyo and many other cities.

India's purple frog

In 2003, the Indian scientist S. D. Biju and his Belgian colleague Franky Bossuyt described a strange frog from the Western Ghats. It is purple-grey, plump and has a small pointed snout, a bit like a balloon with a nose. It spends most of the year underground, eating termites, and comes up only for a few days during the monsoon to breed.

Its closest living relatives are tiny frogs on the Seychelles islands. That fits a much bigger story. Over 100 million years ago, India, the Seychelles and Madagascar were joined together. As India drifted north, the frog's ancestors were carried with it, and the two families went their separate ways.

Why some stay the same

There is no single reason. Many living fossils live in places that have stayed fairly steady for a very long time, such as the deep sea or quiet forest floors. Their body plan already works well there, so there is little pressure to change shape.

Some are also tough generalists. Like crocodiles, horseshoe crabs can eat many foods and survive long periods with little to eat. That may have helped them get through mass extinctions that wiped out more specialised animals.

A word scientists argue about

Some biologists say "living fossil" is misleading, because it suggests evolution has stopped. When scientists studied coelacanth genes, they found they had changed more slowly than in many fish, but they had certainly changed.

Others think the phrase is useful, as long as people understand it loosely. Whatever you call them, these animals and plants act like windows into the deep past. Looking at a living horseshoe crab gives scientists clues about how its ancient relatives may have moved, fed and laid eggs.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Living fossils have not changed at all for millions of years.

Truth: They have kept evolving, like every living thing. Their overall body shape has stayed similar, but their genes and many details have changed. Today's coelacanths, for example, are different species from the ones found as fossils.
