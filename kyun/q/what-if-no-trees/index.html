What if all the trees vanished?

If every tree on Earth vanished tonight, you wouldn't run out of air to breathe, but tomorrow would be hotter, dustier and much lonelier.

The short answer

If all the trees disappeared, the air wouldn't run out of oxygen any time soon. The atmosphere holds a huge store of it, and tiny plankton in the oceans make about half of each year's new supply. But almost everything else would change.

Huge amounts of carbon dioxide, the gas that traps heat, would no longer be soaked up, so the planet would warm faster. Rain would change, because forests pump water into the air. Soil on hillsides would wash away. Millions of kinds of animals, fungi and plants that live in forests would lose their homes. And people would lose wood, paper, rubber, many fruits, spices and medicines.

Surprising but true

For most of Earth's history there were no trees at all. The first forests grew only about 385 million years ago, long after the first animals.

Three trillion and counting

In 2015, scientists combined satellite images with counts made on the ground and estimated that Earth has about three trillion trees, roughly 400 for every person. They also estimated that people cut down about 15 billion trees each year, and that there are only about half as many trees now as when people first began farming.

Trees are giant carbon stores. Through photosynthesis, they pull carbon dioxide from the air and lock the carbon into wood. Burn or rot that wood, and the carbon goes back into the sky. That's a big part of the carbon cycle, and why losing forests speeds up climate change.

Forests make rain

A big tree sucks water up from the soil and breathes it out through its leaves as vapour. In the Amazon, scientists estimate the trees release billions of tonnes of water into the air every day. The water forms clouds that drift inland and fall as rain far away. People call these "flying rivers".

Without trees, many regions would get less rain. Forests in India's Western Ghats also help feed the rivers that flow east across the Deccan. Take away the trees and the land dries, and in a dry land, fewer trees can grow back.

Who made the Easter Island statues?The giant stone heads of Easter Island are not just heads. Most of them have whole bodies hidden under the soil.

Hillsides on the move

Tree roots hold soil together like the threads of a net. When hillsides are cleared, heavy rain can wash the soil away, choking rivers with mud and causing landslides. Lost topsoil can take hundreds of years to form again.

On remote Easter Island in the Pacific, forests of giant palm trees once grew. By about the 1600s, nearly all were gone. People cleared land for farms, and rats that arrived with them ate the palm seeds. Historians still argue about how much this harmed the islanders, but the trees never came back on their own.

Hotter cities, emptier forests

Trees work like air conditioners. Their shade blocks sunlight, and water evaporating from leaves cools the air. A tree-lined street can be several degrees cooler than a bare one on a hot afternoon. A city with no trees would bake.

Forests are also home to most of the land's animals and plants. Without trees, tigers, orangutans, hornbills, many monkeys and countless insects would vanish, along with mushrooms and lichens that grow only on wood. Even the banyan, home to hundreds of creatures in a single tree, would be gone.

A world that existed before

Strangely, Earth has been treeless before. For billions of years, the land was bare rock, mats of microbes and, later, small plants. The first true forests appeared about 385 million years ago. Their buried remains later turned into some of the coal people dig up today.

People have fought hard for trees. In the 1970s, women in the Himalayan foothills hugged trees to stop loggers, in what became the Chipko movement. And the strangest part? Many trees can't make fruit or seeds without tiny partners, so forests could also fall apart in a world without insects.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Trees make most of the oxygen we breathe.

Truth: Land plants, including trees, make about half of the world's new oxygen. Tiny plankton in the ocean make the other half. Forests also use up much of their own oxygen at night and as they rot, so they matter more for climate, water and wildlife than for our next breath.

Try this

Measure the cooling power of a tree. On a sunny afternoon, place two cheap thermometers, or two cups of water, one in full sun and one in a tree's shade. After half an hour, compare the readings or feel the water. Then think about a city street with no trees at all.
