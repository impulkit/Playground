When did the internet come to India?

When public internet arrived in India on 15 August 1995, an account cost thousands of rupees and the connection crawled through a telephone line.

The short answer

The internet reached Indian universities and research labs in the late 1980s, through a network called ERNET that started in 1986. Ordinary people got access on 15 August 1995, India's Independence Day, when the government company VSNL launched a public internet service.

Getting online was slow and costly at first. You needed a computer, a phone line and a modem that screeched as it dialled in. Most people first used the internet in cyber cafés. The real explosion came in the 2010s, with cheap smartphones and very cheap mobile data. By 2025, about 95 crore Indians were online.

Surprising but true

Before 2016, a gigabyte of mobile data in India often cost more than ₹200. Within a few years it cost around ₹10, among the cheapest in the world.

The 1995 launch

When VSNL opened its service, an account cost thousands of rupees, at a time when many office workers earned only a few thousand a month. Lines were few and often busy. Only a few thousand people signed up in the first months, mostly in big cities like Mumbai, Delhi, Chennai, Kolkata, Bengaluru and Pune.

A dial-up connection used a normal telephone line. The modem turned computer data into sounds and back again. The best modems reached about 56 kilobits a second, and connections were often much slower. Downloading a single song could take several minutes, and if someone picked up the phone, the connection dropped.

Cyber cafés and email

In the late 1990s and 2000s, cyber cafés sprang up in towns all over India. For a small hourly fee, people could check email, chat with friends, apply for jobs or look up exam results. Many Indians got their first email address in a café.

In 1998, the government opened internet service to private companies. Prices fell and speeds slowly rose. Mobile data was a different story. Before 2016, a gigabyte often cost more than ₹200, so most people used it sparingly. Broadband, a faster always-on connection, spread in cities in the 2000s, often through cable TV operators.

How does the internet cross oceans?When you video-call someone in another continent, your voice most likely races along the sea floor inside a cable about as thick as a garden hose.

The phone in every pocket

Smartphones changed everything. Cheap Android phones arrived around 2010, and 3G and then 4G mobile networks followed. But data was still expensive.

In September 2016, a new company, Reliance Jio, offered free 4G data for several months, and other networks were forced to cut prices. Within a few years, a gigabyte cost around ₹10, and data use jumped. Millions of people who had never owned a computer went online for the first time, usually to watch videos, use WhatsApp and look things up in their own language.

The same year, India launched UPI, a system that lets people send money from their phones in seconds. Today, a vegetable seller in a small town may have a QR code taped to her cart.

Where the internet really comes from

The internet feels invisible, but it runs on cables. More than a dozen undersea cables bring data to India's coasts, many of them landing in Mumbai and Chennai. From there, fibre-optic cables carry it across the country, along railway lines, highways and city streets.

The government's BharatNet project has been laying fibre to connect gram panchayats, the village councils, across rural India. It has made progress, though more slowly than first planned.

Who is still offline

Even with 95 crore users in 2025, hundreds of millions of Indians are not online. Many live in villages, are older, or cannot afford a smartphone. Surveys have also found that fewer women than men use the internet, especially in rural areas, though the gap has been shrinking.

Language matters too. In the early years, almost everything online was in English. Now much of what Indians read and watch is in Hindi, Tamil, Bengali, Marathi and other languages. For many new users, the internet is a voice note, a video or a payment, and a keyboard hardly comes into it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The internet travels through the air from satellites to your phone.

Truth: Your phone talks to a nearby tower by radio, but almost everything after that goes through cables. Most of India's international internet traffic arrives through fibre-optic cables lying on the seabed.
