What was the Salt March?

In 1930, a 60-year-old man walked 385 km to the sea, bent down and picked up a lump of salty mud. It shook the British Empire.

The short answer

The Salt March was a protest led by Mahatma Gandhi in 1930 against the British salt law. Under the Salt Act of 1882, Indians were not allowed to make or sell their own salt. They had to buy salt that was taxed by the government.

On 12 March 1930, Gandhi and 78 followers set out from his Sabarmati Ashram in Ahmedabad. They walked about 385 km in 24 days to the village of Dandi on the coast of Gujarat. On 6 April, Gandhi picked up a lump of natural salt from the shore, breaking the law. Across India, people began making salt, selling it and refusing to obey. Tens of thousands were arrested, and newspapers around the world reported the protest.

Surprising but true

An American journalist, Webb Miller, reported on police beating protesters at the Dharasana salt works in 1930, and his story was printed in newspapers around the world.

Why salt?

Many of Gandhi's colleagues were puzzled when he chose salt. They expected a protest about land taxes or government power.

But Gandhi saw that salt touched everyone. A poor farmer in the heat needs salt just as much as a rich merchant. In a country with a long coastline, salt could be made for almost nothing by drying seawater in the sun. Making it a crime and taxing it showed clearly how unfair foreign rule was. Anyone, anywhere near the sea, could join the protest just by making a handful of salt.

The long walk

Gandhi was 60 years old. He chose marchers from his ashram carefully, from many parts of India and different faiths. They walked through the spring heat, about 16 km a day, stopping in villages.

At each village, Gandhi spoke to crowds about the salt tax, cleanliness, khadi and Hindu–Muslim unity. Thousands joined parts of the walk. Reporters from India and abroad followed, sending stories and photographs around the world. By the time the marchers reached Dandi, the whole country was watching.

Why do we need salt?Every message your brain sends to your muscles depends on salt, and in 1930 a handful of it helped shake the British Empire.

A handful of mud

On the morning of 6 April 1930, after prayers and a dip in the sea, Gandhi bent down and picked up a lump of salty mud. It was a tiny act, but it broke the law in front of the world.

Within days, people all along India's coasts were boiling seawater and making salt. In towns far from the sea, people sold salt in public and boycotted British cloth and liquor shops. Women joined in huge numbers, picketing shops and leading marches.

Blows at Dharasana

On 5 May, Gandhi was arrested. The poet and leader Sarojini Naidu then led a peaceful raid on the Dharasana salt works.

Row after row of marchers walked forward and were struck down by police with steel-tipped lathis, without lifting a hand to defend themselves. The American journalist Webb Miller watched and wrote about it. His report appeared in hundreds of newspapers around the world, and turned many people abroad against British rule in India.

What it changed

The salt law did not end at once. But in 1931, the Gandhi–Irwin Pact released prisoners and let coastal villagers make salt for their own use. Gandhi went to London for talks on India's future.

More importantly, the march showed that non-violent protest could move a whole nation. Decades later, Gandhi's methods guided the marches of Martin Luther King Jr. in the USA. Today a memorial at Dandi shows the marchers in bronze, walking towards the sea.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Salt March forced the British to scrap the salt tax straight away.

Truth: The Salt Act stayed. A deal in 1931 allowed people living near the coast to make salt for their own use, but the salt tax was not abolished until shortly before independence.

Try this

With an adult, dissolve two spoons of salt in a cup of warm water. Pour a little into a shallow dish and leave it in a sunny window for a few days. As the water evaporates, salt crystals will appear, just like the salt Gandhi and the marchers made by the sea.
