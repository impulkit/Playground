Is a tomato a fruit?

In 1893, the highest court in the USA had to decide whether a tomato was a fruit or a vegetable, and the answer came down to taxes.

The short answer

Yes, to a botanist a tomato is a fruit. It grows from a flower and holds the plant's seeds, which is the scientific test. It is even a kind of berry.

But in the kitchen, almost everyone treats it as a vegetable. It goes into salads, curries and sauces, not fruit salad. In 1893, this difference ended up in the US Supreme Court. Both answers are right, depending on whether you are a scientist or a cook.

Surprising but true

Tomatoes reached India only a few hundred years ago. In Bengali they were once called bilati begun, meaning "foreign brinjal".

The court case

In the 1880s, the USA charged a tax on imported vegetables but not on fruit. A New York family of food importers named Nix argued that their tomatoes were fruit and should not be taxed. The tax collector, Edward Hedden, disagreed.

The case reached the Supreme Court. In 1893, the judges ruled unanimously that tomatoes should be taxed as vegetables. They accepted that botanists called them fruits. But the law used everyday language, and in everyday life tomatoes were served with the main meal. So the importers paid.

From the Andes to Mexico

Wild tomatoes still grow in the dry valleys of Peru and Ecuador. They are tiny, about the size of a pea or a cherry. People in what is now Mexico farmed them and bred bigger kinds. The Aztec word tomatl gave us the name "tomato".

When Spanish soldiers conquered the Aztec capital in 1521, they found tomatoes in its markets. They took seeds back to Europe, along with chillies, maize and potatoes.

Europe was nervous

The tomato belongs to the nightshade family, which includes some poisonous plants. Its leaves and stems really do contain mildly toxic chemicals. So many Europeans grew tomatoes only as decorative plants at first.

In 1544, an Italian writer named Pietro Andrea Mattioli described the tomato. About ten years later he gave a yellow kind the name pomi d'oro, golden apples. That's why the Italian word for tomato is still pomodoro. Even so, tomato sauce didn't become common in Italian cooking until the late 1600s and 1700s. Today the tomato and pizza seem inseparable.

How tomatoes came to India

The Portuguese and British brought tomatoes to India, probably in the 1600s or 1700s. For a long time they were grown mainly for European residents. In Bengal they got the name bilati begun, foreign brinjal, because the plants look a little like brinjal plants.

Only in the 1800s and 1900s did tomatoes enter everyday Indian cooking. Now they are everywhere: in dal tadka, in rasam, in butter chicken gravy, in chutneys and in ketchup. India is the second biggest grower in the world, and the price of tomatoes makes national news when bad weather spoils the crop.

Why tomatoes turn red

A green tomato is full of chlorophyll, the same pigment that makes leaves green. As it ripens, the plant breaks down the green and builds a red pigment called lycopene, along with sugars and smells. Some tomato varieties ripen yellow, orange, purple or even striped.

Ripening is triggered partly by a gas called ethylene, which the fruit itself releases. Farmers often pick tomatoes while they are still pale and firm so they survive the trip to market. They finish ripening on the way, which is why a shop tomato and one fresh from a garden can taste so different.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Tomatoes have always been part of Indian and Italian cooking.

Truth: Tomatoes come from the Americas and reached Europe only in the 1500s. Italians took a long time to cook with them, and in India they became common only in the last two hundred years or so.

Try this

Ask an adult to slice a ripe tomato. Scoop a few seeds onto a paper towel, let them dry for a few days, then plant them in a pot of soil on a sunny windowsill. Water a little every day and watch for seedlings in one or two weeks.
