Who were the Navajo code talkers?

In the Second World War, a secret code built from a Native American language was never cracked by the enemy.

The short answer

The Navajo code talkers were Native American Marines who sent secret radio messages for the United States during the Second World War. They used a code built on their own language, Navajo, which very few outsiders could speak.

A short message that took a coding machine about half an hour could be sent by a pair of code talkers in well under a minute. The Japanese forces in the Pacific listened in constantly. They never broke it.

Surprising but true

Many code talkers had been punished at boarding schools for speaking Navajo. Years later, that same language helped the US win battles.

Why Navajo worked

By 1942 the enemy had become good at cracking codes made from numbers and letters. A spoken language was harder. Navajo has complex grammar and sounds that are hard for outsiders to hear correctly, and a small change in tone can change a word's meaning.

It also had almost no written form at the time, so there were no dictionaries for an enemy to study. The idea came from Philip Johnston, the son of a missionary, who had grown up on the Navajo reservation in the American Southwest and spoke the language.

Building the code

The first 29 Navajo recruits created the code themselves at a Marine camp in California. They kept their messages short and made two layers.

The first layer was an alphabet. Each English letter had one or more Navajo words standing for it: "wol-la-chee" (ant) meant A. The second layer was a list of about 200 military words, later grown to over 400. Birds stood for aircraft and fish for ships. A bomber was a buzzard. Every code talker learned it all by heart, because a written copy could be captured. Common letters such as E, T and A were given several different Navajo words, so a listener could not spot a word repeating and guess what it meant.

On the battlefield

Code talkers served in every major Marine battle in the Pacific from 1942 to 1945. They worked in pairs, one speaking into a field radio and one listening, often under fire. In the first two days of the battle of Iwo Jima in 1945, six code talkers sent more than 800 messages without a single mistake.

They were not the only Native code talkers. Choctaw soldiers had sent messages in their language in the First World War, and Comanche soldiers did so in Europe during the Second World War, including on D-Day.

A language that was nearly lost

Many code talkers had gone to government boarding schools that tried to make Native children give up their languages. Some were punished for speaking Navajo. The irony was not lost on them.

The code stayed a military secret until 1968, so for over 20 years the men could not tell even their families what they had done. Recognition came slowly. In 1982 the US named 14 August National Navajo Code Talkers Day, and in 2001 the original 29 received the Congressional Gold Medal. Chester Nez, the last of that first group, died in 2014.

Why it still matters

The story is now one of the best-known examples of how a language carries knowledge nobody else has. Navajo is still spoken by well over 100,000 people, which makes it one of the strongest Native languages in the United States. Many others are far closer to vanishing, as you can read in endangered languages. Each one that survives keeps a way of naming the world that no cipher could copy.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Anyone who spoke Navajo could understand the code.

Truth: The code was built on Navajo, but it used invented words and spelled-out letters. A Navajo speaker without the training heard only strange lists of animals and objects.

Try this
