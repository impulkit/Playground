How does an idea become a law in India?

A law starts as an idea written on paper. Then it has to pass a lot of tests.

The short answer

India's laws are made by Parliament, which has two houses: the Lok Sabha and the Rajya Sabha. A proposed law is called a bill. Most bills must be passed by both houses and signed by the President before they become an Act, the official name for a law.

Along the way, a bill is read, debated, sent to committees, changed and voted on. Members can argue about every line.

Courts make sure new laws follow the Constitution. Even after a law is passed, anyone can challenge it in court if they think it breaks people's rights.

Surprising but true

Even the government has to follow the Constitution. If a law breaks it, the Supreme Court can strike it down.

Two houses

The Lok Sabha, or House of the People, has 543 members elected directly by voters. Elections are held every five years.

The Rajya Sabha, or Council of States, can have up to 250 members. Most are elected by members of the state assemblies, and the President nominates up to 12 people known for their work in art, science, literature or social service. The Rajya Sabha is never dissolved. A third of its members retire every two years.

Since 2023, both houses have met in a new Parliament building in New Delhi.

From idea to bill

Ideas for laws come from many places: a minister's plan, a court ruling, a public campaign or a news story. Most bills are written by government ministries, with lawyers checking the wording.

A bill is introduced in one house, which is called the first reading. In the second reading, members debate it in detail. It may go to a committee of MPs from different parties who study it, invite experts and suggest changes. In the third reading, the house votes. Then the bill goes to the other house to do it all again.

When the houses disagree

Sometimes the two houses can't agree. The Constitution then allows a joint sitting, where members of both meet together and vote. Because the Lok Sabha is bigger, it usually wins. This has happened only three times, most recently in 2002.

Money bills, about taxes and spending, are different. They can only start in the Lok Sabha, and the Rajya Sabha can only suggest changes within 14 days.

Questions and debates

Parliament does more than pass laws. In Question Hour, MPs ask ministers questions, and ministers must answer, which keeps the government on its toes. Debates are shown live on Sansad TV, so anyone can watch.

The presiding officer keeps order: the Speaker in the Lok Sabha and the Vice-President of India, who chairs the Rajya Sabha. When debates get heated, they can suspend members or adjourn the house.

The President's signature

Once both houses pass a bill, it goes to the President for assent, meaning approval. For most bills, the President can send it back once and ask Parliament to think again. If Parliament passes it a second time, the President must sign.

Laws in the states

Parliament doesn't make every law. The Constitution splits subjects into three lists. Parliament handles things like defence and railways. State assemblies handle things like police and farming. Both can make laws on shared subjects, like education, but if they clash, Parliament's law usually wins. Village panchayats make local rules too.

A law you can feel

Laws can change daily life. The Right to Information Act of 2005 lets any citizen ask government offices for information. The Right to Education Act of 2009, part of India's laws on children's rights, promises free schooling to children aged 6 to 14. If a law goes too far, the Supreme Court can step in, as it has many times since 1950.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Prime Minister can make any law he or she wants.

Truth: The government can propose laws, but both houses of Parliament must pass them and the President must sign. Courts can strike down any law that breaks the Constitution.

Try this

Turn your class into a parliament. Someone writes a "bill" for a new class rule. Split the class into two houses, debate the bill in each, and allow changes. If both houses pass it, your teacher acts as President and signs it. If one house rejects it, try holding a joint sitting.
