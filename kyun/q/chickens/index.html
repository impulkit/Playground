How did chickens spread around the world?

There are more chickens on Earth than any other bird, more than 20 billion at once, and their wild ancestor still lives in the forests of India.

The short answer

Chickens come from a wild forest bird called the red junglefowl, which still lives across South and Southeast Asia, from India to Indonesia. About 3,500 years ago, in Southeast Asia, people farming rice and millet began living closely with these birds. Over generations, the birds became tame.

From there, chickens travelled with people: by land along trade routes, by boat across seas, and later on sailing ships to every continent except Antarctica. Today there are more than 20 billion of them, roughly three for every human alive. Hardly any other animal has followed people so far.

Surprising but true

The first chickens kept by people may not have been eaten at all. Some early ones were buried whole, alone or with people, as if they were special.

A bird from the forest

If you walk through a forest in central or northern India early in the morning, you might hear a familiar crow. That's the red junglefowl. The male looks like a slim, glossy farmyard rooster, with a red comb, golden neck feathers and a long, dark tail.

Scientists who read chicken DNA found that most of it comes from red junglefowl. A little comes from a relative, the grey junglefowl, which lives only in India. The gene that gives many chickens yellow skin seems to have come from that Indian cousin.

The rooster's famous dawn crow is older than farming too. In 2013, researchers in Japan kept roosters in dim light all day and found they still crowed just before their usual dawn. An internal body clock, not the sunrise, sets them off. In a flock, the top rooster usually crows first and the others wait their turn.

Rice fields and roosting birds

For a long time, people claimed chickens were tamed 8,000 or even 10,000 years ago. In 2022, two big studies checked the bones carefully. Many "early chicken" bones turned out to be younger than thought, or from other birds.

The oldest bones certainly from tame chickens came from Ban Non Wat in north-east Thailand, about 3,500 years old. Researchers think rice and millet farming was the key. Grain in the fields drew junglefowl out of the forest and closer to villages, until they got used to people. It's a good example of how farming changed animals as well as plants.

Not food at first

Surprisingly, early chickens may not have been dinner. In Europe and elsewhere, archaeologists have found early chickens buried whole and uneaten, sometimes alongside people. They may have been prized for cockfighting, for their crowing at dawn or for religious reasons.

Chickens reached Europe about 2,800 years ago. Only in Roman times, centuries later, did eating chicken and eggs become common there.

Across the oceans

Chickens travel well. They are small, eat almost anything and lay eggs on board. Polynesian sailors carried them in canoes across the Pacific, island by island, as far as Hawaii and Easter Island.

Spanish and Portuguese ships brought chickens to the Americas in the 1500s. Some researchers think Polynesians may have reached South America with chickens earlier, but the evidence is still debated.

Chickens today

People have bred hundreds of different kinds. In Madhya Pradesh, the Kadaknath has black feathers, black skin and even dark meat. The Aseel, an Indian breed, is tall, muscular and fierce. Modern meat chickens grow very fast, reaching full size in about six weeks, which raises questions about animal welfare that farmers and scientists still discuss.

India is one of the world's biggest egg producers, and chicken is the most popular meat in the USA. Yet every one of those birds carries the same ancestry, from a backyard hen in Assam to a broiler in Georgia. Each descends from a shy, quick-flying forest bird that farmers let stay a little closer to home, some 3,500 years ago.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Chickens can't fly.

Truth: Most chickens can fly short distances, enough to clear a fence or reach a tree branch to roost. Heavy farm breeds fly poorly, but their wild cousins, red junglefowl, fly well.

Try this

Next time eggs are used in your kitchen, rinse an empty shell and look at it under a bright torch in a dark room. You'll see tiny spots where light shines through. These are pores, thousands of them, that let a growing chick breathe through the shell.
