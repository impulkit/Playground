How was safety glass discovered?

In 1903, a French scientist knocked a glass flask off a shelf. It cracked all over but didn't shatter, and that accident now protects millions of car passengers.

The short answer

Safety glass was discovered by accident in 1903 by the French chemist Édouard Bénédictus. He dropped a glass flask in his lab. It cracked into a spider web of lines, but the pieces stayed together in the flask's shape.

When he looked closer, he found the flask had once held cellulose nitrate, a liquid that dries into a clear plastic film. That film was gripping the broken glass. Bénédictus realised that a sandwich of glass and plastic could stop sharp shards flying. He patented the idea in 1909. Today, car windscreens use the same trick.

Surprising but true

Before car windscreens, one of the first big uses of safety glass was in the eyepieces of gas masks in the First World War.

Glass that cuts

Ordinary glass, the kind in old windows and picture frames, breaks into long, knife-sharp pieces. In the early 1900s, as more cars took to the roads, crashes became a big problem. Windscreens made of normal glass caused terrible cuts.

Early cars often had no windscreen at all. Drivers wore goggles and long coats to keep out dust, rain and insects. Some cars reached speeds of 60 km/h or more, so a beetle in the eye was a real danger. When windscreens did arrive, they made driving more comfortable but crashes more dangerous.

The flask on the shelf

The story told about Bénédictus goes like this. He climbed a ladder to fetch something, knocked over an empty flask, and it fell to the floor. Instead of exploding into bits, it cracked but held.

Around that time, he had read reports of people badly cut in car accidents. He put the two ideas together. If glass could be coated or layered with plastic, it would stay in one piece when hit.

From gas masks to cars

Bénédictus called his product Triplex: glass, then plastic, then glass. At first it was expensive. Its first big use came in the First World War, for the eyepieces of soldiers' gas masks, where shattering glass could blind someone.

After the war, carmakers began to use laminated glass for windscreens. Early versions turned yellow or cloudy over time. In the 1930s, a tougher, clearer plastic called PVB replaced the old film, and laminated windscreens became standard. Today, in India, the USA and most countries, car windscreens must be made of laminated glass by law.

Two kinds of safety glass

Laminated glass is a sandwich. When something hits it, the glass cracks but sticks to the plastic in the middle. It is used for windscreens, shop windows and glass floors on viewing platforms. Thick versions with many layers make bulletproof glass.

Toughened glass, also called tempered glass, works differently. It is heated to more than 600°C and then cooled very quickly with blasts of air. That locks tension inside the glass and makes it about four to five times stronger than normal glass. When it does break, it crumbles into thousands of small, blunt cubes. It is used in car side windows, oven doors and shower screens.

Stronger every year

Safety glass keeps changing. Your phone screen is probably made from glass strengthened in a chemical bath, where small atoms in the glass are swapped for bigger ones. These squeeze the surface and help it resist scratches and cracks, one of several tricks in how phone screens are made.

Safety glass also works alongside other inventions that protect people in cars. A cracked windscreen that stays in place helps hold people inside, while a seatbelt and an airbag stop bodies being thrown forward. Together, they have saved countless lives.

Bénédictus's dropped flask joins a long list of happy accidents. Another chemist, trying to make clear plastic for gun sights, got something so sticky it glued everything it touched, and that mistake became superglue.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Safety glass can't break.

Truth: It does break. The point is that it breaks safely. Laminated glass cracks but stays stuck to its plastic layer, and toughened glass crumbles into small, blunt pieces instead of long, sharp shards.

Try this

See how a film holds pieces together. Stick a sheet of clear tape over one side of a crisp, dry leaf or a cracker. Gently crunch both. The taped one cracks but stays in one piece, just like laminated glass.
