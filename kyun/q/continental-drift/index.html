How did we discover that continents move?

A weather scientist said the continents were drifting. Experts laughed at him for decades, until maps of the sea floor proved him right.

The short answer

For a long time, most scientists believed the continents had always been where they are. In 1912, a German scientist named Alfred Wegener argued that they had once been joined and had slowly drifted apart. He pointed to coastlines that fit like puzzle pieces, matching rocks, and identical fossils on lands now separated by oceans.

Most geologists rejected the idea, because nobody could say what force could push a continent. The answer came from the bottom of the sea. In the 1950s and 1960s, scientists mapped a long mountain range down the middle of the Atlantic and found that new sea floor was forming there and spreading outwards.

By the late 1960s, the evidence had become a theory called plate tectonics. Today, satellites measure the continents moving a few centimetres every year.

Surprising but true

Fossils of the same small reptile, Mesosaurus, are found in both Brazil and South Africa, with the whole Atlantic Ocean between them.

The puzzle that didn't go away

People noticed the fit of the coastlines as soon as there were good maps. In 1596, the Flemish mapmaker Abraham Ortelius suggested that the Americas had been torn away from Europe and Africa.

Wegener collected far more than shapes. The fossil reptile Mesosaurus lived in shallow inland waters and couldn't have crossed an ocean, yet it turns up in Brazil and in South Africa. Leaves of an ancient plant called Glossopteris appear in India, Africa, South America, Australia and even Antarctica. Rocks in hot, tropical India carried scratches left by glaciers. To Wegener, it all made sense if these lands had once formed one giant continent, which he called Pangaea.

Laughed out of the room

Wegener studied weather, not rocks, and many geologists saw him as an outsider. They asked a fair question: how could a continent plough through solid ocean floor? Wegener's guesses about the forces involved turned out to be wrong.

He kept gathering evidence and updating his book. In 1930, he died on an expedition across the Greenland ice sheet, his idea still unaccepted. A few geologists kept the idea alive, among them the South African Alexander du Toit, who added more matching rocks from both sides of the South Atlantic.

A crack in the middle of the ocean

After the Second World War, ships used echo sounders to map the sea floor. In the 1950s, the American scientist Marie Tharp, working with Bruce Heezen, plotted the soundings by hand. She found a deep valley running along the top of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, where the sea floor seemed to be splitting.

Then came the stripes. Rocks record the direction of Earth's magnetic field when they cool, and that field flips every so often. Scientists found matching stripes of flipped and unflipped rock on both sides of the ridge, like a barcode printed in mirror image. New crust was forming at the ridge and slowly moving away on both sides.

Here, at last, was Wegener's missing engine. Hot rock deep in the mantle flows slowly, carrying the plates with it.

Watching it happen

Today, GPS stations bolted to rock show the plates moving in real time. The Atlantic widens by about 2.5 cm a year, roughly as fast as your fingernails grow. India is still pushing north into Asia, which is why the Himalayas are still rising and the region has strong earthquakes.

Wegener's name is now on a crater on the Moon, a crater on Mars and a research institute in Germany that studies the polar ice where he died.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The continents drift around by floating on an ocean of liquid lava.

Truth: The rock beneath the plates is almost entirely solid. It is so hot that over millions of years it slowly flows, like very stiff toffee, and the plates ride on top of it.

Try this

Lay thin paper over a world map and trace the coastlines of South America and Africa on two separate sheets. Slide the tracings together and see how well they fit. Then look up where Mesosaurus fossils are found and mark both spots.
