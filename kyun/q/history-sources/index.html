How do historians know what's true?

Historians work like detectives, questioning every letter, coin and diary, because even old documents can be wrong, biased or completely fake.

The short answer

Historians find out about the past by studying sources: anything that gives evidence about what happened. These include letters, diaries, laws, newspapers, coins, inscriptions, photographs, buildings and people's memories.

Then they ask hard questions. Who made this source? When, and why? Were they there? Did they have a reason to exaggerate or hide something? A single source is never enough, so historians compare many sources to see where they agree. Where the evidence is thin, good historians say so. History is less a list of facts than a careful argument built on evidence.

Surprising but true

In 1983 a famous German magazine paid millions for 60 notebooks it believed were Adolf Hitler's secret diaries. Tests showed the paper and glue were made after the Second World War, and the diaries were fakes.

Primary and secondary

A primary source comes from the time being studied. A diary written during a war, a king's coin or a photo of a protest are primary sources. A secondary source is written later by someone studying the past, like a textbook or this article.

Primary sources are closer to events but can be narrow or biased. A secondary source can see the bigger picture but depends on how well its author used the evidence.

Asking who and why

Kings often carved records of their victories. They rarely mentioned their defeats. Historians call this bias, when a source leans one way. Bias doesn't make a source useless, but it means reading it carefully.

Some sources survive only in pieces. The Greek visitor Megasthenes wrote a book about India around 300 BCE, in the time of the Mauryan empire. The book is lost. We know parts of it only because later writers quoted it, and they may have changed things.

Others are surprisingly honest. The Mughal emperor Babur wrote a memoir, the Baburnama, describing his mistakes, his homesickness and the fruits he missed from Central Asia.

Cracking old scripts

Sometimes a source exists, but nobody can read it. For centuries, the edicts of Emperor Ashoka, carved on rocks and pillars across India, were a mystery, because the Brahmi script had been forgotten. In 1837, James Prinsep, an official at the Calcutta mint, worked it out. Suddenly a great emperor's own words could be heard again.

In Egypt, the Rosetta Stone, found in 1799, carried the same text in three scripts, including hieroglyphs and Greek. Using the Greek as a key, Jean-François Champollion announced in 1822 that he could read hieroglyphs.

Spotting fakes

Forgeries are a real problem. In 1983, the German magazine Stern announced it had found Hitler's secret diaries. Experts tested the paper and found a whitening chemical that wasn't used until after the war. The glue and threads were modern too. The diaries had been forged by a dealer in fake war souvenirs.

Checking where and when something was made, from its paper to its handwriting to its words, is the same habit people now need to spot fake news online.

Changing stories

History changes as new evidence appears and new people ask questions. For a long time, history books focused on kings and generals. Today historians also study farmers, women, children, workers and enslaved people, using sources such as songs, recipes, court records and oral histories, the memories people tell aloud.

Old beliefs get corrected. Many people still picture Vikings in horned helmets, but the evidence says otherwise. A good historian is always ready to say "we used to think this, but now the evidence shows something else."

Myth or fact?

Myth: Vikings wore helmets with horns.

Truth: No Viking-age helmet with horns has been found. The idea became popular in the 1800s, helped by costume designs for an 1876 opera by Richard Wagner. The one well-preserved Viking helmet ever found has no horns.

Try this

Ask two people in your family to describe the same event, like a wedding or a trip, separately. Write down what each says. Compare the stories and list where they agree and where they differ. You've just done what historians do every day.
