Why is neem called the village pharmacy?

In 2000, India helped win a fight in Europe over who owned the secrets of a tree that grows in almost every Indian village.

The short answer

Neem is a tough, shady tree that grows across India, even in dry, hot places. For thousands of years, people have used almost every part of it. Twigs became toothbrushes. Leaves went into grain stores and trunks of clothes to keep insects away. Oil from the seeds was burned in lamps and used on skin. Bitter leaves were eaten as a tonic.

Because one tree supplied so many everyday remedies, people came to call it the village pharmacy. In Sanskrit it has a grand name, sarva roga nivarini, meaning "healer of all ailments".

Science has backed up some of this, especially neem's power against insects. Other claims are still being tested, and the tree is not a cure for everything.

Surprising but true

Since 2015, almost all the urea fertiliser sold in India has been coated with neem oil.

A natural insect repellent

Neem's seeds and leaves contain a chemical called azadirachtin. Many insects find it so unpleasant that they stop eating. Young insects exposed to it struggle to grow and shed their skins properly.

That is why neem works as a gentle pesticide. It harms many crop pests but is far less dangerous to people, birds and most helpful insects than many factory-made poisons. Today neem oil is sold as an organic spray in India, the USA and many other countries.

In 1992, an American scientific body, the National Research Council, published a whole book about neem, calling it "a tree for solving global problems".

Twigs, teeth and tradition

Before plastic toothbrushes reached villages, many Indians cleaned their teeth with a fresh neem twig, called a datun. You chew one end until it frays into bristles, then scrub. Some studies suggest neem may help fight the germs that cause gum disease, though it doesn't replace brushing with toothpaste.

At festivals like Ugadi and Gudi Padwa, which mark the new year in parts of India, families eat neem leaves or flowers mixed with sweet jaggery. The bitter and the sweet together stand for the sorrows and joys of the year ahead.

Who owns a tree?

In 1994, the European Patent Office granted a patent to an American company and the US Department of Agriculture for a way of using neem oil to fight fungus on plants. A patent gives its owner the right to stop others using an invention.

Many people in India were angry. Farmers had used neem against pests for generations. The scientist and activist Vandana Shiva and others challenged the patent, showing that the method was already known in India. In 2000, the patent was cancelled. India also had a patent on turmeric in the USA cancelled in 1997.

These fights led India to build a huge digital library of traditional knowledge, so patent offices around the world can check it before granting patents.

Neem on every farm

In 2015, the Government of India made it compulsory to coat nearly all urea, the country's most-used fertiliser, with a thin layer of neem oil. The coating slows down how fast the nitrogen in urea is released, so crops can use more of it. It also made the urea less useful for factories that had been illegally buying cheap farm fertiliser.

Neem has spread far beyond India too. It is planted for shade across dry parts of Africa, and huge numbers of neem trees shade pilgrims on the plain of Arafat near Mecca in Saudi Arabia.

Handle with care

Natural doesn't always mean safe. Neem oil can be poisonous if swallowed, and doctors have treated young children who became seriously ill after being given it as a home remedy. Neem is best kept for gardens, grain stores and the outside of the body.

Some scientists are studying neem chemicals as possible medicines, but finding a safe, working drug takes many years of tests. Unlike antibiotics, which are proven to kill bacteria, most of neem's health claims still wait for strong evidence.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Neem cures every illness.

Truth: Neem really does repel many insects, and lab studies show some of its chemicals can slow germs. But that doesn't make it a cure-all. Neem oil can be harmful if swallowed, and real illnesses need a doctor.

Try this

Ask an adult to help you find a neem tree and pick a few leaves. Crush one between your fingers and smell it, then wash your hands. Count the small leaflets on one leaf stalk and draw the jagged edges. Don't swallow neem leaves or oil.
