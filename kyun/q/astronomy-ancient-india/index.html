What did ancient Indian astronomers discover?

About 1,500 years ago, a 23-year-old astronomer in India wrote that the Earth spins, and that the stars only seem to move.

The short answer

Astronomers in ancient and medieval India made careful observations of the sky and built mathematical systems to predict the movements of the Sun, Moon and planets. They worked out the length of the year very precisely, predicted eclipses and developed new tools in mathematics to do it.

The best-known is Aryabhata. In 499 CE, aged 23, he wrote the Aryabhatiya. In it, he said the Earth spins on its axis, explained eclipses as shadows rather than demons, and gave pi as 3.1416. Later astronomers, like Brahmagupta, Bhaskara II and the scholars of Kerala, added much more.

Indian astronomy also learned from, and taught, other cultures, including Greek, Persian and Arab astronomers.

Surprising but true

Around 1500 CE, the Kerala astronomer Nilakantha Somayaji described a system in which Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn all circle the Sun.

Calendars and lunar mansions

The oldest Indian astronomy grew out of keeping time for rituals and farming. A text called the Vedanga Jyotisha gives rules for a calendar based on the Sun and Moon. Scholars disagree about its age, with estimates spread across several centuries in the first millennium BCE.

Ancient Indian astronomers divided the Moon's path across the sky into 27 parts, called nakshatras or lunar mansions. Each night, the Moon sits in a different one, and each has a name, like Ashwini or Rohini. Rohini's brightest star is the orange giant that Western astronomers call Aldebaran, the eye of the bull in Taurus. These names are still used in Indian calendars, festivals and family traditions today.

Aryabhata's bold ideas

Aryabhata worked near Pataliputra, close to today's Patna. In just 121 verses, the Aryabhatiya covered mathematics and astronomy.

He wrote that the stars seem to move westwards because the Earth turns, like a person in a boat who sees the trees on the bank moving backwards. Most thinkers of his time, in India and elsewhere, believed the Earth stood still. He described lunar and solar eclipses as shadows, and he estimated the Earth's size with good accuracy.

In 1975, India named its first satellite after him.

Brahmagupta, Bhaskara and the Kerala school

In 628 CE, Brahmagupta wrote a large book on astronomy that included the earliest known rules for calculating with zero and negative numbers. He also wrote that objects fall towards the Earth because it attracts them, though he did not have a theory of gravity like Newton's.

Around 1150, Bhaskara II wrote the Siddhanta Shiromani, which was studied for centuries. From the 14th to the 16th century, scholars in Kerala, starting with Madhava, found new ways to calculate pi and the positions of planets. Around 1500, Nilakantha Somayaji described a system in which the five visible planets circle the Sun, while the Sun circles the Earth. A Danish astronomer, Tycho Brahe, proposed something similar about 90 years later.

Ideas that travelled

Astronomy in India did not grow alone. Varahamihira, in the 6th century, summarised five astronomical systems, including two linked to Greek and Roman ideas. In the 8th century, Indian astronomical texts were translated into Arabic in Baghdad. Through Arab scholars, Indian numerals, including zero, eventually reached Europe.

Historians enjoy tracing these links, though they don't always agree on who learned what from whom. Some popular claims that ancient texts predicted modern discoveries in detail are not supported by experts. What is well documented is impressive enough: careful observation, clever mathematics and a long tradition that ran from Pataliputra to Ujjain, Kerala and the stone instruments of Jantar Mantar.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Aryabhata said that the Earth goes around the Sun.

Truth: Aryabhata said the Earth spins on its axis, which was a bold idea. But in his system the Sun and planets still moved around the Earth. The idea of the Earth orbiting the Sun was argued in Europe by Copernicus about a thousand years later.

Try this

In a dark room, shine a torch at a ball (the Moon) and slowly move a bigger ball (the Earth) between them. Watch the Earth's shadow fall on the Moon. This is the explanation for a lunar eclipse that Aryabhata wrote down about 1,500 years ago.
