Why do Indian films have so many songs?

In many countries, musicals are rare. In India, most films sing.

The short answer

Indian cinema grew out of traditions where stories were sung and danced, like folk theatre.

Songs show feelings, dreams and celebrations in a way words alone can't. A song can show a couple falling in love across a whole season, or a hero's sorrow, in four minutes. They also give a long film breaks, like the songs in a stage show.

Playback singers record the songs, and actors lip-sync on screen. Songs are often released before the film, and a hit soundtrack can fill cinema halls.

Surprising but true

India's first talking film, Alam Ara (1931), is lost forever. No copy of it survives.

Songs before cinema

Indian storytelling has long mixed words, music and dance. Sanskrit drama, temple performances, the Ramlila and village folk theatre all used song. In the 1800s, Parsi theatre companies toured India with plays full of music, drama and spectacle. Early filmmakers grew up watching them.

So when films learned to talk, it seemed natural for them to sing. Alam Ara, India's first talking film, made by Ardeshir Irani in 1931, had seven songs. A year later, a film called Indrasabha had around 70. Audiences loved them.

The voice behind the face

At first, actors had to sing for themselves, live on set, with musicians sometimes hidden behind scenery out of camera range. That meant actors were often hired for their voices, not their acting.

In 1935, filmmakers in Calcutta began recording songs separately and having actors mime to them. This was playback singing, and it changed everything. Now the best singers could sing for any actor. Singers such as Lata Mangeshkar, Mohammed Rafi, Kishore Kumar and Asha Bhosle sang thousands of songs. In 2011, Guinness World Records named Asha Bhosle the most recorded artist in music history.

Radio and the countdown

In the early 1950s, India's information minister decided film songs were too low-class for All India Radio, and they were largely taken off the air. Listeners simply retuned to Radio Ceylon, broadcasting from Colombo in Sri Lanka. Its weekly countdown, Binaca Geetmala, presented by Ameen Sayani, drew huge audiences from 1952.

All India Radio soon gave in and launched Vividh Bharati in 1957, packed with film music. Songs became the soundtrack of daily life: in tea stalls, buses and wedding processions.

Making a song today

Today a film song is usually recorded months before it is filmed. The composer, lyricist and singer work in a studio, often recording each instrument and voice separately. Then the director and a choreographer plan the dance, and the actors learn to mime every word in time with the recording.

Songs are often shot in beautiful places far from the story's setting. Director Yash Chopra filmed so many songs in the Swiss Alps that Indian tourists began visiting the same valleys.

Composers and their sound

Music directors became stars too. S.D. Burman and his son R.D. Burman mixed Indian folk with jazz and rock. Ilaiyaraaja shaped Tamil film music with orchestras. A.R. Rahman used synthesisers and computers to create new sounds in the 1990s, and won two Oscars in 2009 for Slumdog Millionaire.

In 2023, "Naatu Naatu" from the Telugu film RRR, composed by M.M. Keeravani, became the first song from an Indian film to win the Oscar for Best Original Song.

Musicals around the world

Hollywood once made many musicals, like The Wizard of Oz in 1939 and Singin' in the Rain in 1952, but they became less common. Indian cinema kept singing. Today some films have fewer songs, or play them in the background instead of having stars dance. But songs still carry stories, and still give listeners the shivers that scientists link to music and emotion.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Indian film stars always sing their own songs.

Truth: Most actors mime, or lip-sync, to songs recorded by professional playback singers. Fans often know the singer's name as well as the actor's, and some singers are as famous as the stars.

Try this

Watch a song from an Indian film with the sound off, then again with it on. Write down what the song tells you that the dialogue does not, such as a feeling, a dream or time passing. Then try making up a short song for a scene from your own day.
