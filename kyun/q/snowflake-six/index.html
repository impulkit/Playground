Why does every snowflake have six arms?

Every snowflake is different. But almost all have six arms.

The short answer

Snowflakes are six-sided because ice crystals are six-sided, the same reason ice floats.

A water molecule is shaped a bit like a boomerang: one oxygen atom with two hydrogen atoms sticking out at an angle. When water freezes, the molecules link up in rings of six, making a honeycomb-like pattern. A tiny ice crystal therefore starts life as a flat hexagon, and arms sprout from its six corners.

The shape of the arms depends on the temperature and wetness of the air the flake falls through.

Surprising but true

Snowflakes are like tiny weather diaries: their shape records the air they fell through.

Built from vapour, not water

Snowflakes begin high in a cloud, where the air is below freezing. Water vapour, the invisible gas form of water, settles onto a speck of dust and turns straight into ice. More vapour keeps arriving and freezing, so the crystal grows.

The corners of the hexagon stick out a little further into the damp air, so they catch vapour faster and grow into arms. All six arms sit in the same air at the same moment, so they grow almost alike. That is why the result has symmetry, even though no part of the flake "knows" what the others are doing.

A diary written in ice

In the 1930s, the Japanese physicist Ukichiro Nakaya grew the first artificial snowflakes in a cold laboratory. He found that the temperature and moisture of the air decide the shape. Near −2 °C, crystals tend to form flat plates. Around −5 °C, they grow as needles and hollow columns. Near −15 °C in damp air, they grow the classic stars with fern-like arms.

As a flake falls, it passes through layers of different air, and each layer adds its own style. So a snowflake's shape records its journey, like a diary written in ice. Nakaya called snow "a letter sent from heaven".

The farmer with a camera

Wilson Bentley grew up on a farm in Jericho, Vermont, in the USA. On 15 January 1885, aged 19, he became the first person known to photograph a single snowflake, by fixing a camera to a microscope. He caught flakes on a black board and worked fast in the cold before they melted or his breath spoiled them.

Bentley photographed about 5,000 snowflakes over 40 years and never found two alike. In 1931, a book of his pictures came out, and he died later that year.

Not always six

Most snowflakes have six arms, but nature sometimes bends the rule. Scientists who photograph snow crystals have found rare three-sided flakes, and even twelve-armed ones, made when two six-armed crystals grow stuck together at a twist.

Many snowflakes are also lopsided, because one arm hit a different patch of air or bumped into another flake. And plenty of snow falls as broken bits, clumps or tiny columns that look nothing like the perfect stars on greeting cards. The neat six-armed star is real, but it is only one member of a big, untidy family.

Are no two really alike?

A large snowflake contains many billions of water molecules, and each takes a slightly different path through the cloud. The chances of two complex flakes matching exactly are so tiny that scientists treat it as impossible.

Very simple crystals are another matter. In 1988, a scientist named Nancy Knight found two tiny crystals from a storm cloud over the USA that looked the same under a microscope. In India, you can see snowflakes for yourself in winter in places like Gulmarg, Shimla and Ladakh. The rules are the same as in a freezer: ice builds in sixes, and snow records the weather on its way down.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Snowflakes are frozen raindrops.

Truth: Snowflakes grow straight from water vapour turning into ice, with no liquid stage. Raindrops that freeze on the way down make tiny ice pellets called sleet, which look nothing like snowflakes.

Try this

Take a square of paper, fold it in half to make a triangle, then fold that into three equal parts so it looks like a narrow wedge. Tear small notches along the edges with your fingers and unfold it. You get a pattern with six matching arms, just like a real snowflake.
