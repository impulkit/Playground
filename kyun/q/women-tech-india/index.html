Who are some Indian women in science and tech?

In 1933, Kamala Sohonie was refused entry to India's top science institute because she was a woman. She got in anyway, passed with distinction, and went on to earn a PhD at Cambridge.

The short answer

India has a long line of women scientists and engineers, though many of their names are less known than they should be. Anandibai Joshi became one of the first Indian women to earn a medical degree, in 1886. Kamala Sohonie forced her way into research and became a leading biochemist. E. K. Janaki Ammal helped breed better sugarcane. Anna Mani built weather instruments for the whole country.

In recent decades, women have led missile and space projects. Tessy Thomas headed work on long-range Agni missiles, and Muthayya Vanitha and Ritu Karidhal led the Chandrayaan-2 mission to the Moon.

Surprising but true

The sugarcane that sweetens much of India's tea owes a debt to E. K. Janaki Ammal, a botanist who helped breed sweeter, hardier varieties at Coimbatore in the 1930s.

Early doctors

Anandibai Joshi was married at nine and became a mother at 14. Her baby died within days, partly for lack of medical care. She decided to become a doctor. In 1883 she sailed to the US and studied at the Woman's Medical College of Pennsylvania, graduating in 1886. She fell ill with tuberculosis and died in 1887, aged 21, before she could practise for long. Her courage inspired many.

Around the same time, Kadambini Ganguly in Kolkata became one of the first women in India to graduate in medicine and went on to practise for decades.

Getting through the door

In 1933, Kamala Sohonie applied to the Indian Institute of Science in Bengaluru. Its director, the Nobel-winning physicist C. V. Raman, at first refused to admit women. She protested and was allowed in on probation. She passed with distinction, and the next year the institute began accepting more women.

She went on to Cambridge, and in 1939 she earned a PhD in biochemistry. Back in India, she studied the nutrition in foods like neera, a drink made from palm sap, and her work helped improve diets for poor children.

E. K. Janaki Ammal, from Kerala, earned a PhD in botany at the University of Michigan in 1931. At the Sugarcane Breeding Institute in Coimbatore, she helped create varieties suited to Indian conditions, helping India grow more of its own sugar.

Measuring the sky

Anna Mani studied physics with C. V. Raman. In 1948, she joined the India Meteorological Department, the IMD. At the time, India imported almost all its weather instruments. Anna Mani led a workshop that designed and built them in India, from rain gauges to tools for measuring sunlight and ozone. She later mapped wind and solar energy across India, long before renewable energy was popular.

Missiles and Moon missions

Tessy Thomas, an engineer from Kerala, joined the Defence Research and Development Organisation and worked on India's Agni missiles. She was project director for Agni-IV, first successfully tested in 2011, and the newspapers called her "Agni Putri". She is often called the first woman to head a missile project in India.

At ISRO, women have played big roles in recent missions. For Chandrayaan-2, launched in 2019, Muthayya Vanitha was project director and Ritu Karidhal was mission director. In 2014, photos of women scientists celebrating India's Mars Orbiter reaching Mars were shared around the world.

Still a long way to go

Women make up a large share of science students at Indian universities, but far fewer reach senior research jobs. Many leave after marriage or children, or face bias at work. Programmes now offer fellowships to help women return to research after a break.

Astronaut Kalpana Chawla, born in Karnal, flew to space on NASA missions in 1997 and 2003. As a girl she watched small planes at the local flying club, and she went on to study aerospace engineering in Chandigarh and the US. She died in 2003 when the shuttle Columbia broke apart on its return, and India named a weather satellite after her.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Indian women only began working in science and technology recently.

Truth: Indian women were earning medical degrees in the 1880s and doing research in physics, chemistry and botany by the 1930s and 1940s. They often worked against rules and attitudes that tried to keep them out.
