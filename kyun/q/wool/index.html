How does sheep's wool become a sweater?

A sheep's coat grows all year, and once it is cut, washed, combed and twisted, it can keep a person warm on a Himalayan night.

The short answer

Wool is the hair of sheep, grown into a thick, curly coat called a fleece. Once a year, usually in spring, a shearer clips the fleece off in one piece, a bit like peeling an orange.

The raw fleece is greasy and full of dirt and bits of grass. It is washed, then combed so the fibres lie in the same direction. Next, the fibres are twisted together into yarn by spinning. Finally, the yarn is knitted into a sweater or woven into a shawl or blanket. Some yarn is dyed bright colours before it is used.

Surprising but true

Each wool fibre is covered in tiny overlapping scales, like roof tiles. In hot, soapy water they lock together, which is why a woollen sweater can shrink to doll size in the wash.

Hair that keeps you warm

Wool is warm because of its shape. Each fibre is wavy, with a natural bend called crimp. When millions of crimped fibres pile together, they trap tiny pockets of air, and still air is very good at holding in heat.

Wool can also soak up a lot of moisture from sweat or damp air before it starts to feel wet. That is why mountain shepherds and sailors have trusted woollen clothes for centuries, and why a wool blanket still feels cosy in a damp Shimla winter.

The first woolly sheep

People tamed wild sheep about 10,000 years ago in West Asia, in the same region where farming began. Those early sheep were kept for meat and milk. Their coats were mostly hair, with a short woolly undercoat that they shed each year.

How did farming begin?The ancestor of corn was a skinny wild grass with a few rock-hard seeds. It took thousands of years of farmers' choices to turn it into a cob.

Over thousands of years, farmers bred sheep with more and finer wool. By the time of Mesopotamia's first cities, around 5,000 years ago, wool cloth was one of their most important products.

Shearing and scouring

A skilled shearer can clip a sheep in a couple of minutes with electric clippers. The fleece comes off in one big piece. It is sorted by quality, since wool from the shoulders is finer than wool from the legs.

Raw wool contains a natural grease called lanolin, which keeps rain off the sheep. Washing wool to remove it is called scouring. The lanolin is collected and used in skin creams and lip balms. Then the clean wool is carded, combed between wire brushes, until the fibres form a soft, even web ready for spinning.

India's warmest fibres

In the cold deserts of Ladakh, herders raise Changthangi goats at heights above 4,000 m. In winter, these goats grow an extremely fine, soft undercoat. Collected by combing, it becomes pashmina, the fibre behind famous Kashmiri shawls.

Rajasthan has long been one of India's biggest wool-producing states, though much of its wool is coarse and used for carpets. Shahtoosh, an even finer wool from the Tibetan antelope, is now banned, because hunting for it pushed the antelope towards extinction.

Sheep around the world

Australia and China produce the most wool in the world. Australia is the biggest seller of wool to other countries and leads in fine wool, most of it from Merino sheep. Merinos were first bred in Spain and grow very fine, soft wool.

In the United States, the Navajo people have raised Navajo-Churro sheep for centuries. Navajo weavers use their wool to make rugs and blankets with bold patterns, a craft that is still practised today.

From fleece to sweater

Knitting turns yarn into fabric using loops instead of the over-and-under of weaving. That makes knitted wool stretchy, which is perfect for a sweater. A hand-knitted sweater can take a skilled knitter many evenings, and in many Indian and American homes, grandparents still knit for the youngest children every winter.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Shearing hurts sheep.

Truth: Shearing is like a haircut and does not hurt when done well. Most modern sheep have been bred to keep growing wool and cannot shed it, so they need shearing to stay cool and healthy. Rough or careless shearing can cause cuts, which is why skill and care matter.

Try this

Take a small tuft of woollen yarn or raw wool and fluff it out. Dip it in warm, soapy tap water and roll it between your palms for a few minutes, pressing gently. It should slowly turn into a firm little felt ball. Let it dry and see how tightly the fibres have gripped.
