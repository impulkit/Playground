How far have the Voyager probes gone?

Launched in 1977, Voyager 1 is so far away that a radio message from Earth takes almost a whole day to reach it.

The short answer

The two Voyager space probes are the most distant human-made objects. Voyager 1 is more than 25 billion km from Earth, about 170 times farther than Earth is from the Sun. Voyager 2 is more than 21 billion km away. Both are still travelling outward at around 55,000 to 61,000 km/h, and both are still sending messages home.

They were launched in 1977 to visit the giant planets. After that job was done, they kept going. Voyager 1 crossed into interstellar space, the space between the stars, in 2012. Voyager 2 followed in 2018.

Surprising but true

Voyager's radio transmitter has about the power of a fridge light bulb, yet giant dishes on Earth still pick up its signal from over 25 billion km away.

A once-in-176-years chance

In the late 1970s, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune were lined up in a way that happens only about once every 176 years. A spacecraft could swing past one planet and use its gravity to fling it on to the next, like a slingshot. NASA took the chance.

Voyager 2 launched first, on 20 August 1977. Voyager 1 followed on 5 September, on a faster path, so it overtook its twin. Each probe weighed about 800 kg, about the weight of a small car.

The grand tour

Both Voyagers flew past Jupiter in 1979. They showed its Great Red Spot up close and discovered volcanoes erupting on its moon Io, the first active volcanoes seen beyond Earth. Both then visited Saturn. Voyager 1 swung close to Saturn's giant moon Titan, which bent its path up and out of the plane of the planets.

Voyager 2 carried on. It reached Uranus in 1986 and Neptune in 1989. It is still the only spacecraft to have visited either planet. Almost everything we know about them up close comes from those brief flybys.

On 14 February 1990, Voyager 1 turned its camera back towards home. From about 6 billion km away, Earth appeared as a tiny speck, less than a single pixel, in a beam of sunlight. The astronomer Carl Sagan called it the Pale Blue Dot.

A message for strangers

Each Voyager carries a golden record, a gold-plated copper disc 30 cm wide, with a needle and pictures showing how to play it. It holds greetings in 55 languages, sounds of Earth such as rain, birds and a mother's kiss, and 90 minutes of music from around the world.

One track is "Jaat Kahan Ho", a song in raga Bhairavi sung by the Hindustani classical singer Kesarbai Kerkar. Listen to more about Hindustani and Carnatic music. Other tracks include Bach, Beethoven, and Chuck Berry's rock and roll song "Johnny B. Goode".

The chance that anyone finds a record is tiny. Its makers saw it as a message about who we are as much as a letter to aliens.

Calling home

The Voyagers run on electricity from the heat of decaying plutonium. That power drops a little every year, so engineers have switched off heaters and instruments one by one to keep the most important ones working.

Their transmitters are about as strong as a fridge light bulb. By the time the signal reaches Earth, it is billions of times weaker than the power of a watch battery. NASA's Deep Space Network catches it with giant dishes in California, Spain and Australia.

In late 2023, Voyager 1 began sending back gibberish. Engineers traced the problem to a single failed memory chip. Working with computer code written in the 1970s, they moved the affected instructions to other parts of its memory. By April 2024, Voyager 1 was sending sensible data again.

How long can they last?

NASA expects the Voyagers to run out of power for any instrument some time in the 2030s. After that, they will drift silently on. In about 40,000 years, Voyager 1 will pass within about 1.6 light-years of a small star called Gliese 445. Even then, it will have crossed only a tiny fraction of our galaxy.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Voyagers have left the solar system completely.

Truth: They have passed out of the Sun's bubble of solar wind into interstellar space. But the Sun's gravity still holds a vast cloud of icy objects much farther out, and it will take the Voyagers tens of thousands of years to get beyond it.
