What do flag colours mean?

Very few national flags use purple, and the reason goes back to an ancient dye made from thousands of crushed sea snails.

The short answer

Flag colours mean whatever the people who choose them decide they mean. There is no worldwide code. Red might stand for courage on one flag, for blood shed in a war on another, and for a revolution on a third.

Some countries write the meaning into law or official guides. Others leave it open, and people tell different stories about the same colours. Often a colour was picked because it was already on an old royal banner, a political party's flag or a religious symbol, and the meaning came later. Flags are a kind of shorthand. They pack a nation's history and hopes into a piece of cloth that a child can draw in a minute.

Surprising but true

The US Congress never gave the colours of the American flag official meanings. The often-quoted meanings were written for the country's Great Seal in 1782.

India's tricolour

India's flag was adopted on 22 July 1947, just before independence. It is based on a design by Pingali Venkayya used during the freedom struggle. It has three equal bands: saffron on top, white in the middle and green at the bottom.

According to the Government of India, saffron stands for strength and courage, white for peace and truth, and green for fertility, growth and the land. In the centre is a navy-blue wheel with 24 spokes, the Ashoka Chakra, taken from the Lion Capital at Sarnath, built by the emperor Ashoka. It stands for the wheel of law and movement. For years the Flag Code required the flag to be made of hand-spun khadi, linking it to Gandhi's freedom movement. In 2021–22 the rules were changed to allow machine-made and polyester flags too.

The Stars and Stripes

The US flag has 13 red and white stripes for the 13 colonies that declared independence from Britain in 1776. The 50 white stars on blue stand for today's 50 states. A new star is added whenever a state joins, and the current design has been used since 1960, after Hawaii became a state.

The colours have no official meaning in law. In 1782, when Charles Thomson described the Great Seal, he wrote that white meant purity and innocence, red meant hardiness and valour, and blue meant vigilance, perseverance and justice. People later applied those words to the flag.

Why some colours are common

Red, white, blue, green, yellow and black fill most flags. One reason is practical. These dyes were cheap and lasted in sun and rain. Purple was the opposite. The famous ancient dye, Tyrian purple, came from sea snails, and making it was slow, smelly and costly.

Today only a couple of national flags include purple, such as Dominica's, which shows a purple-feathered parrot.

Colours that travel

Neighbouring countries often share colours. Many African countries use red, yellow and green, colours taken from the flag of Ethiopia, one of the few African countries never colonised. Many Arab countries use red, white, green and black, from a flag flown in the Arab Revolt of 1916.

Many Muslim-majority countries use green, a colour linked with Islam. Pakistan's flag is green and white, with the white standing for religious minorities. Nordic countries, such as Denmark, Sweden and Norway, all use a cross shifted to one side.

Odd shapes and old flags

Nearly all national flags are rectangles. Switzerland and Vatican City use squares. Nepal is the only one that is neither, with two stacked pennants. Denmark's red flag with a white cross is often called the oldest national flag still in use, going back at least to the 1300s.

Studying flags has its own name: vexillology, from the Latin word for a flag.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The colours of the US flag have official meanings set by law.

Truth: No law gives them meanings. The idea that red stands for valour, white for purity and blue for justice comes from a description of the Great Seal of the United States written in 1782, not of the flag.
