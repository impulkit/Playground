How are films dubbed into other languages?

When a Hollywood hero speaks Tamil or a Telugu hero speaks Hindi, someone in a dark recording booth is matching every breath he takes.

The short answer

Dubbing means replacing the voices in a film with new voices speaking another language. Translators rewrite the script, voice actors watch the film in a studio, and they speak the new lines in time with the actors' lips. Sound engineers then mix the new voices with the original music and sound effects.

Subtitles take a different route. The original voices stay, and short lines of translated text appear at the bottom of the screen. Many films reach the world both ways: dubbed for people who like to listen, subtitled for people who want to hear the actors' real voices.

Surprising but true

In India, Doordarshan once showed film songs with the lyrics running along the bottom of the screen, karaoke style, to help millions of people practise reading.

Inside a dubbing studio

A dubbing studio is a small, quiet room with a screen, a microphone and headphones. The voice actor watches a few seconds of a scene, listens to the original, then records their line. They repeat it until the timing feels right. A director sits nearby, checking that the emotion fits as well as the timing.

Good dubbing artists become famous in their own right. In India, the same artist may voice a Hollywood superhero in Hindi for years, film after film. Children who grow up with dubbed cartoons often know the dubbed voice better than the original.

The translator's puzzle

Translating for dubbing is tricky. A line must mean the same thing, fit the same number of seconds and roughly match the mouth. If a character closes their lips on screen, the new line needs a sound such as "m", "b" or "p" near that moment.

Jokes are the hardest. A pun in English may make no sense in Marathi, so translators often invent a new joke that gets a laugh at the same moment. Songs may be rewritten completely so they rhyme and fit the tune.

A country of dubbed films

India has 22 official languages listed in its Constitution and hundreds more spoken every day, as languages of India explains. So dubbing is big business here. Hollywood films regularly come out in Hindi, Tamil and Telugu as well as English.

South Indian films dubbed into Hindi became huge on TV channels and online, years before they filled cinemas in the north. Some big films, such as Baahubali, were shot in both Telugu and Tamil and dubbed into Hindi and Malayalam. This helped South Indian cinema reach audiences across the country.

Subtitles that teach reading

In the 1990s, the Indian researcher Brij Kothari had an idea while watching a Spanish film with Spanish subtitles. What if Indian film songs on TV showed their lyrics in the same language, word by word? People singing along would practise reading without trying.

His project, Same Language Subtitling, ran with Doordarshan for years on popular song programmes. Studies by the project found that regular viewers improved their reading. The idea has since been tried in other countries.

Dubbing or subtitles?

Countries choose differently. Germany, Italy and Spain have dubbed most foreign films for decades. The Netherlands and Scandinavian countries usually show subtitles, partly because their audiences are smaller and dubbing costs more. Many people there grow up hearing English every day on screen.

Subtitles also matter for people who are deaf or hard of hearing. Captions for them describe sounds too, such as "[door slams]" or "[soft music]". Some streaming viewers now switch subtitles on even in their own language, so they never miss a whispered line.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Subtitles are a word-for-word translation of what is said.

Truth: Subtitles usually shorten speech, because people read more slowly than actors talk. Subtitle writers keep the meaning and cut repeated or less important words.

Try this

Pick a short cartoon clip and play it with the sound off. With a friend, write your own lines in a different language or in your own words, then perform them live while the clip plays. Try to start and stop exactly when the mouths move.
