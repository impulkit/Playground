Who are India's nomadic communities?

Some families in India still walk hundreds of kilometres a year with their sheep, goats, buffaloes or camels, and carry their whole home on a few animals' backs.

The short answer

India's nomadic communities are groups who move from place to place for their living, instead of staying in one village. Some are herders who follow fresh grass with their animals. Others were traders, performers, healers or craftspeople who travelled from village to village selling their skills.

There are hundreds of such communities, from the Gaddi shepherds of Himachal to the Rabari camel herders of Gujarat and Rajasthan. Many have now settled down, at least for part of the year. Together with groups once called "criminal tribes", they may number around 10 to 12 crore people, though nobody has ever properly counted.

Surprising but true

The fine pashmina wool in costly shawls comes from goats herded by Changpa nomads on the Changthang plateau of Ladakh, at around 4,500 metres.

Following the grass

Herding nomads move with the seasons. Each spring, Gaddi shepherds lead their sheep and goats up into the high meadows of the Himalaya. In autumn, before the snow, they walk back down to the warm valleys. The trek can take weeks and cross passes higher than 4,000 metres.

Bakarwals in Jammu and Kashmir do the same with goats and sheep. Van Gujjars in Uttarakhand move herds of buffaloes between forests and hills. High in Ladakh, Changpa families live in yak-hair tents and herd goats whose soft undercoat becomes pashmina wool. The goats grow that fleece to survive winters that drop well below minus 20 °C.

Traders, acrobats and snake charmers

Not every nomad herded animals. For centuries, Banjara traders drove long lines of bullocks loaded with salt and grain across India. Some caravans had thousands of animals, and armies hired them to carry supplies. Their women still wear some of India's most dazzling clothes, stitched with tiny mirrors, cowrie shells and old coins that flash and jingle as they walk.

Today Banjara and Lambadi families live mostly in settled hamlets called tandas, from Rajasthan to Telangana and Karnataka. Many still speak their own language, Gor Boli, which has no script of its own but is passed on through songs.

Nat families were acrobats and tightrope walkers. Bahurupis were mimics and actors who dressed up as gods, kings or police officers. In Rajasthan, the Kalbelia were snake charmers. Their swirling black-skirted dance is now on UNESCO's list of the world's living heritage, and some Kalbelia dancers have performed on stages around the world.

A law that branded whole communities

In 1871 the British passed the Criminal Tribes Act. It labelled entire communities, often wandering ones, as "born criminals". Every adult had to report to the police, and children grew up under suspicion. It was a deeply unfair law that judged people by their birth.

India repealed it in 1949, and in 1952 the communities were "denotified", or removed from that list. Many still mark 31 August as their freedom day. But prejudice lasted much longer than the law, and some of these families still struggle to get land, school places or ID papers.

Life on the move today

Modern life has squeezed the old routes. Grazing land has become farms, roads and towns. Forests have fences. Camels and bullocks are no longer needed to carry goods. Many children of herders now go to school and take city jobs, while grandparents keep the flocks.

Some government schemes now run seasonal hostels, so herders' children can study while families move. India's 2027 census may count denotified tribes separately for the first time since 1911, which would finally show how many people belong to these groups. Herders still bring animals to great fairs like Pushkar, and their wandering songs fed much of India's folk music. And the long walks themselves? Shepherds are not the only ones. Wildebeest, caribou and elephants make journeys every year that rival any herder's, as the story of animal migration shows.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Nomads wander about with no plan.

Truth: Most herding communities follow the same routes, at the same times of year, that their families have used for generations. They know exactly which meadow will be green and which well will have water.
