What is the hottest place on Earth?

The world's hottest official temperature, 56.7 °C, was recorded in a place called Furnace Creek, and some scientists still doubt it.

The short answer

The highest air temperature ever officially recorded is 56.7 °C (134 °F), measured at Furnace Creek in Death Valley, California, on 10 July 1913. That is hot enough that a metal car door handle left in the sun could burn your hand.

But "hottest place" can mean different things. It might mean the highest air temperature on one day, the hottest ground, or the place that is hottest all year round. Each has a different winner. And because old records were taken with simple instruments, scientists still argue about some of them.

Surprising but true

For 90 years the world record was a reading of 58 °C from Libya, until experts reinvestigated it in 2012 and threw it out.

Death Valley

Death Valley is a long, deep desert valley in eastern California. Parts of it lie below sea level, the lowest at about 86 m below. Hot air gets trapped between the steep mountains on either side, and the dark, bare ground soaks up sunshine and heats the air further.

Summers there regularly pass 50 °C. In 2020 and 2021, it reached about 54.4 °C, among the highest temperatures reliably measured anywhere. Some weather historians think the 1913 record was a mistake, and that these newer readings may be the true highest. Others accept the old record. The question is still open.

The record that was thrown out

For 90 years, the world record was 58 °C, measured at El Azizia in Libya in 1922. In 2012, a panel of experts from the World Meteorological Organization re-examined it.

They found several problems. The observer was new to the job, the instrument was a type that is easy to misread, and the reading did not match nearby places. The panel threw the record out, and Death Valley took over. A similar thing happened in India in 2024, when a weather station in Delhi reported 52.9 °C. The India Meteorological Department checked it and found the sensor was faulty.

The hottest ground

Air temperature is measured in the shade. The ground in full sun gets much hotter. NASA satellites have measured land surface temperatures of around 70 °C in the Lut Desert of Iran, a region of dark rock and sand.

At Dallol, in Ethiopia, the average temperature over a whole year was measured at about 34 °C in the 1960s, one of the highest averages known for any inhabited place. Dallol also has bubbling hot springs, acid pools and salt crusts in yellow, green and orange.

India's hottest days

India's official record is 51 °C, measured at Phalodi in Rajasthan on 19 May 2016. Churu, also in Rajasthan, reached 50.8 °C in 2019. Before the monsoon arrives, much of north and central India can swelter above 45 °C for days at a time.

Heat is harder on the body when the air is also humid. Your body cools itself by sweating, and the sweat needs to evaporate. In very humid air it can't, which is why a 38 °C day in Chennai can feel worse than a 42 °C day in Jaipur. Drinking enough water and staying in the shade really do matter.

Hotter than Earth

As the climate warms, record-breaking heatwaves are becoming more common, as explained in climate change. But Earth's hottest places are still mild compared with other spots in the solar system.

The surface of Venus is about 465 °C, hot enough to melt lead, as you can read in Venus. The surface of the Sun is about 5,500 °C. Deep inside the Earth, the core is thought to be about as hot as the surface of the Sun.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The world record for heat is 58 °C, measured at El Azizia in Libya.

Truth: That reading, from 1922, was the official record for 90 years. In 2012, a World Meteorological Organization panel found problems with how it was measured and threw it out.

Try this

On a sunny day, ask an adult to help you put one thermometer in the shade and another in full sun, both off the ground, for 15 minutes. Compare the readings. Then feel a dark surface and a light surface that have been in the sun, and notice which is hotter.
