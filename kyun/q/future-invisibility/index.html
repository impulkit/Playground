Can scientists make things invisible?

In 2024, scientists rubbed a yellow food dye on the skin of living mice and, for a while, could see their insides through it.

The short answer

Sort of, but not like in films. Scientists have made "cloaks" that bend waves around small objects so that they seem to vanish. The first, built at Duke University in North Carolina in 2006, hid a small copper cylinder from microwaves, the kind of waves used for radar and wifi.

Hiding something from visible light, the light our eyes see, is much harder. Visible light has very short waves and many colours. The cloaks that work for it so far are small, work only from some angles, or use simple tricks with lenses and mirrors. A coat that makes you vanish from every direction is still science fiction.

Surprising but true

A perfectly invisible person would be blind. To see, your eyes must absorb light, and anything that absorbs light can't be invisible.

How you see anything

You see an object because light bounces off it into your eyes, or because it blocks light coming from behind. To be invisible, an object would have to let light carry on exactly as if it wasn't there, with no reflection, no shadow and no bending.

Glass is a good start, as it lets light through. But you can still see a glass because its edges bend light, which is the same effect that makes lenses work. If you put a glass rod into oil that bends light by the same amount, the rod almost disappears.

Bending light around

In 2006, physicists led by John Pendry in London and David Smith at Duke designed a ring of special materials called metamaterials. These are made of tiny repeating structures, smaller than the waves they control, that can steer waves in unusual directions. The ring guided microwaves around the object inside, like water flowing round a rock and joining up again behind it.

Since then, researchers have made cloaks that hide small objects from visible light, using crystals or tiny patterned surfaces, but usually only for one colour or from one direction. Making a large, flexible metamaterial for every colour is the big unsolved challenge.

Tricks with lenses

In 2014, scientists at the University of Rochester used four ordinary lenses to make a simple cloak that hid objects behind it from a range of viewpoints. Other inventors use "lenticular" sheets, the ridged plastic found on some 3D postcards, which can blur out an object standing behind them.

These cloaks work best if you stand in the right place. They are fun, but you can usually see that something odd is going on.

See-through animals

Nature had the idea first. Glass frogs in Central and South American rainforests have see-through bellies, and when they sleep they hide most of their red blood cells in their liver to become even harder to see. Many jellyfish, shrimp and baby eels in the sea are almost transparent.

In 2024, scientists at Stanford University found a surprising way to make living skin transparent. They rubbed tartrazine, a yellow dye used in snacks and drinks, onto the bellies of mice. It made the skin bend light more evenly, so the scientists could see organs underneath. When the dye was washed off, the skin went back to normal. The method could help doctors look under the skin without cutting.

Hiding from radar

Some of the most useful invisibility has nothing to do with eyes. Stealth aircraft are shaped with flat angles and coated in special materials so that radio waves from radar bounce away instead of back to the radar station. To radar, a large plane can look as small as a bird.

The blind invisible person

There is a strange catch for anyone hoping to become invisible. Your eyes work by absorbing light. A perfectly invisible person would let light pass through their eyes, so they would see nothing at all. A real invisible spy would need two little dark dots for eyes.

Animals have found a cleverer answer than vanishing. They blend in instead, and some of the best at it are explained in camouflage.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Scientists already have Harry Potter-style invisibility cloaks that hide a person completely.

Truth: Real cloaks hide small objects, from certain angles, or for certain kinds of waves such as microwaves. Some cheap lens tricks can hide things from one viewpoint. Nobody has made a flexible cloak that hides a person in ordinary light from every direction.

Try this

Put a clear glass in a slightly bigger clear bowl and fill both with cooking oil (with an adult's help). The small glass almost disappears. This happens because glass and oil bend light by nearly the same amount, so light passes straight through without showing the glass's edges.
