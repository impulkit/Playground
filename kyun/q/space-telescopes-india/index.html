What is India's AstroSat?

India's AstroSat can look at the same star in ultraviolet, visible light and X-rays at the same moment, something few space telescopes can do.

The short answer

AstroSat is India's first space observatory built for astronomy, a satellite carrying several telescopes that study the universe. ISRO launched it on 28 September 2015, on a PSLV rocket from Sriharikota. It circles Earth about 650 km up.

Its special skill is seeing many kinds of light at once. Stars, galaxies and black holes give off visible light, but also ultraviolet light and X-rays, which our eyes can't see and which Earth's air blocks. AstroSat carries five instruments, so it can watch the same object in ultraviolet, visible light and X-rays at the same time.

That is useful because many things in space change quickly. Watching them across several kinds of light at once shows what is really happening.

Surprising but true

In 2020, Indian astronomers using AstroSat reported ultraviolet light from a galaxy about 9.3 billion light-years away, light that set out long before Earth existed.

Why go to space?

Light from space comes in many forms, from radio waves to visible light to X-rays. They are all the same kind of energy with different wavelengths, like notes on a very long piano. Our eyes only see a narrow middle part, the colours of the rainbow.

Earth's atmosphere protects us by soaking up X-rays and most ultraviolet light. That is good for our skin and eyes, but bad for astronomers who want to see those kinds of light. So, like NASA's Hubble, AstroSat works from above the air.

Five instruments in one

AstroSat's ultraviolet telescope, called UVIT, takes sharp pictures of hot young stars and distant galaxies. Its detectors were made with help from the Canadian Space Agency.

Three X-ray instruments measure X-rays of different energies. One of them, LAXPC, is especially good at catching rapid flickers from matter swirling around black holes and dense, dead stars called neutron stars. A fifth instrument scans the sky for sudden X-ray outbursts so the others can turn and look.

The instruments were built by ISRO with research institutes across India, including TIFR in Mumbai, the Indian Institute of Astrophysics in Bengaluru, IUCAA in Pune and the Raman Research Institute. The University of Leicester in the UK helped with one of the X-ray cameras.

Light from far, far away

In 2020, a team led by astronomers at IUCAA reported that UVIT had detected extreme ultraviolet light from a galaxy called AUDFs01. The galaxy is about 9.3 billion light-years away, so that light left it long before the Sun and Earth formed.

Light like this is easily absorbed on its long journey. Catching it helps scientists understand how early galaxies lit up the gas around them, a big question about how the universe changed after the Big Bang.

AstroSat has also studied star clusters, the leftovers of exploded stars and the flashes from black holes eating gas from neighbouring stars.

A telescope for everyone

AstroSat was designed to last about five years, but it kept working long after that. Scientists from India and around the world can send in proposals to use it, and a committee decides who gets observing time. Students at Indian universities have written their research on its data.

Since then, India has launched two more space observatories. Aditya-L1, launched in 2023, studies the Sun. XPoSat, launched on 1 January 2024, measures the direction in which X-rays from bright cosmic sources vibrate, which tells scientists about the magnetic fields around black holes and neutron stars. Each one was carried into orbit from Sriharikota by an ISRO rocket.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Space telescopes are useful because they are closer to the stars.

Truth: A few hundred kilometres makes no real difference to distances of light-years. Telescopes go to space to get above the air, which blurs visible light and blocks X-rays and much ultraviolet light.

Try this

Shine a TV remote at a phone camera and press a button. Many phone cameras can see the remote's flashing infrared light, which your eyes can't. Telescopes like AstroSat work the same way, using special detectors to see light that is invisible to us.
