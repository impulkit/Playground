How does an airport body scanner work?

When you stand in an airport body scanner with your hands up, it sweeps invisible radio waves over you and maps your outline in about two seconds.

The short answer

Most body scanners at airports today are millimetre-wave scanners. When you step inside and raise your arms, two panels sweep around you. They send out very weak radio waves, a bit like the waves your phone uses, but at a higher frequency. The waves pass through clothes but bounce off skin, and off anything hidden on top of it.

A computer measures the echoes and builds a 3D map of your outline in about two seconds. Software looks for anything that shouldn't be there, such as metal, plastic or powder. If it finds something, it marks the spot on a cartoon figure, and a security officer checks just that area.

Surprising but true

Modern airport body scanners don't show the security officer your body at all. They show a cartoon outline of a person with yellow boxes where something needs checking.

Waves that go through cloth

Radio waves come in many sizes. Your FM radio uses waves several metres long. Wi-Fi uses waves about 12 centimetres long. Body scanners use waves around a centimetre long or shorter, which scientists call millimetre waves.

Waves this size slip through cotton and wool like light through glass. But skin is full of water, and water reflects them. Metal reflects them strongly. Plastic, ceramic and powder reflect them differently from skin. By comparing the echoes, the computer spots the odd shape out. These are the same family of radio waves that carry cricket commentary to a car radio, just much shorter.

The old metal arch

Some airports still use the older walk-through metal detector, an arch that beeps when you forget your belt. It works with magnetism. Coils in the arch send out short magnetic pulses hundreds of times a second.

When a pulse hits metal, it makes tiny electric currents swirl inside the metal. Those currents create their own faint magnetic field, which the coils pick up. A big enough echo means a beep. That's why coins and keys set it off, but a plastic comb does not.

The weakness is obvious. A metal detector can't find a ceramic knife or a packet of powder. Body scanners were brought in to close that gap. The scanners also have limits. They only see what sits on the skin, not inside the body, and loose or thick clothing, a bandage or a hair clip can trigger a quick check. A false alarm is common, and it doesn't mean anyone thinks you've done something wrong.

How does a metal detector find coins?A metal detector never touches the coin. It makes the coin send back a tiny magnetic echo, and listens for it.

From X-rays to cartoons

The first body scanners used weak X-rays that bounced off the skin. They showed a ghostly picture of the person's body. Many travellers felt that was too personal, and some worried about radiation, even though the dose was tiny. In 2013, the US removed them from its airports.

Now scanners use software called automatic target recognition. The officer never sees an image of you, just a standard outline, the same for everyone, with boxes showing where to look.

Bags get scanned too

Your bags take a different trip. They ride through an X-ray machine that shows what's inside in false colours. Organic things like food and plastic often appear orange, metals blue or green, and everything else in between.

Newer CT scanners, like hospital scanners, take hundreds of X-ray slices and build a 3D picture. That's why some airports in India, the US and Europe now let you leave laptops and liquids inside your bag. Machines can even recognise the chemical "fingerprint" of some explosives.

Noses still win

For all this technology, some of the best detectors at an airport have four legs. Sniffer dogs can pick out tiny traces of explosives, drugs and even fruit hidden in luggage. A dog's nose has hundreds of millions of scent sensors, compared with about 5 or 6 million in yours, and no machine has quite caught up.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Airport body scanners blast you with harmful radiation.

Truth: Today's millimetre-wave scanners use low-energy radio waves, far weaker than a mobile phone's signal held to your ear. They don't use X-rays and can't damage cells the way X-rays can.

Try this

Make a hidden-object test with a magnet on a string. Ask a friend to hide paper clips, coins and a plastic button inside socks. Dangle the magnet over each sock. Which items does it find, and which slip past? That's why airports use more than one kind of machine.
