Who was the first woman in space?

In 1963, a 26-year-old former textile worker from Russia circled the Earth 48 times, alone, and then parachuted out of her capsule to land.

The short answer

The first woman in space was Valentina Tereshkova, from the Soviet Union. On 16 June 1963, she blasted off alone in a capsule called Vostok 6. She spent nearly three days in orbit, circling the Earth 48 times, before coming back safely.

She was 26 years old. Before becoming a cosmonaut, as Soviet astronauts were called, she had worked in a textile factory and made parachute jumps as a hobby. She remains the only woman ever to fly in space completely alone.

Surprising but true

Valentina Tereshkova spent almost three days in space on her first flight, longer than all the American astronauts who had flown before her put together.

From the factory floor to the launch pad

Tereshkova grew up in a village near the city of Yaroslavl, northeast of Moscow. Her father was killed in a war when she was a toddler, and her mother worked in a cotton mill. Valentina left school at 16 and went to work in a tyre factory, then the same textile mill.

In her spare time she joined a local parachuting club and made dozens of jumps. That turned out to matter. In the early 1960s, the Soviet space programme decided to send a woman into space, partly to beat the USA to another first. They looked for skilled parachutists, because a Vostok pilot had to eject and parachute to the ground. Out of more than 400 applicants, five women were chosen for training, and Tereshkova was picked to fly.

Three days alone above the Earth

Vostok 6 launched from Baikonur in what is now Kazakhstan. Over the radio, Tereshkova used the call sign Chaika, meaning seagull. Her famous words at launch were "Hey, sky, take off your hat!"

She was in space for about 71 hours. At the time, that was more than all the American astronauts' flights added together. She kept a log, took photographs of the horizon that scientists later used to study the atmosphere, and tested how the body coped with weightlessness. She also felt sick and uncomfortable, crammed into a capsule smaller than an autorickshaw. Her mission overlapped with another Vostok flown by a male cosmonaut, and the two capsules came within about 5 km of each other.

What was the space race?In just twelve years, two rival countries went from launching a beeping metal ball to putting people on the Moon.

A jump from 7 km

The trip home was the scariest part. The Vostok capsule hit the ground too hard for a person to survive comfortably. So at about 7 km above the ground, Tereshkova ejected from her seat and came down under her own parachute.

She landed in a field in the Altai region of Russia, bruised her nose and was greeted by villagers. Reports say she ate the food they offered her, against the rules for returning cosmonauts. Within days, she was a world celebrity.

Why it took so long for the next

After Tereshkova, no other woman flew to space for 19 years. The next was Svetlana Savitskaya, also from the Soviet Union, in 1982. She later became the first woman to walk in space. America's first woman astronaut, Sally Ride, flew in 1983.

Since then, about 100 women have flown to space. They include Kalpana Chawla, who in 1997 became the first woman born in India to go to space, and Sunita Williams, who has spent many months aboard the International Space Station. In 2019, NASA astronauts Christina Koch and Jessica Meir made the first spacewalk by two women together.

Tereshkova later became a politician in Russia. Women now command space stations and are training for Moon missions. The full list of their achievements is told in women in space. And for a factory worker who learnt to jump from planes for fun, the space race between two superpowers turned a hobby into history. Among the women who followed her was a girl from Karnal who dreamed of flying, Kalpana Chawla.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The first woman in space was American.

Truth: She was Valentina Tereshkova from the Soviet Union, in 1963. The first American woman in space, Sally Ride, flew 20 years later, in 1983.
