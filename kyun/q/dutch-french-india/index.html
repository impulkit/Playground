Who else tried to rule parts of India?

In 1741, a Kerala king named Marthanda Varma defeated a Dutch army at Colachel, and Dutch power in India never recovered.

The short answer

The British were not the only Europeans who came to India to trade and stayed to rule. The Portuguese arrived first, in 1498. The Dutch, the Danes and the French followed in the 1600s. Each set up trading companies, built forts along the coast and fought both Indian rulers and each other.

In the end, the British East India Company won most of these contests. But small pockets of other European rule lasted a long time. The French held Puducherry until 1954, and the Portuguese stayed in Goa until 1961.

Surprising but true

Puducherry stayed French until 1954, seven years after the rest of India became independent, and some of its streets still have French names.

Pepper and cloth

Why did they all come? Money. Europe wanted pepper, cardamom, cinnamon and, above all, Indian cotton cloth, which was cheap, light and beautifully dyed. Whoever controlled that trade could become very rich. Ships took about six months to sail from Europe round Africa to India, so a single cargo had to be worth the risk of storms, disease and pirates. Companies sold shares to pay for the voyages, an idea that helped create modern stock markets.

The Dutch East India Company, founded in 1602, was the most powerful trading company in the world for much of the 1600s. It mostly wanted the spice islands of what is now Indonesia, but it also took over Portuguese forts in India. In 1663 the Dutch captured Kochi, and they had trading posts at Pulicat, Nagapattinam, Chinsurah in Bengal and elsewhere.

The battle of Colachel

In 1741, the Dutch tried to push into the small kingdom of Travancore in what is now southern Kerala. Its king, Marthanda Varma, met them at Colachel and defeated them.

A Dutch officer named Eustachius De Lannoy was captured. Instead of punishing him, Marthanda Varma made him a commander, and De Lannoy spent years training Travancore's army in European ways. After Colachel, Dutch power in India steadily faded. They handed their last Indian posts to the British in the early 1800s.

The Danes of Tranquebar

Denmark, a small country in northern Europe, set up a trading town at Tranquebar, now called Tharangambadi, on the Tamil Nadu coast in 1620. The Danes also held Serampore near Kolkata.

Tranquebar became a centre of printing and learning. Danish-backed missionaries printed books in Tamil there in the early 1700s. In 1845, Denmark sold its Indian towns to the British. Fort Dansborg still stands by the sea in Tharangambadi, and its rooms are now a small museum.

France and Dupleix

The French East India Company was founded in 1664 and made Pondicherry, now Puducherry, its main base from 1674. Its most ambitious governor, Joseph François Dupleix, wanted France to rule large parts of South India. In the 1740s and 1750s, the French and British fought a series of wars in the Carnatic region, each backing rival Indian princes.

The British won. The French lost the Battle of Wandiwash in 1760, and after that they were left with a handful of small towns: Puducherry, Karaikal, Mahe, Yanam and Chandernagore. These stayed French for almost two hundred years.

How it ended

After 1947, India asked France and Portugal to hand back their territories. The people of Chandernagore voted to join India in 1949. The other French towns came under Indian control in 1954, and France formally signed them over by treaty in 1962. Portugal refused to leave Goa, Daman and Diu, and in 1961 the Indian Army moved in.

You can still see the traces. Puducherry has French street names and a French school. Kochi has Dutch-era buildings, and Tharangambadi has its Danish fort. Each is a reminder that the story of Europeans in India had more than one player.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The British were the only Europeans who ruled any part of India.

Truth: The Portuguese, Dutch, Danes and French all held towns and forts in India. The French kept Puducherry until 1954, and Portugal held Goa until 1961.
