How did the chariot change war and travel?

The first wheels were heavy wooden discs that oxen dragged at walking pace. Then someone cut holes in them, and armies started to fly.

The short answer

A chariot is a light, two-wheeled cart pulled by horses, built for speed rather than for carrying loads. It appeared around 4,000 years ago, when two older inventions came together: the wheel and the tamed horse.

For about a thousand years, chariots ruled battlefields from Egypt to China. A king in a chariot could cross a plain faster than any soldier could run. Chariots also carried messengers and nobles, raced in front of huge crowds and rolled through stories and holy books. Then horse riders took over, and chariots slowly faded from war.

Surprising but true

The earliest known chariots with spoked wheels were buried in graves with their horses, on the grasslands of what is now Russia and Kazakhstan, about 4,000 years ago.

From ox cart to race car

The first wheels, from around 3500 BCE, were solid planks of wood fixed together into a disc. They were strong but heavy. Oxen pulled them at about walking speed, which was fine for moving grain or stones.

The big change was the spoked wheel. An outer rim, held up by thin wooden spokes, weighs much less than a solid disc. The oldest known examples come from graves near the Ural Mountains, on the grasslands of present-day Russia and Kazakhstan, dated to about 2000 BCE. People there buried chariots alongside the horses that pulled them.

Chariots at war

Chariots spread fast. Egypt's pharaohs built huge chariot forces, and so did the Hittites in what is now Turkey. Around 1274 BCE, the two armies met at Kadesh in Syria with thousands of chariots on the field. Pharaoh Ramesses II later carved the battle onto temple walls, claiming a great victory. The Hittite records tell a different story, and historians think it ended in a draw.

A typical chariot had a driver and a fighter with a bow or spears. It rarely smashed into enemy lines. It raced along the edges, its archer shooting, then swung away before anyone could catch it. The young pharaoh Tutankhamun was buried with six chariots. In China, rulers of the Shang dynasty were buried with whole chariots and their horses.

Chariots in India

The Rig Veda, one of the oldest Indian texts, mentions the ratha, or chariot, many times. In the Mahabharata, Krishna drives Arjuna's chariot at Kurukshetra, and their conversation there becomes the Bhagavad Gita.

In 2018, archaeologists at Sanauli in Uttar Pradesh found wooden carts covered in copper, buried in graves about 4,000 years old. Some experts call them India's oldest chariots. Others point out that their wheels were solid and no horse bones were found, so they may have been pulled by bulls. Horses seem to have been rare in South Asia then, and claims of horse bones there from before about 1500 BCE are few and disputed.

Whatever the answer, chariots became central to Indian stories and art. Temple carvings, coins and paintings over many centuries show gods, kings and heroes standing tall in them. The debate goes on, and it connects to bigger questions about the Vedic age.

Racing, temples and the end of an era

By about 900 BCE, armies had learned to fight from horseback. A rider could go where a chariot couldn't, over hills, through forests and across rivers, and chariots lost their place.

They lived on in sport and ceremony, long after generals stopped using them. The ancient Olympic Games held chariot races, and Rome's Circus Maximus could seat well over 100,000 people cheering for their teams. In India, the 13th-century Sun Temple at Konark was carved as the chariot of the sun god Surya, with 24 stone wheels and seven horses. The shadows on some of its wheels can be read like a sundial, and every year at Puri, huge wooden chariots still carry Jagannath through the streets.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Chariots were used like tanks, crashing straight into lines of soldiers.

Truth: Horses won't run into a solid wall of people. Most chariots carried archers or spear-throwers who raced along the edges of a battle, shooting and then wheeling away.
