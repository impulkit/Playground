What are the tallest things humans have built?

For about 3,800 years the tallest thing humans had ever built was a pile of stone blocks in Egypt.

The short answer

The tallest building on Earth is the Burj Khalifa in Dubai, which reaches 828 metres. That is about two and a half Eiffel Towers stacked up, or more than four Statues of Unity. It opened in 2010 and has 163 floors. On a clear day its tip can be seen from about 95 kilometres away.

Next comes Merdeka 118 in Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia, at 679 metres. The tallest tower, meaning a structure people don't live or work in, is the Tokyo Skytree at 634 metres. The record may not last. In Saudi Arabia, the Jeddah Tower had climbed past 100 floors by mid-2026 and is planned to reach about 1,008 metres, the first building a kilometre high.

Surprising but true

The tallest structure ever built before the Burj Khalifa was a radio mast in Poland, 646 metres tall, which fell over in 1991 while workers were changing its cables.

Thousands of years of stone

Around 2560 BCE, Egyptian builders finished the Great Pyramid of Giza for the pharaoh Khufu. It stood about 146 metres tall, roughly a 45-storey block. No human-made structure beat it for about 3,800 years. The first to do so was probably the spire of Lincoln Cathedral in England, finished around 1311. It blew down in a storm in 1548.

In India, the Qutub Minar in Delhi rose to about 73 metres in the 1200s and is still one of the tallest brick towers anywhere.

Iron, then steel

In 1889 Paris unveiled the Eiffel Tower, 300 metres of wrought iron. Many artists hated it and called it ugly. It held the height record until New York's Chrysler Building passed it in 1930, followed a year later by the Empire State Building.

Steel frames changed everything. Instead of thick walls holding up the weight, a skeleton of steel beams does the work. Add electric lifts, and suddenly people could live and work hundreds of metres in the air.

How do the fastest lifts in skyscrapers work?A lift in Guangzhou, China, can shoot upwards at about 75 km/h, faster than a car on most city roads.

The trouble with going higher

The biggest enemy of a tall building isn't gravity. It's wind. Air pushes against the side, and vortices, swirls of wind peeling off the corners, can make a tower rock back and forth. The Burj Khalifa's Y-shaped base and stepped sides break those swirls up. Taipei 101 in Taiwan hangs a steel ball weighing about 660 tonnes near its top. When the building sways one way, the ball swings the other and calms it.

Lifts are another headache. Steel lift ropes get so heavy over very long runs that the tallest buildings use "sky lobbies", where you change lifts partway up, like changing trains.

India reaching up

India's tallest buildings are mostly in Mumbai, where land is scarce and expensive, and several towers there rise well above 250 metres. In 2022 a pair of illegally built towers in Noida, near Delhi, set a different kind of record. The two buildings, about 100 metres tall, were brought down by controlled explosions in roughly nine seconds.

Building tall in India means planning for monsoon storms, and in the north for earthquakes, so engineers test models of big towers in wind tunnels before work begins.

Masts, statues and the one that fell

Some of the tallest things ever built are thin masts held up by steel cables. In North Dakota, USA, a TV mast called KVLY rises about 629 metres. A taller one, the Warsaw radio mast in Poland, reached 646 metres. In 1991, during work on its cables, it bent and crashed to the ground.

Statues have grown too. The Statue of Unity in Gujarat, showing Sardar Patel, opened in 2018 at 182 metres, about twice the height of the Statue of Liberty with its base. And the next giant is already on the way: engineers in Jeddah are learning to make skyscrapers behave at heights nobody has ever tried.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Skyscrapers stand perfectly still, even in a storm.

Truth: Tall buildings are designed to sway a little, often tens of centimetres at the top in strong wind. A building that couldn't bend would be more likely to crack.

Try this

Build the tallest tower you can from 20 sheets of newspaper and some tape. Try a thin straight tube first, then a wide base that narrows as it rises. Blow on each with a fan or a hairdryer on cold to test the wind. You'll discover why the Burj Khalifa is shaped like a stepped spike.
