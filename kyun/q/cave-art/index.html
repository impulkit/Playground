Who made the very first paintings?

Long before paper, pencils or paintbrushes, people were painting on rock. Some of their pictures are more than 50,000 years old.

The short answer

Nobody knows the name of the first painter, but we know where some of the oldest paintings are. On the island of Sulawesi in Indonesia, a cave wall shows three human-like figures beside a wild pig. Scientists have dated it to at least 51,200 years ago. It is the oldest known picture that tells a story.

Early artists ground coloured rocks into powder, mixed it with water or fat, and painted with fingers, chewed twigs or feathers. Some blew paint around a hand pressed on the wall, leaving a hand-shaped stencil that still says "I was here".

In India, the rock shelters of Bhimbetka in Madhya Pradesh hold thousands of paintings made over many thousands of years.

Surprising but true

Scientists date some cave paintings by measuring the thin crust of rock that grew on top of the paint.

Paint from the ground

Red and yellow came from ochre, earth coloured by iron. Black came from charcoal from the fire, or from a dark mineral called manganese oxide. At Bhimbetka, white came from limestone.

These colours have lasted because they are made of minerals, which don't rot. Many paintings also sat in sheltered spots, away from rain and strong sunlight. Paintings made out in the open have mostly washed away, so the pictures we can see today are a lucky sample. The painters of Ajanta, thousands of years later, still used many of the same earth colours.

How old is old?

Dating a cave painting is hard. Charcoal can be carbon-dated, but red ochre contains no carbon. So scientists use a clever trick. Water dripping down cave walls leaves thin crusts of a mineral called calcite. Calcite traps tiny amounts of uranium, which turns into thorium at a known rate. If a crust formed on top of a painting, the painting must be older than the crust.

This method showed that a red disc in El Castillo cave in Spain is more than 40,000 years old. Some scientists have argued that marks in other Spanish caves are over 64,000 years old, which would mean Neanderthals made them. Others question those dates, and the debate continues.

Bhimbetka, a gallery in the rocks

Bhimbetka's rock shelters sit in forested hills about 45 km from Bhopal. More than 700 shelters have been found in the area, and hundreds are painted. The pictures show hunters with bows, dancers holding hands, people riding horses and elephants, and animals such as bison, deer and tigers.

The oldest paintings may be around 10,000 years old, though exact dates are hard to pin down. Later artists painted over earlier ones, so a single wall can hold layers from many different times, rather like the stacked clues that historians use. The site became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2003.

Found by chance

Many painted caves were discovered by accident. In 1957, the archaeologist V. S. Wakankar was travelling by train when he noticed rock formations near Bhimbetka. They reminded him of painted caves he had seen in Spain and France, so he went back to explore.

In 1940, four teenagers in south-west France squeezed down a hole in the ground and found Lascaux, a cave covered in painted horses, bulls and deer about 17,000 years old. So many visitors came afterwards that their breath and the lights began to damage the paintings. The cave was closed to the public in 1963, and visitors now see a careful copy built nearby.

Why did people paint at all? Nobody can ask them. Some experts link the pictures to hunting or ceremonies. Others think they simply told stories, long before anyone learned to write.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Cave people lived deep inside the caves they painted.

Truth: Most people lived in rock shelters, near cave entrances or in camps in the open. The deepest painted caves were dark, cold places that people visited, not homes.

Try this

Make a hand stencil like the first artists. Put your hand flat on a sheet of paper and, with your other hand, dab paint all around it with a sponge or a scrunched-up cloth. Lift your hand away and you will see its outline, just like the stencils left on cave walls tens of thousands of years ago.
