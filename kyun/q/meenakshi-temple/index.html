What is the Meenakshi Temple?

Madurai's Meenakshi Temple has 14 gateway towers covered in thousands of painted gods, demons and animals. The tallest is about 52 m high.

The short answer

The Meenakshi Temple, also called Meenakshi Amman Temple or Meenakshi Sundareswarar Temple, is a huge Hindu temple in the city of Madurai, in Tamil Nadu. It is dedicated to the goddess Meenakshi, a form of Parvati, and her husband Sundareswarar, a form of Shiva.

The temple covers about 6 hectares, around the size of eight football fields, and is surrounded by high walls. Fourteen towering gateways, called gopurams, rise above it, covered in brightly painted statues. Inside are halls, shrines, a sacred tank and long corridors of carved pillars. Thousands of people visit every day, and the old city of Madurai is built in squares around it.

Surprising but true

The famous Hall of a Thousand Pillars doesn't have a thousand pillars. It has 985.

The fish-eyed queen

The name Meenakshi means "the one with fish-shaped eyes", a way of saying her eyes were beautiful. According to the temple's legend, she was born to a Pandya king of Madurai. She grew up a fearless warrior and ruled the kingdom herself, conquering lands in every direction.

When she reached Mount Kailash and met Shiva, the story says, she fell in love. Shiva came to Madurai as Sundareswarar, "the beautiful lord", and married her. Every year, in the Tamil month of Chithirai, around April or May, the city celebrates their wedding in a festival that draws huge crowds.

Towers of gods

Gopurams are the gateway towers of south Indian temples. The Meenakshi Temple has 14. The tallest, on the southern side, is about 52 m high, roughly as tall as a 17-storey building.

Every gopuram is covered in painted figures of gods, goddesses, demons, animals and characters from Hindu stories. They're made of stucco, a kind of plaster, over a frame of brick. The paint fades in the sun and rain, so the towers are repainted every few years during restoration. Each repainting is done by traditional craftspeople, and it can take many months.

A temple rebuilt

A temple has stood in Madurai for a very long time. Ancient Tamil poems from the Sangam era mention Madurai as a great city.

In the early 1300s, the temple was damaged during raids by armies from the north. Later, Madurai came under the rule of the Nayaks, who began as governors for the Vijayanagara Empire. The Nayak kings, especially Thirumalai Nayak in the 1600s, rebuilt and greatly expanded the temple. Most of what visitors see today dates from the 1500s and 1600s.

The golden lotus tank

In the middle of the temple is the Potramarai Kulam, the "golden lotus tank", with a gilded lotus at its centre. Steps lead down to the water on all sides.

A legend links it to the Tamil Sangam, the ancient gathering of poets. It's said that new works were placed on the water to be judged. Good poems floated, while poor ones sank. Nobody takes that as history, but it shows how proud Madurai was of its poets.

Pillars that sing

The Hall of a Thousand Pillars is a great hall with 985 carved stone pillars, built in the 1500s. From many spots inside, the rows of pillars seem to line up perfectly. Today it houses a museum of temple art.

Some pillars in the temple are musical. Tap them gently and they ring with different notes, because of their size and shape. Sound travels through solid stone, and each column vibrates in its own way. Visitors are asked not to tap them, to protect the carvings.

A city around a temple

Old Madurai grew around the temple in rings of square streets. Several are named after Tamil months, such as Chithirai Street and Masi Street, because festival processions pass along them in those months.

Every evening, an image of Sundareswarar is carried in a procession to Meenakshi's shrine, to rest there for the night. In the morning, the temple wakes again to bells, drums and the smell of jasmine sold by the flower sellers at its gates.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Hall of a Thousand Pillars has exactly a thousand pillars.

Truth: It has 985. The name uses "thousand" as a round number, which is common in Indian place names.

Try this

Fill five or six steel tumblers with different amounts of water and line them up. Tap each gently with a spoon and listen to the different notes. Arrange them from low to high. Some temple pillars in Madurai and elsewhere make musical notes when tapped, because each stone column is a slightly different size.
