What if you dug a hole through the Earth?

Jump into a tunnel through the centre of the Earth and you'd pop out on the other side about 38 minutes later, if you could survive the trip.

The short answer

Nobody can dig a hole through the Earth. The planet is about 12,742 kilometres across, and the deepest hole ever drilled reached just over 12 kilometres. Beyond that, rocks get too hot and squeezed for any drill to work. At the centre, the temperature is around 5,000 to 6,000 °C, about as hot as the surface of the Sun.

But physicists love this question, because it shows how gravity works. If somehow there were a tunnel straight through the middle, with no air and walls that didn't melt, you could jump in and fall all the way through. You'd zoom past the centre at roughly 8 to 10 kilometres a second and reach the far side about 38 minutes later.

Surprising but true

The deepest hole ever drilled, in Russia, took about 20 years and reached just 12.2 km, less than one five-hundredth of the way to the far side.

The deepest hole ever dug

In 1970 scientists in the Soviet Union began drilling into the Kola Peninsula, near the Arctic. They kept going for about 20 years. In 1989 the hole reached 12,262 metres, the deepest hole ever made, but only about 23 centimetres wide.

At the bottom, the rock was about 180 °C, far hotter than expected. It became soft and gooey, closing in around the drill. The project stopped. Even this record barely scratched the Earth's crust, the thin outer shell, which under the continents is usually about 30 to 50 kilometres thick.

The fall

Say you jumped into the impossible tunnel. At first you'd fall just as you would off a diving board, speeding up every second. As you went deeper, there would be less Earth below you and more above, so the pull would change.

At the very centre, the Earth would pull equally in every direction, and you'd feel weightless. But you'd be going so fast that you'd shoot straight past. On the far side, gravity would now be pulling you back, slowing you down. With no air, you'd come to a stop just as you reached the surface on the other side. If nobody grabbed you, you'd fall back and keep swinging through the planet for ever, like a pendulum.

Why 38 minutes?

For years, textbooks said the fall would take about 42 minutes. That assumed the Earth was the same all the way through. In 2015 a physics student named Alexander Klotz redid the sum using what we actually know about the inside of the Earth.

The core is made mostly of iron and nickel and is much denser than the rocks near the surface. That extra mass at the centre means gravity stays strong deeper down, so you'd pick up speed faster. His answer was about 38 minutes, roughly as long as a school lesson.

The problems with the plan

Real life would ruin the trip in many ways. Air in the tunnel would slow you to about the speed of a skydiver, and the fall would take days, not minutes. The mantle rock would be hot enough to glow. The outer core is liquid metal, swirling like a slow ocean. Pressure at the centre is more than three million times the air pressure you feel now.

The Earth's spin also causes trouble. A tunnel from pole to pole would work best. Anywhere else, the planet's rotation would push you sideways into the walls as you fell.

Where would you come out?

The point exactly opposite you on the globe is called your antipode. Most land on Earth has ocean on the other side, because about 71 percent of the planet's surface is water.

Dig down from Delhi or Bengaluru and you'd surface in the Pacific Ocean, far west of Chile or Peru. Start in Kansas and you'd pop up in the Indian Ocean, southwest of Australia. China's opposite point is mostly in Argentina and Chile. And the strangest part? Even at the centre, you'd never escape Earth's grip, because leaving it takes a very different kind of speed, called escape velocity.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If you dug straight down from the USA or India, you'd come out in China.

Truth: The point opposite most of the USA is in the Indian Ocean, southwest of Australia. The point opposite most of India is in the Pacific Ocean, west of South America. Most places on land have ocean on the other side.

Try this

Find your antipode, the spot exactly opposite you on Earth. On a globe or online map, note your latitude and longitude. Change north to south (or south to north). Then subtract your longitude from 180 and swap east for west. Look up that point. For most of India and the USA, you'll land in an ocean.
