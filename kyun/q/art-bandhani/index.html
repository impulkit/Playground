How is bandhani tie-dye made?

A single bandhani dupatta from Kutch can have thousands of tiny knots, each one tied by hand around a dot of cloth smaller than a grain of rice.

The short answer

Bandhani is a kind of tie-dye from Gujarat and Rajasthan. Artisans pinch up tiny points of cloth and tie each one tightly with thread. When the cloth is dipped in dye, the tied spots stay their original colour. The result is a pattern of small dots, sometimes forming flowers, birds, dancers or waves.

The word comes from bandh, meaning "to tie". It is one of India's oldest dyeing arts. A fine bandhani saree or dupatta can take weeks, because every dot is tied by hand.

Surprising but true

Some bandhani artisans grow one fingernail long, or wear a small pointed metal ring, to pinch up tiny points of cloth for tying.

Tying thousands of knots

First, a designer marks the pattern on the cloth, often by stamping it with a wooden block dipped in a colour that washes away. Then the tiers take over. Most are women, working at home in villages around Kutch and Jamnagar.

They pinch up a tiny point of cloth, wrap it several times with thread and move on to the next, without cutting the thread. Some grow a fingernail long or wear a small pointed metal ring to lift the cloth. Fine pieces have dots smaller than a grain of rice. A single dupatta can have thousands of knots. Some experts can tie hundreds in an hour.

Light to dark, step by step

Once tied, the cloth is dyed, starting with the lightest colour, often yellow. Some knots are then tied over the yellow areas, and the cloth goes into a darker dye, such as red. Each round of tying and dyeing adds a new colour, always moving from light to dark.

When the cloth is finally dry, the threads are pulled off with a sharp tug. The knots spring open, leaving dots of different colours and a crinkled texture. Many sellers leave the cloth still crinkled, so buyers can see it is real hand-tied work. Dyers say the water around Jamnagar helps make especially bright reds, though nobody has fully explained why.

Ancient dots

Bandhani is old. In the cave paintings at Ajanta, painted around 1,500 years ago, some figures wear clothes with dotted patterns that look like bandhani. Tie-dye of different kinds also grew up in Japan, where it is called shibori, and in West Africa, Indonesia and Peru.

In Gujarat and Rajasthan, the Khatri community has been known for bandhani for generations. Families pass down patterns and colour recipes from parents to children. Traditional dyes came from plants and minerals, such as madder for red, indigo for blue and pomegranate rind for yellow. Many dyers now use synthetic dyes, which are cheaper and brighter.

Colours that mean something

Bandhani colours often carry meaning. Red is linked with weddings and good luck. A Gujarati bride may wear a gharchola, a red and white bandhani saree with a grid of gold threads. Yellow is linked with spring and new beginnings, and some families give a yellow and red bandhani to a new mother.

In Rajasthan, a related style called leheriya makes wavy stripes instead of dots. The cloth is rolled diagonally, then tied at intervals before dyeing. It is especially popular during the monsoon festival of Teej.

In the 1960s, tie-dye became hugely popular in the USA, on T-shirts with swirls of bright colour. The idea is the same as bandhani, even if the patterns are bolder and looser. Dye recipes, from yellow turmeric to deep indigo, are a story of kitchen science and trade. And if you want to see how two dye colours combine on cloth, colour mixing explains why yellow under blue turns green. Across Gujarat, bandhani is just one of the many Kutch crafts that fill village homes with colour.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Bandhani is printed with dots by machines.

Truth: Real bandhani is tied by hand, knot by knot, and dyed by hand. Printed copies look flat, while true bandhani cloth has a slightly crinkled feel from all the knots.

Try this

With an adult's help, wrap rubber bands tightly around small pinches of an old white handkerchief. Dip it in a bowl of warm tap water with a big spoonful of turmeric for 20 minutes. Rinse, remove the bands and see your own pale dots on yellow.
