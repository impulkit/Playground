Why are some numbers lucky or unlucky?

The 2008 Beijing Olympics began at 8:08 in the evening on 8 August 2008, because in Chinese the word for eight sounds like the word for getting rich.

The short answer

Numbers become lucky or unlucky because of what they sound like, what stories people attach to them and what happens to be important in a culture. None of it is maths. It is language, history and habit.

In Chinese, eight is lucky because it sounds like the word for prosperity, while four sounds like death. In Europe and America, thirteen has a gloomy reputation going back centuries. In India, a gift of money often ends in a one, like ₹101 or ₹501, so the amount is never a round, finished number. Once a belief spreads, people notice every time it seems to come true and forget the times it doesn't, so it sticks.

Surprising but true

Many tall buildings in the USA have no floor labelled 13, and many in China, Hong Kong and Korea have no floor 4, so the lift buttons simply skip them.

Sounds like luck

Many lucky and unlucky numbers are really puns. In Mandarin, eight is bā and to grow rich is fā. That is why the Beijing Olympics opened at 8:08 pm on 8 August 2008. In 2003, a Chinese airline reportedly paid millions of yuan for the phone number 8888-8888.

Four, sì, sounds close to sǐ, death. Japanese has the same problem, so hospitals in Japan may avoid room 4, and 9 too, because ku sounds like the word for suffering. Because Chinese characters are read differently across regions, the beliefs shift a little from place to place.

The trouble with thirteen

Fear of thirteen has a long name: triskaidekaphobia. Nobody knows exactly where it began. One idea is that twelve was seen as complete: twelve months, twelve hours on a clock face, twelve signs of the zodiac. Thirteen is one too many. Christian tradition also says 13 people sat down at the Last Supper.

The fear is strong enough to shape buildings. In many American skyscrapers, the button goes from 12 to 14. The 13th floor is still there. It just has a different name. Some streets skip house number 13, and some airlines have no row 13.

Lucky numbers in India

Indian traditions are full of special numbers. In Hinduism, Buddhism and Jainism, 108 is sacred. A prayer mala usually has 108 beads, and many temples have 108 steps or 108 names for a god. Seven matters too: in a Hindu wedding, the couple walk seven rounds, the saat phere, around a sacred fire.

The extra rupee in shagun money is often explained this way: a round number like 100 ends, but 101 keeps going, wishing the growth to continue. Many families also choose wedding dates and house-warming days using astrology and lucky days.

Seven, everywhere

Ask people around the world for a lucky number and seven usually wins. Ancient people could see seven moving lights in the sky: the Sun, the Moon and five planets. That may be why there are seven days in a week. Seven also turns up in the seven wonders, seven seas and seven notes in Indian and Western music.

Seven is a prime number, so it can't be split into equal groups. Some psychologists think that makes it feel special and a little unpredictable.

Numbers in the real world

Lucky numbers cost real money. In China, flats on the 8th floor and number plates full of 8s sell for more. In Las Vegas, so many couples wanted to marry on 7 July 2007, written 7/7/07 in the USA, that it became one of the busiest wedding days in the city's history.

But the numbers have no power of their own. A dice doesn't know which face is lucky, and neither does a cricket scoreboard. The beliefs stay because people share them. So do black cats crossing the road and lemons and chillies hung over shop doors, all part of a much bigger story of superstitions.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Lucky numbers really change your chances, for example in a lottery or an exam.

Truth: A lottery machine has no idea what numbers mean to people. Every combination has exactly the same chance. Feeling lucky can make people calmer and more confident, which may help a little in an exam, but the number itself does nothing.

Try this

Run a lucky-number survey. Ask 10 or 20 family members, friends or classmates to name their lucky number and say why. Make a bar chart of the answers. See whether 7 comes out on top, as it often does in surveys, and look for any number nobody chose.
