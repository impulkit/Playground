What is the story of Chittorgarh?

Chittorgarh fort spreads over about 700 acres on a hilltop in Rajasthan, and it survived three of the most famous sieges in Indian history.

The short answer

Chittorgarh is one of the largest forts in India. It sits on a flat-topped hill about 180 metres above the plains of southern Rajasthan. Its walls wind for kilometres around palaces, temples, towers and water tanks. The whole fort covers about 700 acres, about as much ground as 400 football fields.

For centuries it was the capital of Mewar, a kingdom ruled by Rajput kings called the Ranas. Chittorgarh was besieged and captured three times, in 1303, 1535 and 1568. Each time, the stories of what happened became legends told across India.

Since 2013 it has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Surprising but true

Seen from above, the fort's long, narrow shape is often said to look like a fish.

A city on a hill

Tradition says a local ruler built the first fort here in the 600s. Later, the Guhila and Sisodia families of Rajputs made it their stronghold. To reach the top, you climb a zig-zag road through seven gates, each built to slow down an attacking army.

Inside the walls stood a whole town, with homes, markets and temples, so thousands of people could hold out for months. Water was the key to surviving a siege. Tradition says the fort once had 84 water bodies, though only about 22 remain. Rainwater ran into tanks and step wells. One famous reservoir, Gaumukh, is fed by a spring that trickles out of a rock carved like a cow's mouth.

Three great sieges

In 1303, Alauddin Khalji, sultan of Delhi, besieged the fort for months and captured it. In 1535, Bahadur Shah, the sultan of Gujarat, took it. And in 1567–68 the Mughal emperor Akbar besieged it for about four months before it fell.

Chronicles and legends say that during these sieges, the women of the fort chose to die in fire rather than be captured. This practice was called jauhar. The men then rode out to fight to the end. People in Rajasthan remember these events as both brave and deeply sad.

After 1568, Mewar's rulers moved their capital to Udaipur. The Rana Maharana Pratap, who kept fighting the Mughals for years, never got Chittorgarh back.

Towers of victory

Two stone towers rise above the fort. The Vijay Stambh, or Victory Tower, was built by Rana Kumbha to celebrate a victory over the sultan of Malwa. Finished in 1448, it is about 37 metres tall, with nine storeys covered in carvings of gods and goddesses. You can climb a narrow staircase of about 157 steps inside it to the top, where small windows look out over the town below. Its walls carry so many carved figures that it is sometimes called a dictionary of Hindu sculpture.

Rana Kumbha also built or rebuilt many of the fort's walls and temples. The older Kirti Stambh, or Tower of Fame, was built by a Jain merchant, probably in the 1100s. It is about 22 metres tall and is dedicated to Adinatha, the first Jain tirthankara.

Poets, princesses and legends

Chittorgarh is full of stories. The poet-saint Mirabai lived here as a royal bride in the early 1500s. She was famous for singing to Krishna instead of living like a princess, and her songs are still sung today, as you can read in Mirabai and Kabir.

The best-known legend is that of Rani Padmini, a queen said to be so beautiful that Alauddin Khalji attacked the fort to win her. It comes from a poem written in 1540, long after the siege. Historians still debate whether she was real.

And the strangest part? Chittorgarh is just one of a whole chain of giant hill forts, some with walls long enough to be seen from far away, as you can find in Rajasthan's forts.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The story of Rani Padmini and Alauddin Khalji is proven history.

Truth: Alauddin Khalji really did capture Chittorgarh in 1303. But the tale of Padmini comes mainly from the poem Padmavat, written by Malik Muhammad Jayasi in 1540. Many historians think she may be a legend, while others believe the story holds a memory of real events.
