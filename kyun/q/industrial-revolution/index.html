What was the Industrial Revolution?

About 250 years ago, machines began doing work that hands had done for thousands of years, and the way people lived changed faster than ever before.

The short answer

The Industrial Revolution was a time, starting in Britain around 1760, when people began making things with machines in factories instead of by hand at home. Machines were powered first by water wheels and then by steam engines burning coal.

It began with cotton cloth and spread to iron, coal, railways and much more. Goods became cheaper and more plentiful. Millions of people moved from villages to fast-growing cities to work in factories. It also brought long hours, child labour, smoky air and, in places like India, the collapse of old industries. Our world of electricity, cars and phones grew out of it.

Surprising but true

Before the Industrial Revolution, by some estimates India made roughly a quarter of all the cloth traded in the world. Within a few decades, British machine-made cloth was flooding Indian markets instead.

Why Britain first?

Britain had a lot of coal close to the surface, plenty of rivers to turn water wheels, and money from trade and its empire. It also had inventors and business owners eager to try new ideas, and laws that protected inventions with patents.

There was also demand. People in Britain wanted the light, colourful cotton cloth that came from India. Making it by hand in Britain was slow and expensive, so inventors tried to make machines do it.

Machines that spin and weave

In the 1760s, James Hargreaves built the spinning jenny, which let one worker spin several threads at once. In 1769, Richard Arkwright patented a spinning machine driven by water power. Two years later he opened a mill at Cromford, beside a river in Derbyshire. It is often called one of the first modern factories. Workers came at set times when a bell rang, and the machines never stopped.

Then came the improved steam engine. James Watt's design, developed from the 1760s onwards, used coal much more efficiently than older engines. Factories no longer needed to be beside rivers. They could be built anywhere, especially in cities.

How did the steam engine change the world?The first useful steam engines were built to pump water out of flooded coal mines. Within a century they were pulling trains across India.

Iron, coal and rails

Factories needed iron for machines and coal for fuel. Mines went deeper, and steam engines pumped water out of them. In 1825 the first public railway to use steam engines opened in northern England, and in 1830 a line linked Liverpool and Manchester. Within decades, railways crossed Britain, Europe and North America. In 1853, India's first passenger train ran 34 km from Bombay to Thane, beginning India's railways.

Life in the new cities

Manchester grew so fast from cotton that people nicknamed it "Cottonopolis". Workers, including many children, often worked 12 hours or more a day. Housing was crowded, and chimneys filled the air with soot.

Slowly, laws changed. Britain limited children's working hours in the 1830s and 1840s. Workers formed trade unions. Over time, more children went to school instead of to work.

What happened to India

India had been famous for its cotton cloth for centuries. Fine muslin from Dhaka and printed calico were sold across Asia, Africa and Europe.

Under British rule, India's raw cotton was shipped to Lancashire's mills, woven into cloth by machines, and sold back in India more cheaply than local weavers could match. Millions of handloom weavers lost their living. Historians still debate exactly how much damage this did, but it became one of the strongest complaints of the freedom movement. Gandhi's khadi, hand-spun and hand-woven cloth, was a direct answer to it.

India later built its own mills. The first cotton mill in Bombay opened in 1854, and the city became a great mill town. The first factories in the USA were cotton mills too, starting in New England in the 1790s.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Industrial Revolution happened suddenly, in a few years.

Truth: It unfolded over about 80 years in Britain, from around 1760 to 1840, and much longer elsewhere. Many people still worked on farms or by hand long after the first factories opened.

Try this

Try making a short length of thread the old way. Take some cotton wool, pull out a thin wisp, and twist it between your fingers while gently pulling. Notice how slow it is. One early spinning machine could do the work of eight spinners at once, and later machines did far more.
