Why does milk boil over?

A pot of water can bubble for an hour and stay put. Turn your back on a pot of milk for ten seconds, and it climbs out and floods the stove.

The short answer

Milk boils over because it builds a lid on itself. As milk warms up, proteins and droplets of fat rise and form a thin skin on top. This skin is the malai that some people love and others pick out of their chai.

When the milk underneath gets close to 100°C, steam bubbles start to rise. In plain water, they burst at the surface and the steam escapes. In milk, they get stuck under the skin and inside a froth of proteins. More bubbles pile up below. The foam swells and lifts the skin like a balloon until, in a few seconds, it spills over the edge.

Surprising but true

In many South Indian homes, people boil milk until it overflows on purpose when they move into a new house, as a sign that the home will overflow with plenty.

What milk is made of

Milk looks simple, but it is a crowded mixture. About 87% is water. The rest is fat, a sugar called lactose, minerals, and proteins. The two main proteins are casein and whey.

Casein forms tiny clusters that float in the milk. They are so small that they scatter light, which is part of why milk looks white instead of clear. Whey proteins are dissolved in the watery part.

Building the skin

When milk heats up, whey proteins begin to unfold and stick to each other. At the surface, where water evaporates and leaves them behind, they join with casein and fat. A thin layer starts forming at around 70 to 80°C, well before boiling.

Stir the milk and you break the skin. Leave it, and it gets thicker and stronger. That is why milk on a quiet flame often looks calm right up to the moment it suddenly rises.

Bubbles that won't pop

Ordinary bubbles in water pop because the water film around them drains and thins out quickly. Milk proteins coat each bubble and make its wall stronger, the same way soap does in a soap bubble. So the bubbles last longer and stack up into foam.

Foam takes up far more space than liquid. A thin layer of milk can turn into a tall tower of froth. A steel pot that is only half full can be overflowing within a few seconds.

Why boil milk at all

For thousands of years, boiling was the safest way to kill germs in fresh milk. In the 1860s, the French scientist Louis Pasteur showed that gentle heating could kill many harmful microbes in wine. The same idea, called pasteurisation, was later used on milk. Packet milk in India and the USA is usually pasteurised already, so many families boil it out of habit, or simply because they like it hot.

Kitchen tricks

People have invented lots of ways to stop this. Lower the flame once the milk starts to steam. Use a wider pan, so the skin is thinner and steam escapes. Keep stirring to break the skin.

Some cooks lay a wooden spoon across the pot. It can pop the first bubbles that touch it, but it won't save a pot left on high heat for long. Many Indian kitchens use a metal disc called a milk guard or "milk watcher". It sits at the bottom of the pan and rattles noisily as the milk heats, warning the cook in time.

Boiling over on purpose

Sometimes overflowing milk is good news. In Tamil Nadu, people celebrate Pongal by boiling rice and milk in a new pot until it froths over. Everyone shouts "Pongalo Pongal!" as it spills. In many homes across South India, families boil milk over when they move into a new house, as a wish for plenty.

India drinks and cooks with a lot of milk. In 2024–25 it produced about 247 million tonnes, roughly a quarter of the world's total. That number was far smaller in the 1960s, before farmers' cooperatives and the White Revolution turned India into a milk giant.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Milk boils over because it boils at a much lower temperature than water.

Truth: Milk boils at almost the same temperature as water, just a fraction of a degree higher. The difference is the skin and foam, which trap steam and push upwards.

Try this

Run a bubble test with a grown-up at the stove. Heat half a cup of water and half a cup of milk in two small pans on low heat, side by side. Watch which one forms a skin first and which one foams. Then try laying a clean wooden spoon across the milk pan and see if the foam behaves differently.
