What is the difference between fog and smog?

Fog is a cloud that has come down to the ground. Smog is what happens when that cloud, or sunny air, gets mixed up with pollution.

The short answer

Fog is a cloud that sits on the ground. It forms when air near the surface cools until its water vapour turns into billions of tiny droplets. Weather experts call it fog when it cuts visibility to less than 1 km. It is just water, and it disappears once the air warms up.

Smog is polluted air. The word was made by joining "smoke" and "fog" over a hundred years ago, when coal smoke mixed with fog in cities like London. Today, it also means the brownish haze made when sunlight cooks car exhaust, as in Los Angeles.

In north India in winter, you often get both: natural fog and thick pollution, trapped together near the ground.

Surprising but true

In December 1952, a smog in London was so thick that people could not see their own feet, and it led to one of the first big clean-air laws.

How fog forms

Air always holds some invisible water vapour. Cold air can hold less than warm air. When moist air cools enough, the extra vapour condenses onto specks of dust, salt or soot, making droplets far too small to fall as rain.

On clear, still winter nights, the ground loses heat quickly to the sky, chilling the air just above it. By dawn, fields and roads can disappear in white. This happens often across the plains of Punjab, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, delaying trains and flights.

Another kind forms when warm, damp air drifts over cold water. Summer fog rolls in through the Golden Gate in San Francisco this way, as moist ocean air passes over a cold current along the coast.

The smog that changed a city

In early December 1952, cold, still air settled over London. Millions of coal fires and factories kept pouring out smoke, and it couldn't escape. For about five days, a yellow-black smog hung over the city. Buses crawled behind people walking with torches. One opera performance had to stop because smog had crept inside and the audience couldn't see the stage.

Thousands of people died, mostly from breathing problems. Later studies suggested the true toll was much higher than first counted. In 1956, Britain passed the Clean Air Act, which limited coal smoke in cities.

How do your lungs breathe?Your lungs have no muscles to pull air in. A dome of muscle underneath them does almost all the work, about 20,000 times a day.

Sunshine smog

Los Angeles had a different kind of smog. By the 1940s, the city was often covered in a brown haze that stung people's eyes, even on sunny days without fog. In the 1950s, a chemist named Arie Haagen-Smit showed what was happening. Sunlight was acting on gases from car exhaust, making new pollutants. One of them is ozone close to the ground.

High up, in the ozone layer, ozone protects us from the Sun's ultraviolet rays. Down where we breathe, it irritates the lungs. Cleaner cars and stricter rules have made Los Angeles air much better than it was, though it still has bad days.

Winter in Delhi

Delhi often gets the worst of both worlds. In winter, cold air sits under a layer of warmer air, like a lid on a pot. This is called a temperature inversion. Smoke from vehicles, factories, building dust and crop burning in nearby states gets trapped under that lid. Add natural fog, and the city can disappear into grey for days.

The Air Quality Index, shown on apps and news screens, tells people how bad the air is. On very poor days, schools sometimes close and doctors advise children to play indoors. If the air makes you cough or your chest feel tight, tell an adult. Cities from Delhi to Beijing are trying to cut air pollution with cleaner fuels, electric buses and limits on burning.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Fog and smog are the same thing, just in different cities.

Truth: Fog is only water droplets and is harmless to breathe. Smog contains pollutants like soot, smoke and ozone, which can hurt your lungs. On some winter days, a city gets both at once.

Try this

Ask an adult to pour some warm water into a clear jar, then rest a small plate with a few ice cubes on top. Watch a misty cloud form inside, where warm moist air meets the cold. That is how fog forms when damp air is chilled.
