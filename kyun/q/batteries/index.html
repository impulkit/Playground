How does a battery store energy?

A battery doesn't hold electricity like a jug holds water. It holds chemicals that are waiting to react.

The short answer

A battery is a little chemical factory in a can. Inside are two different materials, one at each end, called electrodes. Between them is a substance called an electrolyte, often a paste or liquid, which lets electrically charged atoms drift through but blocks electrons, the tiny charged particles that make electric current.

The two electrode materials "want" to react with each other, but they can't, because electrons can't cross the electrolyte. When you connect a wire from one end to the other, through a torch bulb or a toy motor, you give the electrons a path. They rush out of one end, through the wire, doing work on the way, and into the other end. The reaction keeps running until the chemicals are used up, and then the battery is flat.

Surprising but true

The first battery, built by Alessandro Volta in 1800, was a stack of zinc and copper discs separated by cardboard soaked in salty water. The unit of electric push, the volt, is named after him.

Frog legs and a pile of coins

In the 1780s, the Italian scientist Luigi Galvani noticed that a dead frog's leg twitched when touched by two different metals. He thought the electricity came from the frog. Another Italian scientist, Alessandro Volta, disagreed. Volta suspected the electricity came from the two metals, with the wet frog only connecting them.

To prove it, in 1800 Volta stacked discs of zinc and copper, with cardboard soaked in salty water between each pair. Touching the top and bottom of his "pile" gave a steady electric current, the first ever made without lightning or rubbing. Scientists across Europe rushed to build their own. The volt, the unit that measures a battery's push, is named after him.

Who invented the battery?The battery was invented because of an argument about frogs' legs. One scientist thought the electricity came from the frog. The other proved it came from the metals.

Rechargeable cells

Some reactions can be pushed backwards. When you plug in a phone, the charger forces electrons back the wrong way, rebuilding the original chemicals. Then the battery is ready to run again.

Car batteries have done this for over a century using lead plates in acid. Phones and laptops use lithium-ion batteries, which pack much more energy into a small, light space. In 2019, three scientists shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for developing them: John Goodenough of the USA, Stanley Whittingham, born in the UK, and Akira Yoshino of Japan. Goodenough was 97, the oldest person ever to win a Nobel Prize.

Big batteries

An AA cell gives 1.5 volts. Put several in a row and their pushes add up, which is why a torch might take two or three. An electric car carries thousands of small lithium cells, linked together into one large pack under the floor that can weigh as much as a small cow.

Power companies now build giant batteries the size of warehouses. They store electricity from solar panels during sunny afternoons and release it after dark. In India, both electric scooters and these large storage batteries are growing fast.

Handle with care

Batteries hold a lot of energy, so they need respect. A damaged or badly made lithium-ion battery can overheat and catch fire. Never puncture, crush or heat one, and let an adult deal with any battery that is swollen or leaking.

Old batteries contain metals that can poison soil and water, but also useful materials that can be recovered. Many shops and towns in India and the USA collect them for recycling. A used AA cell has given up its energy, but its zinc and manganese can still be turned into something new.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A battery stores electricity inside it, like a tank.

Truth: A battery stores chemical energy. Electricity is made only when you connect it in a circuit and the chemicals start reacting. A recharged battery has had those reactions pushed backwards by a charger.

Try this

Go on a battery hunt at home with an adult. Look at the labels on remote controls, clocks, toys and old phones, and write down each battery's voltage and size. Which gives the most volts? Never open, puncture or burn a battery, and give old ones to an e-waste collection point instead of the bin.
