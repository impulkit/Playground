Why put warning labels on junk food?

In Chile, a small black stop sign on snack packets changed what went inside the packets, as well as what people bought.

The short answer

A warning label on junk food is a bold symbol on the front of a packet. It tells you, in one glance, that the food has a lot of sugar, salt or fat. You do not need to turn the packet over or do any maths.

The labels work in two ways. Some shoppers see the warning and pick something else. And many companies change their recipes so their products stay under the limit and avoid the label altogether. In some countries, that second effect has turned out to matter as much as the first.

Surprising but true

When Finland made very salty foods carry a "high salt content" warning in 1993, many of the saltiest breads disappeared from shops, because bakers did not want to print the label.

How it works

The nutrition table on the back of a packet already lists sugar, fat and salt. But it is small and full of numbers, and most people take only a few seconds to choose a snack. A front-of-pack label turns that table into one simple signal.

Step by step, it goes like this:

  • 1. Set the limits. Experts choose a threshold for each nutrient, usually per 100 g of food or 100 ml of drink. Chile's first limits for solid foods were 22.5 g of sugar, 800 mg of sodium, 6 g of saturated fat and 350 kilocalories per 100 g.
  • 2. Check every product. If a food goes over a limit, it must carry the warning for that nutrient. One packet can carry one, two or more.
  • 3. Print it big. The rules fix the shape, size and place, so the warning cannot hide in a corner.

Think of a speed camera on a highway. Most drivers slow down because they know it is there, long before anyone gets a fine. Food companies behave the same way. Many reformulate their recipes, cutting sugar or salt just enough to get under the line.

Using "per 100 g" matters too. It lets you compare a small packet with a family pack fairly, the same idea as working with percentages.

Stars, lights or stop signs

Countries have tried three main designs.

Warning symbols flag only what is too high, like Chile's black octagons that say "high in sugars". They are simple and hard to miss.

Traffic lights, used in the UK, colour each nutrient red, amber or green. They show the good news and the bad news side by side.

Star ratings, used in Australia and New Zealand since 2014, give a whole product one score, measured in stars. Stars can also reward good things, like fibre, nuts or fruit.

Designers also choose what to count. Total sugar includes the natural sugar in milk and fruit. Added sugar counts only what the factory put in, which is closer to what doctors worry about. Each choice puts a different set of foods over the line. Saturated fat, found in palm oil, butter and ghee, is counted because eating a lot of it is linked to heart disease. Salt is counted through its sodium.

Where it came from

The idea was borrowed from cigarettes. Many countries first printed word warnings on cigarette packs, and in 2001 Canada became the first country to require picture warnings. India's cigarette packs now carry picture warnings that cover 85% of the front and the back.

Food followed. From 1993, Finland made very salty foods, such as bread, carry a "high salt content" warning. In 2013, the UK agreed on one traffic-light design that companies could choose to use.

The big step came in Chile in June 2016. Its law put black "high in" octagons on packets, stopped adverts for those foods aimed at children and banned their sale in schools. Peru and Uruguay also brought in warning labels, and Mexico's black octagons arrived in October 2020.

India's story

India's health has been changing quietly. A large national study led by the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) estimated that about 101 million Indian adults had diabetes in 2021. As towns grow and incomes rise, more families buy packaged snacks and sweet drinks. Public health experts see clear labels as one cheap tool among many.

In 2022, India's food regulator, the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI), published a draft called the Indian Nutrition Rating. Packets would get from half a star to five stars, based on energy, total sugar, saturated fat and sodium. Points would come back for fruit, vegetables, nuts, millets, fibre and protein. Many doctors and consumer groups objected and asked for warnings instead.

A health group, 3S and Our Health Society, took the question to the Supreme Court. It argued that clear warnings on the front of packets would help people make healthier choices, as diabetes and other diet-linked illnesses rise. In April 2025, the court told the government to finalise its labelling rules within three months.

In 2024, the ICMR's National Institute of Nutrition published new Dietary Guidelines for Indians. They linked unhealthy diets to more than half of India's disease burden and urged families to read food labels.

What are your rights when you buy something?A shop in India cannot charge you even one rupee above the price printed on a packet, and that rule protects a child buying a chocolate as much as an adult.

The numbers that matter

  • - About a quarter: how much buying of "high in" sugary drinks fell in Chile in the law's first year and a half, compared with earlier trends (2020 study).
  • - 74% to 27%: the share of sausages in Chile that were high in sodium, before and after the law. Companies had cut salt to escape the label.
  • - 13 g to 10 g: the average salt eaten each day by Finnish men, between 1979 and 2002. Researchers linked the fall partly to the salt labels.
  • - About 1 in 9: Indian adults with diabetes. About 136 million more have prediabetes, meaning blood sugar higher than normal (2021 estimates).
  • - 56.4%: the share of India's disease burden that the ICMR linked to unhealthy diets.

What people argue about

Stars or warnings? A large survey by IIM Ahmedabad found that shoppers found stars easier to spot and understand. But warnings did better at showing which nutrient was too high. Critics of stars say a low score does not tell you whether the problem is sugar, salt or fat. Some also warn that adding a little fruit juice could lift a sugary drink's stars.

Where to draw the line? If the limits are very strict, almost every packet gets a warning, and people stop paying attention. If they are loose, many unhealthy foods escape. Health groups and food companies often disagree about where the line should sit.

Business worries. Food makers point to the cost of new recipes and new packets. Some say strict limits would put warnings on most traditional sweets and namkeen. Others argue that nobody eats 100 g of pickle or ketchup at once, so limits should be set per serving. Health groups reply that companies in Chile and Finland changed their recipes and carried on selling.

Myths and mix-ups

"A warning means the food is banned." It does not. The food can still be sold and eaten. The label only tells you what is inside.

"Labels only work if people read them." Companies also change recipes to avoid labels. In Finland, many of the saltiest breads vanished from shops. So the food on the shelf can change even for shoppers who never look.

"Sugar-free on the front means healthy." India's 2024 dietary guidelines point out that "sugar-free" foods can still be high in fat or refined flour. They can also hide sugars under other names, such as corn syrup.

What it means for you

A front label can save a family time in a busy shop. A warning lets you compare two snacks in a second. The label on the back still tells the full story, including how big a "serving" really is.

A warning is not a rule about what you may never eat. A packet of chips with two warnings can still be a treat at a party. The aim is to stop treats from quietly becoming everyday food, which is the main worry about junk food, and especially about sweet fizzy drinks.

The rest of the packet is still trying to sell to you. Cartoons and bright colours are adverts too. A big "high in protein" claim can be true and still give the wrong idea, the kind of trick explained in how numbers can mislead.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A food with no warning label must be healthy.

Truth: A missing label only means the food is under the limits for the few nutrients being checked. It says nothing about fibre, vitamins or how much of it you eat, so the full label on the back still matters.

Try this

Pick three packets at home, such as biscuits, chips and a breakfast cereal. Find the per 100 g column and check each one against Chile's first limits for solid foods: 22.5 g sugar, 800 mg sodium, 6 g saturated fat and 350 kcal. Draw a small black octagon on a sticky note for every limit a packet goes over and stick it on the front. Which packet collects the most?
