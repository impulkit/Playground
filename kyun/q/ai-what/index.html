What is artificial intelligence?

In 1997, a computer beat the world chess champion. Today AI can chat, draw and translate, yet it can still make mistakes a child wouldn't.

The short answer

Artificial intelligence, or AI, means computer systems that do tasks that normally need human intelligence: recognising faces, understanding speech, translating languages, playing games, answering questions and more.

Most AI today works through machine learning. Instead of following rules written by a programmer for every situation, the system is shown huge numbers of examples and finds patterns in them. Shown millions of photos, it learns to spot cats. Trained on vast amounts of text, it learns to write sentences that sound human.

AI is powerful, but it isn't magic, and it doesn't think the way people do. Whether it understands anything at all is still hotly debated.

Surprising but true

The term "artificial intelligence" was coined in 1955, in a proposal for a summer workshop at Dartmouth College in the USA, held in 1956.

Can machines think?

In 1950, the British mathematician Alan Turing asked whether machines could think. Since "thinking" is hard to define, he suggested a test. If a person chatting by text couldn't tell whether they were talking to a human or a machine, the machine could be said to behave intelligently. This became known as the Turing test.

In 1955, the American computer scientist John McCarthy and colleagues proposed a summer workshop at Dartmouth College to explore "artificial intelligence". It took place in 1956, and the name stuck.

Rules, then examples

Early AI used hand-written rules. Programs could solve logic puzzles and play draughts. But real life has too many exceptions for rules alone. How do you write rules for every way a person can write the letter "a"?

Machine learning took a different path. One popular kind uses artificial neural networks, loosely inspired by how nerve cells in the brain connect. The network starts out making random guesses. Each time it gets something wrong, it adjusts millions of tiny settings. After enough examples, it gets very good. This needs powerful computers and lots of data, which is why AI took off in the 2010s.

Games that made headlines

In 1997, IBM's Deep Blue computer beat world chess champion Garry Kasparov in a match. It worked mostly by checking enormous numbers of possible moves very fast.

Who are India's chess champions?In 1988 India had just one grandmaster. Today it has more than 80, and an 18-year-old from Chennai became world champion.

The ancient board game Go has far more possible positions than chess, and was thought too hard for computers. In 2016, a program called AlphaGo, which learned partly by playing millions of games against itself, beat Lee Sedol, one of the world's best players.

Chatbots and pictures

Recent chatbots are built on large language models. They are trained on vast amounts of text to predict which word is likely to come next. Doing this well, at huge scale, lets them answer questions, write stories and help with homework.

But they can make things up, a problem often called "hallucination". They can repeat unfair ideas found in their training data. They have no real experience of the world. So it's wise to check important facts, and many schools have rules about using AI for work.

AI at work

AI helps doctors spot signs of disease in scans and helps scientists improve weather forecasts. An AI system called AlphaFold predicts the 3D shapes of proteins, the tiny machines inside living cells. Its creators shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

In India, the government's Bhashini project uses AI to help people translate between Indian languages and speak to apps in their own language. Self-driving cars, voice assistants and recommendations on streaming apps all use AI.

People disagree about where this is heading. Some expect AI to help cure diseases and solve hard problems. Others worry about jobs, fairness, false information and machines that are hard to control. Many governments are now writing rules for how AI can be used. What's certain is that underneath, it still runs on ordinary computer chips, following algorithms that people designed.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If an AI answers confidently, the answer must be correct.

Truth: AI chatbots produce answers that sound fluent and sure, even when they're wrong. They can invent facts, names or sources. It's wise to check important information in reliable sources.

Try this

Teach a "human AI". Collect 20 pictures of leaves (or draw them) and sort them into two groups by a secret rule, such as smooth or jagged edges. Show a friend the sorted examples without telling them the rule, then hand them new leaves to sort. How many examples did they need to work out your rule?
