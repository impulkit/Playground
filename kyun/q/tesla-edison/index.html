What was the war of the currents?

In the 1880s, two kinds of electricity fought to light America's cities. The loser's champion was Thomas Edison, the most famous inventor alive.

The short answer

The war of the currents was a fierce contest, in the late 1880s and early 1890s, over what kind of electricity should power the United States. Thomas Edison's companies sold direct current, or DC, which flows steadily in one direction. George Westinghouse's company sold alternating current, or AC, which switches direction back and forth many times a second. Its motors and key ideas came from the inventor Nikola Tesla.

AC won because it could be sent long distances cheaply. Today nearly all the electricity reaching homes worldwide is AC. In India it switches direction 50 times a second, at 230 volts. In the United States it is 60 times a second, at 120 volts.

Surprising but true

Direct current, the loser of the war, is making a comeback. Your phone, laptop and solar panels all run on it, and some of the world's longest power lines now carry it too.

Edison's head start

In 1882, Edison opened the first big city power station in New York, using direct current. It worked well, but it had a serious weakness. DC loses a lot of energy as heat when it travels through wires at low voltage. Edison's customers had to live within about a kilometre or two of a power station, so a city would need one in almost every neighbourhood.

Alternating current had an answer. With a simple device called a transformer, AC can be stepped up to very high voltage, sent many kilometres with little loss, then stepped down again near homes. The problem was that nobody had a good motor to run on AC.

Tesla's motor

Nikola Tesla was a Serbian-American engineer, born in what is now Croatia. He moved to New York in 1884 and worked briefly for one of Edison's companies, but left after a few months, unhappy with how he was treated and paid.

In 1888 he patented a clever AC motor that used a rotating magnetic field. George Westinghouse, an inventor and businessman from Pittsburgh, bought the rights. Westinghouse now had the whole AC package: generators, transformers and motors.

A bitter fight

Edison's side fought back with fear. They published warnings that AC's high voltages were deadly and staged public demonstrations meant to make AC look dangerous. High-voltage AC could indeed be lethal, and some accidents with overhead wires horrified the public. But DC at high voltage is also dangerous. The campaign was as much about business as safety.

It didn't work. AC was simply better for big networks. In 1892, Edison's company merged with a rival to form General Electric, and Edison lost control of it. The new company soon began building AC systems too.

Chicago and Niagara

In 1893, Westinghouse won the contract to light the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago. Tens of thousands of electric lamps glowed across the fairgrounds, all on AC, and millions of visitors saw them.

Then came Niagara Falls. A giant power plant there, built using Westinghouse and Tesla's AC system, began generating in 1895. In 1896, its electricity travelled about 40 km to the city of Buffalo, New York. Long-distance power had arrived, and the war was largely over.

DC strikes back

Direct current never vanished. Batteries, phones, laptops, LED lights and solar panels all use DC, so every charger turns AC from the wall into DC. Modern electronics can now also convert DC to very high voltages, and over really long distances, high-voltage DC lines lose less energy than AC ones. India has some of the longest, including a line about 1,800 km long linking Chhattisgarh to Tamil Nadu.

Tesla died in a New York hotel room in 1943, with little money. Edison is remembered for the light bulb and hundreds of other inventions. Between them, they helped bring electricity into homes everywhere.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The war of the currents was a personal fight between Edison and Tesla.

Truth: Tesla invented key AC machines, but the big business battle was between Edison's companies and George Westinghouse, who bought Tesla's patents. Tesla worked briefly for Edison and they disagreed, but stories of a lifelong duel are mostly a modern exaggeration.

Try this

Look at a phone or laptop charger with an adult, without plugging it in. Find the small print. It usually says something like "Input 100–240V AC" and "Output 5V DC". The charger is a tiny peace treaty from the war of the currents, turning the AC from the wall into the DC your device needs. Never open a charger.
