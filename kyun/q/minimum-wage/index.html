What is a minimum wage?

When the USA first set a national minimum wage in 1938, it was 25 cents an hour. Economists still argue about what such laws really do.

The short answer

A minimum wage is the lowest pay an employer is allowed to give a worker by law. It is usually set per hour, per day or per month. If a factory, shop or farm pays less, it is breaking the law.

The idea is to stop workers from being paid so little that they cannot live decently, especially those with little bargaining power. Workers in strong trade unions can push for better pay together. A cleaner or a farm labourer working alone usually cannot, and the law steps in for them. In the USA, there is a national minimum and often higher state ones. In India, the central and state governments set minimum wages, which differ by state, by skill and by type of work.

Surprising but true

The US federal minimum wage has stayed at $7.25 an hour since 2009, but many states and cities set their own, much higher, minimums.

Where it started

New Zealand passed the first national minimum wage law in 1894. Australia followed soon after with wage boards. In 1907, an Australian judge ruled that a fair wage had to be enough for a worker to support a family, a famous decision known as the Harvester judgment.

In the USA, the Great Depression of the 1930s left millions working long hours for very little. In 1938, President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed the Fair Labor Standards Act, which set a national minimum of 25 cents an hour. The same law limited working hours and banned most child labour. Many workers then were not covered, including farm workers and domestic servants, and it took decades for the law to reach them.

What was the Great Depression?In October 1929 share prices in New York crashed. Within three years, about one in four American workers had no job, and the trouble had spread around the globe.

India's many minimums

India passed its Minimum Wages Act in 1948, just after independence. Instead of one number for the whole country, states set rates for many different jobs, from farm work to construction. There were once more than a thousand separate rates.

In 2019, Parliament passed the Code on Wages, which combines several old laws. It also allows a national "floor wage", a lowest level below which no state's minimum should fall. Still, many Indian workers are in informal jobs, such as street selling or domestic work, where wage laws are hard to enforce.

The big argument

Most people agree that very low pay is a problem. The argument is about what a higher minimum wage does.

Some economists say that if labour costs more, businesses will hire fewer people, use more machines or raise prices. Young and less experienced workers might lose out. Others say higher pay means workers spend more, quit less often and work harder, so businesses can afford it.

In 1994, David Card and Alan Krueger compared fast-food restaurants in New Jersey, which had just raised its minimum wage, with those across the border in Pennsylvania. They found no drop in jobs. Other economists challenged their data, and the debate continues. Card shared the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2021, partly for this kind of natural experiment.

Keeping up with prices

A minimum wage that never changes slowly loses value, because prices rise over time through inflation. The US federal minimum has been $7.25 an hour since 2009, so it buys much less than it did then. Many states, such as California and Washington, now set minimums more than twice as high.

Some places adjust their minimum wage automatically every year to match prices. Others leave it to politicians, which can mean long gaps between rises. Deciding the right number is partly maths and partly a judgment about fairness.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Everyone agrees that raising the minimum wage destroys jobs.

Truth: Economists disagree. Some studies find small job losses, especially for young workers, while others find little effect. Much depends on how big the rise is compared with typical local wages.
