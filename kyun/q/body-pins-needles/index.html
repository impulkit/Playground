Why do your feet fall asleep?

Sit on your foot through a long film and it goes numb, then fizzes like soda. A squashed nerve is sending your brain gibberish.

The short answer

Sit cross-legged on the floor through a long film, then stand up. Your foot feels like a bag of fizzing soda, and you can barely feel the floor. Then comes the prickling, as if hundreds of tiny ants were marching under your skin.

Doctors call this paresthesia. It happens when you press on a nerve for a while. Nerves are the body's wiring, carrying messages between your skin and your brain. Squash one and you also squash the tiny blood vessels that feed it. Short of oxygen and pressed flat, the nerve stops sending normal signals, so your foot goes numb.

When you move and the pressure lifts, the nerve wakes up confused. It fires off random signals, and your brain reads them as pins and needles.

Surprising but true

Some of your nerve fibres carry messages at about 120 metres a second, more than twice as fast as a Vande Bharat train at full speed.

Wires inside a cable

Your nervous system is built from long cells called neurons. A nerve is a bundle of thousands of their thin fibres, packed together like the wires inside a cable. The fastest fibres carry messages at about 120 metres a second. That is around 430 km/h, more than twice as fast as a Vande Bharat express at full speed.

Fibres come in different thicknesses. Thick ones carry touch and the sense of where your foot is. Thinner ones carry pain and warmth. The thick fibres need more oxygen, so they give up first under pressure. That is why a sleepy foot loses its sense of position before anything else, and you wobble when you stand.

When blood rushes back, the different fibres switch on again at slightly different times. Your brain gets a jumble of half-working messages, and that jumble is the fizz.

How did the telegraph send messages across oceans?In 1858, Queen Victoria sent a 98-word message to the US president through a cable on the floor of the Atlantic. It took about 16 hours to get through.

Why it's usually your feet

Feet fall asleep more than most body parts because of how we sit. Cross your legs and the top knee presses into a nerve just below the other knee, on the outer side. There it lies close to the skin with very little padding.

Sitting on your feet squashes nerves at the ankle and across the top of the foot. The sciatic nerve, the thickest in your body and about as wide as your thumb, runs through your bottom and down the back of each leg. A long school assembly on a hard floor or a slow train ride on a wooden bench can press on it too.

Arms do it as well. Many people wake up with a dead arm after sleeping on it. And knock the nerve at your elbow, the so-called funny bone, and you get a jolt of tingling in a split second.

A built-in alarm

Numbness feels useless, but the prickling is a warning. It makes you shift before the pressure does real harm. Skin and nerves that stay squashed for hours can be damaged. This is why nurses turn patients who cannot move by themselves, often every two hours or so, to stop painful pressure sores.

The quickest fix is simple. Change position, wiggle your toes and give it a minute. Stand up slowly, though. A numb foot can't tell your brain how it is placed, so it is easy to twist an ankle on the first step.

When to tell someone

Pins and needles after sitting oddly are normal and harmless. Tingling that comes without any pressure, lasts a long time or keeps coming back is different. It is worth telling a trusted adult or a doctor. In adults it can sometimes be linked to low levels of certain vitamins, such as B12, or to illnesses that affect nerves.

And the strangest part of a sleepy foot? For a few minutes it shows you a sense you use all day without noticing: knowing where your body is without looking. Scientists count it among your senses beyond the famous five.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Your foot falls asleep because the blood supply to your whole leg has been cut off.

Truth: The big blood vessels keep working. The problem is mainly a squashed nerve and the very small vessels that feed it, which is why the feeling fades so fast once you move.

Try this

Close your eyes and ask a friend to gently bend one of your fingers up or down. Say which way it moved without looking. That hidden sense of position is the one a sleepy foot loses first, which is why it feels like it belongs to someone else.
