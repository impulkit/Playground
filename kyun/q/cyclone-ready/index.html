How does India get ready for a cyclone?

A few days before a big cyclone hits India's coast, a huge, quiet operation begins. Satellites watch, fishing boats race home, buses carry families to shelters, and rescue teams fly in before the first strong gust.

The short answer

India gets ready for a cyclone in three steps: spot it early, warn people clearly, and move them to safety before it hits. The India Meteorological Department tracks the storm with satellites and radars and sends warnings days ahead.

State governments then move people from low coastal areas to strong shelters, call fishing boats back, stock food and water, and send rescue teams. This approach has saved many thousands of lives.

Surprising but true

In 1999, a super cyclone in Odisha took around 10,000 lives. In 2013, Cyclone Phailin was almost as strong, but because about 10 lakh people were moved to safety in time, far fewer lives were lost.

How it works

A cyclone begins as an area of low air pressure over warm tropical sea. In other oceans, the same kind of storm is called a hurricane or a typhoon. The India Meteorological Department watches these areas closely. As a storm grows stronger, it gets a new label:

  • - Depression: winds of 31 to 49 km/h
  • - Deep depression: 50 to 61 km/h
  • - Cyclonic storm: 62 to 88 km/h, and it gets a name
  • - Severe cyclonic storm: 89 to 117 km/h
  • - Very severe cyclonic storm: 118 to 165 km/h
  • - Extremely severe cyclonic storm: 166 to 220 km/h
  • - Super cyclonic storm: 221 km/h or more

Weather satellites watch the swirl from space, and Doppler radars along the coast measure rain and wind as it nears land. Computers then predict where and when it will cross the coast, called landfall.

Warnings come in stages. First a watch, about three days ahead. Then an alert, then a warning about a day before landfall. Districts are coloured yellow, orange or red to show how serious the danger is.

When the warning comes, everyone has a job. Fishermen are told to return to harbour. Officials move families from huts and low areas to cyclone shelters, schools and other strong buildings. Power may be switched off to stop accidents. Trains and flights may be cancelled. National Disaster Response Force teams take boats, ropes and saws to the area so they are ready the moment the storm passes.

Many shelters are raised on pillars, so storm water can flow underneath. They have strong concrete roofs, toilets and water tanks, and some have space for cattle nearby, because many families will not leave without their animals. In Odisha, trained village volunteers go door to door with megaphones and sirens, making sure the elderly, the sick and young children get out first.

It is a lot like a fire drill at school. Everyone practises the plan before there is any real danger, so on the day itself people act quickly and calmly.

Where it came from

India has always faced cyclones, especially on the east coast, as the story of why the east coast gets more cyclones explains. In the past, warnings were slow, and many people did not hear them in time.

The turning point came in Odisha. In October 1999, a super cyclone struck with winds of about 260 km/h and a huge storm surge. Around 10,000 people lost their lives. Odisha decided this must never happen again. It set up India's first state disaster agency, built hundreds of cyclone shelters and trained village volunteers.

In 2005, India passed a law on disaster management, and in 2006 it formed the National Disaster Response Force. Weather forecasting improved too, with better satellites and computers.

India's story

The new system was tested in 2013, when Cyclone Phailin hit Odisha with winds close to the 1999 storm. This time, about 10 lakh people were moved to safety in time, and the number of lives lost was a tiny fraction of 1999. The United Nations praised the effort. In 2019, Cyclone Fani brought a similar success, with more than 10 lakh people evacuated in Odisha.

In October 2026, Cyclone Arnab formed in the Bay of Bengal. The IMD tracked it for days and predicted its landfall well ahead. It crossed the Andhra Pradesh coast near Kakinada early on 28 October as a very severe cyclonic storm, with winds of 125 to 135 km/h. About 57,000 people had been moved to relief camps, and 39 NDRF teams were in place.

Cyclone names now come from a list made by 13 countries around the north Indian Ocean, including India, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka and Oman. Each country sends names in turn. Arnab was suggested by Bangladesh.

The numbers that matter

  • - About 11,100 km: India's coastline, as measured again with modern maps in 2024. Older books say about 7,500 km. Millions of people live close to the sea.
  • - About 5: cyclones that form in the north Indian Ocean in an average year. Most form in the Bay of Bengal.
  • - About 10,000 and about 10 lakh: lives lost in Odisha in 1999, and people moved to safety before Phailin in 2013. Early warning made the difference.
  • - 221 km/h: the wind speed at which a storm becomes a super cyclone.

What people argue about

Most people agree that early warning works. The debates are about what comes next. Some say India should build stronger homes and protect mangroves, the coastal forests that soften storm waves, instead of clearing them for buildings and ports. Others say ports and coastal towns bring jobs that people need.

Scientists also discuss how climate change affects cyclones. Studies suggest the Arabian Sea is warming and getting more strong cyclones than before, but researchers are still working out how much change to expect.

Myths and mix-ups

"When the wind stops, the cyclone is over." The calm may be the eye of the storm. The other half is still coming.

"Cyclones only hit the coast." A cyclone's rain can flood towns hundreds of kilometres inland.

"Taping windows stops them breaking." Tape does not make glass stronger. Closing shutters and staying away from windows is safer.

What it means for you

If you live near the coast, learn the warning colours, know where your nearest shelter is and keep a small emergency kit. Listen to official alerts on radio, TV or the government's SACHET app, not rumours on social media. Inland, heavy rain can still flood roads, so travel plans may change.

After the storm, disaster response teams clear roads and restore power. Knowing the plan before the storm is what keeps families safe.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In a cyclone, the strong wind is the biggest danger.

Truth: Wind is dangerous, but storm surge and floods usually cause more harm. A cyclone can push seawater several metres high onto low coasts, and its heavy rain can flood rivers far inland.

Try this

Make a family cyclone kit list. Include a torch, spare batteries, drinking water, dry food, a first-aid box, important papers in a plastic bag, and a charged power bank. Then find out where the nearest shelter or high, strong building is in your area.
