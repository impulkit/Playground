What is a runoff election?

In some countries, coming first in an election is not enough. If nobody wins more than half the votes, the top two face each other again a few weeks later.

The short answer

A runoff is a second round of voting. If no candidate wins more than half the votes in the first round, the top two go head to head a few weeks later.

Because only two remain, whoever wins the runoff is sure to have the support of more than half of the people who voted in it.

Surprising but true

In Brazil, voting is compulsory for most adults, and the whole country votes on electronic machines. Results for more than 15 crore voters can be counted within a few hours.

Why have two rounds?

When many candidates stand, the votes can split. Someone might come first with only 30%, even if most voters would have preferred someone else. A runoff makes sure the winner has the backing of a majority, more than 50%.

Between rounds, candidates try to win over the voters whose favourite was knocked out. So a runoff often rewards the candidate who can appeal to the widest range of people, not just the most loyal fans.

Who uses runoffs?

Many countries elect their president this way, including Brazil, France, Indonesia, Argentina and Nigeria. In Brazil, where voting is compulsory for most adult citizens, the runoff is held on the last Sunday of October, four weeks after the first round. In 2026, Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva won a runoff to become Brazil's president for a fourth time.

Some places use a quicker version called instant runoff. Voters rank the candidates 1, 2, 3 on one ballot. If nobody has a majority, the last-placed candidate is removed and their voters' next choices are counted, until someone passes 50%. Australia uses this for many elections.

India does it differently

In India's elections, each seat is won by whoever gets the most votes, even if that is less than half. This is called first past the post. It is simple, fast and needs only one round, which matters when crores of people vote.

Different countries choose different rules, and each has fans and critics. The USA uses yet another system, with an Electoral College. Whether a country has a president or a prime minister also changes what is being voted for. All of these systems are built on one idea, using percentages of votes to turn many choices into one decision, the heart of a democracy.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The person with the most votes always wins an election.

Truth: It depends on the rules. In India and the UK, the most votes wins. In runoff systems, you need more than half, and the second-placed candidate in round one can end up winning round two.

Try this

Hold a class or family vote on a snack with four choices. Count the votes. If nothing gets more than half, cross out all but the top two and vote again. Did the winner change?
