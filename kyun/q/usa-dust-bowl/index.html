What was the Dust Bowl?

On 14 April 1935, a wall of dust hundreds of kilometres wide rolled across the American plains and turned day into night.

The short answer

The Dust Bowl was a disaster on the Great Plains of the USA during the 1930s. A long drought dried out farmland that had been ploughed up only a few years earlier. Strong winds then lifted the loose topsoil into giant dust storms called "black blizzards".

Some storms were so thick that people could not see their own hands. Crops died, animals choked and many families lost their farms. About 2.5 million people left the Plains states by 1940. The disaster changed how the USA, and later the world, thinks about protecting soil.

Surprising but true

In May 1934, dust blown from the Great Plains fell on New York City and Washington, DC, and even on ships far out in the Atlantic Ocean.

How the grass was lost

For thousands of years, the southern plains were covered in tough prairie grass. Its deep roots held the soil in place through droughts and storms, and fed huge herds of bison. Native American nations hunted there long before settlers came.

From the late 1800s, settlers arrived with ploughs. During the First World War, wheat prices soared, and tractors made it easy to plough faster than ever. Millions of acres of grass were ripped up and replaced with wheat. When prices crashed and rain stopped in the early 1930s, fields were left bare. The roots that had held the soil for centuries were gone.

Black blizzards

The dust storms came again and again. On 14 April 1935, now called Black Sunday, a massive storm swept across Oklahoma and Texas. Witnesses said the sky turned black in minutes, birds fled ahead of it and people crawled home along fences. It is thought to have carried hundreds of thousands of tonnes of soil.

Dust got everywhere. It piled up against houses like snow drifts and buried fences and tractors. Families hung wet sheets over windows and still woke up with dust on their pillows. Many children and older people fell ill with "dust pneumonia", a lung illness caused by breathing in the fine soil.

What was the Great Depression?In October 1929 share prices in New York crashed. Within three years, about one in four American workers had no job, and the trouble had spread around the globe.

A storm reaches Washington

In May 1934, one huge storm blew dust over 2,000 km east. It fell on Chicago, New York and Washington, DC, and settled on ships far out in the Atlantic. Suddenly, people in big eastern cities could see the problem for themselves.

A soil scientist named Hugh Hammond Bennett had been warning about soil loss for years. In 1935, as he spoke to members of Congress in Washington, another dust cloud from the plains darkened the sky outside. Soon afterwards, Congress created the Soil Conservation Service to help farmers protect their land.

Leaving home, and coming back

The Dust Bowl hit at the same time as the Great Depression, when banks failed and jobs disappeared. Many families packed everything into old cars and drove west along Route 66 towards California, hoping for farm work. They were often called "Okies", even if they were not from Oklahoma, and many were treated badly when they arrived.

The writer John Steinbeck told their story in his 1939 novel The Grapes of Wrath. Singer Woody Guthrie wrote songs about the dust and the long road west. Photographers hired by the government took pictures that are still in museums today.

Slowly, things improved. The government paid farmers to plant grass, plough along the curves of hills and rotate crops. Workers planted about 220 million trees in long shelterbelts from the Canadian border down to Texas to break the wind. The rains returned around 1939. The lesson that soil takes centuries to make and only days to lose still guides farming everywhere. India met the same question during its own push to grow more food, the Green Revolution: how to farm hard without wearing the land out.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Dust Bowl was caused only by bad weather.

Truth: A long drought set it off, but farming made it far worse. Millions of acres of grassland had been ploughed up, so nothing held the dry soil in place when the winds came.

Try this

Put dry, loose soil on one tray and soil with grass or leaves pressed into it on another. Blow gently across each through a straw. See which one loses more soil. Grass roots and plant cover did the same job on the prairies before ploughs arrived.
