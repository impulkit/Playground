Who were the Chalukyas of Badami?

In one small corner of Karnataka, the Chalukya kings tried out so many temple designs that the area is called a cradle of Indian temple building.

The short answer

The Chalukyas of Badami were a dynasty who ruled much of the Deccan from about 543 to 753 CE. Their capital was Vatapi, today's Badami in northern Karnataka, set among red sandstone cliffs beside a lake.

They are remembered for two things. Their king Pulakeshin II stopped the great northern ruler Harsha from pushing south. And, near Badami, at Aihole and Pattadakal, their builders and sculptors experimented with temple designs, cutting caves and building stone shrines that helped shape Hindu temple architecture across India.

Surprising but true

At Pattadakal, temples in the north Indian style and the south Indian style stand side by side, built by the same dynasty.

A fort among red cliffs

Around 543, a chief named Pulakeshin I built a fort at Vatapi and declared himself king. He chose well. Steep sandstone cliffs made Vatapi easy to defend, and a large tank of water lay at its heart.

His successors carved four cave temples into the cliff face. Three are Hindu, dedicated to Shiva and Vishnu, and one is Jain. Cave 3 carries an inscription dated 578 CE, made by King Mangalesha. Inside the caves are giant sculptures, including Vishnu seated on a serpent and an eighteen-armed Shiva dancing. The soft, even sandstone let sculptors carve fine details that still look sharp today.

The king who stopped Harsha

Pulakeshin II, who ruled from about 610 to 642, was the strongest Chalukya ruler. Harsha, who controlled much of northern India, marched south, but Pulakeshin defeated him near the Narmada river. The river became a frontier between north and south.

We know about this from a long Sanskrit poem carved on the Meguti temple at Aihole in 634 CE. It was written by Pulakeshin's court poet, Ravikirti, and it praises the king's victories. The Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang also passed through and described Pulakeshin's people as proud and warlike.

Rival with the Pallavas

The Chalukyas' great enemies were the Pallavas of Kanchipuram, in Tamil Nadu. Pulakeshin II attacked them, but in 642 the Pallava king Narasimhavarman I struck back, captured Vatapi, and Pulakeshin died.

The Chalukyas recovered after a few years. About a century later, King Vikramaditya II took revenge by capturing Kanchipuram. Instead of destroying it, he admired the Kailasanatha temple there and gave gifts to it. Pallava ideas then flowed north into Chalukya building.

A laboratory for temples

Aihole, about 35 kilometres from Badami, has more than a hundred temples from different periods. Some of the early ones look like village halls with flat roofs. The Durga temple has an unusual rounded end, a bit like the back of a Buddhist prayer hall. Historians often call Aihole a cradle of Indian temple building, because so many experiments happened in one place.

At Pattadakal, on the Malaprabha river, the experiments matured. Some temples have the curved northern tower called a nagara shikhara. Others have the stepped, pyramid-like southern tower called a dravida vimana. The grandest, the Virupaksha temple, was built around 740 by Queen Lokamahadevi to celebrate her husband's victories over the Pallavas. Pattadakal was named a World Heritage Site in 1987.

The end of Badami

In 753, a Chalukya official named Dantidurga rebelled and founded the Rashtrakuta dynasty, which went on to carve the Kailasa temple at Ellora. A later branch of the Chalukya family returned to power in the 10th century, ruling from Kalyani. The red cliffs of Badami, with their carved caves above the green tank, look much as they did when Pulakeshin's soldiers stood guard.

Myth or fact?

Myth: North and south India always had completely separate temple styles.

Truth: At Aihole and Pattadakal, the Chalukyas built in both styles, sometimes in the same complex. Builders experimented and borrowed ideas freely.
