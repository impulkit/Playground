How many people live on Earth?

About 8.2 billion people live on Earth today. In 1800, there were fewer than 1 billion.

The short answer

About 8.2 billion people live on Earth, according to United Nations estimates for 2024. In the Indian system, that is about 820 crore. The world passed 8 billion in November 2022.

For most of human history, the population grew very slowly. It reached 1 billion only around 1800. Then better food supplies, clean water, vaccines and medicines meant far more babies survived and people lived longer. The population shot up, reaching 7 billion by 2011. Now growth is slowing, as families in most countries have fewer children than their grandparents did.

Surprising but true

If all 8 billion people stood shoulder to shoulder, about four to every square metre, they would fit in an area about one and a half times the size of Delhi.

Counting everyone

No one can count every person on Earth at the same moment. Instead, each country runs a census, a count of its own people, usually every ten years. Experts combine these with records of births and deaths and with surveys to estimate the world total. The UN publishes new estimates every couple of years.

India's last census was in 2011. The next one, delayed by the COVID-19 pandemic, is planned for 2027. Until then, India's population is estimated rather than counted.

How do you count a billion people?India's 2011 census needed about 27 lakh helpers, knocking on every door in the country in a few weeks.

Slow, then very fast

When people began farming about 10,000 years ago, there may have been only a few million humans in the whole world. Farming produced more food, so populations grew, but disease, famine and war kept numbers down for thousands of years.

After 1800, things changed fast. The world went from 1 billion people to 2 billion by about 1927, and to 4 billion by 1974. Every billion now arrived in about 12 to 13 years. The Green Revolution of the 1960s helped India and other countries grow far more food from the same fields.

India takes the lead

In 2023, the UN estimated that India had overtaken China as the world's most populous country. India now has about 1.45 billion people, or 145 crore, and China a little over 1.4 billion. The United States comes third, with about 34 crore. One in every six people on Earth lives in India.

Big numbers are hard to picture. If you counted one number every second, day and night, it would take you more than 250 years to count to 8 billion. You can explore numbers like this in big numbers.

Room for everyone?

All 8 billion people standing shoulder to shoulder, about four to a square metre, would fit in about 2,000 square kilometres, about one and a half times the area of Delhi. The real challenge is not space but sharing food, water, energy and land fairly while protecting nature.

People live very unevenly. Bangladesh packs more than 17 crore people into an area smaller than the state of Andhra Pradesh, while huge parts of Canada, Australia and Russia are almost empty. More than half the world's people now live in cities.

What happens next

Population growth is uneven across India too. Kerala and Tamil Nadu had small families early, while states such as Bihar and Uttar Pradesh still have more children per family. That is one reason why India's census numbers matter so much for planning schools, hospitals and seats in Parliament.

Families are getting smaller almost everywhere. Today the world average is about 2.3 children per woman, and in India it has fallen to about two. UN experts expect the world population to peak at about 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s and then slowly decline. Some countries, like Japan, Italy and South Korea, are already shrinking, which brings its own challenges, such as fewer young workers to care for a growing number of older people.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The world's population will keep growing forever.

Truth: Growth is already slowing. In many countries, families now have fewer than two children on average, and UN projections suggest the world's population will peak later this century and then gradually decline.
