How do athletes recover from injuries?

A baseball pitcher's torn elbow was once the end of a career. Now surgeons replace the ligament with a spare tendon from elsewhere in the body.

The short answer

When an athlete gets hurt, recovery usually happens in stages. First comes protection: resting the injured part so it isn't damaged more. Then the body starts healing on its own, laying down new tissue over days and weeks. Last comes rebuilding, when a physiotherapist slowly brings back strength, balance and movement.

The most common sports injuries are sprains, where a ligament (a tough band joining bone to bone) is stretched or torn, and strains, where a muscle or tendon is overstretched. Broken bones, dislocated joints and head injuries are less common but more serious.

The hardest part is often patience, for athletes and for children who just want to get back to the playground. Going back too soon is one of the main reasons injuries happen again.

Surprising but true

The doctor who made "RICE" (rest, ice, compression, elevation) famous in 1978 later said ice may actually slow healing.

How the body repairs itself

Healing starts within minutes. Blood rushes to the injured area, bringing cells that clean up damage. This makes the area red, warm and swollen. It feels bad, but this inflammation is the start of repairs.

Over the next weeks, the body builds new tissue. In a broken bone, a soft bridge called a callus forms across the gap and slowly hardens. A simple break in a child's arm often heals in about six to eight weeks, because young bones grow and mend quickly. Ligaments and tendons have a poor blood supply, so they can take months.

Doctors check bones with X-rays, and soft tissue with scanners that use magnets or sound waves. Muscles heal fastest of all, because they are packed with blood vessels. A mild strain can feel better within a week or two.

The surgery named after a pitcher

Baseball pitchers throw so hard that the ligament on the inside of the elbow can tear. In 1974, the American surgeon Frank Jobe tried something new on a pitcher named Tommy John. He replaced the torn ligament with a tendon taken from John's other arm.

Nobody knew if it would work. Tommy John returned to pitching and played for another 14 seasons. The operation is now so common that it's simply called Tommy John surgery. Doctors worry, though, that young players are throwing too hard and too often, and more teenagers now need it.

A comeback in India

In 2019, the javelin thrower Neeraj Chopra had surgery on his throwing elbow. He missed months of competition and had to rebuild his strength carefully, step by step. Two years later, at the Tokyo Olympics, he won the gold medal.

His story shows the modern approach. Recovery is planned like training. Physiotherapists set small goals, such as moving without pain, then lifting light weights, then throwing gently, before full competition. They also train the muscles around an injury, so that the joint is better protected next time.

Ice, rest and changing advice

In 1978, Dr Gabe Mirkin wrote a popular book that gave the world RICE: rest, ice, compression and elevation. For decades it was standard advice. In 2014, Mirkin wrote that ice might actually delay healing by slowing the inflammation the body needs.

Today many doctors suggest a short rest, then gentle movement as soon as it's comfortable, guided by a professional. Head injuries are different: anyone who bumps their head and feels dizzy, sick or confused should stop playing and see a doctor. Science keeps testing its own advice, and in sports medicine that advice is still changing.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If you can move it, it isn't broken.

Truth: Many people walk or move their fingers on a cracked bone. Pain, swelling, bruising or a strange shape after a fall are reasons to see a doctor, who can check with an X-ray.

Try this

Find your own tendons. Hold your arm out, palm up, and wiggle your fingers. Watch and feel the cords moving at your wrist. Then press your heel and rise onto your toes to feel your Achilles tendon tighten. These cords join muscle to bone and are often what gets strained in sport.
