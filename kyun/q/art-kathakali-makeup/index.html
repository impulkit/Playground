How is Kathakali make-up done?

A Kathakali actor lies still for three hours or more while an artist builds a white paper frame around his face, layer by layer.

The short answer

Kathakali is a dance-drama from Kerala, and its make-up is one of the most elaborate in the world. Before a show, the actor lies on his back on a mat. A make-up artist paints his face in bold colours, then builds a stiff white frame called chutti along his jaw, from ear to ear.

The whole process often takes three or four hours. Colours were traditionally ground from minerals and plants and mixed with coconut oil. Then comes the costume: huge skirts, heavy jewellery and a tall headdress.

Most Kathakali actors are men, even in women's roles, though more women now perform too. By the time he stands up, the actor has disappeared. In his place is a god, a king or a demon.

Surprising but true

Some Kathakali actors traditionally slip a tiny seed under the eyelid before going on stage, so that their eyes turn red and look fierce.

A colour code for faces

Every colour has a job. The audience reads a face the way you read a traffic signal.

  • - Pacha, meaning green, is for noble heroes like Krishna, Arjuna and Nala.
  • - Kathi, meaning knife, is for proud, powerful villains like Ravana. The face is green with a red streak curving up the cheek like a blade, and white knobs on the nose and forehead.
  • - Thadi, meaning beard, comes in three kinds. Red beards are for wicked, violent characters. White beards are for good beings like Hanuman. Black beards are for hunters and forest people.
  • - Kari, meaning black, is for demonesses.
  • - Minukku, meaning shining, is a soft yellow-orange for women, sages and priests.

The stories come mostly from the Ramayana and the Mahabharata, so the cast is full of heroes and villains.

Building the chutti

The chutti is the most striking part. Traditionally it was made from rice paste mixed with lime, laid on in thin layers and shaped as it dried. Today many troupes cut it from white paper and stick it on with rice paste, which is faster.

The artist works slowly, ridge by ridge, until the frame sticks out several centimetres from the cheek. It turns the face into a glowing shape that can be seen from the back of an open-air crowd. That mattered, because performances once ran all night by the light of a single big brass oil lamp.

When the show ends, the chutti is peeled off in pieces and the colours are scrubbed away with coconut oil. Hours of work vanish in minutes, and the next night it all starts again.

What is the Mahabharata?It has about 100,000 verses, making it one of the longest poems ever composed. Hidden inside it is the Bhagavad Gita.

Red eyes on purpose

Kathakali actors train their eyes for years. They learn to roll them, flick them and make each eyebrow dance alone. To make the eyes look even more dramatic, some actors place a tiny seed from the chundapoo plant under the lower eyelid. It makes the white of the eye turn red for the performance.

The make-up is designed to show off that face. Unlike a mask, it moves with every twitch, so a raised eyebrow or a quiver of the cheek still reaches the audience.

A school for faces

Kathakali grew in Kerala in the 1600s, partly from an older temple drama called Ramanattam. By the early 1900s it was losing support. In 1930 the poet Vallathol Narayana Menon helped set up Kerala Kalamandalam, a school that trained a new generation of dancers and make-up artists.

Students there study for years. Make-up artists learn as apprentices, watching masters before they are trusted with a real face. Many of the best actors started training as young as ten.

Kathakali has toured the world, from Paris to New York. Yet a show in a Kerala temple courtyard still begins the old way, with drums and a lamp. And the strangest part? The way actors talk with their eyes is a whole language of its own, decoded in how Kathakali tells stories with faces.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Kathakali dancers wear masks.

Truth: Most of what looks like a mask is painted make-up and a frame built directly on the actor's face. That lets the face move, which matters, because Kathakali actors tell much of the story with their eyes, cheeks and eyebrows.
