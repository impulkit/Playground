What is the Red Fort, and why does India celebrate there?

Every 15 August, India's Prime Minister raises the flag at the Red Fort, a palace built by the Mughal emperor who also built the Taj Mahal.

The short answer

The Red Fort is a huge fortified palace in Old Delhi, built by the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan between 1639 and 1648. Its massive walls of red sandstone stretch about 2.4 kilometres around, enclosing halls, gardens, mosques and private apartments. In its day it was called Qila-e-Mubarak, the Blessed Fort.

For about two centuries it was the home of the Mughal emperors. Today it is a symbol of the Indian nation. Every Independence Day, the Prime Minister raises the national flag on its ramparts and speaks to the country. It became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2007.

Surprising but true

The famous Peacock Throne, covered in gold and gems, once stood in the Red Fort. In 1739 the Persian ruler Nadir Shah carried it away after sacking Delhi.

A new capital for an emperor

Shah Jahan, the emperor who built the Taj Mahal at Agra, decided to move his capital to Delhi. He built a whole new walled city there, called Shahjahanabad, with the Red Fort at its heart beside the Yamuna river. The architect is believed to be Ustad Ahmad Lahori, who is also linked to the Taj Mahal.

The fort took about nine years to build. Its two main gates are the Lahori Gate, facing Lahore, and the Delhi Gate. Through the Lahori Gate, visitors entered a covered market street called the Chatta Chowk, where merchants sold silks, jewellery and fine goods to the court.

Halls of public and private audience

In the Diwan-i-Aam, the Hall of Public Audience, the emperor sat on a marble throne under a canopy and listened to ordinary people's requests and complaints. In the Diwan-i-Khas, the Hall of Private Audience, he met nobles and foreign visitors.

The Diwan-i-Khas once held the Peacock Throne, made of gold and covered in jewels, with two peacocks at its back. A Persian verse is carved in the hall. Roughly translated, it says that if there is a paradise on Earth, it is this, it is this, it is this. A stream called the Nahr-i-Bihisht, the Stream of Paradise, once flowed through the palace rooms, cooling them in summer.

Raids, rebellion and the British

After Shah Jahan, the Mughal empire slowly weakened. In 1739, the Persian ruler Nadir Shah invaded, looted Delhi and carried off the Peacock Throne. Later, Maratha and other armies fought for control of the city.

In 1857, Indian soldiers rebelled against the British East India Company and marched to Delhi, where they declared the old emperor, Bahadur Shah Zafar, their leader. After the revolt of 1857 was crushed, the British put him on trial inside the Red Fort and sent him into exile in Rangoon. They then knocked down many of the fort's buildings and built army barracks in their place.

A trial that stirred a nation

In 1945 and 1946, the British put three officers of the Indian National Army, which had fought alongside Japan under Subhas Chandra Bose, on trial in the Red Fort. The three were a Hindu, a Muslim and a Sikh. People across India protested in their support, and the trials became a rallying point in the last years of British rule.

On 16 August 1947, the day after independence, Jawaharlal Nehru raised the tricolour at the Red Fort. The fort that had been a symbol of empire became a symbol of a free country.

Independence Day today

Every 15 August, crowds of schoolchildren, soldiers, officials and guests gather at the Red Fort before dawn. The Prime Minister arrives, inspects a guard of honour, unfurls the flag from the ramparts above the Lahori Gate and gives a speech broadcast across India. Families watch on television, and kites fill the Delhi sky.

The Red Fort is open to visitors most days. You can walk through the Lahori Gate, along Chatta Chowk and past the marble halls. In the evening, a sound-and-light show tells its story. Like the Qutub Minar, a short drive away, it lets you stand in the same spaces as rulers from centuries ago.

Myth or fact?

Myth: India's independence was declared with a flag-raising at the Red Fort at midnight on 15 August 1947.

Truth: At midnight, Nehru spoke in the Constituent Assembly. The tricolour was raised at the Red Fort on 16 August 1947, and the Independence Day speech from the fort became a tradition after that.
