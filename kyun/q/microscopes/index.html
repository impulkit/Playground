How did microscopes reveal a hidden world?

In the 1670s, a Dutch cloth seller peered through a lens smaller than a pea and saw tiny creatures swimming in a drop of pond water. Nobody had ever seen them before.

The short answer

A microscope uses lenses to bend light so that tiny things look much bigger. Before microscopes, nobody knew that a drop of water could be crowded with living creatures. Nobody knew that blood is made of separate cells, or that germs too small to see could cause disease.

The first microscopes appeared in the Netherlands around 1600, made by spectacle makers who found that stacking lenses could magnify things. In the 1660s and 1670s, two men used microscopes to open up a hidden world: Robert Hooke in England and Antonie van Leeuwenhoek in the Netherlands. Later, electron microscopes let scientists see things thousands of times smaller still.

Surprising but true

The word "cell", used for the building blocks of all living things, came from Robert Hooke in 1665. He thought the tiny boxes he saw in a slice of cork looked like the small rooms monks lived in.

Hooke and the flea

Robert Hooke was a brilliant English scientist who built a compound microscope, one with more than one lens. In 1665 he published a book called Micrographia, filled with large, detailed drawings of what he saw.

One showed a flea, drawn so big it folded out of the book, its armoured body and hairy legs shocking to readers. Another showed a thin slice of cork. Hooke saw it was made of tiny empty boxes and called them "cells". He was looking at the walls of dead plant cells, the first cells anyone had described. The word stuck.

The cloth seller of Delft

Antonie van Leeuwenhoek sold cloth in the Dutch city of Delft. Cloth merchants used magnifying glasses to check the quality of threads, and he became hooked on lenses. He taught himself to grind and polish tiny glass beads into lenses, some smaller than a pea, and mounted each between two metal plates.

His simple one-lens microscopes could magnify up to about 270 times, better than anyone else's at the time. Holding them close to his eye, he looked at pond water, rainwater, blood and the scrapings from his teeth. He saw swimming "animalcules", red blood cells and sperm cells. Over about 50 years he wrote nearly 200 letters to the Royal Society in London describing what he found. At first some members doubted him, until others confirmed his observations.

Germs and medicine

For almost two centuries, the tiny creatures were treated mostly as curiosities. Then, in the 1800s, better microscopes helped scientists such as Louis Pasteur in France and Robert Koch in Germany show that particular microbes cause particular diseases. Koch identified the bacteria behind tuberculosis in 1882. In 1884 he announced the cause of cholera, after studying patients in Egypt and in Calcutta.

This led to washing hands, cleaner hospitals, safer water and vaccines. Most microbes, it turned out, are harmless or even helpful. But the dangerous ones could now be seen, studied and fought.

Seeing with electrons

Ordinary light microscopes have a limit. Light waves are too large to show things smaller than about a fifth of a thousandth of a millimetre. Viruses and the fine details inside cells stay blurry.

In 1931, the German engineers Ernst Ruska and Max Knoll built the first electron microscope, which uses beams of electrons instead of light. Electrons behave like waves far shorter than light, so they can show much finer detail. Modern electron microscopes can show individual atoms. Ruska shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1986.

A microscope made of paper

Microscopes used to be too expensive for many schools. In 2014, Manu Prakash, an Indian-born scientist at Stanford University, and his team introduced the Foldscope, a microscope folded from a sheet of printed card with a tiny lens. It costs less than a dollar to make in parts and can magnify about 140 times. Children and health workers in India and many other countries have used Foldscopes to look at insects, pollen and pond life.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Antonie van Leeuwenhoek invented the microscope.

Truth: Microscopes existed before him, made by lens makers in the Netherlands around 1600. Leeuwenhoek made far better ones than anybody else in his time, and he was the first to see and describe bacteria and many other tiny living things.

Try this

Make a water-drop magnifier. Lay a piece of clear plastic, such as a food bag, flat over a page of small print. Use a finger or a straw to place one small drop of water on the plastic. Look through the drop. The curved water bends light like a lens and makes the letters look bigger. Try different drop sizes.
