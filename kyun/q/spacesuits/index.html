What does a spacesuit do?

A spacesuit is really a small spacecraft shaped like a person, with its own air, heating, cooling and radio.

The short answer

A spacesuit keeps an astronaut alive where a human body cannot survive on its own. In space there is no air to breathe and no air pressure to hold the body's fluids in place. Temperatures swing from hotter than boiling water in sunlight to far colder than Antarctica in shadow. Tiny specks of dust and rock whizz past faster than bullets.

So a spacesuit does many jobs at once. It holds air at a steady pressure around the body and supplies fresh oxygen. It removes the carbon dioxide you breathe out. It keeps you at a comfortable temperature, protects against dust, glare and some radiation, and carries a radio so you can talk to your crew.

Surprising but true

The suits Apollo astronauts wore on the Moon were sewn by hand by a company whose other business was making bras and girdles.

Two kinds of suit

Astronauts actually use two kinds of suit. During launch and landing they wear a lighter pressure suit, in case the cabin springs a leak. These are the orange or white suits you see when astronauts wave on the way to the rocket.

For spacewalks they need something much tougher. NASA's spacewalk suit has many layers, from a cooling layer next to the skin to a tough, white outer shell. Together with its backpack it weighs over 120 kg on Earth. In orbit it weighs nothing, but it is still heavy to move around, like pushing a loaded shopping trolley.

Keeping cool

Strangely, the hard problem is often getting rid of heat, not keeping warm. An astronaut working hard makes lots of body heat, and the suit is sealed. So next to the skin astronauts wear long underwear threaded with about 90 m of thin tubes. Cool water flows through them and carries the heat away, a bit like the radiator in a car.

Inside the suit the air pressure is only about one-third of normal, which makes the suit less stiff. Before going out, astronauts breathe pure oxygen for hours to flush nitrogen out of their blood, which prevents a painful condition divers call the bends.

Close calls

The first spacewalk nearly ended badly. On 18 March 1965, the Soviet cosmonaut Alexei Leonov floated out of his spacecraft for about 12 minutes. In the vacuum, his suit swelled up like a balloon, and he couldn't get back through the narrow hatch. He let out some air, squeezed back in and survived. It happened in the middle of the space race between the Soviet Union and the United States.

What was the space race?In just twelve years, two rival countries went from launching a beeping metal ball to putting people on the Moon.

In 2013 the Italian astronaut Luca Parmitano was on a spacewalk outside the International Space Station when water began leaking into his helmet. It covered his eyes and nose, and he found his way back to the airlock mostly by feel. A clog in part of the suit's cooling system was to blame, and NASA changed the design afterwards.

Sewn like clothes

The suits that Apollo astronauts wore on the Moon were made by a company called ILC, part of the firm that made Playtex underwear. Skilled seamstresses stitched each suit by hand, from about 21 layers of fabric and rubber, sewing so precisely that a stitch out of place could cause a leak.

Moon dust caused trouble too. It is sharp, like tiny bits of broken glass, and it wore through layers of the suits during the Apollo missions. Engineers designing new suits for NASA's return to the Moon are working hard on that problem.

India is building its own suits for Gaganyaan astronauts. Like every spacesuit before them, they will need to fit each astronaut closely, because long hours in a badly fitting suit can leave hands and shoulders sore and bruised.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Without a spacesuit in space, your body would explode at once.

Truth: Nobody explodes. Without air pressure, a person would pass out within about 15 seconds and need help very quickly to survive. A spacesuit's main job is to stop that by keeping the body under pressure with air to breathe.

Try this

Put on a pair of thick winter gloves or rubber kitchen gloves and try to pick up a coin, button a shirt or tie a shoelace. Time yourself, then try without gloves. Spacesuit gloves are stiffer still, and astronauts work in them for six or seven hours at a time.
