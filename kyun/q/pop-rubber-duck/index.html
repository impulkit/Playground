Why are rubber ducks yellow?

In 1992, thousands of plastic bath toys, including yellow ducks, fell off a ship in the Pacific, and scientists used them to track ocean currents for years.

The short answer

Rubber ducks are yellow because they copy baby ducks. Many duck chicks, like those of farm ducks, are covered in fluffy yellow down. Toy makers chose that cheerful colour, and it stuck.

Yellow also helps the toy show up against bathwater and white tiles, which makes it easy for small children to spot and grab. Over the years, the bright yellow duck with an orange beak became one of the most famous toys in the world.

Most rubber ducks today aren't made of rubber at all. They are made of vinyl, a soft plastic that is cheaper and easier to shape.

Surprising but true

Most rubber ducks aren't made of rubber at all. They are made of vinyl, a kind of plastic.

From rubber to vinyl

Natural rubber comes from the sap of rubber trees. On its own it goes sticky in heat and hard in cold. In the 1840s, Charles Goodyear found a way to make it stronger and more stable by heating it with sulphur, a process called vulcanisation.

After that, factories began making rubber toys, including ducks. Early ones were often solid and didn't float well. In the 1940s, an American sculptor named Peter Ganine designed a duck that could be made in large numbers and floated upright, and he patented it.

Later, makers switched to vinyl. Vinyl ducks are hollow and full of air, which helps them float high on the water. The way air and water hold things up is explained in how ships float.

Ernie's famous friend

In 1970, Ernie, a Muppet on the children's TV show Sesame Street, sang a song called "Rubber Duckie" to his bath toy. The song became a hit and even reached the American pop charts.

After that, the rubber duck was a star. Ernie's duck became so famous that it later showed up on stamps, lunchboxes and countless bath mats. In 2013, it was added to the National Toy Hall of Fame in the United States, alongside toys like the yo-yo and Lego.

Ducks on the ocean

In January 1992, a ship crossing the North Pacific hit a storm. A container fell overboard and broke open. Inside were about 28,800 floating plastic bath toys: yellow ducks, red beavers, blue turtles and green frogs.

The toys drifted for years. Some washed up in Alaska within months. Others travelled much further. Ocean scientist Curtis Ebbesmeyer and his colleagues asked beachcombers to report any they found. The sightings helped them map ocean currents, the giant rivers inside the sea.

The sun and salt bleached many of the yellow ducks almost white. Some were reported to have drifted into the Arctic and onwards towards the North Atlantic, though only a few such finds were ever confirmed. People still report the odd faded toy today.

Giant ducks and helpful ducks

In 2007, the Dutch artist Florentijn Hofman made a giant inflatable rubber duck and floated it in a French harbour. Later versions visited cities around the world. In Hong Kong in 2013, a duck about 16.5 metres tall, as high as a five-storey building, drew huge crowds.

Rubber ducks also raise money. In duck races, thousands of numbered ducks are tipped into a river. People pay for a number, and the first duck past the finish line wins a prize.

Computer programmers have their own use. Some keep a duck on their desk and explain their code to it, line by line. It sounds silly, but saying a problem out loud often reveals the mistake. This is called rubber duck debugging, and it's a real trick used in programming.

And the strangest part? The bath toys lost in 1992 showed scientists how far floating plastic can travel. That lesson matters even more today, as millions of tonnes of plastic rubbish drift through the same currents.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Rubber ducks are made of rubber.

Truth: Early ones were, but most are now made of vinyl, a soft plastic. Toy makers kept the old name because everyone already knew it.

Try this

Float a rubber duck or a small plastic bottle in a sink or bath, then gently push it under and let go. Notice how hard the water pushes it back up. Now add a few coins inside a bottle and see how much weight it can carry before it sinks.
