How can we respect different cultures?

In one country, looking a teacher in the eye shows you are honest. In another, it can seem rude. Respect starts with knowing the difference.

The short answer

A culture is the shared way of life of a group of people: their languages, food, clothes, festivals, beliefs and everyday habits. Respecting a culture means treating those ways as worth understanding, even when they are not yours.

In practice it is mostly small things. You learn how to say someone's name properly. You ask before you touch a sacred object. You try a dish before deciding you don't like it. You notice when a joke depends on a stereotype, a lazy idea that everyone in a group is the same. None of this means you must agree with everything or give up your own ways. It means starting from curiosity instead of judgement.

Surprising but true

A thumbs-up means "well done" in India and the USA, but in some parts of West Africa and the Middle East it has long been seen as a rude gesture.

The iceberg under the water

Teachers often compare culture to an iceberg. Above the water are things you can see: dances, clothes, food, festivals. Below the surface, much bigger, are things you can't see. How close do people stand when they talk? Is it polite to say no directly? Is being five minutes late rude or normal?

Most misunderstandings happen below the waterline. In many American classrooms, a child who looks the teacher in the eye seems honest and alert. In parts of India, East Asia and Africa, children are often taught to lower their eyes when an elder speaks to them, as a sign of respect. The same action sends opposite messages. Neither child is being rude.

Why we judge without meaning to

People tend to treat their own habits as normal and everyone else's as strange. Scientists who study societies have a word for this: ethnocentrism, from the Greek for "nation" and "centre". It is very common and usually not meant unkindly.

In the early 1900s, the anthropologist Franz Boas, who worked in the USA, argued that cultures should be studied on their own terms rather than ranked as "advanced" or "primitive". His students carried this idea into schools and museums. It changed how many people think about other societies, although debates about it continue.

Why is it easier to learn languages when you're young?At six months old, a baby can hear the difference between speech sounds from almost any language. By their first birthday, much of that skill has faded.

Small habits that help

Names come first. Getting a name right, whether it is Nguyen, Siobhan or Venkataraghavan, tells someone you see them. If you aren't sure, just ask. A few words in someone's language help too: a "vanakkam", a "gracias" or a "shukriya" often brings a smile.

Food is another easy door. Many families in India eat with their right hand, and many in China, Japan and Korea use chopsticks. Trying the local way, and asking how, is usually welcome. Places of worship have their own rules, such as covering your head in a gurdwara or taking off your shoes in a temple or mosque. People rarely mind a visitor who asks first.

Body language matters as well. A side-to-side tilt of the head, common in south India, can mean "yes" or "I'm listening". Visitors who expect a nod often find it confusing at first.

Sharing, borrowing and copying

Cultures have always borrowed from each other. Chillies came to India from the Americas, and yoga is now practised in American parks. Borrowing is how food, music and words spread around the world.

People do argue about where borrowing becomes disrespect. A sacred headdress worn as a party costume upsets many Native Americans, because in their nations it must be earned. Some say any sharing is good. Others say it matters who is sharing and whether the original culture is credited.

Schools increasingly try to make room for many traditions. In 2023, New York City added Diwali to its public school holidays, alongside holidays like Lunar New Year and Eid. Children who learn about each other's festivals, food and languages often find the strangest customs make sense once someone explains them.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Everyone from the same country shares one culture.

Truth: India has hundreds of languages and many food, dress and festival traditions. The USA mixes the customs of Native nations and of families from almost every country. Two people with the same passport can grow up with very different habits.

Try this

Interview a classmate, neighbour or relative who grew up somewhere different from you. Ask how they greet elders, what they eat on a special day, and one thing that surprised them about your town. Write down one custom of theirs you had never heard of.
