Where are the oldest rocks on Earth?

The oldest pieces of Earth ever found are crystals smaller than a grain of sand, and they are about 4.4 billion years old.

The short answer

The oldest pieces of Earth ever found come from the Jack Hills in Western Australia. They are tiny crystals of a mineral called zircon, and some are about 4.4 billion years old. That is only about 150 million years after Earth formed.

The zircons sit inside a much younger rock. They are survivors: the rock they first grew in was worn away long ago, and they were washed into new sand and gravel.

The oldest whole rock that most geologists accept is the Acasta Gneiss (say "nice") in the far north of Canada, at about 4 billion years old. A rock belt in Quebec, also in Canada, may be older still, but scientists are still arguing about it.

Surprising but true

Some of the oldest known bits of Earth are not rocks at all, but tiny zircon crystals that survived after the rock they formed in wore away.

Crystals that keep time

Zircon makes a perfect clock. When a zircon crystal forms, it traps a little uranium but almost no lead. Over time, the uranium slowly changes into lead at a known, steady rate. By measuring how much lead has built up, scientists can work out how long ago the crystal formed. That steady rate is measured by the half-life of uranium.

Zircons are also tough. They survive being worn down, washed along by rivers and buried again. Some Jack Hills zircons hold chemical clues that water was already around when they formed. That suggests early Earth cooled and grew oceans sooner than scientists once believed.

Why so little old rock survives

Earth is very good at destroying its own past. Moving plates push old ocean floor down into the hot mantle, where it melts. Mountains rise and are ground down by rain, rivers and ice. Rocks get squeezed and heated until they change into new kinds of rock.

What survives sits in the stable, ancient hearts of continents, called cratons or shields. These are usually flat and low, worn down over billions of years. Canada, Greenland, Western Australia, southern Africa and India all have them.

The rock nobody can agree on

On the eastern shore of Hudson Bay, in Quebec, lies a strip of rock called the Nuvvuagittuq belt. In 2008, a team of scientists reported that some of it might be about 4.28 billion years old. That would make it the oldest rock on Earth by a long way.

Other scientists tested the same rocks with a different chemical clock and got about 3.8 billion years. Both groups are careful researchers. The argument is about which clock gives the true age of the rock, and which only records an older ingredient that went into it. Until that is settled, Acasta keeps the title.

India's ancient heart

India has some very old rock too. The Singhbhum region, spread across Jharkhand and Odisha, and the Dharwar region of Karnataka both hold rocks more than 3 billion years old. Geologists study them to learn how India's piece of crust first formed, long before it joined and left the supercontinents of the past.

Older than Earth, from space

Some rocks are older than anything found on Earth, but they didn't start here. Meteorites are bits of asteroid that fall from the sky. Many formed about 4.56 billion years ago, as the solar system was being built, and have hardly changed since. Measuring them is one of the main ways scientists worked out the age of the Earth.

Moon rocks help too. The Moon has no moving plates and no rain, so its surface keeps old rock better than Earth does. Some crystals in rocks brought back by the Apollo astronauts are about 4.4 billion years old, close to the time the Moon formed after a giant collision with the young Earth.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The oldest rocks on Earth are found in the highest, oldest-looking mountains.

Truth: Tall, jagged mountains are usually young, because they haven't worn down yet. The oldest rocks are mostly found in low, flat, worn-down regions called shields, such as northern Canada, Western Australia and parts of southern and eastern India.

Try this

Collect five or six different pebbles from a garden, park or riverbank with an adult. Wash them and look at them with a magnifying glass. Can you see separate grains or crystals in some of them? Sort them into ones made of grains stuck together and ones that look like interlocking crystals.
