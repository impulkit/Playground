How are cricket balls and footballs made?

The best cricket balls are still stitched by hand, with six neat rows of stitches along the seam, in workshops from Meerut to England.

The short answer

A cricket ball starts with a small ball of cork at its centre. Layers of cork and tightly wound string are built up around it until it's hard and the right size. That core is covered in leather, dyed red, white or pink, and stitched together along a raised seam.

A football is built the other way round: it's hollow. Inside is a rubber bladder, which holds the air. Around it go layers of cloth for strength, then an outer skin of synthetic leather made of panels. Some balls are stitched by hand, some by machine and many top balls are now glued and heat-bonded with no stitches at all.

Both have to meet strict rules on size and weight, so that a ball in Chennai behaves like one in Manchester. A full-size football for top matches must measure 68 to 70 cm around and weigh 410 to 450 grams.

Surprising but true

American footballs are nicknamed "pigskins", but they are made from cowhide or synthetic leather. The name dates from early balls blown up with pig bladders.

Inside a cricket ball

The Laws of Cricket say a men's ball must weigh between 155.9 and 163 grams. The best balls have a leather cover cut into four pieces, sewn into two halves and then joined around the middle with the main seam. Six rows of stitches make that seam stand out.

In the top balls, the seam is stitched by hand with waxed thread. The ball is then pressed in a mould, polished and stamped with gold lettering.

Three makers dominate Test cricket. Kookaburra, from Australia, is used in most countries. Dukes, made in England, has a very prominent seam that helps bowlers swing the ball. SG, from Meerut in Uttar Pradesh, is used for Test matches in India.

Red, white and pink

Red balls are traditional for Test matches. White balls are used in one-day and T20 games, because they're easier to see under floodlights against dark backgrounds. White balls tend to wear and get dirty faster, so players in one-dayers use one from each end.

The pink ball was created for day-night Test matches. The first was played in Adelaide, Australia, in 2015. Its colour shows up well both in late sunlight and under lights.

The football's changing shape

The classic black-and-white football has 32 panels: 12 pentagons and 20 hexagons. This pattern, used on the Adidas Telstar at the 1970 World Cup in Mexico, makes a shape mathematicians call a truncated icosahedron. It's very close to a sphere, which is why it rolls so well. Shapes like this show up in chemistry too.

For decades, many hand-stitched footballs came from Sialkot, in Pakistan, and from Jalandhar in Punjab, India, which still make large numbers of balls today.

What are 3D shapes?Count the corners, edges and faces of a dice, a pyramid or a cardboard box, and the same small sum always comes out as 2.

Fewer panels, new tricks

Since the 2000s, top footballs have used fewer, curved panels glued together with heat instead of stitches. That makes them rounder and more waterproof. But fewer seams can also change how a ball moves through the air. Some players complained that smooth balls wobbled unpredictably.

The ball for the 2026 World Cup, played in the USA, Canada and Mexico, is called Trionda. It has just four panels, deep grooves to steady its flight, and a small motion sensor inside that sends data to the video referees.

Footballs with points

American footballs are pointed at both ends, which makes them easier to throw in a spiral. They are made of four leather panels with a row of laces for grip. They're still often called "pigskins", even though the leather is cowhide.

A good grip matters in every sport. Leather cricket balls, laces on American footballs and dimpled panels on soccer balls all rely on friction to let players control where the ball goes.

Myth or fact?

Myth: American footballs are made of pigskin.

Truth: Modern American footballs are made from cowhide or synthetic leather. The nickname comes from the 1800s, when many balls were made by blowing up a pig's bladder and covering it with leather.

Try this

Collect a few old balls at home, such as a tennis ball, a rubber ball and a football. Drop each from the same height onto the same floor and measure how high it bounces with a ruler or a mark on the wall. Then guess what's inside each one that makes it bounce more or less.
