Why did India's vultures almost disappear?

In about fifteen years, India lost almost all of its vultures, and the cause turned out to be a common painkiller given to cattle.

The short answer

India's vultures were poisoned by a medicine. In the 1990s, farmers began giving sick cattle a cheap painkiller called diclofenac. When a treated animal died, traces of the drug stayed in its body. Vultures that ate the carcass suffered kidney failure and died, even from tiny amounts.

In the 1980s, vultures were among the most common big birds in India. By the mid-2000s, three species had fallen by more than 95%. The white-rumped vulture, once seen in huge flocks, dropped by more than 99%.

Surprising but true

Some of India's vulture species fell by more than 99% in about 15 years, one of the fastest crashes of any bird ever recorded.

A mystery in the sky

In the late 1990s, bird scientists such as Vibhu Prakash of the Bombay Natural History Society noticed that vultures were vanishing fast. Dead birds were found slumped on branches, their heads hanging down. Nesting colonies in Keoladeo National Park in Rajasthan went quiet.

At first, nobody knew why. Was it a virus? A pesticide? Scientists tested every idea they could think of. In 2004, a team working in Pakistan, led by the American vet Lindsay Oaks, published the answer. The dead vultures had gout and kidney damage, and traces of diclofenac in their bodies.

How a painkiller became a poison

Diclofenac is safe for cattle at normal doses, and people take it too. But a vulture's body cannot get rid of it. The drug damages the bird's kidneys, and the bird dies within days.

Because vultures feed together, one poisoned carcass could kill many birds at once. Scientists later calculated that less than 1 in 100 carcasses needed to carry the drug to cause a crash this fast. That is how a few treated cows could empty India's skies.

The clean-up crew

Vultures have a job nobody else does as well. A flock can strip a dead cow to its bones within an hour. In a country with many millions of cattle, they quietly cleaned up an enormous amount of dead meat.

When the vultures vanished, carcasses lay rotting for days. Stray dogs and rats moved in to feed, and their numbers grew. More dogs meant more dog bites and more cases of rabies. A 2024 study by two economists even estimated that the loss of vultures may have led to hundreds of thousands of extra human deaths, through dirtier water and more disease.

In Mumbai, the Parsi community had long laid their dead in Towers of Silence, where vultures did their natural work. With the birds gone, the community had to find new ways to keep this tradition going.

Bringing them back

In 2006, India banned diclofenac for animals. Shops still sold human-sized bottles for use on cattle, so in 2015 the government limited the size of the bottles too. In 2023 it also banned two other animal painkillers, aceclofenac and ketoprofen, for the same reason.

Scientists set up breeding centres, starting with one at Pinjore in Haryana in 2001. Vultures breed slowly, laying one egg a year, so progress is slow. Some captive-bred birds have now been released into the wild, in areas where farmers and vets agree to use safer drugs, such as meloxicam.

A comeback is possible. In the USA, the California condor, a giant New World vulture, fell to just 27 birds in 1987. Careful breeding has brought it back to hundreds. In India, vultures were honoured long before scientists studied them. In the Ramayana, the vulture king Jatayu gave his life trying to rescue Sita. The bald eagle shows that birds can recover from a poison once people stop using it. Today's job is to give Jatayu's descendants the same chance.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Vultures are dirty birds that spread disease.

Truth: Vultures mostly eat animals that are already dead, and their very strong stomach acid destroys many dangerous germs. By cleaning up carcasses quickly, they help stop disease from spreading.
