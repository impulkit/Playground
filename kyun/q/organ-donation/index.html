What is organ donation?

The first lasting kidney transplant, in 1954, worked because the donor and the patient were identical twins.

The short answer

Organ donation means giving a part of your body to help someone whose own organ has stopped working properly. The organ is moved in an operation called a transplant. Kidneys, livers, hearts, lungs and pancreases can all be transplanted, and so can tissues like corneas, skin and heart valves.

Most organs come from people who have died in hospital, often after a serious injury to the brain, and whose families agree to donate. Some come from living donors, who give one of their two kidneys or part of their liver. One donor who has died can help up to eight people, and many more through donated tissue.

Surprising but true

A living person can give away part of their liver, and both the donor's liver and the piece they gave usually grow back to nearly full size within a few months.

How it began

For a long time, transplants failed. The patient's immune system, which protects the body from germs, attacked the new organ as if it were an invader.

In 1954, at a hospital in Boston, surgeon Joseph Murray moved a kidney from Ronald Herrick into his identical twin brother Richard. Because twins have almost the same genes, Richard's body accepted it. Murray later won a Nobel Prize. By the 1960s and 1970s, new medicines that calm the immune system made it possible to transplant organs between people who were not twins.

When the donor has died

Most organ donation after death happens in hospital intensive care units. Sometimes a person's brain is so badly injured that it has completely and permanently stopped working, even though machines keep their heart beating and lungs breathing. Doctors call this brain death, and they test for it very carefully, following strict laws.

At that point, a counsellor may ask the family whether their loved one would have wanted to donate. Many families say that knowing their loved one helped others gave them some comfort. The organs are removed with care and respect, and rushed to patients chosen by medical need and how well they match. Speed matters. A heart needs to be transplanted within about four to six hours, so cities sometimes clear roads to let an ambulance race through.

Living donors

You have two kidneys but can live a healthy life with one. So a parent, brother, sister or spouse can donate one to a family member. The liver is even more unusual. A surgeon can remove part of a living donor's liver, and both pieces regrow to nearly full size within a few months.

Living donors go through many tests first, to make sure donating is safe for them. In India, most transplants come from living donors, often mothers and wives giving to family members.

India and the United States

India carried out about 18,900 transplants in 2024, a record. But donation after death is still rare, at less than one donor for every ten lakh people. Tamil Nadu, Telangana, Karnataka, Maharashtra and Gujarat have strong programmes. India's law on transplants, passed in 1994, set rules for brain death and banned buying and selling organs.

In the United States, more than 100,000 people are on the waiting list. Many Americans sign up as donors when they get a driving licence. In India, adults can pledge to donate online through the government's NOTTO website.

Talking about it

Organ donation can feel like a hard subject, because it involves illness and death. Doctors and donor groups say the most useful thing families can do is talk about it calmly ahead of time, so everyone knows each other's wishes.

Children can't register as donors themselves, but they can learn how the body works and ask questions. If you want to know more, a parent, teacher or doctor is a good person to talk to. Every heart, kidney or cornea transplanted began with someone's decision to give.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Only young and perfectly healthy people can donate organs.

Truth: People of many ages and with many health conditions can donate. Doctors check each organ at the time of donation to see whether it can be used, so age or illness alone does not rule someone out.

Try this

Make a simple "body map". Draw an outline of a person and mark where the heart, lungs, liver and kidneys are, using a book or website to check. Then talk with a family member about how each organ helps you live. If they want to, they can tell you what they think about organ donation.
