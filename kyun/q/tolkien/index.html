Who was J.R.R. Tolkien?

Marking dull exam papers, a professor found a blank page and wrote: "In a hole in the ground there lived a hobbit."

The short answer

J.R.R. Tolkien was an English professor and writer, best known for The Hobbit (1937) and The Lord of the Rings (1954–1955). These books created Middle-earth, a world of hobbits, elves, dwarves, wizards and dragons, and helped shape modern fantasy writing.

His day job was the study of old languages. He taught Old English, the language of the Anglo-Saxons, at Oxford University for decades. He loved inventing languages so much that he built whole histories and maps around them. The Lord of the Rings has sold well over a hundred million copies and been turned into famous films.

Surprising but true

Tolkien said he invented his Elvish languages first, and wrote The Lord of the Rings partly to give those languages a world and a history to live in.

A boy from two continents

John Ronald Reuel Tolkien was born in 1892 in Bloemfontein, South Africa, where his father worked for a bank. He moved to England with his mother when he was three. His father died soon afterwards, and his mother died when he was twelve. A Catholic priest became his guardian.

As a boy he was already inventing languages. At Oxford he studied old tongues such as Old English, Old Norse and Gothic, and fell in love with Finnish and Welsh. His two main Elvish languages, Quenya and Sindarin, borrow the sound of Finnish and Welsh.

In 1916 he fought in the Battle of the Somme in the First World War. Most of his close school friends were killed in the war. He fell ill with trench fever and was sent home. Some readers see the dead marshes and ruined lands of Mordor as echoes of the trenches, though Tolkien warned against reading his book as a simple copy of real events.

Words, dictionaries and dragons

After the war, Tolkien worked on the Oxford English Dictionary, researching the history of words beginning with W, such as "wasp", "walrus" and "warm". It suited him perfectly. To Tolkien, every word had a story.

He also studied old poems such as Beowulf, in which a hero fights a dragon, and the Norse sagas. Their dragons, dwarves and rings found their way into his own stories. Even the names of the dwarves in The Hobbit, such as Thorin, Bombur and Fili, come from a list in an old Norse poem. So does the name of the wizard, Gandalf.

From a blank page to Middle-earth

Tolkien told the story of the blank exam page in a letter. He made up The Hobbit for his own children, then published it in 1937. It was a success, and the publisher asked for a sequel.

That sequel took him about twelve years. The Lord of the Rings follows the hobbit Frodo on a journey to destroy a ring of terrible power. It is far darker and longer than The Hobbit. Tolkien read chapters aloud to a group of friends who met in Oxford pubs, called the Inklings. One of them was C. S. Lewis, who encouraged him to keep going.

Middle-earth in New Zealand

Between 2001 and 2003, the director Peter Jackson released three films of The Lord of the Rings, shot in New Zealand. The last one, The Return of the King, won 11 Oscars, tying the record. Hobbiton, the village set, is now visited by tourists from around the world.

Tolkien's languages are now studied by fans who write poems in Elvish. That would surely have pleased the professor who once said he wrote his stories to give his languages a home.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Lord of the Rings is a trilogy, three books written one after another.

Truth: Tolkien wrote it as a single long novel. It was published in three volumes for cost reasons, and he did not like it being called a trilogy.
