What is the lion-tailed macaque?

A shy black monkey with a silver mane lives only in the rainforests of the Western Ghats, and by 2026 rope bridges had been strung across roads to stop it being run over.

The short answer

The lion-tailed macaque is a monkey found only in the rainforests of the Western Ghats, in Kerala, Tamil Nadu and Karnataka. It has glossy black fur and a thick silver-white mane framing its face, like a grey beard. Its tail ends in a little tuft, a bit like a lion's, which gives it its name.

It is one of the rarest monkeys in India. Only a few thousand live in the wild, by most estimates, and the IUCN lists it as Endangered. The main threat is the loss and breaking up of its forest by tea and coffee estates, roads and dams.

Surprising but true

The lion-tailed macaque became the symbol of the Save Silent Valley campaign in Kerala, which stopped a dam in the 1980s and saved one of India's last untouched rainforests.

Life in the treetops

Lion-tailed macaques live high in the canopy of evergreen forests, where they eat fruit, seeds, flowers, insects and sometimes small lizards. They live in troops of about 10 to 20, led by one or a few adult males.

Each morning they move through the treetops, calling to keep the group together. They are shy and quiet compared with other macaques, and they rarely come to the ground in good forest. A favourite food is the fruit of a tall rainforest tree called Cullenia, which only grows in old, undisturbed forest. Females usually give birth only every couple of years, so populations grow very slowly.

The monkey that saved a forest

In the 1970s, the Kerala government planned a hydroelectric dam in Silent Valley, a stretch of rainforest so quiet that it seemed to have no cicadas. Scientists, writers, poets and local people objected. They argued that the dam would flood one of India's last untouched rainforests and the home of the lion-tailed macaque.

The macaque became the face of the campaign, appearing on posters and in newspapers. In 1983 the dam plan was dropped, and in 1984 Silent Valley became a national park. It is often remembered as one of India's first big environmental victories, alongside movements like Chipko, and part of the story of environment heroes.

Islands of forest

Many lion-tailed macaques today live in small forest patches surrounded by tea and coffee estates. One famous area is the Valparai plateau in the Anamalai Hills of Tamil Nadu, where troops survive in fragments of rainforest.

The problem is roads. To reach other patches, the monkeys must come down and cross busy roads, where some are hit by vehicles. Researchers estimated that at least 40 macaques died on Valparai's roads over about two years, mostly in accidents.

Bridges in the sky

Conservationists came up with a clever answer: canopy bridges, ropes or nets strung between trees on either side of the road, so monkeys can cross above the traffic. In 2025 and 2026, the Tamil Nadu forest department put up 10 canopy bridges in the Valparai and Manombolly ranges, and the macaques began using them.

Forest officials also put up speed breakers and signboards, and volunteers stand by roads to slow traffic when troops cross. Planting native trees between forest patches helps reconnect the canopy for the future. Some tea estates now leave strips of forest standing along streams, giving the monkeys safe corridors.

A Western Ghats specialist

The Western Ghats are older than the Himalayas and full of species found nowhere else, including Nilgiri tahrs, purple frogs and many orchids. The lion-tailed macaque is one of their best-known residents.

Saving it means saving its forest, which also protects water supplies for millions of people in southern India. Rope bridges for monkeys may sound new, but people in Meghalaya have been growing bridges across rivers for centuries, as you can see with the living root bridges.

Myth or fact?

Myth: All monkeys in India are common and live happily near towns.

Truth: Rhesus macaques and langurs often thrive near people, but the lion-tailed macaque depends on old rainforest and avoids open country. It is one of the rarest primates in India.

Try this

Make a model canopy bridge. Tie two pieces of string between two chairs, about a ruler's width apart, and tie short strings across them like ladder rungs. Test how far apart the chairs can be before the bridge sags too much to carry a small soft toy. Real canopy bridges face the same problem.
