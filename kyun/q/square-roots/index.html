What is a square root?

A schoolchild's clay tablet from Babylon, nearly 4,000 years old, shows the square root of 2 worked out to about six decimal places.

The short answer

A square root answers the question: which number, multiplied by itself, gives this number? The square root of 25 is 5, because 5 × 5 = 25. The square root of 100 is 10. We write it with a special sign, √, so √25 = 5.

The name comes from squares. If a square has an area of 49 tiles, its sides are √49 = 7 tiles long. Finding a square root is like working backwards from the area of a square to the length of its side. Some numbers, like 2, have square roots whose digits go on forever.

Surprising but true

The square root of 2 can never be written exactly as a fraction or a decimal that ends or repeats. Its digits go on forever without a pattern.

Perfect and not so perfect

Numbers like 1, 4, 9, 16, 25 and 36 are called perfect squares. Their square roots are whole numbers. Most numbers are not like that. √2 is about 1.414. √10 is about 3.162. Their decimals never end.

In ancient Greece, followers of Pythagoras proved that √2 cannot be written as a fraction at all. Numbers like this are called irrational, meaning they can't be written as a ratio of two whole numbers. A legend says a follower named Hippasus was drowned for revealing it, but there is no reliable evidence for the story.

A tablet from Babylon

At Yale University in the USA there is a small, round clay tablet called YBC 7289. It comes from ancient Babylon, in Mesopotamia, and is thought to date from around 1800 to 1600 BCE. It may have been a student's exercise.

On it is a square with its diagonals drawn in. Next to the diagonal is a number, written in the Babylonian base-60 system, that equals about 1.41421296. That is the square root of 2, correct to about six decimal places. The scribe also used it to work out the diagonal of a square with sides of 30.

In India, the Sulba Sutras gave √2 as about 1.4142156, and in 499 CE Aryabhata described a step-by-step method for finding square roots of large numbers.

Guess, check, improve

How did people find square roots before calculators? One good method is very old, and sometimes called the Babylonian method. Make a guess. Divide the number by your guess. Then take the average of your guess and the answer. That average is a better guess. Repeat.

Try it for √10. Guess 3. 10 ÷ 3 is about 3.33. The average of 3 and 3.33 is about 3.17. Try again with 3.17 and you get about 3.1623, which is almost exactly right. A modern calculator uses clever methods like this, repeated very fast.

Where square roots show up

Square roots appear whenever squares and distances do. The Pythagorean theorem says a² + b² = c², so finding the long side of a triangle means taking a square root. A builder checking a diagonal, a game working out how far apart two characters are, and a phone's GPS all use them.

They also show up in nature and science. A pendulum's swing time depends on the square root of its length, so a pendulum four times as long swings only twice as slowly. And what about the square root of a negative number, like −1? No ordinary number works, because any number times itself is positive. So mathematicians invented a new one, called i. It sounds like make-believe, but engineers use it every day to design electrical circuits.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The square root of a number is half of it.

Truth: The square root is the number that, multiplied by itself, gives the original. The square root of 16 is 4, not 8. The two match only for 0 and 4: half of 4 is 2, and 2 × 2 = 4.

Try this

Take 36 coins, buttons or small stones and try to arrange them into a perfect square. How many are on each side? Now try 30 of them. You will find you can't make a square, which is why 30 has no whole-number square root.
