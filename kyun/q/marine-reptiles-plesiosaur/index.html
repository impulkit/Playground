What was a plesiosaur?

When Mary Anning dug up a near-complete plesiosaur in 1823, the most famous scientist in Europe suspected its impossibly long neck was a fake.

The short answer

A plesiosaur was a sea reptile that lived from about 203 to 66 million years ago, alongside the dinosaurs. Most had a small head, a long neck, a broad, flat body, four big flippers and a short tail. Some were the size of a person. The biggest were longer than a school bus.

They breathed air, so they had to come to the surface. They ate fish, squid-like animals and shellfish. They lived in oceans all over the world and died out at the same time as the big dinosaurs, after a giant asteroid hit Earth 66 million years ago.

Surprising but true

In 1868, a famous American fossil hunter put the head of an Elasmosaurus on the end of its tail. Another scientist spotted the mistake, his rival made sure everyone heard about it, and the embarrassing row helped start the "Bone Wars".

Mary Anning's discovery

Lyme Regis is a small town on England's south coast, where the cliffs are packed with fossils. Mary Anning grew up there, poor, selling fossils to tourists to help her family. In 1823, when she was 24, she found an almost complete plesiosaur skeleton.

It looked so odd that the French expert Georges Cuvier wondered if someone had joined two animals together. No living creature had a neck that long. Scientists studied it at a meeting in London and agreed it was real. Cuvier accepted it. But as a woman, Anning was not allowed to join the Geological Society, and for many years others took credit for her finds. Her plesiosaur is now kept at the Natural History Museum in London.

Necks, big and short

The plesiosaur group had two main body shapes. Elasmosaurs had extremely long necks. Elasmosaurus, found in Kansas, had a neck about 7 metres long with more than 70 bones in it. Elasmosaurs had more neck bones than any other animals we know of. A giraffe has just seven.

Pliosaurs had the opposite shape: short necks and enormous heads, with teeth like bananas. Some had skulls more than 2 metres long. They were among the top hunters of the ancient seas, eating fish, other reptiles and even smaller plesiosaurs.

A head on the wrong end

In 1868, the American scientist Edward Drinker Cope rebuilt an Elasmosaurus skeleton and drew it with the head at the end of its tail. He had never seen a reptile with such a short tail and such a long neck, so he assumed it was the other way round. His old teacher, Joseph Leidy, pointed out the error, and his rival, Othniel Charles Marsh, made sure the story got around. Cope tried to buy up all the copies of the article.

The quarrel between them turned into the Bone Wars, a race to dig up and name more fossils than the other, which lasted for decades.

Babies born at sea

For a long time, scientists wondered how plesiosaurs had young. Their flippers would have made it almost impossible to crawl onto a beach and lay eggs like a sea turtle. In 2011, researchers described a fossil plesiosaur from Kansas with a single large baby inside it. This showed that plesiosaurs gave birth to live young in the water, much as whales and dolphins do today.

The baby was big compared with its mother, which suggests plesiosaurs had one large baby at a time and may have looked after it.

The Loch Ness question

Some people believe the Loch Ness Monster in Scotland is a surviving plesiosaur. The most famous photo of "Nessie", from 1934, was revealed in 1994 to be a model on a toy submarine. Loch Ness also only formed after the last ice age, about 10,000 years ago, and its cold, fresh water could not feed a family of large reptiles. Plesiosaurs left no fossils younger than 66 million years. As far as scientists can tell, the last one swam a very long time ago.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Plesiosaurs were swimming dinosaurs.

Truth: Plesiosaurs were reptiles that lived at the same time as dinosaurs, but they belonged to a separate group. Dinosaurs lived on land. Plesiosaurs, ichthyosaurs and mosasaurs were different families of marine reptiles.

Try this

Make a plesiosaur from clay or play dough with a small body, four paddle flippers and a neck about twice as long as the body. Try to make it stand the head straight up like a swan. Most scientists now think real plesiosaur necks were too heavy and stiff to hold high out of the water.
