Why are lemons so sour?

Lemon juice is about as acidic as vinegar, and the whole citrus family, from limes to oranges, probably began in the foothills of the eastern Himalayas.

The short answer

Lemons are sour because their juice is full of acid, mostly citric acid. About 5 to 6 out of every 100 grams of lemon juice is citric acid. That makes it about as acidic as vinegar.

Your tongue has special cells for sourness. They notice acids by sensing tiny charged particles that acids release, and they send a sharp "sour" signal to your brain. That is the pucker you feel.

Oranges have citric acid too, but much less, and lots more sugar. So an orange tastes sweet, while a lemon makes your eyes water. Limes are about as sour as lemons, and a grapefruit sits somewhere in between.

Surprising but true

The lemon isn't a wild fruit. It is a cross between two older citrus plants, the citron and the bitter orange.

Why be so sour

A plant doesn't make fruit for fun. Fruit holds seeds, and the plant needs animals to eat the fruit and spread those seeds when the fruit is ready.

Many unripe fruits are sour or bitter, which puts animals off until the seeds are mature. As most fruits ripen, acid goes down and sugar goes up. Lemons are unusual: they keep much of their acid even when ripe. People like that sharpness, so farmers have picked and grown the sourest kinds for hundreds of years.

A family from the Himalayas

All citrus fruits are relatives. In 2018 scientists compared the genes of dozens of kinds and traced the family back to the foothills of the eastern Himalayas, around Assam, northern Myanmar and Yunnan in China. Wild citrus still grows in northeast India.

Many citrus fruits we eat are crosses. The lemon is a cross between the citron, a big bumpy fruit used in Indian pickles, and the bitter orange. The sweet orange is a mix of the pomelo and the mandarin.

India grows many of its own kinds. Assam has its long, fragrant Assam lemon, and Nagpur is so famous for its loose-skinned oranges that it is nicknamed Orange City. Florida and California are the big orange growers in the USA.

Citrus spread by trade. Arab traders took lemons around the Mediterranean, and Spanish sailors carried them to the Americas. Today India grows more lemons and limes than any other country, with Mexico not far behind.

The sailors' fruit

For centuries, sailors on long voyages fell ill with scurvy. Their gums bled, their teeth loosened and old wounds opened up. Many died.

In 1747 a Scottish naval doctor named James Lind tried an experiment on a ship. He gave groups of sick sailors different treatments. The two who got oranges and lemons recovered fastest. Decades later, the British navy began handing out lemon juice, and later lime juice, to its sailors. That is how British sailors earned the American nickname "limeys".

We now know the cause. Scurvy comes from too little vitamin C, which citrus fruit has plenty of. Your body can't make it, so it has to come from food.

Sour on purpose

Cooks use sourness to wake up a dish. A squeeze of lime lifts a plate of poha or a bowl of dal. In the USA, lemonade stands are a classic summer job for children, and key lime pie is a favourite in Florida.

Acid also keeps food from going bad, because many germs can't grow in it. That is one reason lemon and lime pickles can sit in a jar for months. And acid stops a cut apple from browning, which is why a few drops of lemon keep fruit salad looking fresh.

Our sense of sour is closely tied to taste and smell together. Bite a lemon with your nose pinched and it still tastes sour, but much of the lemony flavour vanishes.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Lemons are acidic, so eating them makes your body more acidic.

Truth: Your body keeps the acidity of your blood within a very narrow range whatever you eat. Lemon juice is acidic in the glass, but it doesn't change your blood's acidity.

Try this

Tear a few red cabbage leaves into small pieces and soak them in warm tap water for half an hour, squeezing now and then, until the water turns purple. Pour some into three cups. Add lemon juice to one, a pinch of baking soda to another, and leave the third alone. Acids turn the purple water pink, and the baking soda turns it greenish blue.
