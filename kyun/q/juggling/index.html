How do jugglers keep so many balls in the air?

A juggler with three balls only ever holds two at a time. The trick is in the throws, not the catches.

The short answer

Jugglers keep many balls in the air by throwing each one to the same height, at a steady beat. That way, a hand is always empty just before a ball lands in it. Each ball spends most of its time flying, not being held.

The hands do very little work. One throws, the other catches and immediately throws again. A good juggler barely watches the hands at all. Their eyes stay on the top of the arc, where each ball pauses for a split second before it falls.

The cascade

Almost everyone learns the cascade first. It uses three balls, or any odd number. Each ball crosses from one hand to the other in an arc, making a pattern like a figure eight on its side.

The rhythm goes like this. The right hand throws. When that ball reaches its peak, the left hand throws. When the second ball peaks, the right hand throws again, just before it catches the first ball. The timing never changes. That's why beginners often practise with scarves, which float down slowly and give the brain time to keep up.

With an even number of balls, jugglers usually use a different pattern called the fountain, where each hand looks after its own set of balls.

Physics in the air

A ball thrown up slows down, stops for an instant at the top and speeds up on the way down. All balls fall at the same rate, whatever they weigh, because of gravity. A juggler's main job is to make every throw the same height. If one throw goes too low, it arrives early. If it goes too high, it arrives late, and the whole pattern breaks.

More balls means higher throws. To keep five balls going, each must stay in the air longer, so jugglers throw them much higher than three. Every extra ball makes the job a lot harder. That's why the world record is only 11 balls. A juggler named Alex Barron caught 23 throws with 11 balls in 2012.

The maths of juggling

In the early 1980s, jugglers in different countries came up with a way to write tricks as numbers, called siteswap. A "3" means a ball thrown to the height of a normal three-ball cascade. A "5" is a higher throw. Strings like 441 or 531 are recipes for tricks.

Engineers and mathematicians enjoyed the puzzle. Claude Shannon, an American engineer famous for the maths behind digital communication, built juggling machines and wrote a formula linking the number of balls, hands, throw times and empty-hand time. Jugglers still use these number patterns to invent new tricks.

Four thousand years of tossing balls

Juggling is very old. On the wall of a tomb at Beni Hasan in Egypt, painted about 4,000 years ago, women are shown throwing and catching balls, some with arms crossed. Juggling also turns up in ancient China and in Roman writing.

In India, travelling performers called nats and street entertainers have juggled balls, knives and flaming torches for centuries, along with tightrope walking. In the 1700s and 1800s, juggling became a star act in the European circus, and later in American variety shows.

What learning does to the brain

In 2004, German scientists taught a group of adults to juggle for three months and scanned their brains. Areas that track moving objects grew slightly. When the people stopped juggling, those areas shrank again.

Most people can learn the cascade in a few days to a few weeks with short, daily practice. You will drop the balls hundreds of times along the way, and every juggler alive has done the same.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Good jugglers must watch every ball with their eyes.

Truth: Expert jugglers look at the top of the arc, where the balls slow down, and track the rest out of the corner of their eye. Some can even juggle three balls blindfolded, using only the feel of each throw.

Try this

Start with two scarves or soft socks. Throw one up from your right hand, and when it reaches its highest point, throw the other from your left. Catch each in the opposite hand. Once that feels easy, add a third and keep the same rhythm of throw, throw, throw.
