Why doesn't Delhi's iron pillar rust?

A 1,600-year-old iron pillar stands in the open air in Delhi, through every monsoon, and has barely rusted.

The short answer

Leave an iron nail outside through one Indian monsoon and it turns orange and flaky. Yet the iron pillar in Delhi's Qutb complex has stood in the open for about 1,600 years and still looks almost smooth. It is about as tall as a two-storey house and weighs more than 6 tonnes, about as much as an adult elephant.

The secret is in the iron itself. The smiths who made it left a lot of phosphorus in the metal. Over time, that phosphorus helped a very thin, very tight layer form on the surface. This layer works like a raincoat, keeping water and air away from the iron underneath.

Surprising but true

The pillar is protected by a rust layer of its own, thinner than a sheet of paper, that formed with the help of phosphorus in the iron.

Who made it?

An inscription in Sanskrit, cut into the pillar in an old script, praises a king called Chandra. Most historians think this was Chandragupta II, one of the great rulers of the Gupta age, around 400 CE. The pillar once had a statue of Garuda on top, the bird who carries the god Vishnu.

Here is the twist: the pillar probably didn't start in Delhi. Many scholars think it first stood somewhere in central India, possibly near Udayagiri in Madhya Pradesh, and was moved to Delhi centuries later. Nobody knows exactly how a 6-tonne pillar was carried hundreds of kilometres.

How do you make 6 tonnes of iron without a factory?

The Gupta smiths had no giant furnace. They made iron in small lumps, each perhaps a few kilograms, in clay furnaces burning charcoal. Then they hammered the hot lumps together, one after another, into a single pillar. This is called forge welding.

Their way of making iron left tiny bits of slag (glassy waste) and lots of phosphorus inside. In modern steel, makers try hard to remove phosphorus because it can make steel brittle. For the pillar, it turned out to be a gift. Similar iron beams were used in temples such as Konark, so these smiths knew their craft.

The thin layer that saved it

Rust is iron joining with oxygen and water. Normal rust is loose and crumbly. It flakes off, exposing fresh iron, which rusts again, and so on until the metal is gone.

In 2002, the metallurgist R. Balasubramaniam at IIT Kanpur studied the pillar closely. He found a protective film only about one-twentieth of a millimetre thick. It contains iron, oxygen, hydrogen and phosphorus, packed tightly together. One compound in it, called misawite, fills the gaps so water can't sneak through. Delhi's long dry months, when the pillar can dry out completely, help too.

A hug for luck

For generations, visitors had a tradition: stand with your back to the pillar and try to wrap your arms around it. If your fingers met, people said, you would have good luck. So many hands rubbed the pillar that the lower part became shiny and worn.

In 1997, a fence was put around it to protect it. Today you can only look. Nearby is a dent where some say a cannonball struck in the 1700s, and the pillar shrugged it off. And there's another iron pillar story: a much larger broken one lies at Dhar in Madhya Pradesh, and it too has resisted rust. Yet both are youngsters: smiths in India were already smelting iron about 3,000 years ago, in what we now call the Iron Age.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The iron pillar never rusts at all.

Truth: It does rust, just very slowly and only on the surface. A thin protective layer stops the rust from eating deeper. The part of the pillar once buried in damp soil has corroded more than the part in the air.

Try this

Put an iron nail in a glass of tap water and another in a dry, sealed jar. Check them every day for a week. Notice how fast the wet nail turns orange, and remember that the iron pillar has stood in the rain for centuries.
