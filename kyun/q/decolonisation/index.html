How did so many countries become free after 1945?

In 1945 the United Nations had 51 members. Within about 30 years, more than 80 new countries had joined, most of them former colonies.

The short answer

At the end of the Second World War, a few European countries still ruled huge parts of Asia, Africa, the Caribbean and the Pacific. Britain, France, the Netherlands, Belgium and Portugal had colonies on almost every continent.

Over the next 30 years, nearly all of those colonies became independent nations. People in the colonies had been demanding freedom for decades, and the war had left the old empires poorer and weaker. This great change is called decolonisation. It added more than 80 new countries to the United Nations.

Surprising but true

In 1960 alone, 17 African countries gained independence, so many that the year became known as the "Year of Africa".

Why 1945 was a turning point

The war cost Britain and France enormous sums, and Japan's early victories in Asia had shown that European armies could be beaten. Millions of soldiers from the colonies, including about 2.5 million from India, had fought for the Allies. They came home asking why they should fight for freedom abroad but not have it at home.

The new United Nations Charter spoke of the "self-determination of peoples", meaning their right to choose their own government. The two new superpowers, the USA and the Soviet Union, disagreed about most things, but both opposed the old European empires.

India leads the way

India was the biggest colony to break free. After a long freedom struggle, British India became independent in August 1947 and was divided into India and Pakistan, a partition that cost many lives. Burma (now Myanmar) and Ceylon (now Sri Lanka) followed in 1948.

India's independence inspired movements across Asia and Africa. Indonesia declared independence from the Netherlands in 1945 and won it after four years of fighting. Vietnam's struggle against France, and later against the United States, lasted decades.

Africa's year

In 1957 Ghana, led by Kwame Nkrumah, became one of the first colonies in sub-Saharan Africa to win independence. At midnight on the big day, Nkrumah told a huge crowd in Accra that Ghana was "free for ever".

Then came 1960, when 17 African nations became independent, including Nigeria, Senegal and the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Some handovers were peaceful. Others came only after bitter wars, such as Algeria's eight-year fight against France and the Mau Mau uprising in Kenya. Portugal held on to its African colonies until the mid-1970s.

Each new nation had to design a flag, write an anthem, print money and train its own officials. Ghana chose red, gold and green, the colours of Ethiopia's flag. Ethiopia had kept its independence for nearly all of the colonial era, and many African countries later borrowed its colours as a sign of shared freedom.

A third way

Many new nations did not want to pick a side in the Cold War. In 1955 leaders from 29 Asian and African countries met at Bandung in Indonesia to talk about cooperation. Nehru, China's Zhou Enlai and Egypt's Gamal Abdel Nasser were among them. In 1961 the Non-Aligned Movement was founded, with India's Jawaharlal Nehru among its leading voices, alongside leaders from Egypt, Yugoslavia, Indonesia and Ghana.

Borders drawn by others

Freedom brought hard problems too. Many borders had been drawn by colonial officials with rulers on maps, cutting across communities, languages and rivers. That left some new countries with disputes and conflicts that still continue.

Some places changed hands much later. India took Goa from Portugal in 1961, and Britain and Portugal handed Hong Kong and Macau to China in 1997 and 1999. A handful of small territories around the world are still governed from far away, and the United Nations keeps a list of them.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Colonial powers gave their colonies freedom as a gift.

Truth: A few handovers were peaceful and planned, but almost all followed years of protest, organising and sometimes war by people in the colonies. Empires let go mostly because holding on had become too costly.
