What was Great Zimbabwe?

Its granite walls stand up to 11 m high and were built without a drop of mortar. A whole country is named after them.

The short answer

Great Zimbabwe was a stone city in southern Africa, built between about 1100 and 1450. It was the capital of a powerful kingdom whose rulers grew rich from cattle and from trading gold and ivory. At its height, perhaps more than 10,000 people lived there, though estimates vary a lot.

Its builders were ancestors of the Shona people, who still live in the region. They raised huge walls of granite blocks without any mortar, and many still stand today. The country of Zimbabwe, which became independent in 1980, took its name from these ruins. In the Shona language, "dzimba dza mabwe" means "houses of stone".

Surprising but true

Glass beads from India and pottery from China and Persia have been dug up at Great Zimbabwe, carried there through Indian Ocean trade.

The Great Enclosure

The most famous part of the site is the Great Enclosure, a huge oval wall up to about 11 m high and roughly 250 m around. It's the largest ancient stone structure in Africa south of the Sahara.

The builders split granite from nearby hills into flat blocks, then stacked them in neat rows without any mortar. Each layer sits slightly back from the one below, so the wall leans gently inwards and stays stable. The top is decorated in places with a zigzag pattern.

Inside stands a solid stone tower shaped like a cone, about 10 m tall. Nobody knows exactly what it was for. It may have been a symbol of the king's power, or a sign of plenty, like a giant grain store.

The Hill Complex

On a rocky hill above the valley sit older stone walls that weave between huge natural boulders. This was probably where the kings lived and where important ceremonies took place. From the top you can see across the whole valley.

Ordinary people lived in round houses of clay and thatch spread around the stone walls. The stone was mostly for the rich and powerful.

Gold, beads and the Indian Ocean

Great Zimbabwe's kings controlled the trade in gold mined on the plateau around them. Traders carried gold and ivory east to ports on the Indian Ocean coast, such as Sofala and Kilwa. Merchants from the Swahili coast sailed these goods north and east across the ocean, using the monsoon winds.

In return, archaeologists have found glass beads from India, pottery from China and Persia, and a coin from Kilwa at the site. Great Zimbabwe was part of a trade network that linked the African interior with India, Arabia and China.

The Zimbabwe birds

Eight carved birds made of soapstone, each on top of a tall pillar, were found at the site. They don't look like any one real bird, though some people see an eagle in them. They may have stood for the spirits of royal ancestors.

Several were taken to South Africa and elsewhere in colonial times. Most have now been returned. The Zimbabwe bird appears on the country's flag and coat of arms.

A long argument

When European visitors reached Great Zimbabwe in the late 1800s, many refused to believe Africans had built it. Some claimed it was the work of the Phoenicians, or the palace of the Queen of Sheba. These ideas helped colonists argue that Africans had no history of their own.

In 1905 the archaeologist David Randall-MacIver, and in 1929 Gertrude Caton-Thompson, studied the site carefully. Both concluded it was African. The white-minority government of Rhodesia still pressured guides and museums to stay vague about this until 1980.

Why the city emptied

By about 1450, Great Zimbabwe was being abandoned. Possible reasons include too many people and cattle for the land, droughts and shifting trade routes. Power moved to other kingdoms nearby, such as Mutapa and Khami. Like Mali in West Africa, it was a rich trading state that the outside world long ignored. Its walls are still there to prove it was real.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Great Zimbabwe was built by foreigners, such as the Phoenicians or the Queen of Sheba, not by Africans.

Truth: These claims were made by European colonists in the 1800s and 1900s and have been disproved. Archaeology, dating and local history all show that the city was built by the ancestors of the Shona people.
