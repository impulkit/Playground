How does a TV remote work?

Every time you press a button, your remote flashes a tiny light thousands of times a second. Your eyes can't see it, but a phone camera can.

The short answer

At the front of most TV remotes is a small bulb called an LED. When you press a button, a chip inside the remote makes the LED flash in a very fast pattern of pulses. The light is infrared, just beyond the red end of the colours we can see, so to your eyes nothing seems to happen.

The television has a sensor that watches for these flashes. Each button has its own pattern, a bit like Morse code, so the TV can tell "volume up" from "channel 7". The whole message takes a fraction of a second.

Surprising but true

One early wireless remote, from 1956, had no batteries at all. Pressing a button struck a small metal rod, which rang at a pitch too high for people to hear, and the TV listened for it.

A code made of light

Inside the remote is a grid of rubber buttons sitting over a circuit board. Pressing one squeezes a dot of carbon onto two metal tracks and completes a circuit. The chip notices which button closed, looks up its code and starts flashing.

That code is a string of on and off pulses, which engineers treat as binary code: ones and zeros. A typical message first says "this is for a TV made by this company" and then "this is the button". That is why a remote for one brand usually does nothing to another brand's TV, and why "universal" remotes store hundreds of codes.

If you hold a button down, the remote keeps sending a short "still pressed" signal, which is how the volume keeps climbing. Air conditioners work the same way, but their remotes send the whole setting each time: temperature, fan speed and mode together. That way, if one message is missed, the next press puts everything right.

Why the light flickers

Sunlight, lamps and fires all give off infrared light too. If the TV just looked for "some infrared", it would get confused all day. So the remote's light flickers on and off thousands of times a second, often about 38,000 times, even within a single pulse.

The sensor in the TV is tuned to that one flicker speed and ignores everything else. It is like being in a noisy classroom and still picking out your friend's voice, because you know exactly how it sounds.

Clickers and sunbeams

The first TV remotes, around 1950, were plugged in with a long wire that people tripped over. In 1955 the American company Zenith sold the Flash-Matic, which an engineer named Eugene Polley designed. It shone a beam of visible light at sensors on the TV. On sunny days, sunlight could hit the sensors and change the channel.

The next year another Zenith engineer, Robert Adler, came up with the Space Command. Pressing a button made a tiny hammer strike an aluminium rod, which rang too high for humans to hear. The TV listened for each rod's note. The remote needed no batteries at all. It made a click as it worked, and many Americans still call a remote "the clicker". Infrared remotes took over in the late 1970s and 1980s.

Remotes that don't need aiming

Some newer remotes, especially for streaming boxes and smart TVs, don't use light at all. They talk to the TV with radio waves, often using Bluetooth, so you can press buttons with the remote under a cushion. Many also have a microphone, so you can just say the name of a show.

All of them still run on batteries, usually two small AAA or AA cells. A typical infrared remote uses so little power that a pair of batteries can last a year or more, because the LED is only on while a button is pressed. Remotes are also built to survive being dropped and sat on, which is why the buttons are soft rubber and the circuit board is sealed inside a tough plastic case.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You have to point the remote straight at the TV, or it won't work.

Truth: Infrared light bounces off walls, ceilings and even windows. You can often aim at the wall behind you and the TV still changes channel.

Try this

Point a TV remote at a phone camera and look at the screen while you press a button. You should see a little light blinking at the front of the remote. If you see nothing, try the phone's other camera, because some have filters that block infrared.
