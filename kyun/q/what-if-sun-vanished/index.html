What would happen if the Sun disappeared?

For 8 minutes and 20 seconds, nobody on Earth would notice anything. Then the sky would go black, and Earth would fly off into space in a straight line.

The short answer

Nothing would seem to happen for 8 minutes and 20 seconds. That's how long sunlight takes to reach us. Then the daytime sky would go black, the Moon would vanish soon after, and the planets would wink out over the next few hours.

At the same moment the light stopped, Earth would stop circling and fly off into space in a straight line. Over the next weeks, the planet would cool fast. Plants would die without light, and most life would follow. But some deep-sea creatures, living on heat from inside the Earth, could carry on for a very long time.

The good news: this can't happen. The Sun has billions of years left, and stars don't just disappear.

Surprising but true

Gravity travels at the speed of light. In 2017, scientists saw this when ripples in space from two crashing dead stars arrived almost exactly with their flash of light, after a journey of 130 million years.

Eight minutes of normal

The Sun is about 150 million km away. Light, moving at about 300,000 km every second, takes about 8 minutes and 20 seconds to cover that distance. So when you look at the Sun, you see it as it was eight minutes ago.

Gravity takes the same time. In 2017, scientists detected ripples in space from two dead stars that crashed together 130 million light years away. A flash of light from the same crash reached telescopes less than two seconds after the ripples. That showed gravity and light travel at the same speed.

Flying off course

Earth races around the Sun at about 30 km every second. The Sun's pull bends that path into a near-circle. If the pull vanished, Earth would carry on straight, like a stone flung from a sling.

It wouldn't crash into anything soon. Space is so empty that Earth could travel for millions of years without hitting another star. The Moon would come along, still circling Earth, because Earth's gravity would still hold it.

A darkening sky

The Moon shines only with reflected sunlight. So it would go dark just over a second after the Sun's light stopped reaching it. Planets would fade one by one, as the last rays reflected off them reached us. Jupiter is so far away that the last sunlight would take ages to reach it and bounce back. It would keep shining for over an hour after our own sky went dark.

The stars would still be there, brighter than ever against a pitch-black sky. Without sunlight, there would be no blue daytime sky, just endless night.

The big freeze

Earth's surface would start cooling at once. Scientists estimate the average temperature would fall below freezing within about a week. After a year, it might be around minus 70°C or even colder.

Oceans would freeze over at the surface. But ice is a good insulator, like a blanket. Under a thick sheet of ice, deep water could stay liquid for thousands, even perhaps a million years, warmed by heat leaking from Earth's hot interior.

What would survive?

Almost all food on Earth comes from photosynthesis, where plants use sunlight to make sugar. Without light, that stops. Small plants would die within weeks. Some large trees, which store a lot of food, might survive for years in the cold.

Animals that eat plants would starve, and then the animals that eat them. People could survive for a while using nuclear power, geothermal heat from hot springs, or by living deep underground near volcanoes. Iceland, with its volcanic heat, might be one of the better places to be.

The real survivors would live at deep-sea vents, cracks in the ocean floor where hot, mineral-rich water gushes out. Tubeworms, shrimps and microbes there get their energy from chemicals, not sunlight. They might hardly notice the Sun was gone.

Earth the wanderer

Earth would become a rogue planet, wandering through space without a star. That sounds strange, but astronomers think our galaxy may contain billions of such planets, flung out of their own solar systems long ago.

Some of them might even have oceans under their ice, kept warm from within. Whether any have life is an open question, and one of the reasons astronomers hunt for rogue planets.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If the Sun vanished, everything on Earth would freeze solid in seconds.

Truth: Earth holds a lot of heat in its rocks, oceans and air, and inside the planet. The surface would cool over days and weeks, and the deep oceans could stay liquid under a thick lid of ice for a very long time.

Try this

Tie a soft ball or a sock to a string and whirl it gently around you in an open space, away from people. Then let go. Watch which way it flies. It shoots off along the curve, not straight out from you. That's what Earth would do without the Sun's pull.
