What is village life like in India?

India has more than 6 lakh villages. In April 2018, the very last of them was connected to the electricity grid.

The short answer

Village life in India is as varied as the country itself. A fishing village in Kerala, a desert village in Rajasthan and a hill village in Nagaland look, sound and eat differently. But many share some things: farming, close-knit families, a weekly market, and neighbours who know each other's business.

About two in three Indians lived in villages at the 2011 census. Most village households depend at least partly on farming, but people also work as weavers, potters, shopkeepers, teachers, drivers and builders. Many villages today have mobile phones, schools and paved roads that did not exist a generation ago.

Surprising but true

About two in every three Indians lived in villages at the last census in 2011, more people than live in the whole of Europe.

The rhythm of the year

In farming villages, the year follows the crops and the rain. Fields are ploughed and sown when the monsoon arrives in June or July. Paddy, cotton, maize or millets grow through the wet months and are harvested in autumn. In many places, a second crop such as wheat or mustard is grown through winter.

A good monsoon can mean a good year. A weak one can mean debt and worry, especially for farmers without wells or canals. That is why many village festivals, from Pongal in Tamil Nadu to Baisakhi in Punjab, are harvest celebrations.

Why does the monsoon come?Every year, the wind turns around. And the rain arrives.

A day in the village

Many villages wake before sunrise. Animals are fed and milked, water is fetched, and fires or gas stoves are lit for breakfast. Children walk or cycle to the village school, where a free hot lunch under the mid-day meal scheme helps keep many of them in class.

Life often happens outdoors, on a shady platform under a big tree, at a place of worship or at the handpump. Then there is the weekly haat, a market where farmers sell vegetables and traders bring clothes, pots and bangles.

Who runs the village

Villages elect a council called a panchayat, headed by a sarpanch. It looks after roads, drains, water supply and local schemes. Since 1993, at least a third of panchayat seats must go to women, and many states reserve half.

Lights, phones and roads

For a long time, many villages had no electricity. On 28 April 2018, the government announced that Leisang, a small village in Manipur, had been connected, the last inhabited village on its list. Not every home in every village had power yet, but the lines had reached all of them.

Since 2000, a national scheme has built lakhs of kilometres of rural roads, linking villages to towns, hospitals and markets. Mobile phones changed things too. Many village shopkeepers now take payments by scanning a QR code with UPI, and farmers check crop prices on their phones before deciding where to sell.

Old and new together

Villages are also places of strong traditions: folk songs, local deities, crafts and stories handed down for centuries. Some have become famous for their own efforts. Mawlynnong in Meghalaya is known across India for its cleanliness, and Hiware Bazar in Maharashtra for collecting and saving rainwater to beat drought.

Village life also has real hardships. Incomes are often lower than in cities, good hospitals may be far away, and many young people leave to find work. Some come back during festivals, bringing city ideas home. Others return for good. For many Indians who live in cities, the family village is still the place they call home.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Most Indians live in cities.

Truth: At the 2011 census, about two in three Indians lived in villages. Cities are growing fast, but village India is still home to most of the country.
