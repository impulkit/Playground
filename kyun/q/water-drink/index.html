Why do we need to drink water?

You can last weeks without food, but only a few days without water, because almost every job in your body happens in water.

The short answer

Most of your body is water. It's in your blood, inside every cell and in the spaces between them. Water carries food and oxygen around your body, and carries waste away. It cushions your brain and joints, keeps your eyes moist and lets you sweat to cool down. Almost every chemical reaction that keeps you alive happens in water.

And you lose water all day: in urine, in sweat, even in every breath you breathe out. If you don't replace it, your blood gets thicker, your heart works harder and your brain gets less efficient. So you need to keep topping up, from drinks and from food.

Surprising but true

The famous rule of eight glasses a day seems to come from a 1945 American report that also said most of that water already comes from food.

How your body keeps the level right

Your body is very good at balancing its water. When you've lost even a little, your blood becomes slightly saltier. A part of the brain called the hypothalamus notices this and does two things. It makes you feel thirsty. And it sends a hormone to your kidneys telling them to save water, so your urine gets darker and smaller in amount.

When you drink more than you need, the kidneys let more go, and your urine turns pale. That's why the colour of your wee is a handy guide. Pale straw is good. Dark yellow usually means drink more.

Not enough water

Losing too much water is called dehydration. Mild dehydration can bring a headache, tiredness and trouble concentrating. It happens more easily in hot weather, when you're running around, or when you're ill with a fever, vomiting or diarrhoea.

Babies and young children dehydrate faster than adults because they are small and lose water quickly. One of the great medical discoveries of the 1900s was oral rehydration solution, a simple mix of clean water, salt and sugar. Research in Kolkata and Dhaka helped show it worked, and it has since saved millions of children's lives around the world.

The eight-glass rule

You may have heard that everyone must drink eight glasses of water a day. Nobody is quite sure where this began, but a 1945 report in the USA suggested about 2.5 litres a day for adults. The next sentence said that most of this comes from food, and people seem to have forgotten it.

Children of around ten need roughly 1.5 litres of drinks a day, more in hot weather or after sport. Water and milk are the best choices. Sugary drinks give water too, but also a lot of sugar. Drinking far too much water very quickly can be dangerous, though this is rare and mostly happens to adults in long endurance races.

Water that is safe

For your body, the water has to be clean. Germs in dirty water cause illnesses like cholera and typhoid. Around the world, about 2 billion people still don't have safe drinking water at home. India's Jal Jeevan Mission, launched in 2019, aims to bring a tap connection to every rural home.

Cities clean water by filtering it and adding small amounts of chlorine. At home, many families in India boil or filter water before drinking. In much of the USA, tap water is treated so it's safe to drink straight away. The water in your glass has travelled through clouds, rivers and underground rock, round and round the water cycle, for billions of years.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Everyone must drink exactly eight glasses of water a day.

Truth: There's no single magic number. How much you need depends on your size, how active you are and the weather, and food provides part of it. Thirst and pale-yellow urine are good everyday guides.

Try this

Breathe slowly onto a cold mirror or a steel tumbler from the fridge and watch it fog up. That mist is water leaving your body with every breath. Count how many breaths it takes to make the mirror properly wet, then think how many breaths you take in a day.
