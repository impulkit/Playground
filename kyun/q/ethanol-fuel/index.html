Can a car run on fuel made from sugarcane?

The same sugarcane that gives you a glass of fresh juice can be turned into a fuel that drives a car.

The short answer

Yes. Sugarcane can be turned into ethanol, a kind of alcohol that burns well in car engines. Almost all petrol sold in India already has about one-fifth ethanol mixed in.

Special cars called flex-fuel vehicles can run on petrol, on almost pure ethanol, or on any mix of the two. A sensor checks what is in the tank, and the engine adjusts itself.

Surprising but true

Henry Ford's famous Model T car, sold from 1908, could run on petrol or on alcohol fuel. Ford called ethanol "the fuel of the future".

How it works

Making ethanol is a bit like making curd. You add a tiny living helper, leave it somewhere warm, and it changes what you started with into something new.

First, sugarcane is crushed to squeeze out its sweet juice. The juice, or the sticky molasses left over after making sugar, goes into big tanks with yeast. The yeast eats the sugar and gives off ethanol and carbon dioxide. This is called fermentation, the same process that makes bread rise and idli batter puff up.

Next, the mixture is heated. Ethanol boils at a lower temperature than water, so it rises first as vapour and is cooled back into liquid. This is called distillation. Grains like maize and broken rice can be used too, after their starch is first broken down into sugar.

Inside a car engine, fuel mixes with air and burns in a tiny explosion that pushes the pistons. This burning is called combustion. Ethanol burns cleanly, with less smoke than petrol, but each litre holds only about two-thirds of the energy. It also soaks up water and can wear out some rubber and plastic parts.

That is why blends are labelled. E10 has 10% ethanol, E20 has 20%, and E100 is almost pure ethanol. Flex-fuel cars have tougher pipes and seals and smarter computers, so they can handle anything up to E100.

Where it came from

Alcohol fuel is as old as the car. Henry Ford's Model T, first sold in 1908, could run on it. But petrol from crude oil was cheap and easy to find, so for decades most countries forgot about ethanol.

That changed in the 1970s, when oil prices shot up. In 1975, Brazil started a giant programme to make ethanol from its sugarcane. Most new cars sold there are now flex-fuel, and drivers choose at the pump. The USA also became a big ethanol maker, mostly from maize.

India's story

India is one of the world's biggest sugarcane growers. Its ethanol journey began with trials in 2001. By 2006, petrol with 5% ethanol was sold in several states. But until 2014, the average blend stayed at only about 1.5%, because there were not enough distilleries.

Then things sped up. A national biofuel policy in 2018 allowed more crops to be used, and new distilleries were built across Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra, Karnataka and other states. The average blend rose to 10% in 2021-22, 14.6% in 2023-24 and 19.2% in 2024-25. India reached its E20 target in 2025, five years earlier than first planned.

In October 2026, India changed its vehicle rules so that flex-fuel vehicles designed by their makers for it can run on E85 and E100.

The numbers that matter

₹1.36 lakh crore. The government says ethanol blending saved this much foreign money over 11 years up to mid-2025, because India had to import less crude oil.

₹1.18 lakh crore. In the same period, the government says farmers were paid more than this for crops that went into ethanol.

3 to 5%. In 2026, the petroleum ministry said E20 may cut a car's mileage by this much, because ethanol holds less energy than petrol.

About 2,860 litres. A NITI Aayog report estimates that making one litre of ethanol from sugarcane uses roughly this much water, most of it for growing the cane.

About ₹71 a litre. That is roughly what oil companies paid distilleries for ethanol in 2026, according to the petroleum ministry. The ministry says E20 costs more to make than plain petrol when crude oil is cheap, but less when oil prices soar.

What people argue about

Supporters say ethanol cuts the oil India must buy from abroad, which keeps more money at home. It gives farmers another buyer for their cane and grain. Government studies say it gives off less greenhouse gas than petrol over its whole life, which helps fight climate change.

Critics raise three worries. First, water: sugarcane is thirsty, and much of it grows in places where groundwater is already running low. Second, food: when maize and rice go into fuel, some say food and animal feed can cost more. Third, cars: some owners of older vehicles say their mileage dropped on E20, and they want pumps to sell lower blends too. The government says E20 is safe for older vehicles and that offering several blends would be costly.

Both sides agree on one point. How much good ethanol does depends a lot on which crop it comes from and how that crop is grown.

Myths and mix-ups

"Ethanol fuel is the same as drinking alcohol." Both are ethanol, but fuel ethanol is made almost pure and has chemicals added so that no one can drink it. Drinking it would be very dangerous.

"Ethanol is always greener than petrol." It can be, but not every time. If forests are cut to grow the crop, or lots of diesel is burnt pumping water, the benefit shrinks.

"Flex-fuel cars must use pure ethanol." They can use any mix, from plain petrol to E100. That is what "flex" means.

What it means for you

The petrol your family puts in a scooter or car already contains ethanol, often made from Indian sugarcane or grain. A small part of every litre comes from farms, not oil wells.

For drivers, E20 may mean slightly fewer kilometres per litre. Owners of older vehicles can check the manual or ask the service centre about rubber parts and fuel pipes. In the years ahead, families buying new vehicles may see flex-fuel models in showrooms, next to electric cars.

For farmers, ethanol can mean steadier prices for cane and maize. And for the country, it is one of several ways, along with electric vehicles and hydrogen, that India is trying to depend less on oil from abroad.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You can pour pure ethanol into any petrol car.

Truth: Ordinary engines are built for petrol or low ethanol blends like E20. High ethanol can damage their seals and pipes and confuse their fuel systems. Only flex-fuel vehicles, made with special parts and sensors, can safely use E85 or E100.

Try this

Mix a teaspoon of sugar and a pinch of dried yeast into a cup of warm water in a bottle, and stretch a balloon over the top. Leave it somewhere warm for an hour. The balloon swells with gas. The yeast is fermenting the sugar, the same first step used to make ethanol.
