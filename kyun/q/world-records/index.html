How do world records work?

The world's most famous record book began with an argument about which bird flies fastest.

The short answer

A world record is the best result ever measured for something, checked by people who follow strict rules. Who checks depends on the kind of record. Sports records, like the fastest 100 m, are approved by the sport's own body, such as World Athletics. Records for almost everything else, from the longest fingernails to the biggest dosa, are judged by organisations such as Guinness World Records.

The key is fairness. A record must be measured the same way each time, with evidence that others can check. Otherwise nobody can say for sure that one result beats another.

Surprising but true

Guinness World Records stopped accepting records for the heaviest pet, because it did not want owners overfeeding their animals to win.

An argument at a shooting party

In 1951, Sir Hugh Beaver, the head of the Guinness brewery in Ireland, argued with friends about which game bird in Europe flew fastest. No book could settle it. He guessed that people in pubs had arguments like this every night.

So he hired twin brothers, Norris and Ross McWhirter, who ran a fact-finding agency in London. Their first book of records came out in 1955. By Christmas it was a bestseller in Britain. It has been published every year since, in dozens of languages.

India got its own record book in 1990, when the Limca Book of Records was first published. It lists feats by Indians and feats done in India, checked by its own editors.

How a record is checked

Guinness first decides whether a category is allowed. It has to be measurable, breakable and possible to repeat under the same rules. Then the person attempting it must send evidence: video, photos, measurements and statements from independent witnesses. Sometimes an official judge, called an adjudicator, attends in person.

Some records are refused or retired for safety. Guinness will not accept record attempts that put people or animals in danger, such as the heaviest pet.

Records in sport

In athletics, a record needs approved timing equipment, a measured track and a drug test. For sprints and long jump, a wind gauge also checks the breeze. A tailwind of more than 2 metres a second is enough to help, so the result cannot become a record.

Usain Bolt's 100 m time of 9.58 seconds, set in Berlin in 2009, is still the men's record. That works out to an average speed of about 37.6 km/h, roughly as fast as a car in a slow city lane. At his top speed he was going faster, about 44 km/h.

Why records keep falling

Records do not stand forever. Better training, food, shoes and tracks help athletes improve. Long-distance runners, for example, now run the marathon in shoes with springy foam and stiff plates that save energy with every step. More people around the world now take part, so the chance of a rare talent turning up grows.

Sometimes records also fall because the rules change. Swimming banned certain full-body suits after 2009, when dozens of records were broken in a short time. Officials decided the suits were doing too much of the work.

The tallest man

Some records may never be beaten. Robert Wadlow of Illinois, USA, who died in 1940, measured 2.72 metres tall. That is about 70 cm taller than a standard doorway. His height came from a medical condition, and how tall people grow usually depends on genes, food and health together.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You have to pay Guinness World Records to set a record.

Truth: Anyone can apply for free, though the review can take weeks or months. People pay only for a faster review or for an official judge to come in person. Either way, the evidence must meet the same rules.

Try this

Hold a "class record" day. Choose fair, safe challenges, like the most beanbags tossed into a bucket in one minute. Write the rules first, choose two witnesses and a timekeeper, and record every attempt in a notebook. Notice how the rules change the results.
