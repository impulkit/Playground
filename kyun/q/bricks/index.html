How are bricks made?

The builders of Mohenjo-daro made bricks to a fixed shape, four times as long as they were thick and twice as wide, more than 4,000 years ago.

The short answer

Bricks are made from clay, the sticky, fine-grained part of soil. Workers dig it, mix it with water until it is like thick dough, and press it into rectangular moulds. The soft bricks are tipped out in rows to dry in the sun for days or weeks.

Then comes the fire. The dried bricks are stacked inside a kiln and heated to around 1,000 °C, several times hotter than a kitchen oven. The heat changes the clay for good. Tiny grains melt at the edges and fuse together, turning the brick into a hard ceramic, like a clay pot. After cooling, the bricks are ready to be laid with mortar into walls.

Surprising but true

Bricks turn red in the kiln because of tiny amounts of iron in the clay, which rusts in the heat. Clay with little iron makes pale yellow or cream bricks.

The oldest building block

People have made sun-dried mud bricks for more than 9,000 years. Early farmers in West Asia shaped them by hand and stacked them into houses. Mud bricks keep homes cool in summer and warm in winter, and many villages still use them.

Fired bricks came later, and they changed cities. The people of the Indus cities, such as Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, used vast numbers of them for houses, drains and wells. Their bricks followed a standard shape, with thickness, width and length in the ratio 1:2:4. The same proportions turned up at sites hundreds of kilometres apart, showing careful planning. Much later, brick became an art material too. The terracotta temples of Bishnupur in West Bengal are covered in carved brick panels showing gods, battles and scenes of village life.

Inside a kiln

In much of South Asia, bricks are still fired in tall chimney kilns standing in fields at the edge of towns. Workers stack thousands of dried bricks in rows, with gaps for hot air. Coal or other fuel is fed in from the top, and the fire slowly moves around the kiln over days, so some bricks heat up while others cool.

Big factories elsewhere use long tunnel kilns. The bricks ride slowly through on carts, passing through warm, hot and cooling zones, like a very slow train. Most factories now shape the clay by squeezing it through a nozzle, a bit like toothpaste, then slicing it into bricks with wires.

Sizes are standard today too. A common modern Indian brick is about 19 cm long, 9 cm wide and 9 cm high. With a centimetre of mortar around it, that makes a neat 20 by 10 by 10, which helps builders plan walls quickly.

Smoke and hard work

India makes more bricks than any country except China, from tens of thousands of kilns. Many burn coal and give off smoke and dust. Around cities like Delhi, brick kilns are one of the sources of winter air pollution. Newer "zigzag" kilns guide the hot air along a winding path, which burns fuel more completely and cuts smoke.

Kiln work is hard and hot. Many workers are migrants who come for the dry season with their families. Some are trapped by debts to kiln owners, and children are sometimes made to work. The government and charities have tried to stop this, with mixed success.

New kinds of bricks

Builders are trying alternatives. Fly ash bricks use ash from coal power stations, mixed with lime and sand, and need no firing. Compressed earth blocks are pressed hard in a machine and dried. Concrete blocks, made with cement, are cheaper to lay in some places.

Still, fired brick lasts. Old Indian forts, British railway stations and the red brick buildings of American cities like Boston have stood for well over a century. Since clay comes from the soil under our feet, the oldest building material is also one of the easiest to find.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A brick is just mud that has dried hard.

Truth: That's true for sun-dried bricks. Fired bricks are different, because the heat of the kiln changes the clay into a kind of stone-like ceramic that will never turn back into mud, even after centuries of rain.

Try this

Make two mini bricks from damp garden soil or clay pressed into an ice-cube tray. Leave them in the sun for a few days until hard. Then put one in a bowl of water and watch what happens. Sun-dried bricks soften in water, which is why kiln-fired bricks were such a big step.
