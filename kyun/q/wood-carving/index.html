How do people carve pictures out of wood?

A carved wooden figure pulled from a Russian peat bog is about 12,000 years old, more than twice the age of Egypt's pyramids.

The short answer

A wood carver starts with a solid block and takes away everything that isn't the picture. There is no glue and no paint needed, just sharp tools and patience. A chisel pushed by hand, or tapped with a wooden mallet, lifts off curls of wood. A curved blade called a gouge scoops out hollows. Small knives do the fine work: an eyelid, a feather, a petal.

The hardest part is the grain. Wood is made of long fibres that run up the trunk, like a bundle of drinking straws. Cut along them and the blade glides. Cut against them and the wood splits or tears. So a carver reads each block before touching it, and plans every cut around the way the tree grew.

Surprising but true

The Shigir Idol, found in a peat bog in Russia in 1890, was carved about 12,000 years ago. That makes it more than twice as old as the pyramids of Giza.

Older than farming

Wood rots, so very old carvings rarely survive. One that did is the Shigir Idol, found by gold miners in a peat bog in Russia's Ural Mountains in 1890. The cold, airless peat kept it safe. Scientists dated it to about 12,000 years ago, when people there still lived by hunting and fishing. It is covered in carved faces and zigzag lines, and its meaning is still a puzzle.

People have carved stone for just as long, but wood was easier to find and quicker to shape, so it became the everyday material for bowls, spoons, doors and toys.

India's carving towns

Saharanpur in Uttar Pradesh is one of India's busiest woodcarving towns. Its workshops turn sheesham, a hard, dark wood, into screens, boxes and furniture covered in vines and leaves. Further south, carvers in Mysuru work in sandalwood, which is soft enough for tiny detail and keeps its scent for years.

In Kerala, temple ceilings and roof brackets are carved with gods, dancers and elephants. In the old lanes of Ahmedabad, many houses have wooden fronts with carved balconies, brackets and doors, some of them a couple of hundred years old. The bright lacquered Channapatna toys of Karnataka start life on a lathe, a machine that spins the wood while a blade shapes it.

Poles that tell a family story

On the Pacific coast of Canada and Alaska, the Haida, Tlingit and other nations carve totem poles from western red cedar. A tall pole can take a team months. The figures, such as ravens, bears, whales and eagles, stand for clans, family histories and important events. Some poles welcome visitors. Others honour someone who has died.

Totem poles are not idols and were never worshipped. They work more like a carved family record. After a ban in Canada was lifted in 1951, carvers revived the art, and new poles are still raised today with feasts and speeches.

Hard wood, soft wood

Carvers choose wood the way cooks choose flour. Lime and basswood are soft, pale and even, which is why many beginners in Europe and the USA start with them. Walnut and teak are tougher and show a beautiful grain. Rosewood is dense and almost black.

Sandalwood and boxwood allow very fine detail. Pine is cheap but its knots and uneven rings can make a blade jump.

Carvers also watch moisture. Wet, freshly cut "green" wood carves easily but can crack as it dries. That's why many traditional carvers store their logs for months, sometimes years, before the first cut.

Every cut is final

Painters can paint over a mistake. A carver can't put wood back. That makes carving a slow, careful art, where a single slip can ruin weeks of work. It also explains why masters in Saharanpur or Kerala often learned from a parent, starting as children with simple leaves before moving on to faces.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The figure at the bottom of a totem pole is the least important.

Truth: Many carvers on the Northwest Coast placed their finest, most important figure at the bottom, at eye level. The phrase "low man on the totem pole" came from people outside those cultures.

Try this

Carve a bar of soap instead of wood. Use a blunt butter knife or a lolly stick, draw a simple shape like a fish on the soap first, then shave away thin flakes from the outside in. Notice how taking off too much in one go can't be undone, which is the first lesson every wood carver learns.
