Who invented the toy brick?

Six ordinary 2x4 LEGO bricks of the same colour can be stacked together in more than 915 million different ways.

The short answer

The famous plastic toy brick was made by LEGO, a company in Billund, Denmark. A carpenter named Ole Kirk Christiansen started it in 1932, making wooden toys. In 1949 the company began making plastic bricks. In 1958 it patented the design that clicks together so well today, with studs on top and hollow tubes underneath.

But LEGO didn't come up with the very first interlocking brick. A British inventor, Hilary Fisher Page, made Kiddicraft Self-Locking Building Bricks in the 1940s. LEGO's early bricks were closely based on them. Toy blocks of wood, stone and clay go back thousands of years before that.

Surprising but true

LEGO's plastic bricks were inspired by an earlier British toy called Kiddicraft Self-Locking Building Bricks, invented by Hilary Fisher Page in the 1940s.

From wooden ducks to plastic

Ole Kirk Christiansen was a carpenter in a small Danish village. In the 1930s, times were hard and few people were buying furniture. So he started making wooden toys: yo-yos, cars and a popular pull-along duck.

In 1934 he named the company LEGO, from the Danish words leg godt, meaning "play well". A favourite story says his son Godtfred once saved money by using cheap varnish on some ducks, and Ole Kirk made him go back and fix every one. The motto "only the best is good enough" was painted on the workshop wall.

Borrowing a British idea

After World War II, plastic was new and exciting. In 1947 LEGO bought a plastic moulding machine, and it came with samples of Kiddicraft bricks. Two years later, LEGO made its own version, called Automatic Binding Bricks.

They didn't sell well at first. The bricks didn't grip very tightly, and towers fell over. Many shops sent them back, preferring wood and metal toys.

The click that changed everything

The answer was hidden underneath. In 1958 LEGO added hollow tubes inside each brick. When you press a brick down, the studs below squeeze between the tubes and the walls and hold firm. The patent was filed on 28 January 1958.

A brick made in 1958 still fits a brick made today. Modern LEGO bricks are moulded so precisely that only about 18 in every million fail the company's quality checks. They're made from a tough plastic called ABS, which keeps its shape for decades.

Numbers that explode

Bricks combine in staggering numbers. Two 2x4 bricks of one colour can join in 24 ways. Three give 1,060 ways. In 2005, a mathematician at the University of Copenhagen, Søren Eilers, worked out that six bricks give 915,103,765 ways. With more bricks, the counts get so big that even computers struggle.

This is the same sort of maths behind codes and puzzles. Each extra piece multiplies the choices, so small kits can lead to endless builds.

Bricks, logs and blocks around the world

LEGO wasn't alone. In 1916, John Lloyd Wright, a son of the famous American architect Frank Lloyd Wright, invented Lincoln Logs, notched wooden logs for building cabins. Britain had Meccano, metal strips with bolts, from the early 1900s.

In India, the town of Channapatna in Karnataka has made bright lacquered wooden toys for about 200 years, including stacking rings and spinning tops. Kids everywhere have always loved building things, knocking them down and building them again.

A brick empire

Today LEGO is one of the biggest toy companies in the world. Legoland parks in Denmark, the US, Japan and elsewhere are built from millions of bricks. In 2000, the British Association of Toy Retailers named LEGO the "Toy of the Century".

Brick building has also gone digital. Minecraft lets players build whole worlds from blocks on a screen, and many children use Minecraft to learn the basics of coding.

Myth or fact?

Myth: LEGO invented the idea of interlocking plastic toy bricks.

Truth: The idea came from a British toy, Kiddicraft Self-Locking Building Bricks. LEGO improved it, especially with the hollow tubes added in 1958 that made bricks hold together firmly.

Try this

Take two 2x4 bricks, or two rectangles cut from cardboard with dots drawn on. Count how many different ways you can join one on top of the other. You should find 24. Now imagine how fast that number grows with every extra brick.
