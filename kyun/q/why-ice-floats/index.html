Why does ice float?

Almost every solid sinks in its own liquid. Water is the weird one.

The short answer

When water freezes, its molecules hold hands in a roomy six-sided pattern.

In liquid water, each molecule jostles about, packing in quite close to its neighbours. As water freezes, the molecules lock into a neat, open framework with gaps in the middle, like a honeycomb. The same amount of water now takes up about 9% more space. So ice has a lower density than liquid water, and it floats.

Because ice floats, lakes freeze from the top down. The ice acts like a lid, and fish survive in the water below.

If ice sank, many lakes would freeze solid from the bottom up.

Surprising but true

A frozen bottle of water can crack, because the ice takes up more room than the water did.

Holding hands

Each water molecule is made of one oxygen atom and two hydrogen atoms. The hydrogen ends are slightly positive and the oxygen end slightly negative, so molecules cling to each other with weak links called hydrogen bonds.

In liquid water, these links keep breaking and re-forming as the molecules tumble around. In ice, they hold firm, and each molecule links to four neighbours at fixed angles. That arrangement builds the six-sided pattern you can see in snowflakes.

Weird at 4°C

Most liquids get steadily heavier as they cool. Water does too, until about 4°C. Below that, it starts to expand again, as molecules begin lining up into their icy pattern.

That matters for lakes. In winter, water at 4°C sinks to the bottom, and colder water floats on top until it freezes. A frozen lake can have ice on top and water at about 4°C below, where fish, frogs and plants wait out the winter.

Only a few other substances, such as silicon and the metals gallium and bismuth, also expand when they freeze. Drop a cube of solid butter or wax into its own melted liquid, and it sinks, the way most solids do.

The tip of the iceberg

An iceberg floats with about 90% of its bulk hidden under the sea. Only the top tenth shows, which is where the saying "the tip of the iceberg" comes from.

In April 1912, the Titanic struck an iceberg in the North Atlantic. The crew had seen only the part above water. Icebergs are pieces of glaciers and ice sheets that break off where they meet the sea, mostly from Greenland and Antarctica. They're made of fresh water from snow that fell long ago.

Ice that cracks rock

Because water expands as it freezes, it can push with great force. A sealed glass bottle of water left in a freezer may crack. Pipes can burst in cold winters, which is why people in very cold places let taps drip or wrap their pipes.

In mountains, rainwater seeps into cracks in rock, freezes at night and widens the cracks. Over many years, this breaks boulders apart. It's one of the ways the Himalaya is slowly worn down, sending rock and soil into the rivers below.

Frozen lakes of Ladakh

In winter, the high-altitude lake Pangong Tso in Ladakh freezes over, even though its water is salty. Temperatures there can fall far below freezing. Further north, in places like Canada and the northern USA, lakes freeze thick enough for people to fish through holes in the ice and play ice hockey on top.

On Jupiter's moon Europa, scientists think a thick shell of ice may float on a hidden ocean in the same way. Under every one of those frozen surfaces, life carries on in the darker water below, protected by a lid of floating ice.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Icebergs are frozen seawater.

Truth: Icebergs are chunks broken off glaciers and ice sheets, which formed from snow that fell on land, so they are made of fresh water. Frozen seawater is a different thing, called sea ice, and it's usually much thinner.

Try this

Fill a small plastic cup exactly to the brim with water and mark the level with tape. Put it in the freezer overnight. In the morning, the ice will have risen above the rim in a little dome, because frozen water takes up more space. Then drop an ice cube into a glass of water and see how much of it sits below the surface.
