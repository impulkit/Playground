What are the most famous comets?

In 1910, some people bought "comet pills" to protect themselves when Earth passed through the tail of Halley's Comet. The tail was harmless.

The short answer

A comet is a lump of ice, dust and rock, often called a dirty snowball. Most spend their lives far from the Sun, in the cold outer solar system. When one swings close to the Sun, its ice turns to gas and blows off, forming a glowing head and long tails that can stretch for millions of kilometres.

A few comets have become famous. Halley's Comet is the best known, because it returns roughly every 76 years. Others became famous for one dazzling visit, like Hale-Bopp in 1997, or for something dramatic, like Shoemaker-Levy 9, which smashed into Jupiter.

Surprising but true

In 1994, more than 20 pieces of a broken comet crashed into Jupiter one after another, leaving dark scars bigger than Earth.

Halley's Comet

People had seen this comet for thousands of years without knowing it was the same one coming back. In 1705, the English astronomer Edmond Halley worked out that comets seen in 1531, 1607 and 1682 followed the same path. He predicted a return in 1758. He died before it arrived, but it came, and it was named after him.

Halley's has been spotted in records going back over 2,000 years. In 1066 it appeared before the Battle of Hastings in England. It is stitched into a famous embroidered cloth, about 70 metres long, made in the 1070s to tell the story of that war. Medieval people in castles and villages often saw comets as warnings of disaster.

In 1910, Earth passed through the comet's tail. Newspapers reported that the tail contained a poisonous gas, and some sellers made money from "comet pills". The gas was far too thin to harm anyone. On its 1986 visit, the European probe Giotto flew past and photographed the comet's dark, peanut-shaped heart for the first time. Halley's will be back in 2061.

Hale-Bopp, the long show

Hale-Bopp was discovered in 1995 by two Americans, Alan Hale and Thomas Bopp, on the same night in different places. It was unusually big and bright. People could see it without a telescope for about 18 months, a record for modern times. In early 1997, millions of people in India, the USA and around the world watched it hang in the evening sky with two tails, one white and one blue.

The comet that hit Jupiter

Shoemaker-Levy 9 was found in 1993, already broken into a string of pieces like pearls on a necklace. Jupiter's strong gravity had torn it apart. In July 1994, more than 20 pieces crashed into the giant planet over six days. Each impact left dark marks in Jupiter's clouds, some larger than Earth.

It was the first time people had watched two objects in the solar system collide. It also showed how Jupiter's pull can capture and break up passing comets.

Landing on a comet

In 2014, the European Rosetta spacecraft reached comet 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko after a ten-year journey. It dropped a small lander, Philae, onto the surface. The lander bounced twice and ended up in a shadow, so its batteries soon ran out. Still, it was the first soft landing on a comet. Rosetta sent back photos of cliffs, boulders and jets of gas, from a comet shaped like a rubber duck.

Recent visitors

Comet NEOWISE lit up the sky in July 2020, when many people were at home during lockdowns, and photographers in Ladakh and the USA captured it over mountains. Comet Tsuchinshan-ATLAS was easy to see in October 2024.

In 2025, astronomers spotted 3I/ATLAS, a comet coming from another star system. It was only the third such interstellar visitor ever found. It moved too fast to be caught by the Sun, so it will leave and never return.

Most comets come from two icy reservoirs: the Kuiper Belt beyond Neptune and the much more distant Oort Cloud. Every year, sky-watchers discover dozens of new ones. Many leave behind trails of dust that Earth crosses, producing shooting stars on the same nights each year.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Comets are fireballs burning as they fly through space.

Truth: Comets are cold lumps of ice, dust and rock, a few kilometres across. Their glowing heads and tails form when sunlight warms the ice and turns it to gas, which then reflects sunlight and glows.

Try this

Make a model comet. Scrunch a ball of foil or paper for the nucleus and tape several long ribbons or paper strips to one side. Stand near a fan, which plays the Sun. Walk in a circle around it holding the comet out, and watch the tail always stream away from the fan.
