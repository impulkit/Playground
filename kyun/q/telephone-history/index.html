Who invented the telephone?

On the same day in 1876, two inventors handed in papers about telephones at the same patent office in Washington. One became world-famous. The other is mostly forgotten.

The short answer

Alexander Graham Bell is usually named as the inventor of the telephone. A Scottish-born teacher of deaf students living in the United States, he was granted a US patent on 7 March 1876 for a way to send speech along a wire using electricity. Three days later, he spoke the first clear sentence over his telephone to his assistant in the next room.

But the full story is more crowded. On the very day Bell's lawyer filed his patent application, another inventor, Elisha Gray, filed papers describing a similar idea. An Italian immigrant in New York, Antonio Meucci, had been working on a talking telegraph for years before that. People still argue about who deserves the most credit.

Surprising but true

Alexander Graham Bell's mother and his wife were both deaf. He spent much of his life teaching deaf students, and his work on sound and speech led him to the telephone.

"Mr. Watson, come here"

Bell had been trying to send several telegraph messages along one wire at once, using different musical notes. Along the way, he became convinced that a wire could carry the rise and fall of a human voice.

His telephone used a thin, flexible sheet that vibrated when someone spoke into it. The vibrations changed an electric current flowing through the wire, and at the other end, a similar sheet vibrated in the same pattern, recreating the sound. On 10 March 1876, Bell spoke into his device: "Mr. Watson, come here, I want to see you." Thomas Watson, listening in another room, heard him.

A race to the patent office

On 14 February 1876, Bell's lawyer filed his patent application in Washington, D.C. That same day, Elisha Gray filed a caveat, a notice that he was working on a telephone design and planned to patent it. Which arrived first, and whether anyone shared Gray's ideas with Bell, became the subject of lawsuits for years. Bell won every major court case.

Antonio Meucci, meanwhile, had built voice-carrying devices in the 1850s and 1860s, but he was poor and could not afford to keep up a full patent. In 2002, the US House of Representatives passed a resolution honouring his work on the telephone.

Operators and switchboards

Early telephones were sold in pairs, wired directly to each other. That quickly became impractical. Soon each phone was connected to a central exchange, where operators sat in front of switchboards and connected calls by plugging in cords.

The first telephone exchanges in India opened in 1882, in Calcutta, Bombay and Madras. For the next century, telephones were a luxury for most Indian families. In the 1980s, people often waited years for a landline connection. Then yellow STD and PCO booths spread across towns and villages, letting anyone make a long-distance call for a few rupees.

Phones without wires

On 3 April 1973, Martin Cooper, an engineer at the company Motorola, stood on a New York street and made the first call from a handheld mobile phone. It weighed over a kilogram, about as much as a big bag of sugar. He called a rival engineer at Bell Labs to tell him.

India's first mobile phone call came on 31 July 1995, when the Chief Minister of West Bengal, Jyoti Basu, in Kolkata, called the Union telecom minister, Sukh Ram, in Delhi. Calls were expensive at first. Within about twenty years, India had hundreds of millions of mobile users.

Today's smartphones are distant descendants of Bell's vibrating sheet. They still turn your voice into electrical signals, though now those signals travel as radio waves and flashes of light.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Alexander Graham Bell invented the telephone completely on his own.

Truth: Bell received the patent and built a working telephone, but others had similar ideas. Elisha Gray filed a document about a telephone design on the same day, and Antonio Meucci had worked on a talking telegraph years earlier. Bell's assistant, Thomas Watson, also did much of the hands-on building.

Try this

Make a string telephone. Ask an adult to make a small hole in the bottom of two paper cups. Thread a long piece of string through, knotting each end inside a cup. Stand apart until the string is tight and talk into one cup while a friend listens with the other. Your voice makes the string vibrate, a bit like the vibrating parts in an early telephone.
