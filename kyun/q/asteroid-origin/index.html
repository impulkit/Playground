Where do asteroids come from?

They're leftovers, from when the planets were made.

The short answer

Asteroids are leftover building blocks from when the planets formed.

Most live in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter. There are well over a million known, from Ceres, about 940 km across, down to boulders the size of a house. Many more are too small to have been spotted.

Jupiter's gravity sometimes nudges asteroids into new orbits, and a few end up crossing Earth's path.

Surprising but true

All the asteroids in the main belt together weigh less than our Moon.

Leftovers from the beginning

About 4.6 billion years ago, dust and rock around the young Sun clumped together into planets. Grains stuck together into pebbles, pebbles into boulders, and boulders into small worlds that crashed and merged.

Near Jupiter, its gravity stirred things up too much for a planet to form, so the pieces stayed as asteroids. Instead of gently sticking together, they smashed into each other at high speed and broke apart. Over billions of years, Jupiter also flung many of them out of the belt altogether. What remains today is a small fraction of what was once there.

That makes asteroids time capsules. Some have barely changed since the solar system formed, which is how scientists use meteorites, pieces of asteroids that fall to Earth, to measure Earth's age.

Not a crowded belt

In films, spaceships weave through asteroid belts packed with tumbling rocks. The real belt is nothing like that. Spread the asteroids out over the huge doughnut-shaped region between Mars and Jupiter, and there are usually hundreds of thousands of kilometres between neighbours. Standing on one asteroid, you would probably not see another with your bare eyes.

Put every asteroid in the main belt together and you would get a ball smaller than our Moon, with only about 3 per cent of its mass. Ceres alone makes up about a third of that.

The belt also has gaps. In 1866 the American astronomer Daniel Kirkwood noticed that almost no asteroids orbit at certain distances. Those are places where Jupiter's regular tugs, repeated orbit after orbit, push any asteroid out of its path. They are now called Kirkwood gaps.

A planet that turned out to be an asteroid

On 1 January 1801, the Italian astronomer Giuseppe Piazzi, working in Palermo, spotted a faint moving point between Mars and Jupiter. He named it Ceres, after the Roman goddess of farming. For a while it was called a planet. Then astronomers found more objects nearby, and then more again, and a new word was used: asteroid, meaning "star-like".

In 2006, Ceres was reclassified as a dwarf planet, the same decision that changed the status of Pluto.

Visiting asteroids

Spacecraft have landed on asteroids and even brought samples back to Earth, so scientists can study the oldest material in the solar system.

Japan's Hayabusa brought back grains from the asteroid Itokawa in 2010, and Hayabusa2 returned samples from Ryugu in 2020. NASA's OSIRIS-REx dropped off a capsule from the asteroid Bennu in September 2023. Inside the Bennu sample, scientists found water-bearing minerals and some of the chemical building blocks of life, such as amino acids. NASA's Dawn spacecraft orbited both Ceres and the large asteroid Vesta.

Watching the sky

Telescopes search every night for asteroids that come near Earth. Could one hit us? and could we push one away?

A big one did hit Earth 66 million years ago, helping end the age of the dinosaurs.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Asteroids are the broken pieces of a planet that once blew up between Mars and Jupiter.

Truth: This was a popular idea in the 1800s, but the belt doesn't hold nearly enough material to have made a planet. Asteroids are leftovers that never came together into a planet at all.

Try this

Count out 100 grains of rice and call that pile the Moon. If you made a pile for the whole main asteroid belt at the same scale, it would be only about 3 grains. One of those grains, all on its own, would be Ceres. Try it with dal or lentils too.
