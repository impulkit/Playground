How do vaccines work?

A vaccine is a practice fight. It shows your body a harmless picture of a germ, so it is ready when the real one turns up.

The short answer

Your immune system learns by fighting. The first time it meets a germ, it takes days to build the right weapons, and in that time you can get very ill. A vaccine lets your body have that first meeting safely.

A vaccine contains something that looks like a germ but can't make you sick. It might be a weakened or killed germ, a single piece of its outer coat, or instructions that let your own cells make that piece for a short time. Your body studies it, makes antibodies, and keeps memory cells. If the real germ ever arrives, your body recognises it and fights back fast, often before you feel anything.

Surprising but true

The word vaccine comes from vacca, the Latin word for cow, because the first vaccine was made from a cow disease.

A milkmaid's cow

In the 1700s, smallpox killed huge numbers of people. Country folk in England noticed that milkmaids who caught cowpox, a mild illness from cows, didn't seem to catch smallpox.

In 1796, a doctor called Edward Jenner tested this. He took material from a cowpox sore on the hand of a milkmaid named Sarah Nelmes and scratched it into the arm of an eight-year-old boy, James Phipps. Later he exposed James to smallpox, and the boy didn't fall ill. Today, an experiment like this on a child would never be allowed. But it led to the first vaccine, named after the Latin word for cow.

Older ideas from Asia and Africa

Jenner wasn't the first to fight smallpox this way. For centuries, people in India, China and parts of Africa practised variolation: putting a tiny amount of material from a smallpox sore into a healthy person's skin or nose. It was riskier than vaccination, but it often gave a mild case and lifelong protection.

In 1721, in Boston, an enslaved African man named Onesimus told the preacher Cotton Mather how this had been done in his homeland. Mather pushed for it during an outbreak, and many who tried it survived.

Wiping out a disease

Vaccines work best when most people have them. If enough people are protected, a germ can't find enough new people to spread to, and it dies out. That also protects babies and people who are too ill to be vaccinated. This is called herd protection, or herd immunity.

With a worldwide vaccine campaign, smallpox was declared gone from the planet in 1980. It's the only human disease ever wiped out. India came close with polio too. Through the Pulse Polio drives, millions of volunteers gave drops to children under five, house to house, on trains and at bus stands. India's last case of wild polio was a girl in West Bengal in January 2011.

New kinds of vaccine

Scientists keep finding new ways to show the immune system a germ. During the COVID-19 pandemic, some of the first vaccines used mRNA, a set of instructions that tells your cells to make one harmless piece of the virus's coat for a short time. The body learns from it, and the instructions break down within days.

Like any medicine, vaccines can have side effects, usually a sore arm or a mild fever for a day. Serious reactions are rare, and doctors watch for them closely. In India, the government's Universal Immunization Programme gives children free vaccines against about a dozen diseases. The flu vaccine changes every year because flu viruses keep changing their coats.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Vaccines cause autism.

Truth: This idea came from a 1998 study that was later found to be dishonest and was withdrawn by the journal. Very large studies of millions of children since then have found no link between vaccines and autism.

Try this

Try a herd protection game with ten friends. Give three people a protected card. One person is a germ who can pass the germ on by touching someone unprotected, who then becomes a germ too. Play again with eight protected cards and watch how quickly the germ runs out of people to reach.
