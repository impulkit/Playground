Who reached the North and South Poles first?

The race to the South Pole was won by five Norwegians and their dogs. The North Pole is so hard to prove that we still argue about who got there first.

The short answer

The first people to reach the South Pole were Norwegian explorer Roald Amundsen and four companions, on 14 December 1911. A British team led by Robert Falcon Scott arrived about five weeks later and found the Norwegian flag already there.

The North Pole is trickier. In 1909, American Robert Peary said he had reached it with Matthew Henson and four Inuit men. Another American, Frederick Cook, said he had got there a year earlier. Neither could prove it, and most experts now doubt both. The first undisputed view of the North Pole came from an airship flying over it in 1926.

Surprising but true

The North Pole has no land at all. It sits on floating sea ice about 4 km above the floor of the Arctic Ocean, and the ice drifts all the time.

Why the poles?

By 1900, explorers had mapped most coastlines on Earth. The poles, the very top and bottom of the planet, were the last great "firsts". Reaching one meant fame for the explorer and pride for their country.

The two poles are very different. The South Pole sits high on the ice sheet of Antarctica, a frozen continent, about 2,800 m above sea level. The North Pole is out on the sea ice of the Arctic Ocean.

The race to the south

Amundsen had first planned to go north. When he heard Peary and Cook had claimed the North Pole, he secretly turned his ship south instead.

His expedition brought nearly 100 sled dogs to Antarctica, and the polar team travelled on skis. They dressed in fur clothing based on what Inuit people wore. They left supplies along the way and marked their route with flags. They reached the Pole after about eight weeks of travel and left a tent with a letter for Scott.

Scott's team used ponies, dogs and motor sledges. The ponies struggled, the motors broke and the men ended up pulling heavy sledges themselves. They reached the Pole on 17 January 1912, exhausted and disappointed. All five died of cold and hunger on the long journey back. Their diaries were found later and made them famous in Britain.

Arguments in the north

Peary had spent years in the Arctic, relying on Inuit guides and on Matthew Henson, an African American explorer who worked with him for about twenty years. In April 1909 Peary claimed they had reached the Pole.

Cook said he had arrived in April 1908 with two Inuit companions. The two men argued bitterly in newspapers. Experts later questioned their records. Peary's speeds seemed too fast, and Cook's evidence was thin.

Proving it was hard because the ice drifts, and navigators had to work out position from the Sun with handheld instruments. Near the poles the Sun stays low, and in the Arctic spring it never sets, so the North Star could not help either.

The airship Norge, carrying Amundsen, American Lincoln Ellsworth and Italian Umberto Nobile, flew over the North Pole in May 1926. That made Amundsen and his teammate Oscar Wisting, who had also stood at the South Pole, the first people accepted as having seen both poles.

India at the poles

India sent its first expedition to Antarctica in 1981–82. In 1983 it set up its first research station, Dakshin Gangotri. Today it runs two stations there, Maitri and Bharati, where scientists study ice, weather and climate.

In the Arctic, India opened a research station called Himadri in Norway's Svalbard islands in 2008.

The poles today

The USA has run a research station exactly at the South Pole since 1956, named after Amundsen and Scott. In summer, more than a hundred people live there. Scientists study the stars, the ice and Earth's changing climate, in the place two teams once struggled for weeks just to reach.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The North and South Poles are both frozen land.

Truth: The South Pole is on land, on top of an ice sheet nearly 3 km thick. The North Pole is in the middle of the Arctic Ocean, covered by drifting sea ice a few metres thick.
