How do headphones make sound?

Inside each earcup is a magnet, a coil of wire and a disc thinner than paper, shaking back and forth thousands of times a second.

The short answer

Music on your phone is stored as numbers. The phone turns those numbers into an electric signal that rises and falls very quickly, in the same pattern as the sound waves of the song.

That signal flows into a small coil of wire inside each earcup. The coil sits next to a magnet. When current flows one way, the coil is pushed. When it flows the other way, the coil is pulled. The coil is glued to a thin, light disc called a diaphragm, so the disc shakes in and out too.

The shaking disc pushes on the air in your ear, and that is sound.

Surprising but true

Plug a pair of ordinary wired headphones into a microphone socket and talk into them. They work as a microphone, because the two are built almost the same way.

Faster than you can count

To play a high note, the diaphragm moves back and forth thousands of times every second. To play a deep bass note, it moves fewer times, but further. A song is many notes at once, so the disc does all these wobbles together, in one complicated dance.

A loudspeaker on a stage works the same way. It just has a much bigger cone and a stronger magnet, because it has to shake a whole hall of air instead of the little space inside your ear.

The same machine, backwards

A microphone is a headphone running in reverse. Sound waves shake its diaphragm, the diaphragm moves a coil past a magnet, and that movement makes a tiny electric current. The current copies the shape of the sound.

That simple pairing makes all recorded sound possible. The microphone turns sound into electricity. Something stores it. Later a speaker turns the electricity back into sound, ready for your ears.

Mary had a little lamb

In 1877, Thomas Edison's team in New Jersey built the phonograph. A needle cut wobbly grooves into a sheet of tinfoil wrapped round a turning cylinder. Run the needle along the groove again, and it played the sound back. One of the first things Edison recorded was the nursery rhyme "Mary had a little lamb".

Twenty years earlier, in 1857, a Frenchman named Édouard-Léon Scott de Martinville built a machine that drew sound waves as squiggly lines on paper blackened with soot. He never meant them to be played. In 2008, researchers in the USA used computers to turn one of his squiggles from 1860 back into sound. They heard a few seconds of a voice singing the French song "Au clair de la lune".

India's music was recorded early too. In 1902, the singer Gauhar Jaan recorded songs in Calcutta for the Gramophone Company. She became one of the first Indian performers whose voice could be bought and played at home.

A kitchen in Utah

Early headphones were heavy and meant for telephone operators. In 1910, an inventor named Nathaniel Baldwin built a better pair at his kitchen table in Utah. The US Navy wanted them for radio operators, and soon he was making them in large numbers. Radio helped headphones become common in homes.

From grooves to numbers

Today, sound is stored as numbers. A CD measures the sound wave 44,100 times every second and writes down each measurement. A phone keeps songs in files that squeeze those numbers to save space, as described in data storage.

Noise-cancelling headphones add another trick. Small microphones on the outside listen to the hum of a bus or an aeroplane. The headphones then play the exact opposite wave, and the two cancel out, leaving much less noise. The quietest sound in your headphones can be one the speaker is making on purpose.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If loud music through headphones doesn't hurt, it can't damage your hearing.

Truth: Hearing damage can build up quietly, with no pain at all. The World Health Organization advises keeping the volume moderate and taking listening breaks. Ringing ears after music are a sign it was too loud.

Try this

Stretch a piece of cling film tightly over a bowl and sprinkle a few grains of rice on top. Hold a metal tray close by and bang it with a wooden spoon, or hum loudly near the bowl. Watch the rice jump. The film is acting like the diaphragm inside a headphone, moved by sound instead of electricity.
