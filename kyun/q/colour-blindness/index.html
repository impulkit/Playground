What is colour blindness?

In 1794 the chemist John Dalton told scientists that a flower looked sky-blue to him by day but red by candlelight. He had worked out that his own eyes saw colour differently.

The short answer

Colour blindness means seeing colours differently from most people, usually by mixing up certain shades. It's not really blindness. Most colour-blind people see many colours, but some pairs, such as red and green, or brown and green, look alike to them.

It happens because of the cells in the retina that sense colour, called cones. Most people have three kinds, tuned roughly to red, green and blue light. The brain compares their signals to work out every colour. In colour blindness, one kind of cone works differently or is missing, so some colours become hard to tell apart.

Surprising but true

Dalton asked for his eyes to be kept after his death so they could be studied. In 1995, scientists tested DNA from them and found exactly which kind of colour blindness he had.

How common is it?

Among people of northern European descent, about 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women have red-green colour blindness. Rates differ between populations, and studies in India generally report somewhat lower figures, but in almost every country it's far more common in boys.

The reason lies in genes. The instructions for red and green cones sit on the X chromosome. Boys have one X and one Y, while girls have two X's. If a boy's X carries the change, he will be colour blind. A girl needs the change on both X's, which is much rarer.

Dalton's discovery

John Dalton, the English chemist famous for his ideas about atoms, noticed something odd about himself. A geranium flower that looked sky-blue to him in daylight looked red by candlelight, though most people saw it as pink all along. His brother saw colours the same way.

In 1794 he described this to a scientific society in Manchester. It was one of the first careful scientific accounts of colour blindness, which became known as Daltonism. Dalton wanted his eyes examined after his death to test his theory about them. They were kept in a jar for more than 150 years. In 1995, scientists extracted DNA and showed he was missing the green-sensing cone.

The dot test

In 1917, the Japanese eye doctor Shinobu Ishihara published plates covered in coloured dots. Hidden among them is a number made of dots of a slightly different colour. Most people see it at once. Someone with red-green colour blindness may see a different number, or none.

Versions of the Ishihara test are still used in schools and clinics worldwide. Some jobs, such as flying planes or driving trains, need good colour vision, so people applying for them are tested.

Living with it

Many colour-blind people only find out when they mix up colours in art class or at a test. Small things can cause trouble: telling ripe fruit from unripe, reading charts, or knowing whether meat is cooked.

There are clever workarounds. Traffic lights always keep red at the top and green at the bottom, so drivers can go by position. Good designers add patterns or labels to maps and graphs instead of relying on colour alone. Phones and computers now offer colour filters, one of many tools in accessible technology. Special glasses can help some people tell certain colours apart better, though they don't restore normal colour vision.

Other animals

Many mammals, including dogs and cats, have only two kinds of cones. Dogs see blues and yellows well but find reds and greens hard to tell apart, much like a person with red-green colour blindness. Many birds and insects, on the other hand, can see ultraviolet light, a colour completely invisible to us.

Myth or fact?

Myth: People with colour blindness see only in black and white.

Truth: That's extremely rare. Most colour-blind people see plenty of colours but confuse certain ones, usually reds, greens, browns and oranges.

Try this

Make a sorting test. Collect objects in shades of red, brown, green and orange, like leaves, crayons and buttons. Ask family members to sort them by colour under bright light, then under dim light. Notice which colours people mix up, and how much harder it gets for everyone when the light is low.
