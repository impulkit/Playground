What are exoskeletons?

Beetles and crabs wear their skeletons on the outside. Now engineers are building outside skeletons for people, with motors at the knees.

The short answer

An exoskeleton is a skeleton on the outside of a body. Beetles, ants, spiders and crabs all have one. Instead of bones inside and soft skin outside, they have a tough outer shell that holds them up, protects them and gives their muscles something to pull on.

Engineers have borrowed the idea. A robotic exoskeleton is a frame that a person straps on over their clothes, usually around the legs, hips or back. Motors, springs and sensors help the person move. Some help people who are paralysed to stand and take steps. Others help factory workers lift heavy things or hold their arms up for hours without getting tired or injured.

Surprising but true

In the 1960s, General Electric built a powered suit called Hardiman that was meant to let one person lift about 680 kg. The suit itself weighed roughly as much as the load it was meant to lift, and it never worked safely as a whole.

Armour that comes with a catch

Most animals on Earth wear an exoskeleton. Insects, spiders, centipedes and crustaceans together make up more than three-quarters of all known animal species. Their shells are made largely of a tough material called chitin, sometimes hardened with minerals.

There is a catch. An outer skeleton can't grow. To get bigger, the animal must moult, splitting its old casing and crawling out soft and helpless until a new one hardens. Exoskeletons also get very heavy as an animal gets bigger, which is one reason you'll never meet a beetle the size of a dog.

How do crabs grow?To grow, a crab must climb out of its own skeleton, legs, eyes and all, and then hide for days while its new body hardens.

Suits for people

The idea of a strength suit is old. In the 1960s, General Electric in the USA built Hardiman, a huge powered frame meant to let one person lift around 680 kg. The suit itself weighed about as much, and it jerked about dangerously. Only one arm ever worked properly.

Modern suits are smaller and smarter. Each has sensors that feel how the wearer is leaning or which muscles are starting to move. A small computer decides what the person is trying to do, and electric motors at the hips and knees help do it. Batteries in a backpack keep everything running.

In 2014, the US Food and Drug Administration allowed a suit called ReWalk to be used at home by people with certain spinal cord injuries. With crutches for balance, a user can stand up, walk and even climb a few stairs.

A kick at the World Cup

Also in 2014, the football World Cup in Brazil opened with an unusual moment. Juliano Pinto, a young man paralysed from the chest down, wore an exoskeleton and kicked a ball on the pitch in São Paulo. Scientists from the Walk Again Project had designed the suit to respond to signals from his brain, picked up by a cap of sensors.

The kick lasted only seconds, and TV cameras barely showed it. Still, it pointed to a future where machines could read what a person intends and help their body follow. Researchers continue to test similar ideas in hospitals today.

Factories, armies and treadmills

Some of the most common exoskeletons have no motors at all. In car factories, workers who spend all day fitting parts above their heads wear vests with springs that hold up their arms. These passive suits don't make anyone stronger. They take some of the load off shoulders and backs, which can mean fewer injuries.

Armies have tested suits that help soldiers carry heavy packs over long distances. Hospitals use fixed exoskeletons on treadmills to help stroke patients relearn walking. Together with prosthetic limbs and other robots that help the body, they are changing what people with injuries can do.

The biggest problems now are cost, weight and battery life. A good medical suit can cost as much as a car. Engineers are working to make them lighter, cheaper and simple enough to put on without help.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Robotic exoskeletons give people superhuman strength, like an Iron Man suit.

Truth: Real exoskeletons are much more modest. Most help a person stand, walk slowly or lift with less strain. Batteries, weight and safety limits keep them far from film-style super suits.

Try this

Look for a cicada or dragonfly skin on a tree trunk or wall during the monsoon or summer. It's the empty exoskeleton left behind after moulting. Without touching sharp edges, look at it with a magnifying glass and find the split in the back where the insect climbed out.
