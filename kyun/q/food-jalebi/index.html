Where did jalebi come from?

The sticky orange spiral sold at your corner sweet shop has a cousin in a cookbook written in Baghdad over a thousand years ago.

The short answer

Jalebi is a swirl of fermented batter, fried until crisp and dunked in sugar syrup. It feels completely Indian, from Diwali boxes to winter mornings with hot milk. But its story most likely starts in West Asia.

An Arabic cookbook from the 900s describes zalabiya, latticed fritters soaked in honey and rosewater. Food historians think this sweet travelled to India with traders, cooks and rulers from Persia and Central Asia, probably during medieval times. The name jalebi probably comes from the Arabic zulabiya or the Persian zolbiya. By about 1450, an Indian Jain text was already mentioning it. Since then, India has invented dozens of versions.

Surprising but true

An Indian Jain text from around 1450 already mentions jalebi, which means people in India have been eating it for well over 500 years.

A recipe from old Baghdad

In the 10th century, a writer named Ibn Sayyar al-Warraq collected recipes from the courts of Baghdad. Among them was a fried batter sweet drenched in syrup. Another cookbook from Baghdad, written in the 1200s, describes something similar.

These were sweets for festivals and rich tables. In Iran, a version called zoolbia is still eaten during Ramadan. In Tunisia and Algeria, a sweet called zlabia fills shop windows in the same season. The family resemblance is easy to spot.

How it came to India

Nobody knows the exact day jalebi arrived. Persian-speaking traders and rulers had strong links with India for centuries, and cooks moved with them. Historians think the sweet came along one of these routes.

Historians have found a Jain work from about 1450 that mentions jalebi being served at a feast. A Sanskrit cookery text written before 1600 gives a recipe much like today's, using wheat flour batter and sugar syrup.

The science of the swirl

The batter is made from maida (refined wheat flour), sometimes with gram flour and curd. It is left to ferment, often overnight. Yeasts and bacteria eat a little of the starch and release gas, so the batter fills with tiny bubbles.

A cook squeezes it through a cloth or a bottle with a small hole into hot oil, drawing loops by hand. The bubbles make it crisp and full of tiny holes. While it is still hot, it goes into warm sugar syrup, which soaks into all those holes. A good jalebi crackles when you bite it, then floods your mouth with syrup. Many sweet shops add a pinch of saffron or food colour for the bright orange shade, while homemade jalebi is often pale gold.

A spiral for every region

India has turned one sweet into many. In Bengal it is jilapi. Madhya Pradesh is famous for mawa jalebi, made with milk solids, often credited to a sweet maker in Jabalpur in 1889. Imarti, also called jangiri, is made from urad dal and shaped like a flower. Odisha makes chhena jalebi from fresh cheese.

People eat it differently too. In Indore, crowds gather for jalebi and poha at breakfast. In parts of north India, hot jalebi is dipped into rabri or milk in winter. Gujarat has a famous pairing: hot jalebi with fafda, a crunchy, salty strip made from gram flour. On the morning of Dussehra, sweet shops in Ahmedabad and Surat have long queues as families buy both to share.

Cousins across the world

Fried swirls soaked in syrup are found from North Africa to Nepal. In Nepal, a version is called jeri. Some people even link the funnel cake sold at American fairs to this family of sweets, though that link is not certain.

Jalebi is one sweet among many that came to India and were transformed. And the strangest part? Gulab jamun has a similar story, a Persian-inspired treat that India turned into its own, as you'll find in the tale of gulab jamun.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Jalebi was invented in India and never left.

Truth: Similar fried, syrup-soaked swirls are eaten from Morocco to Iran to Nepal, and the oldest written recipes come from West Asia. India made jalebi its own, with countless local versions.

Try this

Mix two spoons of flour with a little curd and water into a thick batter in a cup. Cover it and leave it in a warm spot for a day, checking it every few hours. Look for bubbles and smell how it changes. Ask an adult to throw it away afterwards rather than eating it.
