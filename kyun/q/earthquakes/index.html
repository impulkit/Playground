Why do earthquakes happen?

The ground under you is moving right now. Very, very slowly.

The short answer

Earth's outer shell is cracked into giant pieces called plates. They move slowly, but their edges get stuck.

Pressure builds up for years. When the rocks finally slip, the energy shakes the ground: that's an earthquake.

The Himalayas sit where two plates meet, so northern India and Nepal get many earthquakes.

Surprising but true

Nobody can predict the exact day of an earthquake yet. That's why strong buildings matter so much.

Stuck, then suddenly free

Plates move about as fast as your fingernails grow, a few centimetres a year. Where two plates meet, along a crack called a fault, their rough edges grip each other. The rest of each plate keeps moving, so the rock near the fault bends, like a ruler pushed sideways at one end.

Eventually the rock can't bend any more. It snaps and slips, sometimes several metres in a few seconds. The stored energy races outwards as waves through the ground. That's the shaking you feel.

In California, two plates slide past each other along the San Andreas Fault. In 1906, a slip there shook San Francisco, and the fires that followed destroyed much of the city. In India, the plate carrying India is still pushing into Asia, building the Himalayas and squeezing the rocks until they crack.

Measuring a quake

Seismometers record the ground's shaking as wiggly lines. Scientists use the size of the waves to calculate the earthquake's magnitude. The first waves to arrive, called P waves, push and pull the rock. Slower S waves shake it from side to side, and usually do more damage.

Each step up the scale means about 32 times more energy. A magnitude 7 is not a bit stronger than a magnitude 6. It's in a different league. The biggest earthquake ever recorded, in Chile in 1960, measured 9.5. India's National Center for Seismology runs stations across the country and reports each quake within minutes.

When India shook

On the morning of 26 January 2001, as people gathered for Republic Day, a magnitude 7.7 earthquake struck Bhuj in Gujarat. Many buildings collapsed, and towns across Kutch were badly damaged. Afterwards, Gujarat rebuilt with stronger rules for construction, and Bhuj's recovery is now studied as an example.

In 1950, an even bigger earthquake, of magnitude 8.6, struck Assam and Tibet. It changed the courses of rivers and set off landslides across the hills.

India's earthquake map divides the country into zones by how strongly the ground is likely to shake. Delhi sits in a high-risk zone. Much of the Himalaya, Kutch and the Northeast are in the highest, so buildings there must follow the strictest rules.

Staying safe

If the ground shakes: drop, take cover under a sturdy table, and hold on. Stay away from windows. Strong buildings and practice drills save lives.

Most injuries come from things falling or buildings collapsing, not from the ground itself. That's why engineers design buildings to sway without breaking. Some skyscrapers in Japan and the USA sit on giant rubber pads, or carry huge swinging weights near the top to cancel the sway. Schools in earthquake zones practise drills, so everyone knows what to do without thinking.

Waves through the Earth

Earthquake waves travel right through the planet. By timing them at stations around the world, scientists discovered what's inside the Earth.

Some animals may notice the first, faster waves a few seconds before people feel the stronger shaking. Elephants, for instance, can pick up vibrations through their feet, though nobody has shown that animals can predict earthquakes days ahead.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Big earthquakes happen in "earthquake weather", on hot, still days.

Truth: Earthquakes start many kilometres underground, where the weather can't reach. They happen in every kind of weather, at any time of day, in any season.

Try this

Build two towers of blocks or playing cards on a tray, one tall and narrow, one wide at the bottom. Gently slide the tray back and forth, faster and faster. Which falls first? Try adding cross braces made of folded card. Engineers test real building designs on shaking tables the same way.
