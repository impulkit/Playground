How do metro trains run on time?

At rush hour, a busy metro line can send a train every two or three minutes, and computers make sure no two trains ever get too close.

The short answer

Metro trains keep time because they run on their own tracks, with no cars, cows or traffic lights to get in the way, and because computers watch every train all the time. The system knows exactly where each train is, how fast it's going and how far it is from the one ahead.

The tightest part of the timetable is not the journey between stations. It's the few seconds a train spends at each platform, letting people off and on. Engineers plan stations, doors and even floor markings to keep those stops short. Get them right and a line can send a train every two or three minutes at rush hour.

Surprising but true

Tunnels full of steam

The very first underground railway opened in London in 1863. Its trains were pulled by steam engines, and passengers complained about the smoke. Electric trains took over in the 1890s and 1900s. New York's subway followed in 1904.

India's first metro opened in Kolkata in 1984, running under the city's crowded streets. The Delhi Metro started in 2002 and grew quickly. By 2025, Indian cities together had around 1,000 km of metro lines, one of the largest networks in the world, with more being built in cities from Pune to Patna.

Computers that keep trains apart

On ordinary railways, track is divided into blocks, and only one train is allowed in each block at a time. Coloured signals beside the track tell drivers when a block is clear. Blocks have to be long enough for a train to stop safely inside them, so trains must stay well apart.

Many modern metros use a smarter system, often called communication-based train control. Each train constantly radios its exact position and speed to a control computer. The computer works out a safe "bubble" ahead of each train and tells it how fast it may go. If a train gets too close to the one in front, the system brakes it automatically. Because the bubbles move with the trains, they can run closer together, safely.

The battle at the doors

A station stop might last only 20 or 30 seconds. If crowds hold the doors or squeeze in late, a train leaves a little behind time. The train behind it then catches up, and the delay can ripple down the whole line.

So metros fight for every second. Wide doors and wide platforms help people move quickly. Painted arrows show where to stand so people getting off aren't blocked. Many stations, especially newer ones, have glass walls and doors along the platform edge that line up exactly with the train doors. They keep people off the track and speed up boarding. Some metros also run extra trains on festival days or during big cricket matches, when stations suddenly fill.

No driver needed

In December 2020, part of the Delhi Metro's Magenta Line began running trains with no driver in the cab, a first in India. Computers start, stop and open the doors. Staff in a control room watch over everything and can step in.

Driverless metros already run in cities such as Dubai, Singapore and Copenhagen. They can run trains closer together and adjust quickly to crowds, the way a good algorithm adjusts to new information. A metro is a moving piece of urban planning too. Where a line goes decides where shops, offices and homes will grow for decades.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Metro trains are mostly late because the trains themselves are slow.

Truth: Metro trains are fast enough. Most delays come from the seconds trains spend at stations, especially when crowds block doors. A few seconds lost at every stop adds up along the whole line.

Try this

Time a metro, bus or school-bus stop with a watch. Note how long the doors stay open and how long the vehicle takes to leave. Then try loading a "train" of chairs with classmates, first through one door, then through two, and time the difference.
