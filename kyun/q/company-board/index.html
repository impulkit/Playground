What does a company's board do?

Even the person who started a company can lose their job there. A small group called the board has the power to hire and fire the boss.

The short answer

A company's board of directors is a small group of people who watch over the company for its owners. Those owners are the shareholders, the people who hold its shares.

The board does not run the company day to day. That is the chief executive's job. Instead, the board picks the chief executive, sets their pay, checks the company's money and big plans, and can sack the boss if things go wrong. That includes a founder.

Surprising but true

In 2023, the board of OpenAI, the company behind ChatGPT, fired its co-founder and chief executive Sam Altman. Less than a week later he was back, with a new board, after nearly all of the company's staff signed a letter asking for his return.

How it works

Think of a school. The principal runs it every day. But a managing committee chooses the principal, approves the budget, and can replace the principal if the school is badly run. A board is that committee for a company.

Here is how the chain of power works, step by step:

  • 1. Shareholders own the company. Anyone who buys shares on the stock market becomes a part-owner.
  • 2. Shareholders elect the directors. At a yearly meeting, called the annual general meeting (AGM), they vote. Usually it is one vote per share, so someone with a million shares has a million votes.
  • 3. The board chooses the chief executive officer (CEO). The CEO hires the managers and runs the business.
  • 4. The board keeps watch. It checks the accounts, approves big decisions like buying another company or paying a dividend, and asks hard questions.
  • 5. If trust breaks, the board acts. It can cut the CEO's pay, remove them, or bring someone back.

Directors come in kinds. Executive directors work in the company, like the CEO. Independent directors are outsiders with no business ties to it, there to give an honest view. The person who leads board meetings is the chair. In some companies the chair and the CEO are the same person; in others they are different people. Founders such as Jensen Huang, who co-founded Nvidia in 1993 and became its chief executive, often sit on the board as well.

Where it came from

The idea of a board is more than 400 years old. In 1600, a group of London merchants set up the East India Company and raised money from investors who bought a stake in it. Its owners, called the Court of Proprietors, elected 24 directors to a Court of Directors. Later, each director served four years, and six stood down every April. From offices on Leadenhall Street in London, these directors sent orders to India.

Two years later, the Dutch East India Company sold shares to the public in Amsterdam. It was a joint-stock company: many people pooled their money, shared the profits, and handed control to a small group of directors. The Dutch and British companies, and the other Europeans who traded in India, helped spread this idea around the world.

As the British company grew into a ruler, Parliament grew worried. The Regulating Act of 1773 and Pitt's India Act of 1784 put a government committee over the directors. Their Court was finally closed in 1858.

Rules for boards have grown stricter over time. In 2003, the New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq began requiring most directors of their listed companies to be independent.

India's story

India's first big company law after independence was the Companies Act, 1956. It was replaced by the Companies Act, 2013, most of which came into force on 1 April 2014. The new law, passed the way any law is made in India, made boards stronger. Every listed company had to have at least one woman director, and the market regulator, SEBI, set a deadline of 1 April 2015.

Indian boards have made big headlines. In January 2009, Ramalinga Raju, the founder of the software company Satyam, admitted that about 7,000 crore rupees of the cash and other figures in its accounts were false. Several independent directors had quit shortly before, after the board approved, then cancelled, a deal to buy two companies linked to Raju's family. The government dissolved Satyam's board and appointed new directors.

On 24 October 2016, the board of Tata Sons removed Cyrus Mistry as chairman, about four years after he took over. Six of the nine directors voted for it. Ratan Tata returned as interim chairman for four months.

In August 2017, Infosys's chief executive Vishal Sikka quit after a long public row between co-founder N. R. Narayana Murthy and the board. Murthy had questioned a pay rise, a large purchase of another company, and a payout to a former finance chief. Within days, the chairman and other directors resigned too, and co-founder Nandan Nilekani returned as non-executive chairman.

The numbers that matter

India's rules turn a board into something you can count.

  • - 15: the most directors an Indian company can have unless shareholders pass a special resolution, a vote that needs a bigger majority.
  • - One-third: the share of a listed company's board that must be independent directors. If the chair is also an executive, or is linked to the founding family, SEBI raises it to half.
  • - 1 woman: the minimum for every listed company, and for big public companies with at least 100 crore rupees of share capital or 300 crore rupees of yearly sales.
  • - 5 years, twice: an independent director serves up to five years at a time and no more than two terms in a row, so outsiders do not get too cosy.
  • - 182 days: at least one director must live in India for this many days a year.

What people argue about

Can outsiders really be independent? In many Indian companies, a founding family owns a large share and helps choose the "independent" directors. Some experts say this makes them too friendly with the family. Others say good independent directors still ask tough questions, and point to how rules were tightened after Satyam.

One person or two? Some people think the CEO should never be the chair, because nobody should mark their own homework. Others say a single leader makes faster decisions.

Founders or boards? Some argue that founders understand their companies best and should be protected from being removed. Others say no one should be above checking, especially at a company that millions of people depend on.

Myths and mix-ups

"The CEO owns the company." The shareholders own it. The CEO is employed to run it, and the board can replace them.

"The board runs the company every day." Most boards meet only a few times a year. They set direction and check on the managers, who do the daily work.

"Directors must agree with the founder." Their legal duty is to the company and its shareholders. That is why boards have pushed out founders, as Apple's board did in 1985 when it backed its chief executive, John Sculley, against co-founder Steve Jobs. Jobs left that September and came back in 1997, after Apple bought his new company, NeXT.

What it means for you

If your family invests through a mutual fund or a monthly SIP, you own small pieces of dozens of companies, through the fund. Since 2021, SEBI has required Indian mutual funds to vote on important matters at company meetings, and since 2022 on every resolution. Fund managers must declare that their votes were cast only in the best interest of their investors.

Anyone who owns even one share can attend an AGM and vote on who sits on the board, and many companies let shareholders vote online. It works a little like a democracy, though votes are counted by shares, not by people.

Boards also explain why a company's market value can jump or fall when its leadership changes. Investors are betting on who is in charge. Whether you one day start a company, buy your first share or save money that grows over time, knowing who really holds the power helps you understand the news. Myth or fact?

Myth: The chief executive (CEO) owns the company.

Truth: A company is owned by its shareholders. The CEO is an employee who runs it day to day, and the board, chosen by the shareholders, can replace the CEO. Some CEOs also own many shares, but that is a separate thing.

Try this

Turn your family or class into a pretend company. Give each person some paper "shares", with some people holding more than others. Vote to choose a three-person board, one vote per share. Then let the board pick a "chief executive" to plan a small event, like a picnic menu. Did the people with more shares get more say? Was that fair?
