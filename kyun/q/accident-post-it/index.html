How did a failed glue become sticky notes?

In 1968 a scientist made a glue that barely stuck. It took a choir singer, a pile of yellow scrap paper and 12 years to turn it into the sticky note.

The short answer

In 1968, a chemist named Spencer Silver was working at 3M, a big American company that makes tapes and glues. He was trying to make a super-strong glue. Instead he made one that stuck lightly, peeled off cleanly and could be stuck down again and again.

Nobody could think of a use for it. Six years later, a colleague named Art Fry was singing in his church choir. His paper bookmarks kept slipping out of his hymn book. He remembered Silver's odd glue, and the sticky note was born.

Surprising but true

Sticky notes are yellow by accident. The lab next door happened to have only yellow scrap paper when the first ones were made.

A glue made of tiny balls

Most glues spread out flat and grab every bit of a surface. Silver's glue was different. Under a microscope it is made of tiny sticky spheres, far smaller than a grain of sand.

Because they are round, only part of each ball touches the paper. That gives enough grip to hold a note on a wall, but not enough to tear the paper when you pull. It is a bit like holding a ball against your palm with your fingertips instead of your whole hand.

This trick depends on friction and on how closely two surfaces touch. A note sticks well to smooth paper or glass, and badly to a dusty shelf, because dust fills the gaps.

Silver called it "a solution without a problem". For years he gave talks inside 3M, hoping somebody would find a problem it could solve.

The choir bookmark

Art Fry worked in a different part of 3M. In 1974, he coated the edge of some paper with Silver's glue. The bookmarks stayed put, and lifted off without ripping the thin hymn book pages.

Then Fry realised something bigger. He wrote a question on one, stuck it on a report and passed it to his boss. His boss wrote the answer on the same note and stuck it back. That was the moment the idea changed from bookmark to message.

Why is glue sticky?Glue works by doing two jobs at once. It has to cling to the things you're joining, and it has to cling to itself.

The accidental yellow

When the team made their first test notes, they needed some paper. The lab next door had a supply of scrap paper, and it happened to be canary yellow. So the first sticky notes were yellow, and yellow stuck too.

Today you can buy them in dozens of colours and shapes, from neon pink to stars and hearts. Yellow is still the classic. One reason it lasted is practical: dark ink shows up clearly on pale yellow, and a yellow square stands out on a messy desk or a grey computer screen.

A flop before a hit

In 1977, 3M tested the notes in four American cities under the name "Press 'n Peel". Hardly anyone bought them. People didn't see why they needed sticky paper.

So in 1978, 3M flooded one city, Boise in Idaho, with free samples. Once office workers tried them, about nine out of ten said they would buy more. The notes went on sale across the USA in 1980 as Post-it Notes, and reached Canada and Europe the next year. Today they are sold in well over 100 countries, including India, where students use them to mark pages in thick exam guides.

Spencer Silver lived to see his "failed" glue become one of the best-known office products ever made. He died in 2021.

Why it worked

Silver's glue was a polymer, a substance made of long chains of molecules. Getting the balls to the right size took real chemistry. But the success came from people noticing a use that nobody had planned.

Plenty of famous inventions began this way. Accidents gave us a purple dye, a heart pacemaker and a famous explosive. And Silver's story has a cousin: superglue was first thrown aside for being far too sticky.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Sticky notes were a clever product idea from day one.

Truth: The glue sat unused for about six years. Spencer Silver was trying to make a strong glue and ended up with a weak one. Nobody at 3M knew what to do with it until Art Fry needed a bookmark that wouldn't fall out.
