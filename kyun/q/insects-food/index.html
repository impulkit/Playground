Do people eat insects?

In the forests of Odisha, people crush red weaver ants into a tangy chutney. In 2024 it got India's official seal as a regional speciality.

The short answer

Yes. About 2 billion people around the world eat insects as a normal part of their diet, according to the UN's Food and Agriculture Organization. More than 1,900 kinds of insects are eaten, especially beetles, caterpillars, bees, wasps, ants, grasshoppers and crickets.

In many parts of Asia, Africa and Latin America, insects are snacks, side dishes or treats. In India, people in forest and tribal areas of central and eastern India, and in the Northeast, have long eaten certain insects. In the US and Europe, most people find the idea strange. Yet many of them eat insect products every day without knowing, in food colours, sweet coatings and honey.

Surprising but true

Almost everyone eats a few insect bits by accident. Food rules in the US allow tiny amounts in foods such as chocolate and flour, because it's impossible to keep them all out.

Red ant chutney

In the Similipal forests of Odisha, people collect red weaver ants and their eggs from leafy nests in trees. They grind them with salt, ginger, garlic and chilli into a sharp, sour chutney called kai chutney. In January 2024 it was given a Geographical Indication tag, a label that protects foods linked to a particular place.

People in Bastar, in Chhattisgarh, make a similar chutney called chaprah. The ants taste sour because they contain formic acid, a natural acid that gives them a bite. Weaver ants are the same species famous for stitching leaves together with silk.

Insects in Northeast India

Northeast India has some of the country's richest insect dishes. In Assam, the pupae of eri silkworms, left over after silk is made, are fried and eaten. In parts of Nagaland and Manipur, people cook hornet and wasp larvae, water beetles and crickets. Insects are often sold in local markets alongside vegetables and fish.

For many families these foods are seasonal treats, gathered at the right time of year.

How do ants work together?No ant is in charge. Not even the queen. Yet a colony of a million ants can build, farm, fight and find food together.

Around the world

In Thailand, fried crickets and grasshoppers are a popular street snack. In Mexico, especially Oaxaca, toasted grasshoppers called chapulines are eaten with lime and chilli, often in tacos. In parts of Africa, mopane worms, which are caterpillars, are dried and stewed. In Japan, grasshoppers simmered in soy sauce and sugar are a traditional dish.

In 2021, the European Union approved dried mealworms as a food. Since then, insect flour, burgers and snacks have appeared in some European shops.

Why scientists are interested

Insects are packed with protein, and many also have healthy fats, iron and zinc. They are also efficient. The FAO reports that crickets need much less feed than cattle to make the same amount of protein. They use far less water and land too, and give off fewer greenhouse gases.

That is why some scientists think insects could help feed a growing world population. Insect farms already raise insects to feed chickens and fish.

Insects you already eat

Many people eat insect products without noticing. Carmine, a red colouring made from crushed cochineal bugs, colours some sweets, drinks and yogurts. Shellac, a shiny coating on some sweets and tablets, comes from a resin made by the lac insect. India produces much of the world's lac, much of it from Jharkhand.

Honey isn't an insect, of course, but bees make it. And the US Food and Drug Administration accepts that a few tiny insect fragments end up in foods like chocolate and flour, because it's impossible to remove every single one. They're harmless.

Not for everyone

People who are allergic to prawns or crabs may also react to insects, because they share similar proteins. Wild insects can carry pesticides, so people who eat them choose them carefully. Eating insects is a food choice like any other: normal in some homes, unthinkable in others, and slowly changing.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Eating insects is a weird new trend.

Truth: People have eaten insects for thousands of years, and about 2 billion people still do. What's new is that some Western countries have started selling insect foods in shops.

Try this

Become a label detective. Look at sweets, jellies and fruit-flavoured yogurts at home for the words carmine, cochineal or E120. Then look at shiny sweets and coated tablets for shellac or E904, which comes from the lac insect. Count how many insect ingredients you find.
