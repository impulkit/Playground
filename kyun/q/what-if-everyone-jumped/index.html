What if everyone on Earth jumped at once?

Eight billion people leave the ground at the same second. Earth moves less than the width of an atom, and then comes the real problem.

The short answer

Almost nothing would happen to the planet. When you jump, your legs push Earth away from you, just a tiny bit. All of humanity weighs roughly 400 million tonnes. Earth weighs about 6 thousand billion billion tonnes, or around 15 trillion times more.

So if 8 billion people leapt half a metre in the air at the same second, Earth would shift by less than a thousandth of the width of one atom. Then everybody would land, gravity would pull Earth back, and the score would be even again. The bigger story is what happens if everyone first has to gather in one place to do it.

Surprising but true

In 2023, about 70,000 fans dancing at a Taylor Swift concert in Seattle shook the ground as much as a small earthquake of magnitude 2.3.

Where would we all stand?

Packed shoulder to shoulder, about four people fit in a square metre. Eight billion people would need roughly 2,000 square km. That is about one and a half times the size of Delhi, or about two-thirds the size of the US state of Rhode Island.

The cartoonist Randall Munroe worked through this exact idea in his book What If? He put everyone in Rhode Island. With every human squeezed into one patch of land, the jump suddenly becomes something you could measure.

The landing

When 400 million tonnes of people come down at once, they hit the ground with about as much energy as a few hundred tonnes of TNT explosive. That sounds alarming. But it would be spread across a huge crowd, and the soil and rock would soak much of it up.

Instruments called seismometers, which measure shaking, would record it clearly, probably across the world. People nearby would feel a thud. Scientists expect it would be more like a small earthquake than a disaster.

Why do earthquakes happen?The ground under you is moving right now. Very, very slowly.

Crowds really do shake the ground

We know this because smaller crowds have done it. In 2011, Seattle football fans cheered so wildly after one famous run that a nearby seismometer picked it up. People nicknamed it the "Beast Quake".

In July 2023, Taylor Swift played two concerts in the same stadium. About 70,000 fans dancing and jumping each night produced shaking equal to an earthquake of magnitude 2.3. A seismologist at Western Washington University studied the readings and the "Swift Quake" made news worldwide. Cricket fans at a big IPL final probably make their own small wobbles too, though nobody has measured them.

The real problem comes next

Munroe pointed out that the jump is the easy part. The hard part is going home. Picture all 8 billion of us standing in one small area with no food, no water and no toilets. There would be nowhere near enough roads, trains, ships or planes to carry everyone back.

Even a city of a crore people struggles when a few lakh pilgrims arrive for one festival. Moving the entire planet's population would take months, maybe years. Most of the danger would come from the crowd itself, not from Earth.

There is also the timing problem. With people spread across every time zone, "at once" means midnight for some and lunchtime for others. Even counting down together would be hard, because a radio signal takes about a fifteenth of a second to go halfway round the planet.

The bottom line in numbers

A single jump lasts less than a second. Earth's mass barely notices. Gravity pulls us all back down at the same rate, whether we weigh 20 kg or 100 kg, as you can see in how falling objects behave.

And here is the oddest twist: Earth moves under your feet every time you jump in the playground, just by an amount far too small to ever detect.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If everyone jumped together, Earth would be pushed out of its orbit.

Truth: Every push down on Earth is matched by a pull back when you land. Earth also moves around the Sun at about 30 km every second, so a jump is nothing compared with that speed.

Try this

Stand on a skateboard, or wear socks on a smooth floor, and throw a heavy cushion forwards. You will slide backwards a little. Now think about doing that against something 15 trillion times heavier than everyone you know put together.
