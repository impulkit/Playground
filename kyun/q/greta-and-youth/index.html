How have young people changed the world?

In 1960, six-year-old Ruby Bridges walked into an all-white school in New Orleans, guarded by US marshals, and became a symbol of the fight to end segregation in American schools.

The short answer

Young people have changed the world many times, usually by joining together and refusing to accept something they thought was wrong. They have marched, gone on strike from school, written, spoken in public and simply showed up where they weren't wanted.

Some became famous, like Malala Yousafzai, who spoke up for girls' education, or Greta Thunberg, who started a school strike for the climate at 15. Many more are unknown: the thousands of students who left their colleges during India's freedom struggle, or the children who marched for civil rights in the US. Change usually came from crowds, not lone heroes.

Surprising but true

Malala Yousafzai was 17 when she won the Nobel Peace Prize in 2014, the youngest person ever to receive it. She shared it with Kailash Satyarthi of India.

Children who walked into history

On 14 November 1960, Ruby Bridges, aged six, became the first Black child to attend William Frantz Elementary School in New Orleans. Crowds shouted at her. Four US marshals walked her in. Most white parents pulled their children out, and for most of that year she was taught alone by one teacher, Barbara Henry.

In 1963, in Birmingham, Alabama, civil rights leaders asked children and teenagers to march, because many adults feared losing their jobs. Over several days in May, more than a thousand young people were arrested. The protests became a turning point in the civil rights movement.

Students in India's freedom struggle

When Gandhi launched the Non-Cooperation Movement in 1920, he asked Indians to boycott British schools and colleges. Thousands of students walked out. New national schools and universities, such as Jamia Millia Islamia and Gujarat Vidyapith, were founded for them.

During the Quit India Movement of 1942, students again led protests, strikes and marches. Some of the youngest freedom fighters were teenagers. In Odisha, a 12-year-old boat boy named Baji Rout was shot in 1938 when he refused to ferry British-Indian police across a river. He is remembered as one of India's youngest martyrs.

Speaking up for school

In Pakistan's Swat Valley, an armed group banned girls from going to school. A schoolgirl named Malala Yousafzai wrote a blog about it, and spoke on TV. In 2012, when she was 15, a gunman attacked her on her school bus. She survived, recovered in Britain and kept campaigning.

In 2014 she shared the Nobel Peace Prize with Kailash Satyarthi, an Indian activist who has spent decades rescuing children from forced labour. At 17, she was the youngest person ever to win it.

Striking for the climate

In August 2018, Greta Thunberg, a 15-year-old in Sweden, began sitting outside her country's parliament with a hand-painted sign reading "School strike for climate". Other students joined. By September 2019, millions of people in many countries had taken part in climate strikes.

Young people in India have joined too, protesting about air pollution in Delhi and the cutting of trees in cities like Mumbai and Bengaluru. Not everyone agrees with school strikes. Some adults say students should stay in class. Others say young people have the most at stake, because they will live with climate change the longest. Many have moved on from strikes to quieter work on climate action, such as planting trees or pushing their schools to use solar power.

What young people bring

Movements led by young people often share a few things. Young people may have less to lose, like jobs or businesses, and more time to organise. They are often quick to use new tools, from printed pamphlets in the 1920s to social media today.

They also ask simple, hard questions. Why can't she come to our school? Why are we still burning coal? A question like that is easy to understand and hard to wave away. It's one reason the UN's Convention on the Rights of the Child, agreed in 1989, says children have a right to be heard on matters that affect them.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Children are too young to change anything important.

Truth: Children and teenagers have played big parts in many movements, from civil rights in the US to India's freedom struggle and today's climate protests. They often brought energy, courage and a way of seeing problems that adults had stopped noticing.
