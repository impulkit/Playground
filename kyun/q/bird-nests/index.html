How do birds build nests?

With no hands, just a beak and feet, birds weave, sew, glue and plaster homes for their eggs.

The short answer

Birds build nests with their beaks, feet and bodies, one piece at a time. A bird may fly back and forth hundreds of times, carrying twigs, grass, mud, moss, feathers or spider silk.

Each kind of bird has its own style. Some pile up sticks. Some weave, some sew, and some plaster with mud. Most birds shape the cup by sitting in it and turning round and round, pressing the sides out with their chest. The finished nest keeps eggs and chicks warm, hidden and safe from falling.

Surprising but true

Some swiftlets build their nests almost entirely out of their own hardened spit.

The weaver's hanging basket

India's baya weaver builds one of the cleverest nests of all. During the monsoon, the male tears long strips from grass or palm leaves and knots them around a branch, often hanging over water. Then he weaves the strips in and out to make a ball with a long entrance tube pointing down. Snakes and crows find it hard to get in.

The male builds a half-finished nest first, then hangs from it, flapping and calling to females. A female inspects it, tugging at the strips. If she likes it, the male finishes the job. If not, he may start again. Studies of weaverbirds in Africa found that males get neater and faster with practice, so building is partly a skill they learn.

The tailor and the plasterer

The common tailorbird, a tiny green bird found in Indian gardens, sews. It pokes holes along the edges of one or two big leaves with its beak, then pulls spider silk or plant fibres through to stitch them into a cone. Inside, it builds a soft cup. In Hindi it is called darzi, which means tailor. Rudyard Kipling made one famous in "Rikki-Tikki-Tavi".

Swallows and martins are plasterers. They collect tiny beakfuls of wet mud and stick them together under a ledge or bridge, layer by layer, like bricks. A single nest can take well over a thousand trips.

How strong is spider silk?Weight for weight, the safety line a spider dangles from is stronger than steel, and it can stretch.

Spit, silk and stacks of sticks

Some swiftlets in Southeast Asia, and on India's Andaman and Nicobar Islands, build small cup nests almost entirely from their own saliva, which hardens like glue. People collect these nests to make bird's nest soup, which has put some colonies in danger.

Hummingbirds use stretchy spider silk to bind their nests together, so the nest can stretch as the chicks grow. Eagles go the other way. They add sticks to the same nest year after year. A bald eagle nest near St. Petersburg, Florida, measured about 2.9 m wide and 6 m deep, and was thought to weigh around 2 tonnes, about as much as a car.

No nest at all

Not every bird bothers. Many seabirds and lapwings lay eggs straight on bare ground, where their speckled colours hide them. Emperor penguins balance their egg on their feet.

Some birds borrow other nests. In India, the koel, famous for its loud call in summer, lays its eggs in crows' nests and lets the crows raise its chicks. Hornbills use tree holes. The female great hornbill seals herself inside with mud and droppings, leaving only a narrow slit. The male feeds her through the slit for weeks while she sits on the eggs.

Built for the job

Nests have inspired human builders, from woven baskets to the "Bird's Nest" stadium built for the 2008 Beijing Olympics. Weavers knot grass much as people knot thread on a loom, as you can see in how a saree is woven. But a bird has no hands, no plan on paper and no teacher, just a beak, practice and instinct.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If you touch a baby bird, its parents will smell you and abandon it.

Truth: Most birds have a weak sense of smell, and parents rarely leave a chick because a person touched it. It is still best to leave fledglings alone, as their parents are usually nearby.

Try this

Collect a handful of twigs, dry grass, leaves and wool. Using just two fingers of one hand, as if they were a beak, try to build a small cup-shaped nest big enough to hold a ping-pong ball. Then try it with a little wet mud as glue.
