What was the Code of Hammurabi?

About 3,750 years ago, a king of Babylon had nearly 300 laws carved on a black stone taller than a grown man, so that anyone could see the rules.

The short answer

The Code of Hammurabi is a set of about 282 laws written by order of Hammurabi, king of Babylon, near the end of his reign around 1750 BCE. The laws cover everyday life: buying and selling, borrowing money, renting fields, marriage, theft, injuries, wages and building houses.

The best-known copy is carved on a tall black stone slab called a stele, about 2.25 metres high. It is covered in wedge-shaped cuneiform writing in the Akkadian language. Today it stands in the Louvre Museum in Paris, where crowds walk all the way around it to see the tiny, neat signs.

Surprising but true

The stone was carried off as war loot about 600 years after it was carved, and lay buried in a city in Iran until French archaeologists dug it up in 1901.

A king and a sun god

At the top of the stone is a carved picture. Hammurabi stands with one hand raised in respect before Shamash, the sun god and god of justice, who sits on a throne. The message was clear: the king's laws had the gods' approval.

Below comes a long introduction, in which Hammurabi says he was chosen "to make justice appear in the land" and to stop the strong from harming the weak. Then come the laws. At the end, he curses any future king who dares to change them. Letters Hammurabi sent to his officials also survive, giving orders about canals, taxes and court cases.

"If a man..."

Almost every law follows the same pattern. If someone does this, then this happens. Law 196 says that if a man destroys the eye of another man of the same rank, his own eye shall be destroyed. This is where the famous phrase "an eye for an eye" comes from.

Other laws are about ordinary business. A doctor who heals a broken bone gets paid a set fee. Someone who leaves a canal gate open and floods a neighbour's field must pay for the lost crop. A builder whose house collapses and kills the owner is put to death.

Not equal for everyone

The laws did not treat all people the same. Babylonian society had three main groups: free people of high rank, ordinary free people, and enslaved people. Hurting a high-ranking person brought the harshest punishment. Hurting an ordinary person often meant paying silver. Hurting an enslaved person usually meant paying the owner.

Some laws protected women, for example by letting a wife keep her dowry if her husband left her. Others gave fathers and husbands much more power than we would accept today. Historians still argue about whether judges used the code word for word, or whether it was more like a royal list of good examples.

The stone that travelled

The stone probably once stood in a temple in the city of Sippar, and other copies were set up elsewhere. Around 1150 BCE, a king of Elam, a kingdom to the east, raided Mesopotamia and carried the stone back to his capital, Susa, in today's Iran. A few lines near the bottom were scraped away, perhaps so he could add his own words.

French archaeologists found it at Susa in the winter of 1901 to 1902, broken into three pieces. A scholar translated it within months, and it soon became famous worldwide.

Remembered in courtrooms

Hammurabi's laws were copied by Babylonian students for more than a thousand years. Today his face looks down from a marble panel in the US House of Representatives. He also appears, alongside other famous lawgivers, on a carved frieze inside the courtroom of the US Supreme Court. Modern courts follow very different ideas, but the belief that laws should be written down for all to see, rather than kept in a ruler's head, goes back a long way. Centuries later, documents like the Magna Carta built on that same idea.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Code of Hammurabi was the first written law code in history.

Truth: It is the most famous and one of the most complete, but older ones exist. The laws of Ur-Nammu, from the Sumerian city of Ur, were written about 300 years earlier.

Try this

Write five rules for your classroom or home in Hammurabi's style, starting each with "If". For example, "If a person borrows a pencil and loses it, that person shall give back two pencils." Then ask whether each rule is fair, and whether it should be the same for everyone.
