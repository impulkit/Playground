What keeps a kite in the air?

In 1901 a kite held up the wire that caught the first radio signal sent across the Atlantic Ocean.

The short answer

A kite flies because wind pushes on it at an angle while the string holds it back. The kite is tilted, so the moving air pushes it up as well as backwards. That upward push is called lift. The backward push is drag.

The string is the secret ingredient. It stops the wind from simply blowing the kite away, so the air has to flow over and under it. When lift, drag, the kite's weight and the pull of the string balance each other, the kite hangs in the sky. On a breezy day over a cricket ground, that balance can hold for hours.

Surprising but true

A kite flies because the string holds it back. Let go of the string and the kite stops flying and tumbles away with the wind.

Four forces in a tug of war

Every kite is in a constant four-way tug of war. Gravity pulls it down. Lift pushes it up. Drag pushes it downwind. The string pulls it towards your hand.

Tilt matters most. This tilt is called the angle of attack. Too flat, and the wind slides past without lifting much. Too steep, and the kite acts like a wall, catching plenty of drag but little lift. The bridle, the little loop of string where the line joins the kite, sets that angle. Moving the knot a few centimetres can turn a sulky kite into a soaring one.

Why tails help

A kite without a tail often spins and dives. A tail adds a little weight and plenty of drag at the back, which keeps the nose facing into the wind, much as feathers steady an arrow. Box kites and stunt kites are shaped to stay stable without one.

Wind is the other ingredient. Most kites like a breeze of roughly 10–25 km/h, about the speed of a bicycle. Wind is also often steadier higher up, away from trees and buildings, so kites tend to fly better once they climb.

Two thousand years of kites

People in China were flying kites more than 2,000 years ago, at first perhaps made of wood or silk. Kites spread across Asia, where they were used for signalling, fishing and festivals. In Gujarat, the skies over Ahmedabad fill on 14 January for Uttarayan, when fliers try to cut each other's lines with glass-coated string called manja.

In America, Benjamin Franklin flew his famous kite into a thunderstorm in 1752 to show that lightning is electricity. He was lucky to survive. Nobody should ever try it.

Kites that changed science

Kites carried science upwards long before planes or balloons were reliable. From 1898, the US Weather Bureau flew kites carrying instruments to measure air temperature and wind. In 1919 a chain of kites over Germany climbed to about 9,740 m, higher than Mount Everest.

In 1899 the Wright brothers tested their idea of twisting wings on a large kite before building their first glider. And in December 1901, Guglielmo Marconi used a kite in Newfoundland to lift a long wire aerial. It picked up the first radio signal sent across the Atlantic, the letter S in Morse code from England. Those three faint clicks had travelled about 3,500 km, and the invention of radio suddenly looked like it could connect whole continents.

Kites today

Kites still work hard. Kitesurfers on the sea are dragged along at speeds faster than a car in town traffic. Engineers are testing huge kites that fly in loops and pull a cable to make electricity from strong winds high above the ground.

A kite and an airliner use the same basic science of tilted surfaces in moving air. The difference is that a plane makes its own wind by racing forward with engines, which is how aeroplanes stay up without anyone holding a string.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Benjamin Franklin's kite was struck by lightning.

Truth: A direct lightning strike would very likely have killed him. Franklin's kite picked up electric charge from a storm cloud, enough to make a spark jump from a key on the string to his knuckle.

Try this

Make a simple kite from a plastic bag, two drinking straws and tape, then fly it in an open field with an adult. Try it with no tail, a short ribbon tail and a long ribbon tail. Note which one flies most steadily.
