Why are Hoysala temples so detailed?

On a Hoysala temple, a stone woman gazes into a hand mirror, every bead of her jewellery carved out of the rock 900 years ago.

The short answer

The Hoysalas ruled southern Karnataka from about the 11th to the 14th century. Their kings and nobles paid for more than a hundred temples. The best known, at Belur, Halebidu and Somanathapura, are famous for carving so dense that hardly a patch of wall is left plain.

Two things made this possible. The sculptors worked in a grey-green soapstone, called chloritic schist, which is soft and fine-grained when freshly cut, so it could be carved almost like wood. And Hoysala patrons wanted their temples to dazzle, competing to hire the most skilled artists. Many sculptors signed their work, which tells us they were proud of it.

Surprising but true

Many Hoysala sculptors carved their names on their work, so we know artists such as Mallitamma and Dasoja by name, 800 years later.

A king, a tiger and a name

Hoysala legend tells of a young man named Sala who was learning at a temple when a tiger attacked. His teacher shouted, "Hoy, Sala!", meaning "Strike, Sala!", and Sala killed the tiger. The story gave the family its name, and a figure fighting a tiger became the royal emblem.

The Hoysalas began as hill chiefs in the Western Ghats. By the early 1100s, under King Vishnuvardhana, they had become a major power. Their capitals were first Belur and then Dorasamudra, today's Halebidu, whose name means "old capital".

Belur: a temple for a victory

Vishnuvardhana began the Chennakeshava temple at Belur in 1117, to celebrate a victory over the Cholas. It took about a century to complete, with his grandson finishing it.

Around the outer walls run bands of carving, one above another: elephants at the bottom for strength, then lions, horses, scrolling plants and scenes from the epics. Leaning out from the top of the pillars are about 40 bracket figures called madanikas, carved women dancing, playing music or doing their hair. The best known, Darpana Sundari, looks into a hand mirror. Her necklaces, bangles and the folds of her clothes are carved in such detail that they look as if they could be lifted off.

Halebidu and the turned pillars

The Hoysaleswara temple at Halebidu, begun around 1121, is a pair of shrines joined together. Its walls carry thousands of figures, including gods, dancers, musicians and animals, and scenes from the Ramayana and Mahabharata.

Inside, the pillars are round and ringed, as if made on a giant lathe. Historians think the stone was turned while workers held cutting tools against it, though exactly how is still discussed. The surfaces are so smooth that they reflect light like polished metal.

Stars and shadows

Many Hoysala temples stand on a raised platform, called a jagati, wide enough for worshippers to walk around the shrine. Seen from above, many of these platforms are shaped like stars.

The star plan multiplied the corners of the building. Every corner made another wall for carving and threw a different shadow as the sun moved. The Keshava temple at Somanathapura, completed in 1268, has three star-shaped shrines on one platform. In 2023, Belur, Halebidu and Somanathapura were named World Heritage Sites together as the Sacred Ensembles of the Hoysalas.

How it ended

In 1311 an army of the Delhi Sultanate, led by Malik Kafur, attacked Dorasamudra. The Hoysalas survived, but grew weaker. The last major king, Veera Ballala III, died in battle in 1343. Out of the turmoil, new rulers founded the Vijayanagara Empire, which carried south Indian art forward. The Hoysala temples, many still in use, kept their stone dancers frozen mid-step.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The tiny details on Hoysala carvings must have been made with modern tools.

Truth: They were carved by hand with chisels, hammers and files about 800 years ago. The soapstone the sculptors used is soft when freshly quarried, which made very fine work possible.
