Why do people pay taxes?

In 1696, England taxed windows. People bricked theirs up to pay less, and you can still see the blank walls on old houses.

The short answer

Taxes are money that people and businesses pay to the government. The government uses it to pay for things that everyone shares and that would be hard to buy alone: roads, schools, hospitals, police, the army, clean water, streetlights and space programmes.

You could not easily buy your own highway or hire your own fire brigade. When crores of people each pay a share, the country can afford them together. Taxes also help pay for pensions for the elderly, free school meals and help after floods and earthquakes.

Surprising but true

Even a child buying a chocolate bar in India pays tax, because GST is already included in the price on the wrapper.

Taxes you see and taxes you don't

Some taxes are paid directly. Income tax is taken from what people earn, and richer people usually pay a bigger share of their income. Companies pay tax on their profits.

Other taxes are hidden inside prices. India's Goods and Services Tax, or GST, began on 1 July 2017. It replaced a tangle of separate state and central taxes with one system. Different goods carry different rates, shown as percentages. In India GST is included in the price you see. In most of the United States, sales tax is added at the till, and it varies by state. A few states, such as Oregon and Delaware, have no statewide sales tax at all.

Taxes that changed history

People have argued about taxes for thousands of years, and some arguments became revolutions. In 1773, American colonists angry at a British tax on tea threw crates of it into Boston Harbour. Their slogan was "no taxation without representation": they would not pay taxes set by a parliament they could not vote for. Within a few years the American Revolution had begun.

In 1930, Mahatma Gandhi led thousands of people on a walk of about 385 km to the sea at Dandi to make salt, breaking a British law that taxed it. Salt was something even the poorest family needed, so the tax touched everyone.

What was the Salt March?In 1930, a 60-year-old man walked 385 km to the sea, bent down and picked up a lump of salty mud. It shook the British Empire.

Strange taxes

In 1696, England introduced a window tax. Houses with more windows paid more, on the idea that bigger houses had more windows. Many owners simply bricked some up. Doctors later complained that dark, airless homes made people ill, and the tax was abolished in 1851.

Where the money goes

Every February, India's Finance Minister presents the Union Budget in Parliament. It lists how much the government expects to collect and how it plans to spend it, on things like defence, railways, farming, health and roads. A budget works much like a family's monthly plan, just with far more zeros. You can learn how to make one in budgets.

India's modern income tax began in 1860, when the British introduced it to pay debts after the revolt of 1857. In the United States, a federal income tax became permanent after the 16th Amendment to the Constitution in 1913. Americans file their returns each spring, usually by 15 April.

Fair shares

Nobody enjoys paying taxes, and people disagree strongly about how much tax is fair and what it should be spent on. Some want lower taxes and a smaller government. Others want higher taxes to pay for more public services. Those disagreements are a big part of what elections are about. Most people do agree on one thing: a country where nobody paid tax would have no schools, roads or hospitals for anyone.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Only rich people pay taxes.

Truth: Almost everyone pays some tax. Income tax is paid mainly by people who earn above a certain amount, but taxes on goods, like GST in India or sales tax in the USA, are paid by anyone who buys things, including children.

Try this

Pick five things your family buys often, such as soap, milk or a notebook, and look up their GST rate online with an adult. Work out roughly how much tax is hidden in each price. You may find that everyday foods like fresh milk have no GST at all.
