Why are the USA and Iran fighting?

For about 25 years after 1953, the USA and Iran were close friends. Since 1979 they have been rivals, and in 2026 that rivalry became a war that reached Indian kitchens, airports and ships.

The short answer

The USA and Iran have distrusted each other since 1979, when a revolution in Iran overthrew a king the USA had supported. Since then, they have clashed over Iran's nuclear programme, over armed groups Iran supports, and over Iran's long conflict with Israel, a close US ally.

In February 2026, the USA and Israel began a war against Iran. Each side says it is protecting itself from the other. The fighting reached the busy sea lanes of the Gulf, where about a crore Indians work and much of India's fuel passes.

Surprising but true

Iran's nuclear story began with help from the USA. In 1957 the two countries signed a deal to share nuclear know-how, and about ten years later the USA supplied Iran with a research reactor and uranium.

What each side says

Think of two neighbours who once fought over a piece of land. Years later, each one locks the gate, watches the other and expects the worst. Every new step one takes looks like a threat to the other. That is how long rivalries keep going.

The USA and Israel say Iran must never get a nuclear bomb. They say Iran's missiles, and the armed groups it backs, threaten Israel, US soldiers and the Gulf countries around it. Israel called its first strikes a "pre-emptive attack" to "remove threats to the State of Israel". US leaders have also said they wanted a different government in Iran.

Iran says it is defending itself against attacks on its land and its leaders. It says its nuclear work is for making electricity, not bombs. It points to the 1953 coup and to decades of sanctions as proof that the USA wants to control it. It calls its own strikes on US bases a response to attacks on Iran.

Kyun does not decide who is right. It is enough to know what each side believes.

Where it came from

In 1951, Iran's elected prime minister, Mohammad Mosaddegh, took control of Iran's oil from a British company. In 1953, a coup run by the USA's spy agency, with British help, removed him. The Shah, Iran's king, then ruled as a close US friend during the Cold War.

In 1979, a revolution overthrew the Shah. Iran became an Islamic Republic led by Ayatollah Khomeini, a religious leader. On 4 November 1979, students seized the US embassy in Tehran. They held 52 Americans for 444 days, until January 1981. The USA cut ties in 1980, and the two countries have had no embassies in each other's capitals since.

From 1980 to 1988, Iran fought a long war with its neighbour Iraq, and the USA backed Iraq. In 1988, a US warship shot down an Iranian passenger plane by mistake, killing all 290 people on board.

Iran also built up friends across the region. Its Revolutionary Guards set up Hezbollah in Lebanon in 1982, and it supports the Houthis in Yemen, armed groups in Iraq and, for years, Hamas in Gaza. Iran calls them its "Axis of Resistance". The USA calls several of them terrorist groups.

Then came the nuclear question. Iran built machines to make uranium purer. Slightly pure uranium can fuel a nuclear power plant; very pure uranium can make a bomb. In 2015, Iran signed a deal with world powers: it accepted limits, and sanctions were eased. In 2018, the USA left the deal and brought back sanctions. Iran then made its uranium purer than the deal allowed. In June 2025, Israel and then the USA bombed Iran's nuclear sites.

How the 2026 war unfolded

On 28 February 2026, the USA and Israel launched hundreds of air strikes across Iran. Iran's Supreme Leader, Ali Khamenei, was killed in the first strikes, and his son Mojtaba Khamenei was later chosen to replace him. Iran fired missiles and drones at Israel and at US bases in Gulf countries, and ships almost stopped using the Strait of Hormuz.

On 8 April, Pakistan helped arrange a ceasefire, but talks failed and the USA began a naval blockade of Iran's ports. On 17 June, the two sides signed an agreement meant to end the war. It did not last. In July 2026, fighting began again and the blockade returned.

Why does the narrow Strait of Hormuz matter so much to the world?Between Iran and Oman lies a strip of sea less than 40 km wide. In normal times, about one in every five barrels of oil the world burns sails through it, and so does much of the cooking gas in Indian kitchens.

India's story

India and Iran are old neighbours in history. The Parsis of India came from Persia, home of the great Persian Empire, more than a thousand years ago, and Persian was the language of the Mughal court.

Today the link is people and fuel. India's Ministry of External Affairs says almost 1 crore Indians live and work in Gulf countries such as the UAE, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Qatar, Oman and Bahrain. They are a big part of the Indian diaspora. After the war began, so many came home that by mid-April 2026 the ministry said more than 9 lakh people had returned. Indian sailors crew many of the tankers that cross the Gulf, and some were hurt or killed when ships were hit.

India also helped equip and run a terminal at Chabahar, a port on Iran's coast. It gives India a sea route to Afghanistan and Central Asia that avoids Pakistan. US sanctions and the war have made that work much harder.

India has not taken sides. From the first day, the ministry expressed "deep concern" and called for "dialogue and diplomacy". It has asked that ships be allowed to sail freely, and has kept talking to the USA, Israel and Iran.

The numbers that matter

  • - 26 years: how long the Shah ruled after the 1953 coup, until 1979.
  • - 444 days: how long 52 Americans were held at the US embassy in Tehran, from 1979 to 1981.
  • - 8 years: the length of the Iran–Iraq war, in which the USA backed Iraq.
  • - About 60% and about 90%: by 2025 Iran had made uranium about 60% pure. A bomb needs about 90%. Most nuclear power plants use uranium about 3 to 5% pure, says the World Nuclear Association.
  • - Almost 1 crore: Indians living and working in Gulf countries, which is why every war there worries Indian families.

What people argue about

Was the 2026 attack self-defence? The USA and Israel say Iran was getting dangerously close to a bomb and had to be stopped. Iran says its nuclear work is peaceful. In 2025, the head of the UN's nuclear watchdog said it had no proof of an organised effort by Iran to build a bomb. The United Nations was set up to help stop wars, but its Security Council cannot act if one of its five permanent members, such as the USA, says no.

Pressure or talks? Some say sanctions and force are the only things Iran's leaders understand. Others say every attack makes Iran trust the world less, and that only patient deals, like the one in 2015, can work.

Whose sea is it? Iran says ships need its permission near its coast in wartime. Most other countries say the strait is open to all.

Myths and mix-ups

"Iranians are Arabs." Persians are the largest group in Iran, with their own long history, language and festivals, such as Nowruz, the Persian New Year.

"Persian is just Arabic." Persian is a cousin of Hindi and Sanskrit. It only uses a script that grew from the Arabic script.

"The USA and Iran were always enemies." From 1953 to 1979 they were close friends, and the USA even helped start Iran's nuclear programme.

What it means for you

When fighting closes the Gulf, ships carrying oil and cooking gas to India slow down. Petrol, diesel and gas cylinders can cost more, and when trucks cost more to run, prices of many things rise.

Many families have a father, mother, uncle or cousin working in Dubai, Riyadh or Kuwait City. A war there can mean worry, lost jobs and less money sent home. Flights can be cancelled or rerouted when airspace closes, so trips take longer and cost more. A quarrel that began over oil in 1953 can still reach your kitchen today.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Iranians are Arabs and speak Arabic.

Truth: Persians are the largest group in Iran, and the main language is Persian, also called Farsi. Persian belongs to the same big family of languages as Hindi and Sanskrit, while Arabic belongs to a different family. Persian is written in a script that grew from the Arabic one, which is why the two can look alike.
