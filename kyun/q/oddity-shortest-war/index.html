What was the shortest war in history?

On 27 August 1896, Britain and Zanzibar fought a war that was over before most people had finished breakfast.

The short answer

The shortest war on record was fought between Britain and Zanzibar, an island state off the coast of East Africa, on 27 August 1896. It began a little after nine in the morning and was over in roughly 40 minutes. Accounts from the time differ slightly, so you will see anything from 38 to 45 minutes.

The war was about who would rule Zanzibar. When a new sultan took the throne without Britain's approval, British warships opened fire on his palace. It was a lopsided fight. Britain had five warships and hundreds of marines and sailors. Zanzibar had a few old cannons, a palace made largely of wood and one small armed ship. The palace was soon in ruins and the new sultan had fled.

Surprising but true

For years, the masts of the sunken Zanzibari ship HHS Glasgow stuck up out of the harbour, a reminder of the war that lasted less than a school lesson.

An island of cloves and trade

Zanzibar was a rich place. Its farms grew huge amounts of cloves, and for a time it was the world's leading producer of the spice. Ships rode the monsoon winds between Zanzibar, Arabia and India, carrying ivory, cloth and spices along old trade routes.

Indian merchants from Gujarat and Kutch lived there in large numbers. The Indian rupee was used as money on the island. Zanzibar was also a centre of the East African slave trade, which Britain was trying to end. By the 1890s, Britain treated the sultanate as a protectorate and expected a say in who ruled it.

A sultan who would not step down

On 25 August 1896, Sultan Hamad bin Thuwaini died suddenly. Within hours his cousin, Khalid bin Barghash, moved into the palace and declared himself sultan. The British wanted someone else, Hamud bin Muhammed, who they thought would do as they asked.

Khalid gathered a few thousand guards, servants and armed supporters at the palace. The British gathered warships in the harbour. They sent an ultimatum: leave by 9 o'clock on the morning of the 27th, or be fired on.

Forty minutes of thunder

Khalid did not leave. At about 9:02, the ships opened fire. The wooden palace was badly damaged within minutes and caught fire. Zanzibar's only real warship, a former royal yacht called HHS Glasgow, fired back with small guns and was sunk where it lay. Its crew raised a British flag to show they had given up.

By around 9:40 the palace flag was down and the guns went quiet. Khalid had already slipped away and found shelter in the German consulate. The masts of the sunken Glasgow poked out of the harbour water for years afterwards.

After the war

Britain put Hamud bin Muhammed on the throne that same day. The following year, under British pressure, he issued a decree abolishing slavery on the islands. The story of how that cruel trade ended is told in slavery and freedom.

Khalid escaped to German East Africa on the mainland. British forces captured him there during the First World War, about 20 years later. He was sent into exile and later allowed to return to East Africa, where he died in 1927.

Why it is remembered

The war is famous mainly for being so short. It also shows how the great powers of the 1890s used gunboats to decide who ruled smaller states. Zanzibar stayed under British protection until 1963, then joined the mainland country of Tanganyika in 1964 to form Tanzania.

Today Stone Town, the old heart of Zanzibar City, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, with carved wooden doors that show Indian, Arab and African styles side by side. From its harbour on a clear day, you look towards the coast where, further north, Kenya's grasslands begin the great safari country of East Africa.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Nobody got hurt in the shortest war because it was so quick.

Truth: The war was short but not harmless. About 500 people on the Zanzibari side were killed or wounded, mostly when the wooden palace was shelled. Only one British sailor was badly hurt.
