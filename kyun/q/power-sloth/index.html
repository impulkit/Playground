Why are sloths so slow?

A sloth may travel less than 40 metres in a whole day, and that slowness is its secret weapon for staying alive.

The short answer

A sloth lives on one of the worst diets in the rainforest: leaves. Leaves are tough, low in energy and full of chemicals that plants make to stop animals eating them. A sloth's stomach can take a week or more to break down a single meal.

With so little fuel coming in, a sloth cannot afford to rush. Its whole body is built for saving energy. It has roughly a quarter less muscle than most mammals its size. Its body temperature drifts up and down with the weather, so it spends less energy keeping warm. In a 2016 study, three-toed sloths used less energy per day than any other mammal measured that does not hibernate.

So slowness is not laziness. It is a careful budget. A sloth's top speed in a tree is only a few metres a minute, slower than a toddler crawling across a room.

Surprising but true

Hanging around for a living

Sloths live in the rainforests of Central and South America. There are six species, split into two-toed and three-toed sloths. All of them spend almost their whole lives upside down.

Hanging takes very little effort for a sloth. Its long curved claws hook over branches like coat hangers, and the grip needs hardly any muscle to hold. Even its insides are adapted. Some organs are fixed to the ribs so they don't press down on the lungs while the animal dangles.

Three-toed sloths also have extra bones in their necks, 8 or 9 instead of the usual 7. They can turn their heads about 270 degrees. That lets them reach leaves all around without moving the rest of their bodies.

Too slow to be seen

The slowness has a second job. The sloth's main enemies include jaguars and harpy eagles, a bird with talons as long as a grizzly bear's claws. Like many hunting birds, the harpy eagle watches for movement. An animal that moves very little is very hard to spot.

Sloths add a disguise. Their shaggy fur has grooves where green algae grow, giving them a mossy tint among the leaves. That is camouflage you don't have to work for. The fur is also home to moths, beetles and fungi, a tiny zoo travelling on one animal.

The weekly bathroom trip

Here is the oddest habit of all. Three-toed sloths climb down to the ground about once a week to poop at the foot of their tree. It is the riskiest thing they do, because on the ground they crawl slowly and are easy to catch.

Nobody is fully sure why they take the risk. One idea links it to those fur moths. The moths lay eggs in sloth dung, and their young later fly up to live in the sloth's fur. Some scientists think this helps the algae grow. Others disagree, and the question is still open.

Surprisingly good swimmers

Drop a sloth in a river and it changes. It paddles with its long arms and moves several times faster than it ever does on land. A sloth can also slow its heart and stay underwater for a long time, by some reports up to 40 minutes.

That is handy in forests that flood every rainy season. Sloths are also tougher than they look. They can survive falls from tall trees, heal quickly from wounds and live for decades. In zoos, some two-toed sloths have lived for more than 40 years.

And the strangest part? Today's sloths are small, but not long ago their relatives were giants as heavy as elephants that walked the Americas on the ground. Their story is told in the giant ground sloths.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Sloths are lazy animals that sleep 15 to 20 hours a day.

Truth: Those numbers came from sloths in zoos. A study of wild brown-throated sloths in Panama found they slept closer to 10 hours. They are slow to save energy, not because they are lazy.

Try this

Time how long it takes you to cross your room at normal speed. Then cross it at "sloth speed", taking a whole minute for every step. Notice how hard it is for anyone watching to spot that you are moving at all.
