What are ocean currents?

In 1992, thousands of plastic bath toys fell into the Pacific. Their journeys taught scientists how the ocean moves.

The short answer

Ocean currents are huge streams of water flowing through the sea, a bit like rivers without banks. Some flow along the surface, and some move slowly through the cold, dark deep. Together they carry heat, food and living things around the planet.

Surface currents are pushed mainly by the wind. As the wind drags across the sea, it sets the top layer moving. Earth's spin bends these currents, so they swing into giant circular loops called gyres. In the northern half of the world, the big gyres turn clockwise. In the southern half, they turn anticlockwise. Deep currents are driven differently, by the way cold and salty water sinks.

Surprising but true

Water sinking near Greenland can take around a thousand years to travel the world's deep oceans and return to the surface.

Rivers in the sea

The most famous surface current is the Gulf Stream. It carries warm water from the Gulf of Mexico up the east coast of the USA and across the Atlantic towards Europe. It moves faster than most rivers, and carries more water than all the world's rivers put together.

Sailors noticed it long ago. Ships sailing from Britain to America took longer than ships sailing back, because they were fighting the current. Around 1770, Benjamin Franklin helped run the mail in Britain's American colonies. With his cousin, a whaling captain, he printed one of the first charts of the Gulf Stream, to help mail ships avoid it.

The great conveyor belt

Near Greenland and Antarctica, seawater gets very cold. When sea ice forms, it leaves salt behind in the water. Cold, salty water is heavy, so it sinks to the bottom and creeps along the sea floor. More water flows in at the surface to replace it.

This sets up a slow loop through all the oceans that scientists sometimes call the global conveyor belt. Water that sinks in the North Atlantic may spend around a thousand years in the deep before it rises again somewhere far away. Saltiness plays a big part, as you can read in why the sea is salty.

India's reversing currents

The Indian Ocean does something unusual. In most oceans, the big surface currents flow the same way all year. In the northern Indian Ocean, they reverse with the seasons, because the monsoon winds reverse. In summer, south-west winds drive currents one way; in winter, north-east winds push them back.

Off the coast of Kerala and Karnataka, monsoon winds also bring cold, nutrient-rich water up from the deep. This is called upwelling. It feeds huge numbers of tiny plankton, which feed sardines and mackerel, which in turn feed fishing families. Off Peru in South America, upwelling makes one of the richest fishing grounds on Earth, except in El Niño years, when warm water shuts it off.

Ducks on the move

In January 1992, a container fell off a ship in a storm in the North Pacific. Inside were about 29,000 plastic bath toys, including yellow ducks, blue turtles and green frogs. An American ocean scientist, Curtis Ebbesmeyer, asked beachcombers to report any they found. Some toys reached Alaska within a year. Others washed up in Hawaii, South America and Australia.

Their journeys helped scientists test computer models of the currents. The same currents also carry rubbish, which is why floating plastic collects in the middle of gyres, as you can read in ocean plastic. Baby sea turtles ride currents too, drifting for years before returning to the coasts where they hatched.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Water drains clockwise in one half of the world and anticlockwise in the other because of Earth's spin.

Truth: Earth's spin does steer huge things like ocean currents and storms. But in a sink or bathtub its effect is far too weak. The way water swirls there depends on the shape of the basin and how the water was moving.

Try this

Make a mini current. Fill a clear dish with room-temperature water. Gently lower in an ice cube made from water and a few drops of food colouring. Watch the cold, coloured water sink and spread along the bottom. Cold, dense water sinking near the poles drives the deep ocean in the same way.
