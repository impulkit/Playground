How was the Kailasa temple carved from one rock?

The Kailasa temple at Ellora was not built up from blocks. It was carved down into a cliff, like a giant sculpture cut from a single rock.

The short answer

The Kailasa temple at Ellora, in Maharashtra, is a full-sized Hindu temple, with towers, halls, courtyards, bridges and pillars, all carved out of one rock. Instead of building it from separate stones, workers in the 8th century cut deep trenches into a basalt hillside. They left a giant block standing in the middle and then carved that block into a temple, inside and out.

It's one of the largest monoliths in the world. The temple is dedicated to Shiva, and it was designed to look like Mount Kailash, Shiva's home in the Himalayas.

Surprising but true

Carvers removed an estimated 2 lakh (200,000) tonnes of rock to free the temple, and there was no way to stick a piece back if they made a mistake.

Carving from the top down

Most buildings go up from the ground. The Kailasa temple went down from the sky. Carvers began at the top of the hill and cut three huge trenches around a block of rock, opening a courtyard nearly the length of a football field.

Then they shaped the block, starting with the top of the tower and working downwards. This meant they could stand on the rock itself as they worked, instead of building tall scaffolding. Rooms and halls were hollowed out from the inside. Every pillar, staircase and window had to be planned in advance, because any rock removed by mistake was gone for good.

A rock made by volcanoes

The cliff at Ellora is made of basalt, a dark, hard rock formed when lava cooled. About 66 million years ago, enormous volcanic eruptions poured layer after layer of lava across western India, creating what geologists call the Deccan Traps.

Basalt is tough but even, so a skilled carver can cut it into sharp, fine shapes. The flat layers of old lava flows also helped workers judge levels as they cut down into the hill.

Who made it

Most historians link the temple to Krishna I, a king of the Rashtrakuta dynasty, who ruled in the 700s CE. The work probably took many years and huge numbers of workers, and some parts may have been finished by later rulers.

Removing the rock was the biggest task. Estimates suggest around 2 lakh tonnes of stone were chipped away using iron chisels, hammers and picks. That's roughly the weight of 40,000 elephants.

Elephants, gods and stories

The temple is crowded with carvings. Life-size stone elephants seem to hold up the main platform on their backs. Two tall stone pillars and two stone elephants stand in the courtyard.

On the walls are scenes from the Ramayana and the Mahabharata and many images of Shiva and Parvati. One famous panel shows the demon king Ravana trying to shake Mount Kailash, while Shiva calmly presses down with one toe to keep it still. Traces of paint and plaster suggest the temple was once coloured, perhaps white like a snowy mountain, though most of it has faded.

Ellora's three faiths

Kailasa is Cave 16 of 34 caves carved into the same cliff at Ellora. Some are Buddhist, some Hindu and some Jain, made over several centuries, side by side. The cave sites show that different religions were practised and supported in the same place.

Ellora became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1983, the same year as the nearby Ajanta caves, famous for their ancient paintings. Today visitors can walk through the courtyard and look up at a temple that was never built, only uncovered.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Kailasa temple was built by stacking huge stone blocks, like most temples.

Truth: There are no stacked blocks. The whole temple, with its towers, halls, bridges, pillars and life-size elephants, was cut out of one continuous mass of rock still attached to the hillside.

Try this

Take a small block of soap or a firm bar of modelling clay. Using a blunt plastic knife, with an adult nearby, carve a tiny temple out of it, working only from the top down. Plan the shape first, because whatever you cut away can't be put back.
