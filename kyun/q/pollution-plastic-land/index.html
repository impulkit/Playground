What happens to plastic on land?

Most of the plastic ever made has never been recycled or burned. It is still out there, much of it in rubbish dumps and in the soil.

The short answer

When you throw away a plastic wrapper, it goes on a long trip. Some of it is collected and recycled, melted down and made into new things. Some is burned. Much of it ends up in a landfill, a huge pile of rubbish, or blows into fields, drains and roadsides.

Unlike banana peels or paper, most plastic does not rot. Tiny living things in the soil, such as bacteria and fungi, can't easily break it down. Instead, sunlight, heat and rubbing slowly crack it into smaller and smaller bits. Those bits, called microplastics, can stay in the ground for a very long time.

Surprising but true

A 2017 study estimated that only about 9% of all plastic waste ever made had been recycled.

Mountains of rubbish

The biggest home for old plastic on land is the landfill. Delhi's Ghazipur landfill was reported in 2019 to be about 65 metres high, taller than a 20-storey building. Landfills in the USA are usually lined with clay and plastic sheets and covered with soil each day, which keeps rubbish from blowing away.

Inside a landfill there is little air or light. That slows down the cracking. A plastic bottle buried deep may stay in one piece for decades. Over time, rain trickling through the pile can carry chemicals out into nearby water.

Breaking, not vanishing

Out in the open, plastic has a harder life. The Sun's ultraviolet light weakens the long chains of molecules that make it strong. A bucket left on a roof for years turns pale and brittle and finally cracks when you touch it.

Each crack makes more pieces. Scientists call anything smaller than 5 millimetres, about the size of a grain of rice, a microplastic. They have found microplastics in farm soil, river mud, city dust and even on mountains. Some farms add them by accident, when thin plastic sheets used to cover crops tear and get ploughed into the soil.

Animals and plastic

Plastic on land doesn't stay put. Wind and rain wash it into drains and rivers, which is one reason why so much plastic reaches the sea. Blocked drains full of bags have made city floods worse in places like Mumbai.

Animals suffer too. Stray cattle in Indian towns often eat plastic bags along with food scraps, and vets have removed many kilograms of plastic from their stomachs. Birds and small animals can get tangled in strings and wrappers. Monkeys and dogs tear open rubbish bags looking for food and scatter the plastic even further.

Burning is not the answer

In many places, rubbish heaps are set on fire to make them smaller. Burning plastic in the open releases thick smoke and poisonous gases that harm the people who breathe them, especially children. Modern incinerators can burn waste more cleanly and use the heat for electricity, but they are costly and still make some pollution.

What is being done

On 1 July 2022, India banned many single-use plastic items, such as plastic cutlery, straws and some thin carry bags. Several US states have banned or charged for plastic shopping bags. Companies in India now have to help collect and recycle the packaging they sell. Scientists are also testing microbes and insect larvae that can slowly digest some plastics, though nothing yet works on the scale of a city dump.

India also has a huge army of waste pickers, people who collect bottles and other plastic by hand and sell it to recyclers. They recover a large share of the PET bottles thrown away in cities. Still, the simplest fix is using less plastic in the first place: carrying a cloth bag, refilling a steel bottle, or saying no to a straw.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Plastic eventually rots away like food or leaves.

Truth: Microbes can't easily digest most plastics. Sunlight and weather crack them into smaller and smaller pieces, called microplastics, but those pieces stay in the soil, water and air.

Try this

With an adult, bury a small piece of newspaper, a slice of apple and a scrap of plastic wrapper in a pot of soil. Water it lightly every few days. After four weeks, dig them up and compare. The apple and paper will have started to vanish. The plastic will look almost new.
