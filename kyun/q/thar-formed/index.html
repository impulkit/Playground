How did the Thar Desert form?

Every summer, monsoon winds blow right over the Thar Desert carrying plenty of moisture, and still most of it falls somewhere else.

The short answer

The Thar, or Great Indian Desert, covers much of western Rajasthan and stretches into Pakistan. It is dry mainly because of where it sits and how the winds blow.

The Thar lies in a belt of the planet, north of the tropics, where sinking dry air is common. When the southwest monsoon arrives, one branch of it blows in from the Arabian Sea. But nothing forces that moist air to rise over the Thar. The Aravalli hills run alongside the winds instead of across them, so the clouds pass over and drop their rain further away, in the Himalayan foothills and beyond.

Hot, dusty air over the desert also makes clouds less likely to form. The result is a land where some places get less than 100 mm of rain in a year.

Surprising but true

The Thar is one of the most crowded deserts in the world, with far more people per square kilometre than most other big deserts.

A desert of many shapes

Most people picture the Thar as golden sand dunes, and dunes do cover large parts of it. Some are long ridges, while others are crescent-shaped dunes that shift with the wind. Near Jaisalmer, the Sam sand dunes draw visitors on camel rides at sunset.

But the Thar also has rocky hills, salt lakes and wide gravel plains. Strong winds before the monsoon lift huge amounts of dust. Some of it blows as far as Delhi, turning the sky a hazy orange. In summer, the ground in places like Phalodi and Jaisalmer can bake at well over 45 °C.

How old is the Thar?

Scientists are still piecing together the Thar's history. Studies of buried sand layers suggest that dry, dune-building periods came and went over hundreds of thousands of years, as Earth swung between ice ages and warmer times. When the monsoon weakened, the desert spread. When it grew stronger, lakes filled and grass grew.

Along the desert's northern edge runs the dry bed of the Ghaggar-Hakra river. Several cities of the Indus civilisation, such as Kalibangan in Rajasthan, stood near it. Many experts think the river carried more water in the past, but how much, and when it dried, is still debated.

Living with very little water

The Thar is one of the most crowded deserts on Earth. People have lived here for thousands of years by being clever with water. Villages dug stepwells and built small covered tanks, called kundis, to catch every drop of rain. Herders raise camels, goats and sheep that can cope with heat and drought.

The Bishnoi community has protected khejri trees and blackbuck for centuries. Their story of the people of Khejarli, who gave their lives to save trees in 1730, is still told across India. Life in the desert, from music to food to building, shows how much people can do with very little.

Turning sand green

The biggest change came with the Indira Gandhi Canal. Work started in 1958, and it now carries water from the Himalayan rivers of Punjab hundreds of kilometres into the desert. Farms of wheat, mustard and cotton have sprung up where there were once only dunes.

The canal brought food and jobs, but also new problems. In some areas, water has soaked in and raised salt to the surface, making the soil harder to farm. Desert animals like the great Indian bustard, a bird as tall as a small child, have lost much of their open grassland, and only a small number of them survive.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Thar Desert is empty land with almost no people or wildlife.

Truth: Millions of people live in the Thar, along with blackbuck, chinkara gazelles, desert foxes and the critically endangered great Indian bustard. Its towns, music and crafts are some of the best known in India.
