What is jaggery?

The English word "sugar" comes from the Sanskrit "sharkara", from a time when India was a pioneer of sugar-making.

The short answer

Jaggery, called gur in Hindi, is a traditional sweetener made by boiling sugarcane juice until it turns thick, sticky and golden-brown. It is then poured into moulds and left to set into blocks or cones. In some regions, especially Bengal and Tamil Nadu, jaggery is also made from the sap of date or palmyra palms.

Unlike white sugar, jaggery isn't refined. It keeps the brown molasses and a little of the minerals from the cane. That gives it a rich, caramel-like taste. India makes most of the world's jaggery and uses it in sweets, dals, drinks and festival foods.

Surprising but true

The word "candy" probably comes from the Sanskrit "khanda", meaning a piece of crystallised sugar.

From cane to block

In the winter harvest season, sugarcane is cut and taken to small workshops called kolhus or gur-making units. There, crushers squeeze out the juice.

The juice is poured into huge, shallow iron pans over a fire. As it boils, water evaporates and the juice thickens. Workers skim off the scum that rises to the top, sometimes adding plant extracts to help clean it. After a few hours, the thick, golden liquid is stirred, cooled and poured into moulds.

The whole process is simple and has changed little in centuries, though many units now use better furnaces. Kolhapur in Maharashtra is especially famous for its jaggery, and its market yard trades huge amounts every season.

Palm jaggery

In Bengal, a special winter jaggery called nolen gur is made from the sap of date palms. Tappers climb the trees in the cold early morning to collect the sap. Nolen gur has a smoky, fragrant taste and goes into Bengali sweets like sandesh and rosogolla.

In Tamil Nadu and Kerala, palmyra palms give karupatti, a darker palm jaggery with a deep, earthy flavour.

An Indian invention

Sugarcane probably first grew in New Guinea, but India was one of the first places where people learned to turn its juice into solid sugar. Ancient Indian texts mention gur and other sugars. By around the Gupta period, about 1,500 years ago, Indians were making crystallised sugar.

Which English words came from India?Every time you put on pyjamas, wash with shampoo or talk about a jungle, you are borrowing from Indian languages.

The Sanskrit word for sugar, "sharkara", travelled through Persian, Arabic and Latin into English as "sugar". The Sanskrit "khanda", meaning a piece of sugar, likely gave us "candy". These borrowed words carry the memory of India's role in the sugar story.

Jaggery in festivals

Jaggery is part of many celebrations. On Makar Sankranti in Maharashtra, people exchange tilgul, sweets made of sesame seeds and jaggery. In Tamil Nadu, sweet pongal is made with new rice, moong dal and jaggery during the harvest festival of Pongal.

Chikki, a crunchy sweet of peanuts and jaggery, is famous in Lonavala in Maharashtra. Many families also add a small piece of jaggery to dal, especially in Gujarat and Maharashtra, for a sweet-sour taste.

Is it healthier?

Many people think jaggery is much healthier than white sugar. It does contain small amounts of iron, potassium and magnesium, which white sugar lacks. But it's still around 80 per cent or more sugar.

Too much of any sugar can cause tooth decay and other health problems. The bacteria in your mouth don't care whether the sugar came from a factory or a village pan. Jaggery's taste and traditions are good reasons to enjoy it, but it's still a treat, not a health food. Sweet things, in any form, are best eaten in small amounts.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Jaggery is healthy, so you can eat as much as you like, unlike sugar.

Truth: Jaggery has small amounts of iron and other minerals, but it is still mostly sugar. Eaten in large amounts, it affects teeth and health much like white sugar does.

Try this

Do a taste-and-dissolve test with an adult. Put a small piece of jaggery in one cup of warm water and a spoon of white sugar in another. Stir both and compare how fast they dissolve and what colour the water turns. Then taste a tiny bit of each and describe the difference.
