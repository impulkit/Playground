Who invented the radio?

In 1895, a scientist in Calcutta used invisible waves to ring a bell in another room. Two years later, an Italian in England patented radio.

The short answer

No single person invented radio. It was built step by step, by scientists in Scotland, Germany, India, Russia, Britain, Italy and the United States.

The Italian inventor Guglielmo Marconi usually gets the credit. In the 1890s he turned a laboratory curiosity into a system that could send messages across the sea, and he won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1909. But in India, many remember Jagadish Chandra Bose, who sent and detected short radio waves in Calcutta in the mid-1890s. Others point to Nikola Tesla, Oliver Lodge in Britain and Alexander Popov in Russia. The honest answer is that radio had many parents.

Surprising but true

In 1943, the US Supreme Court ruled that one of Marconi's most important radio patents was not valid, because others had done similar work first.

Waves nobody could see

The story starts with an idea. In the 1860s, the Scottish scientist James Clerk Maxwell worked out, using mathematics, that electricity and magnetism could travel through space as waves. Light, he said, was one kind of these waves. There should be others too, invisible to the eye.

In the late 1880s, the German physicist Heinrich Hertz proved Maxwell right. He made sparks jump across a gap in one circuit and saw a tiny spark appear in a separate loop of wire across the room. Hertz did not think his waves had any practical use. Others soon proved him wrong.

A bell rings in Calcutta

Jagadish Chandra Bose was a physics professor at Presidency College in Calcutta. He built his own equipment to make very short radio waves, now called microwaves, and invented sensitive detectors to catch them.

In a public demonstration in Calcutta in 1895, he is said to have sent waves through walls and across a hall. At the far end they rang a bell and set off a small charge of gunpowder. Bose was more interested in science than in business and showed little interest in patents. In 2012, the engineering society IEEE honoured his early work as a milestone in the history of electrical engineering.

Marconi crosses the ocean

Marconi was in his early twenties when he began experimenting at his family's home in Italy. He moved to England, patented his system and set up a company. His great talent was making radio work over longer and longer distances, with tall aerials and powerful transmitters.

On 12 December 1901, at Signal Hill in Newfoundland, now part of Canada, he listened through an earphone. From a station in Cornwall, England, about 3,500 km away, came faint clicks: the letter S in Morse code. Some scientists doubted him at first, but within a few years ships were using his radios. When the Titanic sank in 1912, its radio operators called for help with Marconi equipment.

Some historians believe the detector Marconi used in 1901 was based on a design Bose had described. Others disagree. The question is still argued today.

From clicks to voices

At first radio sent only dots and dashes, like the telegraph. The Canadian inventor Reginald Fessenden is said to have broadcast voice and music on Christmas Eve 1906, though some historians question the details. By the 1920s, families across the United States and Europe were gathering around radio sets to hear news, music and shows.

In India, regular broadcasting began from Bombay and Calcutta in 1927. In 1936 the service was renamed All India Radio. The same discoveries now carry Wi-Fi, mobile calls and signals from spacecraft billions of kilometres away.

Myth or fact?

Myth: One person invented the radio.

Truth: Radio came from a chain of discoveries. Maxwell predicted the waves, Hertz proved them, scientists such as Bose, Lodge, Popov and Tesla built ways to send and detect them, and Marconi turned them into a working business.
