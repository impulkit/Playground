How can cow dung make cooking gas?

Seal cow dung and water inside a tank, and tiny microbes turn it into a gas that burns with a clean blue flame.

The short answer

When animal dung rots in a sealed tank with no air, tiny living things called microbes eat it. As they feed, they give off a mix of gases called biogas. More than half of it is methane, the same gas that makes up most natural gas. Methane burns.

So a farming family with a few cows can mix their dung with water, pour it into an underground tank, and pipe the gas to a stove in the kitchen. It lights with a match and cooks dal and rotis like the gas from a cylinder. What comes out of the other end of the tank is a soft slurry, which goes onto the fields as fertiliser.

Surprising but true

Biogas made from dung hardly smells at all. Its main gas, methane, has no smell of its own.

Microbes that hate air

The microbes doing the work are called anaerobic, which means "without air". Oxygen harms them. That's why the tank has to be sealed.

They work in teams. Some break the dung into simpler stuff, like sugars. Others turn that into acids. The last group, which are among the oldest kinds of life on Earth, turn those acids into methane. A cow's stomach holds the same kind of microbes, which is partly why cows burp out methane.

They like warmth. A tank works well in a hot Indian summer and slows down in a cold winter, the way compost does.

Are all germs bad?Right now, about as many bacteria live in and on you as there are cells in your body, and most of them are on your side.

Inside a village plant

A common Indian design looks like a buried dome of brick or concrete. Fresh dung and water go in through one pipe, a bit every day. The gas collects at the top of the dome and pushes out through a thin pipe to the kitchen. The used slurry flows out of a second opening into a pit.

India has built family biogas plants since the 1980s. China did the same on an even larger scale, with millions of rural families building tanks from the 1970s onwards. About 50 lakh have been installed under the national programme. A family plant can often supply most of a small household's cooking, though it needs a steady supply of dung and water to keep going.

Running one takes care. If the plant isn't fed every day, the microbes go hungry and the gas dwindles. Government audits have found that some village plants stopped working because of leaks, too few cattle or nobody nearby who could fix them, so newer schemes train local mechanics.

Why it matters

In many villages, people still cook on wood, crop waste or dried dung cakes burned in a chulha. The smoke fills kitchens, and the World Health Organization lists smoky indoor cooking as a big cause of lung disease, especially for women and young children. A biogas flame gives off almost no smoke.

It also helps with waste. Dung left in heaps gives off methane straight into the air, where it traps heat much more strongly than carbon dioxide. Burning that methane as fuel turns it into carbon dioxide, which is less harmful, and saves wood and cooking gas at the same time.

From farms to cities

Biogas works on more than dung. Kitchen scraps, sewage, crop stalks and market waste can all feed the microbes. Some Indian cities run plants on vegetable waste from markets. In the USA, large dairy farms use covered ponds to trap gas from manure, and old rubbish dumps are piped to collect the methane seeping out of them.

Cleaned and squeezed into tanks, biogas can even run buses and cars. India has a scheme to build plants that sell this compressed biogas like any other fuel. It is a slightly odd thought: one day the bus to school might run partly on cow dung.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Food cooked on biogas tastes or smells of cow dung.

Truth: The gas is piped away from the tank and burns cleanly. Methane has no smell, and the small trace of rotten-egg gas is too faint to reach the food.

Try this

Watch microbes make gas. Ask an adult to help you mix a spoon of dried yeast and a spoon of sugar in half a cup of warm water inside a small plastic bottle. Stretch a balloon over the neck and leave it somewhere warm for an hour. The yeast gives off carbon dioxide, not methane, but it shows how living things too small to see can blow up a balloon.
