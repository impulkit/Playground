What is Pi Day?

Every 14 March, people around the world eat round pies, recite long strings of digits and celebrate a number that never ends.

The short answer

Pi Day is a celebration of pi, the number you get when you divide the distance around any circle by the distance across it. Pi starts 3.14159 and its digits go on forever, never settling into a repeating pattern.

In the United States, dates are written month first, so 14 March becomes 3/14, the first three digits of pi. People mark the day with maths games, digit-reciting contests and, of course, pies. Since 2020, 14 March has also been the International Day of Mathematics, set up by UNESCO. In India, where dates are written day first, some people prefer 22 July, written 22/7, because 22 divided by 7 is close to pi.

Surprising but true

NASA scientists say they need only about 15 decimal places of pi to steer spacecraft across the solar system, yet computers have worked out about 314 trillion.

A party at a science museum

The first Pi Day party was held in 1988 at the Exploratorium, a hands-on science museum in San Francisco. A physicist there, Larry Shaw, led staff in a circular parade and shared fruit pies. The idea spread to schools and universities.

In 2009, the US House of Representatives passed a resolution backing Pi Day. Some schools hold contests where students make paper chains with a different colour for each digit. Others serve pizza, another circle, because pies are rarer in many countries.

A day of birthdays

Pi Day has another link. Albert Einstein, the physicist who changed how we think about time and space, was born on 14 March 1879. Many people celebrate his birthday on Pi Day too.

By chance, the British physicist Stephen Hawking died on 14 March 2018. Fans of science now remember both scientists on the same day.

What did Albert Einstein discover?A young clerk in a Swiss patent office wrote four papers in one year that changed how we understand light, time and energy.

India's pi pioneers

Indian mathematicians studied pi long ago. Around 499 CE, Aryabhata wrote that a circle 20,000 units across has a circumference of about 62,832 units. That gives 3.1416, correct to four decimal places. He also hinted that this was only an approximation.

Around 1400, Madhava of Sangamagrama in Kerala found an endless series of fractions that adds up to pi. European mathematicians found similar formulas nearly 300 years later. And in the early 1900s, Srinivasa Ramanujan discovered formulas for pi so fast that computers still use ideas from them.

How many digits do we need?

Not many. NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory uses about 15 decimal places to plan the paths of spacecraft. With around 40 decimal places, you could work out the distance around the whole observable universe to within the width of a single atom.

So why calculate more? Partly as a test for computers and their storage, and partly because people love a challenge. In December 2025, a team used a single computer running for about four months to work out about 314 trillion digits. Printed out, they would fill a library of books. Each record is double-checked with a different formula, so that no digit is wrong. Pi also turns up where there are no obvious circles. It appears in the maths of waves, swinging pendulums and even dropped needles landing across floorboards.

Memory champions

Some people memorise thousands of digits. In 2015, Rajveer Meena, a student from Rajasthan, recited 70,000 digits of pi in about 10 hours, wearing a blindfold. That is listed by Guinness World Records as the most digits of pi memorised.

People use tricks to help, such as sentences where the number of letters in each word matches a digit. "How I wish I could calculate pi" gives 3, 1, 4, 1, 5, 9, 2. And the strangest part? In 1897, lawmakers in the US state of Indiana almost passed a bill that gave pi a wrong, simpler value. Real circles would have ignored them completely.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pi is exactly 22/7.

Truth: 22/7 is a handy shortcut, equal to about 3.1429. Pi is about 3.14159, and its digits go on forever without repeating, so no fraction can be exactly equal to it.

Try this

Find three round things at home, like a plate, a bangle and a tin lid. Wrap a string around each one to measure the distance around, then measure across the middle with a ruler. Divide the first number by the second. Each time you should get close to 3.14.
