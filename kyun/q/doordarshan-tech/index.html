How did TV signals reach Indian villages?

In 1975, a borrowed American satellite beamed farming lessons and cartoons to TV sets in about 2,400 Indian villages that had never seen television.

The short answer

TV pictures travel as radio waves, and radio waves mostly go in straight lines. A tower in a city can only reach about as far as the horizon, so most of India's villages were out of range for years.

The answer was to put the transmitter in the sky. In August 1975, India borrowed time on an American satellite called ATS-6. For a year it beamed programmes to about 2,400 villages in six states. Later, India built its own INSAT satellites and hundreds of ground towers, and Doordarshan's signal spread across the country.

Surprising but true

Many SITE villages had never had a cinema or a TV, yet for a year they watched programmes sent from a satellite about 36,000 km above the Earth.

Television starts small

Doordarshan began on 15 September 1959, in Delhi, with a tiny studio and a transmitter that reached only a few kilometres. At first it broadcast for an hour or so, twice a week. Many shows were for schools and farmers. Programmes like Krishi Darshan, which began in 1967, taught farmers about seeds and crops.

The trouble was distance. A TV signal from a tower behaves a bit like the light from a lighthouse. Beyond the curve of the Earth, it simply misses you.

A dish made of chicken wire

Vikram Sarabhai, the scientist who founded India's space programme, believed satellites could teach a whole country at once. His idea became the Satellite Instructional Television Experiment, or SITE.

NASA lent India its ATS-6 satellite from August 1975 to July 1976. Indian engineers built the ground stations and the receivers. Many villages got a dish of wire mesh about 3 metres wide, roughly the length of a small car. A community TV set sat in a school or panchayat hall, and people gathered in the evening to watch.

The programmes were in local languages: lessons on health, farming and family planning, plus songs and stories for children. Morning broadcasts went to schools. Evening ones were for adults.

Making TV for people who had never seen it

Building the hardware was hard, but making the shows was harder. Producers had to talk to a farmer in Odisha, a weaver in Rajasthan and a schoolchild in Karnataka, each in their own language. Teams travelled to villages to film real people instead of actors in city studios.

Keeping the sets running was its own job. A village TV could break, run out of power or get damaged by monsoon damp, so technicians from nearby repair centres did regular rounds. Researchers visited too, asking villagers what they understood, what they enjoyed and what bored them.

How do satellites help us every day?Weather forecasts, maps on your phone, card payments and cricket on TV all depend on machines circling far above your head.

From experiment to everywhere

SITE showed the idea worked. India then launched its own satellite, INSAT-1A, in 1982, though it failed the next year. INSAT-1B, launched in 1983, lasted much longer and carried Doordarshan's signal nationwide.

Satellites were only half the system. Doordarshan also built hundreds of low-power transmitters, small towers that picked up the satellite signal and rebroadcast it to the surrounding towns. In 1984, new transmitters were being switched on at a rate of almost one a day.

Colour, and a nation glued to one channel

Colour TV arrived in India in 1982, just in time for the Asian Games in Delhi. Families who could afford a set bought one. Neighbours crowded into those homes.

By the late 1980s, Doordarshan was the only channel most Indians had, and big serials like Ramayan (1987) emptied streets on Sunday mornings. The mix of Indian TV changed again in the 1990s, when private channels arrived on satellite dishes. Today, DD Free Dish lets households in remote places pick up dozens of channels without paying a monthly fee, using the same trick SITE tested: a dish pointed at a satellite.

Myth or fact?

Myth: TV in India started in big city homes and only later spread to villages.

Truth: Some of India's earliest TV broadcasts were made for villagers. Doordarshan began in 1959 with educational programmes, and the 1975 SITE experiment put TV in rural community halls long before most city families owned a set.

Try this

Stand in one corner of a room and ask a friend to shine a torch at a mirror held high by another friend, so the light lands on you. That is how a satellite works, bouncing a signal from one place to a faraway spot it can't reach in a straight line.
