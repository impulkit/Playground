How does India control the price of medicines?

Two strips of the same medicine, with the same ingredient, can sit side by side in a chemist's shop at very different prices. India has rules to stop the gap growing too wide.

The short answer

In India, a government body called the National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority, or NPPA, keeps an eye on what medicines cost. For the most important medicines, it sets a ceiling price that no company may charge above.

For other medicines, companies set their own prices, but they cannot raise them by more than 10% a year. And when prices hurt patients badly, as with some cancer drugs, the government can step in with special limits.

Surprising but true

When India capped the price of heart stents in 2017, some of them became about 85% cheaper almost overnight.

How it works

A medicine passes through several hands before it reaches you. The company makes it and sells it to a stockist, a big trader. The stockist sells it to a chemist or a hospital, and the chemist sells it to you at the maximum retail price, or MRP, printed on the pack.

The difference between what the company charges the first trader and the MRP is called the trade margin. It pays for transport, storage and the shopkeeper's profit. But with some costly medicines, the MRP was set many times higher than the company's price. The huge gap went to whoever sold it, often hospitals.

Imagine a cricket bat that leaves the factory for ₹1,000 but is sold for ₹5,000 because each shop wants a big cut. A 30% margin cap says the bat can now be sold for no more than ₹1,300. The factory still earns the same, but you pay far less.

For 384 essential medicines, the rules work differently. The NPPA looks at every brand of that medicine with at least 1% of sales and works out the average price. It adds a small amount for the shopkeeper and fixes that as the ceiling. Each April, these ceilings may rise a little in line with inflation, the general rise in prices.

The rules cover medicines for common illnesses too, like fever, diabetes, high blood pressure and tuberculosis. These are medicines that millions of families buy every single month, so even small savings add up.

Where it came from

India's first medicine price order came in 1970. A patent gives an inventor a short monopoly, the sole right to make and sell an invention. In the same year, a new patent law allowed Indian companies to make copies of medicines invented abroad, as long as they found their own way of making them. Indian firms became experts at cheap generic medicines.

The NPPA was set up in 1997. The rulebook used today, the Drugs (Prices Control) Order, came in 2013. Its Paragraph 19 lets the government act in "extraordinary circumstances" for the public good. This is the power used for cancer drugs.

In 2017, it was used to cap the price of heart stents and knee implants. In 2019, a test capped trade margins on 42 cancer medicines at 30%, and prices of 526 brands fell by up to 90%.

India's story

India is often called the pharmacy of the world. It makes about one in five generic medicine doses used across the globe, and exports them to more than 200 countries. Yet many Indian families still struggle to pay for treatment.

About two-fifths of all money spent on health in India comes straight from people's own pockets, not from insurance or the government. Medicines are one of the biggest parts of that bill.

Cancer shows the problem sharply. India records about 15 lakh new cancer cases a year. Many newer treatments, like immunotherapy, which helps the body's immune system attack cancer cells, cost lakhs of rupees. That is why the 2026 cap covered such hospital medicines.

The government also runs Jan Aushadhi Kendras, shops that sell generic medicines at low prices. There are now more than 15,000 of them across the country.

The numbers that matter

  • - 137: cancer medicines added to the margin cap in October 2026. Their prices could fall by up to 70%, from 9 November.
  • - 30%: the cap on trade margin, as a percentage of the company's price. On a medicine that leaves the company at ₹10,000, the MRP can be no more than ₹13,000.
  • - 384: medicines on the 2022 National List of Essential Medicines, whose prices have a strict ceiling.
  • - 10%: the most a company can raise the price of a medicine outside the essential list in a year.
  • - About 80%: the share of cancer treatment costs that families pay themselves, according to one recent study. The median bill was about ₹4.1 lakh.

What people argue about

Patient groups and many doctors say price controls save lives. They point to stents and cancer drugs, where prices fell steeply without the medicines disappearing.

Medicine companies say that if prices are squeezed too hard, they may earn too little to research new drugs or launch the latest ones in India. Some warn of shortages, or that firms will stop making low-priced medicines altogether.

Others say the real fix lies elsewhere: more government spending on public hospitals, wider health insurance, and clear prices that every patient can see.

Myths and mix-ups

"The MRP is the price the government sets." Not always. For most medicines, the company sets the MRP within the rules. Only essential medicines have a government ceiling.

"Costlier medicine works better." Two medicines with the same ingredient and dose work the same way. The difference is usually in branding, packaging and marketing, not in how the medicine works.

"Price caps mean the company loses money." A margin cap mostly trims what traders and sellers add on top. The company's own selling price can stay the same.

What it means for you

When you buy medicine, always check the MRP and the expiry date, and ask for a bill. A chemist or hospital cannot charge more than the MRP. That is one of your rights as a buyer.

You can also ask your doctor whether a generic version is available. You can check fair prices on the government's Pharma Sahi Daam app.

If a family member ever needs costly treatment, these rules can make a real difference. A medicine that might have cost ₹1 lakh could, under a margin cap, cost a fraction of that.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A cheaper generic medicine is weaker than a famous brand.

Truth: A generic contains the same active ingredient, in the same amount, as the branded version. It must pass tests showing it works the same way in the body. The price is lower mainly because the company spends less on marketing.

Try this

Ask an adult to show you a strip of medicine from home. Find the MRP, the name of the active ingredient and the company. Then search the Pharma Sahi Daam app or website to see if the same ingredient is sold by other companies at a different price.
