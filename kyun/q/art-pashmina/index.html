Why is pashmina so soft?

Pashmina comes from goats that live 4,000 metres up in Ladakh, where winter nights can drop below minus 30°C.

The short answer

Pashmina is soft because its fibres are extremely thin. They come from the undercoat of the Changthangi goat, which lives on the high, cold plateau of Changthang in Ladakh. To survive winters that drop below −30°C, the goat grows a fine, fluffy layer under its rough outer hair.

Each fibre is about 12 to 16 microns thick. A human hair is around 50 to 100 microns. Thin fibres bend easily, so they feel smooth on skin instead of scratchy. They also trap lots of tiny air pockets, which is why a light pashmina shawl can be so warm.

Surprising but true

A single pashmina fibre is about 12 to 16 microns thick, roughly a sixth of the width of a human hair.

Goats of the cold desert

The Changthang plateau, east of Leh, sits at around 4,000 to 5,000 metres. That's higher than most of the Alps. The air is thin, the wind is fierce and very little grows. Nomadic herders, the Changpa people, move their goats, sheep and yaks between pastures through the year.

The goats are small and tough, with long outer hair and curling horns. Their fine undercoat is what keeps them alive. Herders say the colder the winter, the finer and thicker the fibre grows.

Combing, not cutting

In spring, as the weather warms, the goats begin to shed their winter undercoat. Herders hold each goat and comb out the soft fibre by hand. One goat gives only a few hundred grams of raw fibre a year, and much of that is lost when the coarse hairs are picked out.

It can take the fibre from three or four goats to make a single shawl. That's one reason real pashmina is expensive.

From fibre to shawl

The raw fibre travels to Kashmir, where much of the work has been done by hand for centuries. First it's cleaned and sorted. Then women spin it on a wooden spinning wheel called a yinder. The thread is so fine that machines used to snap it, though some now manage.

Weavers then make the cloth on handlooms. A plain shawl can take days. A kani shawl, where patterns are woven using dozens of small wooden bobbins, can take a year or more. Other shawls are hand-embroidered after weaving with sozni needlework, which can cover almost the whole surface.

Shawls fit for emperors

Kashmiri shawls were prized by Mughal emperors, who gave them as royal gifts. In the late 1700s and 1800s, they became a craze in Europe after travellers and soldiers brought them home. The French empress Josephine reportedly owned hundreds.

Factories in Paisley, Scotland, began copying Kashmiri shawl patterns on machines. That's why the curved teardrop motif, called buta or kalga in India, is known in English as "paisley". The same motif appears on Banarasi silks, linking two great textile traditions of Kashmir and Varanasi.

Pashmina and its forbidden cousin

Pashmina is sometimes confused with shahtoosh, an even finer wool from the Tibetan antelope, or chiru. Getting shahtoosh meant killing the antelope, and numbers crashed. Trading in shahtoosh is now banned in India and around the world, and the chiru is protected.

Pashmina is different. The goats aren't harmed, and combing them is part of normal herding life.

Real or fake?

Many shawls sold as "pashmina" are actually blends with silk or wool, or even made of acrylic. In 2008, Kashmir Pashmina got a Geographical Indication tag. Since then, a testing lab in Srinagar can certify genuine handmade shawls and add a special label.

Climate change and young people leaving herding worry the Changpa community. Warmer winters may mean fewer goats with the finest fibre. Few fabrics depend so directly on cold, and the shift is happening fast.

The pashmina story starts with a goat surviving the cold. Other mountain animals, like the yak and the snow leopard, have their own clever ways of staying warm high in the Himalaya.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If a shawl can pass through a finger ring, it must be real pashmina.

Truth: The ring test is an old trick, but many fine shawls, including blends and machine-made fakes, can pass through a ring. Only a lab test or a proper GI label can show a shawl is pure pashmina.

Try this

Collect a few fibres: a strand of your own hair, a cotton thread, a wool thread and a polyester thread. Look at them with a magnifying glass and feel them between your fingers. Arrange them from thickest to thinnest, then check by rolling each between finger and thumb.
