What is the Great Rift Valley?

Africa is slowly splitting in two, and in one Ethiopian desert in 2005, the ground cracked open by several metres in a matter of days.

The short answer

The Great Rift Valley is a giant system of valleys where the Earth's crust is being slowly pulled apart. It runs from the Red Sea down through Ethiopia, Kenya and Tanzania towards Mozambique. Along the way are steep cliffs, deep lakes and volcanoes.

Beneath it, hot rock rising from the mantle is stretching the crust like dough. As the crust thins, long strips of land drop down between cracks called faults, making the valley floor. East Africa is splitting away from the rest of the continent at a few millimetres a year.

The name was given in the 1890s by the British geologist John Walter Gregory, who explored the valley in Kenya. Today, scientists also call the African part the East African Rift.

Surprising but true

In the Afar region of Ethiopia in 2005, a crack about 60 km long opened in roughly ten days as the ground pulled apart.

A continent tearing in slow motion

Africa sits on a plate that is slowly breaking into two. The larger part is called the Nubian plate and the eastern part the Somali plate. The continents drift all the time, but here you can watch the very start of a split, before any ocean has formed.

The Afar region of Ethiopia is where it is happening fastest. In 2005, the ground there cracked open along about 60 km, in roughly ten days. In places the gap was several metres wide. Scientists rushed in with satellites and instruments to measure it. Further north, the rift has already flooded. The Red Sea itself is a rift that the ocean poured into millions of years ago.

Lakes, volcanoes and flamingos

When land drops in a rift, water fills the low places. Lake Tanganyika is about 1,470 m deep, the second-deepest lake on Earth. Lake Malawi and Lake Turkana are rift lakes too.

Some rift lakes, like Lake Natron in Tanzania, are so salty and full of soda that few animals can live in them. Yet huge flocks of lesser flamingos breed there, feeding on the tiny algae that thrive in that harsh water.

Where the crust is thin, magma pushes up. Mount Kilimanjaro and Mount Kenya were built by this rift activity. Ol Doinyo Lengai in Tanzania erupts a rare lava that is black when it comes out and cools to white, unlike any other active volcano on Earth.

Where did humans come from?About 3.6 million years ago, three upright walkers left footprints in wet volcanic ash in Tanzania. The prints are still there.

A cradle for our ancestors

Some of the most important fossils of early humans and their relatives have been found in the rift. In 1974, scientists at Hadar in Ethiopia found much of a skeleton about 3.2 million years old. They nicknamed her Lucy. She walked upright but had a small brain, closer in size to a chimpanzee's.

At Olduvai Gorge in Tanzania, Mary and Louis Leakey and their team found stone tools and fossils almost 2 million years old. They changed ideas about when our ancestors began making tools, a big chapter in the story of human evolution.

Why are there so many fossils here? Rifts sink and fill with layers of mud and volcanic ash, which bury bones quickly. Later, faults and erosion lift those layers back up where people can find them. The ash can also be dated, so scientists know how old the bones are.

The ocean of the future

If the stretching continues, the Somali plate will keep drifting east. Over millions of years the valley floor will sink lower, until the sea breaks in. Geologists think a new ocean could one day separate the Horn of Africa from the rest of the continent, giving East Africa a brand new coastline.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Africa will split in two any day now, because of a giant crack that opened in Kenya in 2018.

Truth: That crack was real, but scientists think it was mostly made by heavy rain washing out soft volcanic ash. The rift itself moves only millimetres a year, and a new ocean is millions of years away.

Try this

Roll out a slab of play dough or soft clay on a table and hold both ends. Pull them apart slowly. Watch where the middle thins, sags and cracks. That sagging middle is a model of a rift valley.
