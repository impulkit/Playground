Could an asteroid hit Earth today?

Small ones hit every year. The question is: how big?

The short answer

Small space rocks hit Earth all the time. Most burn up high in the sky as shooting stars.

The bigger the rock, the rarer the hit. Pieces the size of a car arrive about once a year and usually explode harmlessly high up. Rocks big enough to flatten a city come perhaps once every few hundred or few thousand years. Ones big enough to threaten the whole planet, like the asteroid that helped end the age of the dinosaurs, come only once in tens of millions of years.

Scientists watch the sky every night. They've found almost all the really big ones near Earth, and none is on course to hit us in the next 100 years.

Surprising but true

About 100 tonnes of space dust and tiny rocks fall on Earth every day.

Dust, pebbles and fireballs

Every day, about 100 tonnes of space material falls on Earth. Nearly all of it is dust, grains smaller than sand, that drifts down unnoticed. Slightly bigger bits make shooting stars. Every so often a rock survives the fall and lands as a meteorite.

Bigger pieces tend to explode in the air before they reach the ground, because the pressure of rushing through the atmosphere at tens of kilometres a second tears them apart. Satellites spot several such airbursts every year, mostly over oceans and empty land.

Two warnings from Russia

On 30 June 1908, something exploded over a remote forest near the Tunguska River in Siberia. It flattened about 80 million trees over an area larger than the city of Delhi. No crater was found, so it was probably a rock tens of metres wide that blew apart several kilometres up. Because almost nobody lived there, few people, if any, were killed.

On 15 February 2013, a rock about 20 m wide, as long as two school buses, streaked over the city of Chelyabinsk. It burst about 30 km up with a flash brighter than the Sun. The shock wave smashed windows across the city, and about 1,500 people were hurt, mostly by flying glass. It was a reminder that even fairly small rocks can do damage if they arrive over a city.

Keeping watch

Since the 1990s, telescopes have systematically searched for near-Earth asteroids. Astronomers have now found more than 9 out of 10 of those over 1 km wide, the size that could cause worldwide disaster. None of them is on a path to hit Earth for at least the next century.

Smaller asteroids, 140 m and up, could still devastate a region. Fewer than half of those are thought to have been found. New telescopes are joining the hunt, including the Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile, which began observing in 2025, and NASA's NEO Surveyor, a space telescope planned for launch in 2027.

How scientists work out the risk

When a new asteroid is found, astronomers know its path only roughly at first. They calculate the chance that it might hit Earth, using probability. As more observations come in, the path becomes clearer, and almost always the chance drops to zero.

That is what happened with an asteroid called 2024 YR4, found in December 2024. For a few weeks in early 2025, its chance of hitting Earth in 2032 rose to about 3 per cent, one of the highest ever recorded for an asteroid that size. Then new measurements ruled out an Earth impact completely. This is how the system is meant to work.

What if one were coming?

If a dangerous asteroid were found years in advance, it might be possible to nudge it off course. In 2022, NASA's DART mission crashed a spacecraft into a small asteroid moon and changed its orbit, proving the idea can work.

The key is time. A gentle push decades ahead is far easier than a desperate one at the last minute. That is why astronomers keep searching every clear night, long before anything is on its way. And the big one, 66 million years ago, explained in extinction, remains the exception rather than the rule.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The asteroid Apophis is going to hit Earth in 2029.

Truth: Apophis will pass very close on 13 April 2029, closer than some satellites, and may be visible without a telescope. But careful tracking has ruled out any chance of it hitting Earth then, or for at least the next 100 years.

Try this

Scoop a cup of garden soil or gravel onto a sheet of newspaper and sort it by size into tiny grains, small stones and big stones. Count each pile. You will almost certainly find lots of tiny bits and very few big ones. Space rocks are the same, which is why small hits are common and big ones are very rare.
