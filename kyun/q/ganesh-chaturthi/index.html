How is Ganesh Chaturthi celebrated?

In Mumbai, people queue for up to a day and a night to see one famous Ganesh idol, the Lalbaugcha Raja, for just a few seconds.

The short answer

Ganesh Chaturthi is a Hindu festival that celebrates the birth of Ganesh, the elephant-headed god. Many Hindus pray to Ganesh before starting anything new, because he is believed to remove obstacles. The festival falls in the month of Bhadrapada, usually in late August or September.

Families bring home a clay image of Ganesh, decorate a small shrine and offer prayers, flowers and sweets. After a day and a half, five, seven or ten days, the image is carried to a river, lake or the sea for immersion. In Maharashtra, especially Mumbai and Pune, the festival fills the streets with music, drums and huge public idols.

Surprising but true

Ganesh Chaturthi became a big public street festival in 1893, when Bal Gangadhar Tilak encouraged it as a way to bring people together under British rule.

How Ganesh got his elephant head

There are several stories, but the best known goes like this. The goddess Parvati made a boy from clay and asked him to guard her door while she bathed. When her husband Shiva came home, the boy did not know him and refused to let him in.

In anger, Shiva cut off the boy's head. Parvati was heartbroken. To make amends, Shiva promised to bring the boy back to life and replaced his head with that of an elephant. He made Ganesh the leader of his followers and declared that people would worship Ganesh first, before any other god.

Tilak takes it to the streets

For centuries, Ganesh Chaturthi was celebrated in homes and temples. In 1893, the freedom fighter Bal Gangadhar Tilak promoted public celebrations in Pune and Bombay. Gathering in the streets let people meet, talk and organise at a time when British rulers restricted political meetings.

The public festival, called Sarvajanik Ganeshotsav, spread quickly. It became part of the story of India's freedom struggle. Today Maharashtra has tens of thousands of public Ganesh pandals, each run by a local committee.

Modaks and music

Ganesh is said to love modaks, sweet dumplings filled with coconut and jaggery. In Maharashtra they are traditionally steamed in rice-flour shells shaped like little pouches. Families often offer 21 of them.

Every evening, families sing aartis, songs of prayer, with clapping and bells. Out on the streets, groups of drummers playing big dhol-tasha drums practise for weeks and lead the processions.

The big idols

In Mumbai, some public idols are taller than a two-storey house. The most famous, the Lalbaugcha Raja in Lalbaug, has been set up by a local committee since 1934. Millions of people visit it during the festival, waiting in queues that can last many hours.

Ganesh also has an old link with writing. According to one legend, he wrote down the Mahabharata as the sage Vyasa recited it. That is why he is sometimes shown holding a broken tusk as a pen.

Goodbye, and come back soon

On Anant Chaturdashi, the last day, huge processions carry the idols to the sea at places like Girgaon Chowpatty in Mumbai. People chant "Ganpati Bappa Morya, pudhchya varshi lavkar ya", which means "Lord Ganpati, come back soon next year."

Immersion has caused problems. Idols made of plaster of Paris and painted with chemical colours do not dissolve easily and harm fish and water. Many families now choose clay idols or immerse them at home in a bucket, then use the clay in the garden. Cities also build artificial ponds, so the water can be cleaned afterwards. Similar changes have reached Kolkata's Durga Puja too.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ganesh Chaturthi has always been a giant street festival.

Truth: For centuries it was mainly celebrated at home and in temples. The large public pandals began in the 1890s, when Bal Gangadhar Tilak and others promoted community celebrations in Maharashtra.
