What was the first photo of Earth from space?

The first photo of Earth from space was taken in 1946 by a camera on a captured rocket, and it survived a crash into the desert.

The short answer

On 24 October 1946, a rocket blasted off from White Sands in the New Mexico desert in the USA. It was a V-2, a German war rocket captured at the end of the Second World War. Tucked inside was a 35 mm movie camera, set to snap a frame every second and a half.

The rocket climbed to about 105 km, roughly twelve times higher than Mount Everest. Then it fell back and smashed into the sand. The camera broke, but the film was safe inside a steel case. When the scientists developed it, they saw grainy black-and-white pictures of the curved Earth under a black sky. Nobody had ever seen that before. Each frame covered a patch of the American Southwest bigger than many Indian states, all in one glance.

Surprising but true

The first photos of Earth from space were taken eleven years before the first satellite, Sputnik, ever reached orbit.

Jumping up and down

The pictures were blurry, and they showed only clouds and desert. But people who were there later remembered the scientists jumping up and down like children when they saw them. Newspapers and magazines printed them as the view a space traveller would see.

Over the next few years, more rocket cameras flew. In 1954, a small rocket called an Aerobee caught a huge storm swirling over Texas. Weather forecasters were stunned. From the ground, they had seen only bits of the storm. From above, it was one giant spiral. That idea would later lead to weather satellites, which now watch every cyclone on Earth.

Farther and farther away

In 1959, the American satellite Explorer 6 sent back the first crude picture of Earth from orbit. In 1966, a spacecraft circling the Moon called Lunar Orbiter 1 photographed Earth hanging over the Moon's grey edge.

The most famous version came on 24 December 1968. Three astronauts on Apollo 8 were the first people ever to circle the Moon. As they came round its far side, Earth rose over the horizon, blue and white against black. Astronaut Bill Anders grabbed a camera loaded with colour film. His photo, called Earthrise, is one of the most printed pictures in history. Many people say it helped start the modern environmental movement. The first Earth Day was held just over a year later, in April 1970.

How was photography invented?The oldest surviving photograph from a camera needed hours, perhaps days, of sunlight to appear. It shows a blurry view of rooftops from a window in France.

The Blue Marble

On 7 December 1972, the crew of Apollo 17 took a photo of the whole Earth, fully lit, from about 29,000 km away. The Sun was behind them, so there were no shadows. You can see Africa, the Arabian peninsula and the swirling clouds over Antarctica. It became known as the Blue Marble.

For decades it was the best-known photo of the whole round Earth taken by a person's own hands. Most later full-Earth images come from robot spacecraft, and many are stitched together from smaller pictures.

India has taken its own. In November 2013, a camera on ISRO's Mangalyaan, heading for Mars, snapped Earth with the Indian subcontinent clearly visible.

A dot in a sunbeam

On 14 February 1990, the spacecraft Voyager 1 turned its camera back towards home from about 6 billion km away. Earth showed up as a tiny speck, smaller than a single pixel, caught in a streak of scattered sunlight. The astronomer Carl Sagan called it the Pale Blue Dot.

So the story goes from a grainy desert snapshot to a dot almost too small to see. A photo taken from a Mumbai rooftop can show more detail of a cricket match than the Voyager picture shows of the whole planet. And Voyager is still out there, now more than 25 billion km away (2026), carrying a golden record of Earth's sounds in case anyone ever finds it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The first photo of Earth from space was taken by an astronaut.

Truth: The first ones were taken in 1946 by an automatic camera on an uncrewed rocket. No human went to space until Yuri Gagarin in 1961.

Try this

Look at a small object, like a coin, from your nose, then from across the room, then from the far end of a corridor. Notice the point where you stop seeing any detail. That is roughly what happened to Earth in photos as spacecraft flew farther and farther away.
