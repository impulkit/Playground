Why does a glacier in Antarctica bleed red?

On a white glacier in Antarctica, a stream the colour of blood oozes out of the ice, and for decades nobody knew why.

The short answer

Blood Falls is a reddish-orange stain flowing from the end of Taylor Glacier in Antarctica. The water starts out clear, deep under the ice. It is extremely salty and full of dissolved iron. When it seeps out and meets the air, the iron reacts with oxygen and turns red, the same chemistry that makes rust.

The water comes from an ancient pool of salty water sealed under the glacier, probably for more than a million years. Even stranger, tiny microbes live in it, in total darkness and with almost no oxygen. Scientists study them for clues to how life might survive on other worlds.

Surprising but true

The water at Blood Falls is so salty that it stays liquid at about −7 °C, and microbes live in it without sunlight or oxygen.

A strange sight in 1911

In 1911, the Australian geologist Griffith Taylor was exploring the area with Robert Falcon Scott's British expedition. He found a glacier with a red, frozen waterfall at its end. The glacier was later named after him.

Taylor and others guessed the colour came from red algae, tiny plant-like organisms that sometimes tint snow pink. That guess was wrong, but it took decades of research to prove it. Scientists didn't fully map the source of the red water until the 21st century.

Iron, salt and air

Long ago, when sea levels were higher, seawater flooded this valley. Later, the glacier advanced over it and trapped a pool of that salty water underneath. Over time, some water froze, which left what remained even saltier.

The brine also picked up iron from the bedrock beneath. With no air under the ice, the iron stayed dissolved and the water stayed clear. Salt lowers the freezing point of water, so the brine stays liquid at about −7 °C. When it squeezes out through cracks at the glacier's front, it meets oxygen for the first time. The iron oxidises, and within moments the water starts turning rusty red.

In 2023, scientists at Johns Hopkins University looked at the red solids under powerful microscopes. They found tiny round grains rich in iron, far smaller than a red blood cell. These nanospheres, not regular crystals, give the falls their colour.

Life in the dark

In 2009, scientists reported that the brine holds a community of microbes. There is no sunlight and almost no oxygen down there. Instead, the microbes get their energy from chemical reactions using iron and sulphur compounds in the water.

They have probably lived cut off from the world for a very long time. Finding them showed that life can survive in places once thought far too harsh.

Clues for other worlds

That makes Blood Falls exciting to space scientists. Jupiter's moon Europa and Saturn's moon Enceladus have oceans of salty water under thick ice. Mars has salts and ice too. If microbes can live under an Antarctic glacier, perhaps something similar could survive there.

The tiny iron grains matter as well. A Mars rover looking for minerals might miss these nanospheres, because they are not regular crystals. Scientists think future missions need tools to look for them.

One of the driest places on Earth

The McMurdo Dry Valleys, where Blood Falls is found, are among the driest places on Earth. Fierce, dry winds sweep away snow, so much of the ground is bare rock and gravel. NASA has tested equipment there because it is a little like Mars. Most visitors are scientists, flown in by helicopter from research stations.

In 2015, scientists flew radar over the glacier and found a network of salty water beneath it, feeding the falls. And the strangest part? Elsewhere on Earth, salt-loving microbes do something just as colourful, turning whole lakes bright pink, as the story of pink lakes shows.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The red at Blood Falls comes from blood or from red rocks.

Truth: It comes from iron dissolved in very salty water trapped under the glacier. The water is clear when it is underground. It turns red only when it reaches the air and the iron reacts with oxygen, like rust.

Try this

Put a piece of steel wool or an old iron nail in a saucer with a little salty water, and another in fresh water. Leave them for a few days, checking each day. The one in salty water usually rusts faster and turns orange-red, the same chemistry that colours Blood Falls.
