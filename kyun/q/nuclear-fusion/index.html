What is nuclear fusion?

Every second, the Sun squeezes about 600 million tonnes of hydrogen into helium. Scientists are trying to build a tiny Sun on Earth to do the same.

The short answer

Nuclear fusion is when two very light atoms crash together so hard that their centres, called nuclei, join into one heavier atom. A little bit of mass goes missing in the process, and it turns into a huge amount of energy. This is what Einstein's famous equation, E = mc², describes.

Fusion powers the Sun and every other star. In the Sun's core, hydrogen atoms fuse into helium, giving off the light and heat that reach us eight minutes later. Scientists have spent about 70 years trying to make fusion work on Earth as a clean source of electricity. It is one of the hardest engineering problems ever tackled.

Surprising but true

To make fusion work on Earth, the fuel must be heated to over 100 million °C, several times hotter than the centre of the Sun.

Why it's so hard

All atomic nuclei carry positive electric charge, and like charges push each other away. To make two nuclei fuse, you have to slam them together fast enough to overcome that push.

The Sun manages this with crushing gravity and a core temperature of about 15 million °C. On Earth we can't copy the Sun's weight, so we make up for it with heat. Fusion machines must reach over 100 million °C. At that temperature, atoms break apart into a glowing soup of charged particles called plasma. No container could touch it without melting, and the plasma would cool and stop fusing the moment it touched a wall. The fuel is usually two heavier kinds of hydrogen, called deuterium and tritium, which fuse more easily than ordinary hydrogen.

Holding a star in a magnetic bottle

The most common answer is a machine called a tokamak, first designed in the Soviet Union in the 1950s. It is shaped like a doughnut. Powerful magnets wrapped around it create invisible fields that hold the plasma in a ring, floating away from the walls.

The biggest tokamak ever is being built in southern France. Called ITER, Latin for "the way", it is a joint project of more than 30 countries, including India, the United States, the European Union, China, Japan, Russia and South Korea. India's share includes the cryostat, a giant steel vessel that surrounds the machine, built by the company Larsen & Toubro. India also runs its own smaller tokamaks at the Institute for Plasma Research in Gandhinagar, Gujarat.

A breakthrough with lasers

There is another approach. At the National Ignition Facility in California, 192 giant lasers fire at a fuel pellet smaller than a peppercorn, crushing and heating it in a billionth of a second.

In an experiment on 5 December 2022, for the first time, the fusion reaction there released more energy than the lasers delivered to the fuel. It was a historic moment. But the lasers themselves needed far more electricity from the grid than the reaction produced, so there is still a long road to a working power station.

The prize

Fusion fuel can come from seawater and from lithium, so supplies would last a very long time. Fusion makes no carbon dioxide while running, so it would not add to climate change. It also can't run out of control the way some people fear, because if anything goes wrong, the plasma simply cools and stops.

Scientists like to joke that fusion power has been "30 years away" for decades. Many private companies now say they will do it sooner. Others are more cautious. If it works, fusion could one day give the world electricity from the same process that lights the stars.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Nuclear power stations today run on fusion.

Truth: All working nuclear power stations use fission, which splits heavy atoms. Fusion is still in the experimental stage. Scientists hope it could one day give power with far less long-lasting radioactive waste.

Try this

Show why fusion is hard with two magnets. Push the same poles of two magnets toward each other and feel them shove apart. Atomic nuclei repel each other in a similar way, because they all carry positive charge. Only enormous heat and pressure can force them close enough to join.
