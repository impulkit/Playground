How did cheap books change Europe?

Before the 1450s, a single book in Europe could take a scribe months to copy. Fifty years after Gutenberg, millions of printed books were in circulation.

The short answer

Around 1450, in the German city of Mainz, a goldsmith named Johannes Gutenberg developed a new way to print books. He used movable metal letters, oil-based ink and a press adapted from those used for squeezing grapes. A team could now produce hundreds of identical pages a day.

Books became far cheaper and more common. Printing spread to hundreds of European towns within about fifty years. Ideas could travel faster and further than ever. Printed pamphlets helped spread new religious ideas, scientists could share exact drawings and data, and more people had reasons to learn to read. Historians call this the printing revolution.

Surprising but true

The oldest surviving printed book with a date is not European at all. It is the Diamond Sutra, printed in China in 868 CE and now kept in the British Library.

Before the press

In medieval Europe, books were copied by hand, often by monks working in monasteries. Copying a long book like the Bible could take a scribe many months, and each copy might contain new mistakes. Books were rare and expensive. A large library might hold a few hundred volumes.

Printing itself was not new. In ancient China, printers carved whole pages onto wooden blocks. The Diamond Sutra, a Buddhist text printed in 868 CE, is the oldest surviving dated printed book. Around the 1040s, a Chinese craftsman named Bi Sheng made movable type from baked clay. In Korea, a Buddhist book called Jikji was printed with movable metal type in 1377, decades before Gutenberg.

Gutenberg's system

Gutenberg's breakthrough was putting several ideas together. He invented a hand mould that could quickly cast many identical metal letters. He mixed an ink that stuck well to metal. He used a screw press to push paper evenly onto the inked type.

His most famous book, the Gutenberg Bible, was finished around 1455. About 180 copies were printed, some on paper and some on vellum made from calfskin. Gutenberg himself did not grow rich. He lost a lawsuit to his business partner and lost much of his workshop.

Ideas on the move

Printing spread fast. By 1500, presses were working in more than 200 European towns, and historians estimate that millions of books had been printed. Venice became a major printing centre.

In 1517, a German monk named Martin Luther wrote objections to practices of the Catholic Church. Printers quickly turned his writings into cheap pamphlets, which spread across Germany within weeks. This helped start the Protestant Reformation, which split Western Christianity. Scientists benefited too. In 1543, printed books by Copernicus and Vesalius shared new ideas about the planets and the human body, with detailed drawings that every reader saw in exactly the same form.

Printing in India and America

The first printing press in India arrived in Goa in 1556, brought by Jesuit missionaries. In 1780, James Augustus Hicky started Hicky's Bengal Gazette in Calcutta, the first newspaper printed in India. From 1800, the Serampore Mission Press near Calcutta printed books in many Indian languages, including Bengali, Marathi, Odia and Sanskrit.

In British America, the first press began working in Cambridge, Massachusetts, around 1639. Benjamin Franklin started out as a printer's apprentice and later ran his own newspaper and almanac.

Good and bad

Printing made it easier to share knowledge, standardise spelling and build libraries. It also made it easier to spread rumours, insults and false stories, and authorities in many countries tried to control what could be printed.

Some historians, such as Elizabeth Eisenstein, argue that printing changed European thinking deeply. Others think she gave it too much credit, since many forces were at work. Most agree that a book which once took months to copy could now be made in days.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Gutenberg invented printing.

Truth: Printing with carved wooden blocks existed in China centuries earlier, and movable type was made in China and Korea before Gutenberg. His achievement was a fast system of metal type, oil-based ink and a press, which made printing cheap enough to spread across Europe.
