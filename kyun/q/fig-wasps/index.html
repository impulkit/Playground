Why does every fig need a tiny wasp?

A fig is a flower turned inside out. Only one creature can get in.

The short answer

Each kind of fig tree has its own kind of tiny wasp that pollinates it.

Without the wasps, no fig seeds. Without figs, no wasp babies. They need each other.

This partnership is tens of millions of years old. One major study of fig and wasp family trees put it at about 75 million years.

Surprising but true

Banyans and peepal trees are figs, and each depends on its own tiny wasp.

A flower inside out

A fig looks like a fruit, but it starts as something stranger. It is a hollow ball lined on the inside with hundreds of tiny flowers. They never see sunlight and no bee can reach them. The only way in is a narrow hole at the bottom, sealed by tight, overlapping scales.

That hole is the fig wasp's doorway. Each wasp species is built to fit the figs of its own tree species, and in most cases, no other insect can do the job.

A one-way trip

A female fig wasp, smaller than a grain of rice, hatches inside a ripe fig. Before she leaves, she collects pollen from the fig's male flowers. Then she flies off, following the scent of a tree of her species whose figs are ready.

She forces her way through the scales of a young fig, tearing off her wings. Inside, she lays her eggs in some of the flowers and brushes pollen onto others. The flowers with eggs feed her young as they grow. The flowers with pollen make seeds. Then she dies.

Weeks later, her young hatch. The wingless males hatch first, find the females, mate with them, and chew a tunnel to the outside so the females can escape. The males never leave the fig. The females fly off carrying pollen, and the cycle starts again.

Two partners, one story

Figs and their wasps have been evolving together for so long that their family trees match closely. When a new kind of fig appeared, a new kind of wasp often appeared with it. Scientists call this coevolution, when two species shape each other over many generations, a special case of evolution.

The partnership isn't always perfectly neat. Some figs have two or more wasp species, and some wasps visit more than one kind of fig. There are also cheater wasps that lay eggs in figs without carrying pollen. Even so, it is one of the closest partnerships in nature.

Food for the forest

Different fig trees in a forest fruit at different times, so there are almost always ripe figs somewhere. In dry seasons, when other fruit is scarce, figs keep many animals alive. Hornbills, barbets, bulbuls, fruit bats, monkeys and squirrels all feast on them. That makes figs a lifeline for whole forests, from the Western Ghats to the Amazon.

India has many native figs, including the banyan, the peepal and the cluster fig, or gular, whose figs grow in bunches straight from the trunk. The peepal is sacred in Hinduism, Buddhism and Jainism. The Buddha is said to have reached enlightenment beneath one at Bodh Gaya.

Tiny partners everywhere

Fig wasps aren't the only tiny partners plants depend on. Cacao trees need midges, and many crops need bees. If the fig wasps of one tree species disappeared, that fig could no longer make seeds, and the animals that eat its fruit would feel it too.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Every fig you eat is full of dead wasps.

Truth: Many figs sold in shops come from varieties that ripen without wasps at all. In wild figs that are pollinated, the wasp that dies inside is broken down by the fig's own enzymes, so there is nothing crunchy left of her.

Try this

Look inside a fig. Ask an adult to cut a fresh or dried fig in half. Use a magnifying glass to look at the hundreds of tiny strands and seeds inside. Each one came from a tiny flower. Find the little hole at the bottom of the fig, the only doorway a fig wasp can use.
