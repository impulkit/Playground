How do trains stay on the tracks?

Train wheels aren't flat. They are slightly cone-shaped, and that small slope steers a train around every curve.

The short answer

Look closely at a train wheel and you'll see that its rolling surface isn't flat like a car tyre. It slopes slightly, so the wheel is wider on the inside, near the middle of the train, and narrower on the outside. Two such wheels are fixed firmly to one axle and turn together.

If the train drifts to the left, the left wheel rolls on its wider part and the right wheel on its narrower part. The wider wheel covers more ground in each turn, so the axle steers back to the right. Drift right, and the opposite happens. The wheels keep nudging themselves back to the centre.

Each wheel also has a lip, called a flange, on its inside edge. It catches the rail if something goes badly wrong. On a good track, though, the cone shape does nearly all the work.

Surprising but true

The rim on the inside of a train wheel, called the flange, is only a backup. On a well-built track it rarely touches the rail at all.

Rolling round the bend

On a curve, the outer rail is longer than the inner one. A car solves this with a clever set of gears that lets its wheels spin at different speeds. A train's wheels can't, because they are joined by a solid axle.

The cone shape solves it. As the train enters a curve, it shifts slightly outwards. Now the outer wheel rolls on its wider part and the inner wheel on its narrower part. In one turn of the axle, the outer wheel travels a little further, exactly as needed. The wheels correct themselves so eagerly that, at high speed, they can overshoot and make the carriage sway gently from side to side. Engineers call this hunting, and they design springs and dampers to calm it.

Engineers also tilt the track on curves, with the outer rail slightly higher, so trains lean into the bend like a cyclist.

Steel on steel

Train wheels and rails are both made of hard steel, and they touch over an area about the size of a small coin. There is very little rolling friction, so a train needs far less energy to move a tonne of cargo than a truck does. One locomotive can pull a goods train more than a kilometre long.

Low friction has a downside. Steel wheels can slip on wet rails or fallen leaves, and trains take a long time to stop. That's why railway signals warn drivers far ahead, and why level crossings have gates. A heavy goods train moving at full speed can need a kilometre or more to come to a stop.

How did the steam engine change the world?The first useful steam engines were built to pump water out of flooded coal mines. Within a century they were pulling trains across India.

Rails and gauges

The gap between the two rails is called the gauge. Much of the world, including the USA and most of Europe, uses standard gauge, 1,435 millimetres. Most Indian railway lines use a wider broad gauge, 1,676 millimetres, which allows bigger, steadier carriages.

India's first passenger train ran on 16 April 1853, from Bori Bunder in Bombay to Thane, about 34 kilometres. It had 14 carriages pulled by three steam locomotives. Today Indian Railways carries more than 2 crore passengers a day.

Mountains, toys and the circle trick

Not every train runs on a wide track. The Darjeeling Himalayan Railway, nicknamed the Toy Train, runs on rails just 610 millimetres apart. It climbs steep hills using loops and zigzags, and it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

The cone-wheel trick works everywhere, from toy trains to giant freight trains. It comes down to a simple fact about circles: a bigger circle covers more ground in one turn than a smaller one. In Mumbai, the local trains use the same idea thousands of times a day, round every curve between Churchgate and Virar.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The lips on train wheels are what keep trains on the track.

Truth: The flanges are a safety backup. The cone shape of the wheels does almost all the steering, keeping the train centred and guiding it round curves without the flanges grinding against the rails.

Try this

Tape the wide rims of two paper cups together, so they make a shape fatter in the middle. Lay two metre rulers or straight sticks side by side as rails, slightly apart, and roll your cups along them. They stay on, even round a gentle bend. Now tape two cups together by their bases, narrow ends in the middle, and try again. They fall off.
