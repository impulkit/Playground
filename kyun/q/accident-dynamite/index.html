How did Nobel invent dynamite?

The explosive that blasted railway tunnels through the Alps was made safe with a crumbly powder of fossil algae, and its inventor's fortune now pays for the Nobel Prizes.

The short answer

In the 1860s, the strongest explosive known was nitroglycerine, an oily liquid so jumpy that a sharp knock could set it off. Alfred Nobel, a Swedish chemist and businessman, found a way to tame it. He soaked the liquid into kieselguhr, a light, crumbly earth, and packed the paste into paper tubes. In 1867, he patented it as dynamite, from the Greek word for power.

Dynamite was much safer to carry and handle, and it only exploded when set off by a small detonator, which Nobel had also invented. It made him enormously rich, and he left most of his fortune to fund the Nobel Prizes.

Surprising but true

Nitroglycerine, the dangerous liquid inside early dynamite, is also a heart medicine. Doctors prescribed it to Alfred Nobel himself late in his life.

A liquid that scared its inventor

Nitroglycerine was first made in 1847 by an Italian chemist, Ascanio Sobrero. He was badly hurt by an explosion and was so worried about its dangers that he warned people against using it. A little nitroglycerine contains enough energy to shatter rock, but it could also blow up from heat, shaking or even a drop on the floor.

Nobel saw its power for mining and building. Back then, miners still broke rock with gunpowder, picks and muscle, and a single tunnel could take decades. His family had a factory, and he set out to make the liquid useful.

A terrible accident

In 1864, an explosion at the Nobel family's workshop in Stockholm killed five people, including Alfred's younger brother Emil. The city banned nitroglycerine experiments within its limits. Nobel moved his work to a barge on a lake outside the city.

Elsewhere, factories and ships carrying nitroglycerine blew up. Some countries banned it altogether, and many shipping companies refused to carry it. Nobel badly needed a safer form.

Fossil algae to the rescue

Nobel tried mixing nitroglycerine with many materials, including sawdust, paper and charcoal. The best was kieselguhr, a powder dug from the ground in Germany. It is made of the glassy shells of diatoms, single-celled algae too small to see. Each shell is full of tiny holes, so the powder soaks up nearly three times its own weight of liquid and still feels dry.

The result could be rolled into sticks, dropped and even burned without exploding. It needed a blasting cap, a small charge set off by a fuse, to detonate.

Blasting through mountains

Dynamite changed building almost overnight. Miners used it to reach deeper ores. Railway builders used it to blast tunnels, like the 15 km Gotthard tunnel through the Swiss Alps, finished in 1882. It cut paths for roads, harbours and the Panama Canal.

It also went to war, and that troubled many people. Nobel made other explosives too, and owned factories in many countries. He held more than 350 patents, from synthetic rubber to artificial silk.

From explosives to prizes

A famous story says that in 1888, when Nobel's brother Ludvig died, a French newspaper wrongly printed Alfred's obituary and called him a "merchant of death". Historians are not sure the article ever existed in that form, but the story is often used to explain what Nobel did next.

When he died in 1896, his will left most of his fortune to fund yearly prizes for physics, chemistry, medicine, literature and peace. The first were awarded in 1901. Since then, winners have included Marie Curie, twice, and Indian winners from Rabindranath Tagore to C. V. Raman. Peacemakers receive the Nobel Peace Prize in Oslo.

The oddest twist is medical. Tiny doses of nitroglycerine widen blood vessels, and it is still used to ease chest pain from a struggling heart. Late in life, Nobel's own doctors prescribed it to him.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Nobel discovered dynamite by accident, when nitroglycerine leaked into packing material.

Truth: The story is popular but probably not true. Nobel said he found the method through careful experiments, testing different materials until kieselguhr worked best.

Try this

Set out a spoonful each of sand, flour and a piece of sponge on a plate. Add a few drops of water to each and watch which soaks it up best and holds it without dripping. Kieselguhr worked the same way, holding liquid tightly inside millions of tiny holes.
