Who made the Nazca lines, and why?

In a Peruvian desert, a hummingbird as long as a football field has been drawn on the ground for about 2,000 years.

The short answer

The Nazca lines are giant drawings scratched into the desert of southern Peru. There are straight lines running for kilometres, triangles and spirals, and pictures of animals: a hummingbird, a monkey, a spider, a whale. They were made by the Nazca people, who lived there roughly 2,000 years ago, between about 500 BCE and 500 CE.

We know how they made them. They moved the dark stones on the surface to show the paler ground underneath. Why they made them is less certain. Most archaeologists think the lines were part of religious ceremonies, perhaps walked along like sacred paths, and many seem linked to water, which was precious in the desert.

Surprising but true

In 2024 a team using artificial intelligence found 303 new figure drawings in just six months, almost doubling the number known.

Drawing by moving stones

The Nazca desert floor is covered with small stones, stained reddish-brown by iron over time. Just below is lighter, yellowish soil. Rake the stones aside into neat edges and a pale line appears.

The desert is one of the driest places on Earth, gets almost no rain and has little wind at ground level. So the lines have barely changed for centuries. The hummingbird is about 93 metres long, nearly the length of a football field. The monkey has a curly tail and nine fingers.

Seen from the sky

Local people always knew about the lines, but their full size became clear when planes began flying over the region in the 1920s and 1930s. A German mathematician named Maria Reiche spent about 50 years mapping and protecting them, sweeping them clean and measuring them by hand, often from a ladder.

UNESCO named the lines a World Heritage Site in 1994. They're fragile: in 2018, a lorry driver drove across part of the area and left deep tyre tracks.

Why make them?

Early researchers thought the lines might be a giant calendar pointing to the stars. When scientists checked, only a few lines lined up with the Sun or stars, no more than chance would give.

Today, most experts favour ideas tied to ritual. Some lines appear to have been walked many times, worn down by feet. Others point towards water sources or mountains, where rain came from. Broken pots found at the ends of some lines suggest offerings were left there. The Nazca relied on underground channels for water, and rain was a matter of life and death.

Who were the Nazca?

The Nazca people lived in the dry river valleys of southern Peru many centuries before the Inca. They were skilled farmers. The spiralling wells and underground channels they built to reach water, called puquios, still supply some farms today.

They were superb artists too. Nazca potters painted bowls and jars in many colours, with the same hummingbirds, monkeys, killer whales and fierce mythical beings that appear on the desert floor. Their weavers made fine cloth. The Nazca culture faded around 500 to 600 CE, perhaps partly because of floods, droughts and the cutting of trees that had held the valley soil together.

New drawings keep appearing

Archaeologists keep finding more. Many smaller figures, drawn along footpaths, are faint and easy to miss. In 2024, a team from Yamagata University in Japan trained artificial intelligence on photos of known figures, then let it search aerial images. With the help of drones and fieldwork, they confirmed 303 new figures in about six months, including people, cats and llamas.

Archaeology doesn't need aliens to explain the lines. In 1982 a small team recreated a big Nazca bird in a few days, using just stakes and string. And the strangest part? The dry desert that saved these drawings isn't even the driest in South America. That record belongs to Chile's Atacama, next door, one of the driest places on Earth.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Nazca lines can only be seen from the air, so ancient people couldn't have made them without help from aliens.

Truth: Many figures can be seen from the surrounding hills. In 1982 a small team recreated a large Nazca bird in a few days using only wooden stakes and string. The Nazca people had everything they needed.

Try this

Make a giant drawing like the Nazca people. On a beach, a sandy patch or a playground with chalk, sketch a small animal on paper first, then divide it into a grid. Scale each square up using string and sticks. Then look at it from a window or high step, and see if it's still recognisable.
