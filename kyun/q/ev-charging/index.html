How long does it take to charge an electric car?

The same electric car can take 12 hours to charge from a home plug, or about half an hour at a fast charger. The difference is all in the numbers.

The short answer

It depends on two numbers: how much energy the battery holds, and how much power the charger delivers. Battery size is measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh). Charger power is measured in kilowatts (kW). Divide one by the other and you get the time.

Take a small electric car with a 40 kWh battery, a common size for Indian city cars. From an ordinary home socket giving about 3 kW, it takes roughly 13 hours to charge from empty. A 7 kW wall box does it in about 6 hours. A highway fast charger can take it from 10% to 80% in around half an hour.

Surprising but true

A fast charger at 150 kilowatts pours in as much power as about 7,500 ordinary 20-watt phone chargers running at once.

The simple maths

Time = energy ÷ power. That's it. A 60 kWh battery on a 7 kW wall box takes about 60 ÷ 7, or 8 to 9 hours. On a 150 kW fast charger it would, in theory, take 60 ÷ 150, or 24 minutes.

In real life, add a bit. Some energy turns to heat in the cables and the battery, perhaps 10% at home. And a battery can't take full power all the way. It works a bit like filling a bucket at a tap: easy at first, then you slow down near the brim so it doesn't spill. The idea is the same as working out speed, distance and time, just with energy instead of kilometres.

Why fast charging slows down

A lithium-ion battery can swallow energy quickly when it's fairly empty. As it fills, pushing more in creates heat and stress, which would wear the battery out sooner. So the car's computer slows things down, often sharply after about 80%.

That's why charging times are usually quoted from 10% to 80%. The last 20% can take almost as long as the first 70%. On a long drive, it's often quicker to stop twice for a short top-up than once for a full charge. The same chemistry is at work in a phone battery, which also slows down near full.

Plugs, cables and power

Home charging uses AC, the same kind of electricity that comes out of the wall. A box inside the car converts it into DC, the kind a battery stores. That converter is small, which limits how fast home charging can go.

Fast chargers do the converting inside the big roadside machine and feed DC straight into the battery. They need thick, often cooled cables, and a supply strong enough to run several houses. In the United States, many car makers are moving to the same plug that Tesla designed. India uses a mix of standards, which is why charging stations often have more than one type of plug.

Two wheels and three

In India, most electric vehicles aren't cars at all. They are scooters, motorbikes and e-rickshaws. A scooter battery is small, often 2 to 4 kWh, so it can charge from a normal socket in a few hours.

Some companies skip charging altogether. At a battery-swapping station, a rider pulls out an empty battery and slots in a full one in a couple of minutes, much like buying a new gas cylinder. This works well for delivery riders and autorickshaw drivers, who can't afford to wait.

Cleaner, if the power is clean

An electric car makes no exhaust, but its electricity has to come from somewhere. If that's coal, some pollution has simply moved to a power station. As countries add more solar and wind power, charging gets cleaner. Many drivers now charge at midday when solar power is cheap, or overnight when demand is low, letting the car's battery soak up spare electricity that would otherwise go to waste.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Electric car owners have to wait for hours at a charging station every time they need power.

Truth: In many countries, most charging happens at home or at work, while the car is parked anyway. Fast chargers on highways are used on long trips, and a stop of 20 to 40 minutes is usually enough to carry on.

Try this

Look at a phone charger and find the small print saying its output, such as "5V 2A" (multiply them to get watts, here 10 W). Ask an adult what your phone battery holds, often around 15 to 20 watt-hours. Work out how long a full charge should take, then time a real one and compare.
