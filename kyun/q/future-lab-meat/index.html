What is lab-grown meat?

In 2013, people in London tasted a burger grown from cow cells in a lab. It cost about 2 crore rupees to make.

The short answer

Lab-grown meat, also called cultivated meat, is real meat grown from animal cells outside an animal's body. Scientists take a few cells from a cow, chicken or fish, then feed them in a warm tank so they multiply into billions. The result is muscle and fat, the same stuff as a chicken nugget, without raising and killing the bird.

It is not science fiction any more. Singapore approved the first sales in 2020, the USA followed in 2023, and a few other countries have since joined. But it is still very expensive and hard to find in shops.

Surprising but true

Not one cow was killed for the first lab-grown burger. All its meat came from a few cells taken from living cows with a small needle.

Growing meat in a tank

Every animal is built from cells. Muscle cells, given food, warmth and a safe place, can divide again and again. Scientists put them in a steel tank called a bioreactor, a bit like a giant pressure cooker that keeps everything clean and at body temperature.

The cells float in a broth of sugars, salts, amino acids and vitamins. In a few weeks, a small sample can multiply into a large mass. For minced meat, that mass is harvested and shaped. For something like a steak, cells need a scaffold, a kind of edible sponge, so they grow in layers like real muscle. That part is much harder.

The 2-crore burger

In August 2013, Dutch scientist Mark Post cooked the first lab-grown beef burger on live television in London. It had taken years of work and about 250,000 euros, then roughly 2 crore rupees. Two food critics tasted it. They said it was a bit dry but clearly meat.

Prices have dropped hugely since then, but lab-grown meat still costs far more than meat from a farm. In 2020 a restaurant in Singapore served the first lab-grown chicken bites that customers could buy. In 2023, two US companies were allowed to sell lab-grown chicken. Israel approved lab-grown beef in 2024, and Australia approved a lab-grown quail meat in 2025.

Why people want it

Supporters point to the numbers. About 80 billion land animals are killed for food every year worldwide. Cows release methane, a gas that warms the planet, and farming uses huge areas of land and water. Growing only the meat, without the rest of the animal, could need far less land and spare animals' lives.

That last point has interested some people in India, where many families avoid meat for religious or moral reasons. Some ask whether lab-grown meat would count as vegetarian. Most say no, since it is still animal flesh. India had not approved any lab-grown meat for sale as of 2025, though some Indian startups are working on it.

Why others are worried

Critics say the climate benefits are not proven. Big bioreactors use a lot of electricity, and if that power comes from coal, the meat might not be greener at all. Early methods also used a liquid taken from unborn calves to feed the cells. Most companies now say they have replaced it.

Some farmers fear for their jobs. In the USA, Florida and Alabama banned the sale of lab-grown meat in 2024. By mid-2025, at least seven states had banned or paused it. Supporters call the bans unfair. Opponents say people deserve time to see if it is safe.

The idea of growing food with tiny living things is not new, though. Humans have used microbes to make curd, bread and dosa batter for thousands of years, through fermentation. Lab meat may still find its place on our plates beside dals and vegetarian curries. What it might share the menu with by 2050, from insects to seaweed, is the story of future food.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Lab-grown meat is fake meat made from plants.

Truth: Plant-based meat is made from soya, peas or wheat. Lab-grown meat is real animal meat, made of animal cells, only grown in a tank instead of inside an animal.

Try this

Fill a small glass with water and drop in a teaspoon of chia or sabja seeds. Watch them swell over an hour. It is not meat, but it shows how something tiny can soak up nutrients from a liquid and grow, which is the basic idea behind growing cells in a tank.
