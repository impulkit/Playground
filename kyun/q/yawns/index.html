Why are yawns catching?

Seeing a yawn, hearing one or even reading about one can set you off. Chances are you'll yawn before you finish this page.

The short answer

A yawn is a slow, deep breath in with your mouth wide open, a stretch of the jaw and face, then a shorter breath out. Everyone yawns, and so do most animals with a backbone. We yawn more when we're sleepy, bored or just waking up.

The strange part is that yawns spread. When you see someone yawn, there's a good chance you'll yawn too, often within a few seconds. Scientists call this contagious yawning. They aren't sure why it happens. The most popular ideas are that it's linked to how we tune in to other people, or that it helps a group of animals become alert or sleepy together.

Surprising but true

Pet dogs can catch yawns from their owners, and chimpanzees catch yawns from watching videos of other chimps yawning.

Not about oxygen

For a long time, people thought we yawn because our blood needs more oxygen. In the 1980s, an American psychologist named Robert Provine tested this. He had students breathe air with extra oxygen, and then air with extra carbon dioxide. Neither changed how often they yawned.

Another idea is that yawns cool the brain. A big gulp of air and a stretch of the jaw pull cooler blood up into the head. Some experiments support this. People yawned less often when holding a cold pack to their forehead, and animals with bigger brains tend to have longer yawns. Other scientists aren't convinced yet.

Caught from a friend

Contagious yawning seems to work best between people who are close. In studies, people caught yawns more often from family and friends than from strangers. Very young children rarely catch yawns, and most only start around the age of four or five, when they also get better at understanding other people's feelings.

That has led some scientists to link catching yawns with empathy, the ability to feel what someone else feels. Others point out that the link is weak, and that some people who are very caring hardly ever catch yawns. It may be more about attention: you have to notice the yawn to catch it. Even so, people can catch a yawn just from hearing one. Many catch it from words on a page, the way you might be doing now.

Dogs, chimps and budgies

Humans aren't the only ones who catch yawns. In 2008, researchers in London yawned in front of pet dogs, and most of the dogs yawned back. Chimpanzees catch yawns from each other and even from videos of other chimps yawning. Budgerigars, the small parrots people keep as pets, catch yawns from other budgies too.

For animals that live in groups, yawning together might keep everyone on the same schedule. When one member gets sleepy and yawns, the others follow, and the group settles down. When they wake up, a round of yawns may help everyone become alert at the same time.

Yawning before you're born

Babies yawn in the womb, long before they are born. Ultrasound scans show yawns from about three months into pregnancy. Newborns yawn a lot, but they don't catch yawns from their parents yet.

Yawns also come at odd moments. Athletes often yawn before a big race, and students before an exam, even when they aren't tired at all. Perhaps a yawn helps your body shift gear, from resting to ready or from ready to sleep. If you have yawned at least once while reading this, you have just taken part in the experiment.

Myth or fact?

Myth: We yawn because our bodies need more oxygen.

Truth: Experiments in which people breathed extra oxygen found no change in how often they yawned. Yawning seems to do something else, perhaps cooling the brain or helping you shift between sleepy and alert.

Try this

Sit opposite a family member and, without warning, do a big, slow yawn. Count how many seconds it takes before they yawn back, or whether they do at all. Try it on a pet dog too. Then try the same with someone you don't know as well and compare.
