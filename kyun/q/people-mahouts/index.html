Who are mahouts?

Some mahouts spend 40 years with the same elephant, an animal that can weigh as much as four cars and still answers to one quiet voice.

The short answer

A mahout is a person who rides, trains and cares for an elephant. In India, mahouts look after elephants that belong to temples, forest departments, festivals and tourist camps. The job is old: Indian texts thousands of years old already describe elephant keepers and the hooked stick, called an ankus, they carried.

A mahout and an elephant often stay together for years, sometimes decades. The elephant learns his voice, his smell and his habits. In many families the work passes from father to son, so a boy may grow up beside the elephant he will one day guide.

Surprising but true

Asian elephants were never fully domesticated. Even after thousands of years of working with people, a mahout's elephant is a tamed wild animal.

A day that starts with breakfast

Elephants are eating machines. An adult can munch through around 150 kg of grass, leaves and branches a day, about the weight of two grown men. Much of a mahout's morning goes on cutting fodder or leading the elephant to graze.

Then comes bath time. Mahouts walk their elephants into a river or pond, where the animal lies on its side while the mahout scrubs its thick skin with a coconut husk or a stone. Elephants have no sweat glands spread over their bodies the way we do, so water and mud help keep them cool.

A language of words and touches

Mahouts guide elephants with a few dozen spoken commands and touches. A press of the foot behind an ear can mean turn. A word can mean kneel, lift your foot or pick that up. Working elephants can learn to hand a fallen object back to their rider with their trunk.

Elephants are clever and remember people for years. Like all elephants in India, they have moods, friends and dislikes. A good mahout learns to read the flap of an ear or the swing of a tail, and to tell when his elephant would rather be left alone.

Mahouts of the forest

Some of the most important mahouts work for forest departments. Their trained elephants, called kumkis, help guide wild herds away from villages and fields. In Assam's flooded grasslands, forest staff ride elephants to patrol areas no jeep can reach.

At the Theppakadu elephant camp in Tamil Nadu's Mudumalai Tiger Reserve, a couple named Bomman and Bellie raised an orphaned baby elephant called Raghu. A film about them, The Elephant Whisperers, won an Oscar in 2023. It was the first Indian production to win that award for a documentary.

Festivals, temples and the circus

In Kerala, elephants carry golden headdresses and deities at temple festivals, most famously at Thrissur Pooram. Each elephant walks with its mahout close by, and the crowd of drums and fireworks tests both of them.

America had its own famous elephant keeper. In 1882 the giant elephant Jumbo was shipped from London Zoo to a travelling circus in the USA, and his keeper, Matthew Scott, went with him. Jumbo was so famous that his name became the everyday English word for anything enormous.

A job under question

The work is changing. Animal welfare groups worry about elephants kept in chains, made to stand on hot roads, or trained with harsh methods. Some mahouts are poorly paid and badly trained, and some elephants are overworked. In 2023 a temple in Kerala began using a life-sized mechanical elephant for its rituals instead of a real one.

Others argue that skilled mahouts are needed more than ever, to care for captive elephants and to help wild ones. Either way, an elephant's memory makes the mahout's job personal. Elephants also do something even stranger with sound, which explains how they talk across kilometres of forest.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Working elephants are domesticated animals, like cows or dogs.

Truth: Asian elephants have never been bred for many generations to change their nature the way cows or dogs have. Most captive elephants were caught from the wild or are only a few generations away from it, so mahouts are handling wild animals that have learned to trust them.

Try this

Mahouts guide elephants with a small set of spoken words and gentle touches. Make up five simple commands with a friend, such as sit, stand, turn and wait, using made-up words only. Take turns as the guide and see how quickly the other person learns them.
