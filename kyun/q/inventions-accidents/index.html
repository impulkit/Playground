Which great inventions happened by accident?

A messy lab, a melted chocolate bar and a glue that didn't stick properly all led to inventions now used by millions of people.

The short answer

Quite a few. Penicillin, the first widely used antibiotic, was found when mould landed on a dish of bacteria. X-rays were discovered when a screen glowed unexpectedly. The microwave oven, Post-it Notes, Teflon non-stick pans and superglue all began with mistakes or surprises.

But "accident" is only half the story. In every case, someone noticed that something strange had happened, wondered why, and then did years of careful work. The French scientist Louis Pasteur put it well in 1854: in the field of observation, chance favours only the prepared mind.

Surprising but true

Penicillin, one of the most important medicines ever found, was discovered because Alexander Fleming left a dish of bacteria uncovered while he went on holiday.

Mould on a dish

In September 1928, the Scottish scientist Alexander Fleming returned to his laboratory at St Mary's Hospital in London after a summer holiday. He had left dishes of bacteria stacked on his bench. On one, a fuzzy blue-green mould had grown. Around it, the bacteria had died.

Fleming realised the mould was making something that killed germs. He called it penicillin. It took another 12 years, and the work of Howard Florey, Ernst Chain and their team at Oxford, to turn it into a medicine. By the end of the Second World War, penicillin was saving countless soldiers from infected wounds. Fleming, Florey and Chain shared the Nobel Prize in 1945.

A glowing screen

In November 1895, the German physicist Wilhelm Röntgen was testing a glass tube that gave off electrical rays. He had covered it with black card, yet a chemical-coated screen across the dark room began to glow. Something invisible was passing through the card.

He soon found the rays went through paper, wood and even flesh, but not bone or metal. One of his first X-ray pictures showed the bones of his wife's hand, with her ring. Within months, doctors around the world were using X-rays to find broken bones and swallowed coins.

A melted chocolate bar

In 1945 an American engineer, Percy Spencer, was working near a magnetron, a device that makes radio waves for radar. He noticed that a chocolate bar in his pocket had melted. Curious, he tried popcorn kernels, which popped, and then an egg, which exploded.

Spencer's company built the first microwave oven in 1947. It was nearly as tall as a fridge and cost thousands of dollars. Smaller ovens for homes became common in the 1970s and 1980s.

Glue that wasn't strong enough

In 1968 Spencer Silver, a scientist at the company 3M, was trying to make a very strong glue. Instead, he made a weak one that stuck lightly and peeled off cleanly. Nobody knew what to do with it.

Years later, his colleague Art Fry was annoyed that his paper bookmarks kept falling out of his church hymn book. He used Silver's glue to make bookmarks that stayed put but didn't tear the pages. Post-it Notes went on sale in 1980.

Not quite accidents

Some inventions come from noticing nature. In 1941 the Swiss engineer George de Mestral went walking with his dog and found burrs stuck in its fur. Under a microscope, he saw tiny hooks catching on loops. That gave him the idea for Velcro, though it took him years to make it work.

A favourite story says an 11-year-old in California, Frank Epperson, left a fruit drink with a stirring stick outside on a cold night in 1905 and found it frozen solid. He later sold the idea as the Popsicle, a cousin of the ice cream on a stick. Like many inventors, he then protected it with a patent, granted in 1924.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Accidental inventions are just pure luck. Anyone could have made them.

Truth: Accidents happen to everyone, but only a trained, curious person notices when an accident is interesting. Fleming knew enough about bacteria to see that his mould mattered. As Louis Pasteur said, chance favours the prepared mind.

Try this

Keep a "surprise notebook" for a week. Whenever something doesn't go as expected, like a cake that sinks, a paper plane that loops or a plant that leans towards the window, write it down and guess why. Pick one surprise and test your guess.
