How did magicians like P.C. Sorcar amaze audiences?

In 1956, an Indian magician sawed a woman in half on British TV, and the programme ended before he could put her back together.

The short answer

P.C. Sorcar, the most famous Indian magician of the 1900s, amazed audiences with huge stage illusions, a sense of drama and clever publicity. He dressed like a maharaja in a jewelled turban and silk robes, with a whole team of assistants. His show was called Indrajal, after an old word for magic.

Like all great magicians, he relied on misdirection, careful practice and hidden machinery. He turned Indian magic, once mostly done on street corners, into a big theatre show that toured the world.

Surprising but true

The famous Indian rope trick, where a boy climbs a rope into the sky and vanishes, was largely popularised by an 1890 newspaper hoax in Chicago.

From Tangail to the world

Protul Chandra Sorcar was born in 1913 in Tangail, in Bengal, now part of Bangladesh. He studied mathematics but loved magic more. He practised for years and began performing in the 1930s.

At the time, Western audiences often saw Indian magic as small street tricks: snake charmers, mango trees that grew in minutes, cups and balls. Sorcar wanted something grander. He built a show with music, costumes, lights and big illusions, and took it to Europe, the USA, Japan and beyond. He won major magic awards in the USA and Germany, and in 1964 India gave him the Padma Shri.

The BBC broadcast

In 1956, Sorcar appeared on the BBC programme Panorama in London. He hypnotised his assistant, laid her on a table and sawed her in half with a buzzing circular saw. Then the presenter stepped in and ended the programme, as it had run out of time.

Viewers were left with the image of a woman cut in two. Many rang the BBC in a panic. The next day, newspapers carried the story, and Sorcar's shows in London sold out. Many people think the abrupt ending was planned. Either way, it was a lesson in how a cliffhanger can grab attention.

How illusions fool the brain

Most magic works by controlling attention. Your brain cannot watch everything at once. When a magician looks at one hand, you look there too, while the other hand does the secret work. Magicians call this misdirection, and scientists who study focus have learnt a lot from them.

Stage illusions add hidden tools. Mirrors, black cloth against black backgrounds, false bottoms and special boxes make things seem to vanish or float. In the sawing trick, the "one" woman is often more than one person, or her body is safely folded somewhere you cannot see. Sorcar's team kept his secrets carefully, and many are still unknown.

The rope trick that never was

The most famous "Indian" trick may never have been done at all. In the story, a fakir throws a rope into the air, where it stays stiff. A boy climbs up and disappears. In 1890, the Chicago Tribune printed a vivid report of the trick. Four months later, the paper admitted it was a hoax, but the story had already spread around the world.

Magicians searched India for decades and offered big prizes for a real performance. No one ever claimed the reward with the full trick. The story became a lesson in how a good tale can travel faster than the truth, much like fake news today.

Sorcar died in 1971 in Japan, just after a performance. His sons carried on. P.C. Sorcar Jr. became famous for illusions that seemed to make the Taj Mahal and a moving train vanish. Kolkata, his home city, has a street named after the elder Sorcar. Many of his tricks borrowed from the circus and theatre. And the reason his audiences never saw the secret move comes down to how easily the eye can be tricked, the science behind optical illusions.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Fakirs in old India really performed the rope trick, climbing a rope into the sky and vanishing.

Truth: Historians have found no reliable eyewitness account of the full trick. Its fame owes a lot to an 1890 Chicago newspaper story that was invented and later admitted to be a hoax.

Try this

Hold a coin in your right hand and pretend to pass it to your left, keeping it hidden in your right. Look at your closed left hand as if the coin is really there, then open it slowly. Practise in a mirror until your eyes, not your hands, sell the trick.
