Who built the Kailasa temple at Ellora?

Rashtrakuta workers carved a whole temple, two storeys high, out of a single hillside, by cutting away the rock until only the temple was left.

The short answer

The Kailasa temple at Ellora, near Aurangabad in Maharashtra, was made in the 8th century under the Rashtrakutas, a dynasty that ruled much of the Deccan from about 753 to 982 CE. It is usually credited to King Krishna I, who ruled from about 756 to 774.

The temple is dedicated to Shiva and named after Mount Kailash, his home in the Himalaya. It was cut from a single basalt hillside. Workers carved deep trenches on three sides of a huge block of rock, then shaped that block into a temple, complete with a tall shrine, halls, pillars, bridges and life-size elephants. Experts often estimate that around 200,000 tonnes of rock were removed.

Surprising but true

The Kailasa temple was carved from the top of the hill downwards, so the workers made the roof before the floor.

Who the Rashtrakutas were

The Rashtrakutas started as officials under the Chalukyas of Badami. Around 753, a chief named Dantidurga rebelled, defeated his overlords and set up his own kingdom. His uncle, Krishna I, took over soon after and expanded it.

At their height, the Rashtrakutas ruled from Gujarat to Tamil Nadu. Their later capital was Manyakheta, today's Malkhed in Karnataka. They fought constantly with two rivals, the Pratiharas of the north and the Palas of Bengal, over the rich city of Kannauj on the Ganga. None of the three ever won for good.

A temple carved backwards

Most buildings go up from the ground. Kailasa went down. Workers started at the top of the hill and cut their way towards the floor, so they finished the tower before the base.

The main shrine rises about 30 metres, as tall as a ten-storey building. Inside the rock, sculptors left pillars to hold up halls and carved stone bridges between the parts of the temple. Around the courtyard, the walls are covered with scenes from the Ramayana and the Mahabharata. A famous panel shows the demon king Ravana shaking Mount Kailash while Shiva calmly holds it steady with one toe.

There was no room for big mistakes. Every pillar and doorway had to be planned before the first chisel touched the rock.

Rock from ancient volcanoes

Ellora's rock is basalt, a dark stone formed from lava. About 66 million years ago, enormous volcanic eruptions poured layer after layer of lava over western India, creating the Deccan Traps. The cliffs at Ellora and Ajanta are made of these layers.

Basalt is hard but even, which made it good for detailed carving. Traces of plaster and paint show that the temple was once painted, possibly white, to look like a snow-covered Himalayan peak.

A king among kings

An Arab merchant named Sulaiman, who wrote in 851, listed the Rashtrakuta king as one of the four great kings of the world, alongside the rulers of Baghdad, China and Constantinople.

That king was probably Amoghavarsha I, who ruled for over 60 years. He was a patron of writers in Sanskrit and Kannada. The Kavirajamarga, one of the oldest surviving works on Kannada poetry, is linked to his court. He was also deeply interested in Jainism, and Jain caves at Ellora were carved in this period.

Three faiths on one cliff

Ellora has 34 main caves along a two-kilometre cliff. The Buddhist caves are the oldest, followed by Hindu caves, including Kailasa, and then Jain caves. Monks, priests and pilgrims of different faiths worked and worshipped side by side for centuries.

The Rashtrakutas lost power in 973, when the Western Chalukyas overthrew them. The last of the family to claim the throne died in 982. Their temple remained. A thousand years later, visitors still stand in its courtyard and look up at a building made by taking rock away.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Kailasa temple was built by stacking stone blocks.

Truth: It was carved out of one solid mass of rock. The workers cut trenches around a block of hillside, then hollowed and shaped it into halls, pillars, bridges and statues.
