Why were cardamom and pepper so valuable?

Peppercorns from Kerala were once worth so much that Romans complained about the gold flowing to India to pay for them.

The short answer

For most of history, the world's black pepper and green cardamom came mainly from one place: the hot, rainy hills and coast of south-west India. People everywhere else wanted them, and the only way to get them was by long, risky sea and land journeys.

Every trader along the way added a price. By the time a sack of pepper reached a market in Venice or London, it could cost many times what a farmer in Kerala had been paid. Pepper earned the nickname "black gold". Cardamom, with its sweet, cooling smell, became known as the "queen of spices", with pepper as the king. Rulers, sailors and companies fought to control where they came from.

Surprising but true

When the Egyptian pharaoh Ramesses II was mummified more than 3,200 years ago, peppercorns from India were placed in his nose.

Where they grow

Pepper is a climbing vine. In Kerala, farmers grow it up tall trees, and pick its small berries in clusters that look like green bunches of grapes. Dried in the sun on mats, the berries turn black and wrinkled.

Cardamom is a leafy plant of the ginger family that likes shade, cool mist and lots of rain. It grows wild in the forests of the Western Ghats, in a range still called the Cardamom Hills. Its pods sprout near the ground on long shoots, and each one must be picked by hand when it is just ripe.

Rome's pepper problem

About 2,000 years ago, Roman ships sailed to the Malabar coast every year, riding the monsoon winds. One of their ports was Muziris, close to today's Kochi. They paid in gold and silver coins, and hoards of Roman coins have been dug up in south India.

The Roman writer Pliny the Elder grumbled that India, China and Arabia took millions of coins from the empire every year. In Rome, pepper was kept in special warehouses. Even today in England, a "peppercorn rent" means a tiny, symbolic rent, from the days when a few peppercorns had real value.

What did Columbus really find?Christopher Columbus set out to reach India and China. He died still believing he had reached Asia.

The race for the source

For centuries, Arab and Venetian merchants controlled the spice trade into Europe. Other countries wanted to cut them out. In 1492, Columbus sailed west hoping to reach Asia and its spices, and bumped into the Americas instead.

Vasco da Gama of Portugal tried the other way, around Africa. In 1498 his ships reached Kozhikode, then called Calicut. The Portuguese, then the Dutch and the British, all built forts and trading posts in India. The East India Company began as a group of London merchants chasing spices and ended up ruling much of the country.

What makes them so strong

Pepper's bite comes from a chemical called piperine. It tickles the same heat sensors in your mouth that chillies do, though more gently. Chillies themselves only reached India from the Americas in the 1500s, so for most of history pepper was what made Indian food hot.

Cardamom's smell comes from oils inside its tiny black seeds. They fade quickly once ground, which is why many cooks crush the pods fresh for chai or kheer. In Sweden and Finland, cardamom buns are a favourite treat, so a spice from Kerala's misty hills ends up in a Nordic bakery.

Who grows them now

India no longer leads the world in either spice. Vietnam now grows the most pepper, and Guatemala grows the most cardamom. Guatemala's crop began in the early 1900s, when a coffee planter brought in cardamom plants.

Kerala still grows both, and its pepper is still famous. The Spices Board, a government body, runs auctions where traders bid on sacks of cardamom. The prices change every day, as they have since Roman ships waited for the monsoon.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In the Middle Ages, Europeans used spices to hide the taste of rotten meat.

Truth: Historians say this is very unlikely. Spices cost far more than fresh meat, so anyone rich enough to buy pepper could easily afford meat that had not gone bad.

Try this

Ask an adult for a few green cardamom pods and some black peppercorns. Gently split a pod with your fingernails and count the tiny black seeds inside. Smell the seeds, then a peppercorn, and describe each smell in three words to someone at home.
