What lives in your bed?

Tonight you'll share your bed with thousands of tiny eight-legged creatures that eat the skin you shed while you sleep.

The short answer

Your bed is home to house dust mites. They are tiny relatives of spiders, about a quarter of a millimetre long, no bigger than the full stop at the end of this sentence. They have eight legs, no eyes and a see-through body.

They live in mattresses, pillows, blankets, sofas and carpets. Their favourite food is the dead skin that falls off you all the time. A bed gives them everything they want: food, warmth and moisture from your breath and sweat. Most homes on Earth have them, and they don't bite or spread disease.

Surprising but true

Dust mites never drink. They soak up water straight from damp air, which is why they thrive in humid places like coastal India.

A buffet of skin

You shed skin all day long, tiny flakes too small to notice. Some estimates say tens of thousands of skin cells fall off you every minute. You spend about a third of your life in bed, so a lot of that skin ends up in the sheets. The full story is in the tale of how skin sheds.

Dust mites, with a little help from moulds in the bed, break those flakes down and eat them. A single mite lives a couple of months and a female can lay dozens of eggs. In warm, damp homes, numbers build up fast.

They drink from the air

Dust mites don't drink water. They soak it up from the air through their bodies. That is why they love humid places like Mumbai, Chennai, Kolkata and Florida, and why they struggle in dry mountain air or desert towns.

They also hate light and avoid it, burrowing deep into fabric. You would need a microscope to see them properly. Under one, they look like plump, hairy little blobs with legs, something like a tiny bear.

How did microscopes reveal a hidden world?In the 1670s, a Dutch cloth seller peered through a lens smaller than a pea and saw tiny creatures swimming in a drop of pond water. Nobody had ever seen them before.

The sneezing problem

For most people, dust mites are harmless. But their droppings contain proteins that can trigger allergies. When you plump a pillow or make a bed, those droppings float up and get breathed in.

For people who are allergic, that can mean a stuffy or runny nose, sneezing, itchy eyes and, for some, asthma attacks. Dust mites are one of the most common allergy triggers in the world. Doctors can test whether someone is allergic with a small skin test.

If you often wake up sneezing, it's worth telling a parent or doctor. They can help work out whether dust mites are the cause.

Keeping numbers down

You can't get rid of dust mites completely, but you can keep them fewer. Washing sheets and pillow covers every week in hot water, around 60 °C, kills most of them. Sunlight and dry air help too, which is one reason families in India often air mattresses and quilts in the sun on rooftops and balconies.

Special covers zip around mattresses and pillows to keep mites in and droppings away from your face. Fewer soft toys on the bed and less carpet also help.

A tiny world around you

Dust mites are just one of the many small creatures sharing your life. Your skin has its own microscopic mites too, called Demodex, which live in the roots of eyelashes and on the face of most adults. They come out at night to move about. They join a huge crowd of bacteria and fungi that live on everyone's body, which you can meet in the story of the germs on your body.

Quick facts

Size
about 0.2–0.3 mm, too small to see without a magnifier
Legs
eight, so they are relatives of spiders, not insects
Food
flakes of dead skin
Best conditions
warm and humid, around 70–80% humidity
Main health effect
their droppings can cause allergies and asthma

Myth or fact?

Myth: A pillow doubles in weight after a few years because it fills up with dust mites and their droppings.

Truth: This claim spread widely but has never been backed by a proper study. Pillows do collect mites, skin and sweat, which is why washing covers regularly helps, but the doubling story is a myth.

Try this

Shine a torch across a dark room on a sunny afternoon, or watch a beam of sunlight from a window, while someone shakes a pillowcase. You'll see thousands of floating dust specks. Mites are too small to see this way, but a lot of that dust is skin flakes, their food.
