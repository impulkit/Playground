How is bread made in a factory?

A big bread factory can turn flour into sealed, sliced loaves in about three hours, faster than many people bake one loaf at home.

The short answer

A factory makes bread the same basic way a home baker does. Flour, water, yeast and salt are mixed into dough. The dough rests and rises, gets baked, then cools. The difference is size and speed. Machines mix dough by the tonne, ovens bake thousands of loaves an hour, and blades slice them before they are bagged.

A large plant can take flour in at one end and send sealed loaves out of the other in about three hours. Most of that time is the dough rising and the bread cooling, because yeast and hot bread can't be hurried much.

Surprising but true

When pre-sliced bread went on sale in 1928, in the small American town of Chillicothe, Missouri, many people thought the slices would go stale too quickly.

Mixing at full speed

Flour arrives by lorry and is blown through pipes into tall silos. Computers weigh out flour, water, yeast and salt for each batch. Then a powerful mixer beats the dough for a few minutes.

In 1961 scientists at a research centre in Chorleywood, England, found that very fast, hard mixing could do in minutes what a long, slow rise usually does. Their method, the Chorleywood bread process, made bread cheaper and quicker. Most sliced bread in Britain is still made this way, and many factories in India use similar fast methods.

Rising in a warm room

Next a machine chops the dough into equal lumps, each weighed to the gram. Rollers shape them and drop them into tins on a moving belt. The tins travel slowly through a warm, damp cabinet called a prover for about an hour.

In there, the yeast gets to work. This is fermentation: the yeast eats sugars and breathes out carbon dioxide. The gas is trapped by gluten, the stretchy protein in wheat, so the dough swells like a balloon in a tin.

A very long oven

Many factories bake in a tunnel oven, which can be as long as a cricket pitch. The tins creep through it for about 20 to 30 minutes. Heat sets the bubbles in place and turns the outside brown, the same chemistry described in baking.

The bread comes out far too hot to slice. So it rides on a cooling spiral, a tall winding track where it slowly drops to room temperature for an hour or more. Slicing warm bread would squash it flat. Along the way, metal detectors and cameras check every loaf. A loaf that is too pale, too small or lopsided gets pushed off the line, and the rejects are often turned into breadcrumbs or animal feed rather than thrown away.

The day bread came sliced

In 1928 the Chillicothe Baking Company in Missouri began selling bread sliced by a machine invented by Otto Rohwedder. People loved the convenience, and the phrase "the greatest thing since sliced bread" was born from it.

Modern slicers use rows of thin, fast blades that cut a loaf in one pass. A machine then slips each loaf into a bag and seals it. A printer adds the date, and a barcode tells the shop's till what it is.

Bread in India

Leavened white bread came to India with European traders, and the Portuguese in Goa are often credited with spreading the bakery loaf known as pav. In 1965 the government set up Modern Bread, a large state company that made packaged loaves common in Indian cities. Today many brands sell sliced bread across the country, and it often ends up in a Mumbai vada pav or a school sandwich.

Factories also have to keep bread from going mouldy. Clean rooms, sealed bags and careful cooling all help it last a few days. Some loaves also contain a little preservative, which is one of the ideas behind food preserving.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Factory bread rises because it is full of chemicals instead of yeast.

Truth: Most factory bread is raised with ordinary baker's yeast. Factories do add small amounts of other ingredients, such as fats or flour improvers, to keep loaves soft and even, and these are listed on the packet.

Try this

Mix a teaspoon of dry yeast and a teaspoon of sugar into half a cup of warm water in a clear glass. Ask an adult to check the water is warm, not hot. Watch for 10 minutes and you will see foam rise as the yeast makes gas.
