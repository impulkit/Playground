How do you count money quickly?

Shopkeepers often work out your change by counting up from the price, not by subtracting, and it is faster and harder to get wrong.

The short answer

To count money quickly, sort it first. Put all the notes and coins of the same value together. Then count the pile with the biggest value first, and work down to the smallest.

Group things into tens or hundreds as you go. Ten ₹10 coins make ₹100. Five ₹20 notes make ₹100 too. Making friendly round totals keeps the numbers small in your head.

And to give change, try counting up from the price to the amount paid, the way many shopkeepers do. It is quicker than subtracting and harder to get wrong.

Surprising but true

Indian banknotes carry raised marks and shapes that let people who are blind tell them apart by touch, and the RBI has a free phone app called MANI that reads a note's value aloud.

Sort, then count

When money is mixed up, your brain has to keep switching. Ten, then fifty, then two, then a hundred. It is easy to lose track.

Sorting turns one hard job into several easy ones. A pile of ₹500 notes is counted in five hundreds: 500, 1,000, 1,500, 2,000. A pile of ₹2 coins is counted in twos. Skip counting like this is simple once you have practised your times tables.

Bank cashiers also face all the notes the same way up and the same way round. That way, a fake or a note of the wrong value stands out at once.

Counting up, the shopkeeper's way

Say a toy costs ₹63 and you hand over ₹100. Many people try to work out 100 minus 63 in their heads, which means borrowing and carrying.

A shopkeeper often does it differently. Starting at 63, they hand you ₹2 and say "65", then ₹5 and say "70". Then come ₹10 and ₹20: "80, 100." The change is ₹37, and you have watched it being counted out.

This counting-up method is a favourite of mental maths experts too. You jump to the nearest round number first, then add in big friendly steps.

Machines that count

Banks and big shops use note-counting machines. You drop in a stack of notes, and rollers flick them past a sensor one at a time, well over a thousand a minute. Many machines also check for fakes by looking at the paper, the ink and special marks that glow under ultraviolet light.

Coins are often weighed instead of counted. A bag of a hundred ₹5 coins, for instance, always weighs about the same. If you know one coin's weight, a bag's weight tells you how many coins are inside.

Money you can feel

India's banknotes come in different sizes and colours, and many have raised marks you can feel, such as small bumps, lines or shapes, near the edge. These help people who are blind or have low vision tell notes apart.

In 2020 the Reserve Bank of India launched a free app called MANI, short for Mobile Aided Note Identifier. Point a phone camera at a note, and it says the value out loud.

In the USA all paper dollars are the same size and colour, which makes them harder to tell apart by touch. Many blind Americans fold different notes in different ways, and the government offers free electronic readers.

Fewer coins and notes

Money keeps changing. India stopped printing ₹2,000 notes and in 2023 began taking them back from circulation. In 2025 the USA made its last pennies for everyday use, because each one cost more than a cent to make.

Many people now pay by phone. In India, UPI payments are so common that even small tea stalls display a QR code. Yet counting money is still a useful skill. It keeps your brain quick with numbers, helps you spot a mistake in your change, and makes it easier to understand bigger numbers like lakhs and crores.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The fastest way to count money is to add up notes in whatever order you pick them up.

Truth: Mixing values makes you switch between numbers all the time, which slows you down and causes mistakes. Sorting first, counting the biggest values first and grouping into tens is faster and more accurate.

Try this

Collect a handful of coins or toy money. Time yourself counting it all in a jumble. Then sort it into piles by value, count each pile in groups of ten and add the piles. Compare your times, and check whether both counts gave the same answer.
