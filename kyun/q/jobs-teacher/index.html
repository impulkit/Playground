What makes a great teacher?

In 1887, a teacher spelled "water" into a deaf and blind girl's hand as cold water ran over it. The girl was Helen Keller.

The short answer

Great teachers explain things clearly, believe their students can do well and find out quickly what each child has understood. They ask good questions, give useful feedback and make a classroom feel safe enough that being wrong is fine.

Researchers have studied teaching for decades. The teachers who help students learn most are not always the most entertaining. They tend to break hard ideas into steps, check often for understanding, and go back over things at the right time. They also care, and their students can tell. Almost everyone can name a teacher who changed something for them, and it's usually for one of these reasons.

Surprising but true

Testing yourself on something helps you remember it better than reading it again. Scientists call this the testing effect, and good teachers use it all the time.

A word in the hand

In 1887, Anne Sullivan arrived at a farmhouse in Alabama to teach Helen Keller, a six-year-old who could neither see nor hear. For weeks, Sullivan spelled words into Helen's hand with finger shapes. Helen copied them but didn't understand that they meant anything.

Then, at a water pump, Sullivan held one of Helen's hands under the cold stream and spelled w-a-t-e-r into the other. Helen later wrote that in that moment she understood that everything had a name. By evening she had learned 30 words. She grew up to graduate from college and write books. Sullivan had found the one thing that made it click, which great teachers keep looking for.

Savitribai Phule's school

In India, one of the most famous teachers is Savitribai Phule. In 1848, she and her husband Jyotirao Phule started a school for girls in Pune, at a time when most girls, and many lower-caste children, were kept out of school.

She is often called India's first woman teacher. Some people threw mud and stones at her on the way to school, so she carried a second sari to change into. She kept going, and the Phules went on to open more schools.

Questions, not just answers

One thing scientists have found again and again is the testing effect. When you try to recall something from memory, even if you struggle, you remember it better later. Good teachers use quick quizzes, questions in class and "explain it to your partner" moments, not to give marks, but to help memories stick.

Spacing helps too. Going back to a topic after a few days, then a few weeks, works better than one long session. And feedback matters most when it's specific. "Check the units in question 3" helps more than "good work" or "try harder".

Knowing each child

A teacher in a class of 40 can't give each child an hour a day. Great teachers notice anyway. They see who has gone quiet, who is bored because it's too easy, and who is hiding confusion behind a smile.

Many children in India learn in a language that isn't the one they speak at home. Good teachers bridge that gap with stories, local examples and patience. In the US, teachers also work with children who are still learning English.

Teaching with new tools

In 2020, Ranjitsinh Disale, a primary school teacher from Solapur in Maharashtra, won the Global Teacher Prize. He added QR codes to textbooks so children could scan them and watch lessons and stories in their own language. He shared half his prize money with the other finalists.

Tools change, from slates to smartboards. The main ingredients don't. A teacher who knows the subject well, explains it clearly and cares whether you understand it is hard to beat.

A job with many parts

Teaching isn't just the time in the classroom. Teachers plan lessons, mark work, meet parents and keep learning themselves. India had about 98 lakh school teachers in 2023 to 2024, one of the largest teaching workforces in the world.

India celebrates Teachers' Day on 5 September, the birthday of S. Radhakrishnan, a philosopher and teacher who became President. The rest of the world marks World Teachers' Day on 5 October. In the US, Teacher Appreciation Week comes in May, when many children bring a card or a flower.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Everyone has a learning style, visual, auditory or hands-on, and learns best when taught that way.

Truth: Many people have a preference, but careful studies have not found that teaching to a person's preferred style helps them learn more. What helps is matching the method to the subject, like using maps for geography and practice for music.

Try this

Teach someone at home something you learned this week, in five minutes, without notes. Then ask them to explain it back to you. You'll quickly find the parts you didn't understand yourself, which is exactly what teachers discover when they plan a lesson.
