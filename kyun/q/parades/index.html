Why do countries hold parades?

The English word "juggernaut", meaning an unstoppable force, comes from Jagannath, whose giant chariots are pulled through the streets of Puri every year.

The short answer

A parade is a crowd of people moving along a route in an organised line, usually with music, flags, costumes or vehicles, while others watch. Countries, cities and communities hold parades to celebrate, to remember, to give thanks, to worship and to show strength.

A national parade, like India's Republic Day parade in New Delhi, tells a story about the whole country. Its soldiers, states, culture and history all pass by in a couple of hours. A street parade on a festival can do the same for a single town or community. In both, the point is that people gather in one place and share the same moment.

Surprising but true

New York's St Patrick's Day parade was first held in 1762, fourteen years before the United States declared independence.

Showing strength

Some of the oldest parades were about power. In ancient Rome, a general who won a great war could be granted a "triumph". He rode through the city in a chariot, followed by soldiers, captured treasure and prisoners, while crowds cheered.

Many countries still hold military parades. Tanks roll by, soldiers march in step and jets fly overhead. These parades are meant to make citizens feel proud and safe. They also send a message to other countries about a nation's strength. Some people think military parades are a fine tradition, while others feel they cost too much or glorify war.

Republic Day in New Delhi

India's biggest parade happens every 26 January. It marks the day in 1950 when the Constitution came into force and India became a republic. The first parade, in 1950, was held in a stadium, and for a few years the venue changed. Since 1955, the parade has moved along the grand avenue that runs from the President's house towards India Gate, now called Kartavya Path.

Soldiers, sailors and air force crews march in step. Camels of the Border Security Force swing by in decorated saddles. States send tableaux, giant moving displays on trucks that show their festivals, crafts and history. A foreign leader is usually invited as chief guest. Then comes the flypast, with aircraft roaring low overhead in formation.

Chariots for the gods

Religious processions are parades too, and some are among the largest gatherings on Earth. In the town of Puri in Odisha, the Rath Yatra takes place every summer. Images of Lord Jagannath and his brother and sister are placed on three towering wooden chariots, built new each year. Huge crowds pull them through the streets with thick ropes.

European travellers who saw the festival centuries ago were amazed by the size of the chariots. Their accounts gave English the word "juggernaut", for something enormous that seems impossible to stop.

Balloons, roses and green hats

In the USA, parades are often about fun. The Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade in New York began in 1924. From 1927, it added huge balloons shaped like cartoon characters. Today they can be as tall as a five-storey building, held down by dozens of handlers each.

In Pasadena, California, the Rose Parade has run on New Year's Day since 1890. Every surface of each float must be covered with flowers, seeds, bark or other plants. New York's St Patrick's Day parade, honouring Irish heritage, is even older. Irish soldiers first marched in 1762.

Remembering and marching

Some parades are quiet. On Remembrance Day and Veterans Day, old soldiers march to honour people who died in wars. Victory parades welcome sports teams home. When India's cricket team won the T20 World Cup in 2024, crowds packed Mumbai's Marine Drive to cheer an open-top bus.

People also march to ask for change. In 1930, Gandhi and his followers walked to Dandi in the Salt March, and in 1963, about 250,000 people joined the March on Washington in the USA. These weren't parades in the festive sense, but they used the same idea: when many people move together in public, everyone notices.

Myth or fact?

Myth: India's Republic Day parade celebrates independence from Britain.

Truth: Independence came on 15 August 1947. Republic Day, 26 January, marks the day in 1950 when India's Constitution came into force and the country became a republic. Independence Day is marked mainly with a flag-raising and a speech at the Red Fort.
