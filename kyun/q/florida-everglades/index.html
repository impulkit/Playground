What are the Everglades?

The Everglades look like a swamp, but they are really a river about 100 km wide and often less than knee-deep, creeping slowly south across Florida.

The short answer

The Everglades are a vast wetland in southern Florida, in the United States. Water spills out of a big lake, Lake Okeechobee, and spreads south in a shallow sheet, often less than knee-deep, flowing slowly to the sea. Tall sawgrass grows out of the water as far as you can see, dotted with islands of trees.

The area is home to alligators, crocodiles, wading birds, manatees and the rare Florida panther. It also supplies drinking water to millions of people in cities such as Miami. About half of the original Everglades has been drained for farms and cities. Since 2000, the USA has been running a long and costly plan to restore what is left.

Surprising but true

According to the US National Park Service, the Everglades is the only place in the world where alligators and crocodiles live side by side.

A river of grass

The Everglades are so flat that the land drops only a few centimetres for every kilometre. Water creeps across this slope so slowly that a single drop can take months to travel from the lake to the coast. In many places, the "river" is about 100 kilometres wide.

In 1947, the writer Marjory Stoneman Douglas published a book called The Everglades: River of Grass. Many people had thought of the area as a useless swamp to be drained. Her book showed them a living river and helped turn public opinion. In the same year, Everglades National Park was opened to protect the southern part.

Alligators and crocodiles together

Alligators and crocodiles are cousins, but they rarely meet. Alligators like fresh water, and crocodiles prefer salty or brackish water near the coast. At the southern tip of Florida, fresh water from the Everglades mixes with sea water from Florida Bay. Both animals live there, making it the only place in the world where they share a home.

The wetlands are also a paradise for birds. Pink roseate spoonbills sweep their flat beaks through the water. Herons, egrets and storks stalk fish in the shallows. In winter, slow, gentle manatees gather in warmer coastal waters.

Mangroves at the edge

Where the river of grass meets the sea, it turns into a maze of mangroves, trees that can grow in salty water on stilt-like roots. These forests shelter baby fish, protect the coast from storm waves and soak up mud. Visitors to India's Sundarbans would find the scenery familiar, though Florida swaps the tigers for alligators and panthers.

The Seminole and Miccosukee peoples have lived in and around the Everglades for about 200 years, and other Native American groups lived there for thousands of years before them. They know its channels and islands better than anyone.

Draining the river

In the late 1800s and 1900s, people began digging canals to drain the Everglades. They wanted dry land for sugarcane fields, vegetable farms and growing cities. Dykes and pumps sent water straight to the sea, and the slow, wide sheet of water was cut into pieces.

The results were serious. Bird numbers fell sharply. Some areas dried out and burned in wildfires. Salt water seeped into wells along the coast. Today, the original Everglades is about half its former size.

New threats and a big repair

Animals from other places now cause trouble too. Burmese pythons, large snakes from Southeast Asia that were probably pets, have spread through the park. With no natural enemies, they eat rabbits, raccoons and birds. They are one of the best-known examples of an invasive species anywhere.

In 2000, the US government approved a huge restoration plan to send more clean water south again, the way it used to flow. Work on it will take decades. The park has been a World Heritage Site since 1979, and for many years it has also been on UNESCO's list of sites in danger.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Everglades is just a big, stagnant swamp.

Truth: The water is always moving, though very slowly. It flows south as a wide, shallow sheet from Lake Okeechobee towards Florida Bay. That is why Marjory Stoneman Douglas called it a "river of grass".

Try this

Make a slow river. Tilt a baking tray very slightly by putting a thin book under one end, and cover it with a layer of grass clippings or crumpled paper. Slowly pour water at the high end and watch how it spreads and seeps rather than rushing. That gentle sheet of water is how the Everglades moves.
