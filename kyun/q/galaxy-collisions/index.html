What happens when galaxies collide?

When two galaxies crash together, hundreds of billions of stars pass through each other, and almost none of them ever touch.

The short answer

Galaxies are huge groups of stars, gas and dust held together by gravity. Sometimes two of them drift close enough for gravity to pull them together. Over hundreds of millions of years, they swing past each other, stretch out into long tails, loop back and finally settle into one bigger galaxy.

Surprisingly, the stars themselves almost never collide, because the gaps between them are enormous. What does crash together is the gas. When giant gas clouds slam into each other, they squeeze and collapse, and new stars are born in great bursts. Collisions are one of the main ways galaxies grow and change shape.

Surprising but true

The Milky Way is swallowing a small galaxy right now. The Sagittarius Dwarf Galaxy is being slowly torn apart on the far side of our galaxy's centre.

Slow-motion crashes

A galaxy collision is far too slow to watch. It takes hundreds of millions of years. But the universe contains so many galaxies that astronomers can see collisions at every stage, like photographs from many different car crashes.

In the Antennae Galaxies, two spiral galaxies are tangled together, with two long curved tails of stars streaming out like an insect's feelers. In the Cartwheel Galaxy, a smaller galaxy punched straight through a larger one, sending a ring of new stars rippling outward. In 2022 the Webb telescope photographed the Cartwheel in sharp detail.

Our galaxy is a swallower

The Milky Way has been growing by eating smaller galaxies for billions of years. Data from Europe's Gaia space telescope revealed stars left over from a galaxy, nicknamed Gaia-Enceladus, that merged with ours roughly 10 billion years ago. Its stars move differently from others, like guests who arrived late to a party. Astronomers think big galaxies like ours were built up this way, swallowing many smaller ones over billions of years.

Right now, the Milky Way is pulling apart the Sagittarius Dwarf Galaxy, stretching it into a long stream of stars wrapped around us. The Large Magellanic Cloud, a small galaxy visible from the southern half of the Earth, may crash into the Milky Way in a couple of billion years.

Will we hit Andromeda?

The Andromeda Galaxy is the nearest big galaxy, about 2.5 million light-years away. On a very dark night you can see it as a faint smudge without a telescope. It is moving towards us at about 110 kilometres per second.

In 2012, astronomers using the Hubble Space Telescope predicted a head-on collision in about 4 billion years. In 2025, a team used newer measurements and included the pull of other nearby galaxies. They ran thousands of simulations and found about a 50 per cent chance of a merger within the next 10 billion years. It is a coin toss, not a certainty. A closer look at Andromeda's motion in the coming years should shrink the uncertainty.

What it would look like

If a merger does happen, the night sky would change slowly over many millions of years. Andromeda would grow larger and brighter, until it filled much of the sky. Then the sky would fill with streams of stars and glowing clouds of new star formation.

The two spiral galaxies would probably end up as one big, rounded galaxy, an elliptical. Astronomers sometimes jokingly call it "Milkomeda". By then the Sun will be near the end of its life, and Earth will be far too hot for life as we know it.

Monsters in the middle

Most large galaxies have a giant black hole at their centre. When galaxies merge, their black holes sink towards the middle, circle each other and eventually join. As they spiral together, they send out ripples in space called gravitational waves. Scientists are building new instruments to detect the biggest of these mergers across the universe.

Myth or fact?

Myth: When the Milky Way and Andromeda collide, Earth will be smashed by other stars.

Truth: Even if they do merge, which is not certain, stars are so far apart that the Sun is very unlikely to hit anything. It may be flung into a new orbit around the merged galaxy. In any case, this is billions of years in the future.

Try this

Spread two handfuls of rice thinly on two large sheets of newspaper. Now picture each grain as a solar system. Slide one sheet slowly over the other. Even this is far too crowded, because real stars are much farther apart than the grains. In a galaxy collision, the stars almost always miss.
