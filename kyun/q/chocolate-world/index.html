Which countries love chocolate most?

In 2024 Swiss shops sold about 10.6 kg of chocolate per person, roughly a hundred big bars each. Yet the beans grow thousands of kilometres away.

The short answer

By most counts, people in Switzerland eat the most chocolate per person, about 10.6 kilograms each in 2024. Germany, Denmark and other northern European countries are close behind, each at roughly 9 to 10 kilograms.

Those numbers aren't perfect. Swiss figures include chocolate bought by tourists and shoppers from neighbouring countries. Still, the pattern is clear. Chocolate is grown mostly in the tropics but eaten mostly in rich, cooler countries.

Surprising but true

Côte d'Ivoire grows more cocoa than any other country, but many of its cocoa farmers have rarely or never tasted a chocolate bar.

Where the beans grow

Chocolate starts as seeds inside pods on the cocoa tree, which only grows in a hot, wet belt near the equator. About two in every five cocoa beans come from Côte d'Ivoire, in West Africa. Ghana is next, followed by countries such as Indonesia, Ecuador, Cameroon and Nigeria.

Millions of small farming families grow most of it, often on plots no bigger than a few football fields. Many earn very little. In some areas children work on cocoa farms instead of going to school, a problem that chocolate companies and governments have promised to fix for over 20 years. Groups campaigning against child labour say progress has been slow.

An old American drink

People in South America were using cocoa more than 5,000 years ago. Later, the Maya and Aztecs of Central America drank it as a frothy, bitter drink, sometimes spiced with chilli. Cocoa beans were valuable enough to be used as money.

Spanish explorers brought cocoa to Europe in the 1500s. There, people added sugar and drank it hot. Solid chocolate bars came much later, in the 1800s.

Who were the Aztecs and the Maya?The Aztec capital stood on an island in a lake, with floating gardens, and it was bigger than any city in Spain when the Spanish arrived.

Why the Swiss?

Switzerland grows no cocoa at all. But in 1875 a Swiss chocolate maker, Daniel Peter, worked out how to mix chocolate with milk, using milk powder developed by his neighbour Henri Nestlé. A few years later Rodolphe Lindt invented a machine that stirred chocolate for days, making it smooth and melting.

Swiss chocolate became a national pride and a big export. Belgium is famous for filled chocolates called pralines, and Britain gave the world the Cadbury bar. Each country's taste is a little different. In Mexico, chocolate is still often drunk hot and frothy, whisked with a wooden tool called a molinillo, much as it was centuries ago. Americans often like sweeter chocolate, while many Europeans prefer it darker.

Chocolate in India and the US

In the United States, chocolate is part of Halloween, Valentine's Day and Easter. Americans eat several kilograms each a year, and companies like Hershey's date back to the 1890s.

India eats far less per person, but that is changing fast. Many Indians first met chocolate through a Cadbury bar. The company began selling in India in 1948 and later opened factories there. Today chocolate is a common Diwali and Raksha Bandhan gift, and India grows some cocoa of its own, mostly in Kerala, Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh. Small Indian companies now make "bean to bar" chocolate from these local beans.

Prices and the future

In 2024 poor harvests in West Africa pushed cocoa prices to record highs, and chocolate in shops got pricier or smaller. Scientists worry that hotter, drier weather and plant diseases could make cocoa harder to grow. Some farmers are planting cocoa among taller shade trees, which can protect the crop and the soil.

Before it reaches you, every bean is fermented in heaps under banana leaves, dried in the sun, shipped across an ocean, roasted and ground. That long path, explained in how chocolate is made, is one reason a bar can carry beans from Ghana, sugar from Brazil and milk from the Alps. A single square may have travelled further than most of the people who eat it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Switzerland and Belgium grow the cocoa for their famous chocolate.

Truth: Cocoa trees need tropical heat and rain, so they can't grow in Europe. Swiss and Belgian makers import beans, mostly from West Africa and South America.
