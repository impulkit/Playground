Can plants move?

Touch a chhui-mui leaf and it folds shut in seconds. Plants move all the time; most are just too slow for us to notice.

The short answer

Yes. Plants can't walk away, but they move all the time. Shoots bend towards light. Roots turn downwards. Climbing stems twist around poles, and many flowers open in the morning and close at night.

Most of these movements are slow, happening by growing more on one side than the other. You would need a camera taking a photo every few minutes to see them clearly. Speed the film up, and a bean seedling looks as if it is waving about, searching for something to climb.

A few plants move fast enough to watch. Touch the leaves of the chhui-mui plant, also called lajwanti or touch-me-not, and they fold up within seconds. A Venus flytrap can snap shut on an insect in about a tenth of a second.

Surprising but true

A Venus flytrap counts. Its trap only snaps shut if a trigger hair is touched twice within about 20 seconds.

How a plant moves without muscles

Plants have no muscles, so they use water instead. At the base of each chhui-mui leaflet is a small swelling full of water-filled cells. When the leaf is touched, an electrical signal travels through the plant, a bit like a nerve signal. The cells quickly let water out, go floppy, and the leaflets fold.

After a few minutes, the cells fill up again and the leaves slowly open. Scientists think the folding may startle insects and make the plant look less tasty. It works a little like a reflex in your own body, fast and without thinking.

Slow movements work differently. A shoot bends towards light because cells on its shady side grow longer than cells on its sunny side.

A trap that counts

The Venus flytrap grows wild in only one small area of the USA, in the boggy pinewoods of North and South Carolina. Each trap has a few stiff trigger hairs inside. If one hair is touched once, nothing happens. If it is touched again within about 20 seconds, the trap slams shut.

This counting stops the plant wasting energy on raindrops or falling leaves. Once closed, the trap keeps counting. Every time the trapped insect struggles, the plant makes more digestive juice.

Darwin and Bose

Charles Darwin and his son Francis spent years watching plants move. In 1880 they published a book called The Power of Movement in Plants. They showed that a seedling's tip senses light and sends a message down the stem telling it where to bend.

In India, Jagadish Chandra Bose built a machine called the crescograph in the early 1900s. It magnified a plant's growth thousands of times, so tiny movements could be recorded. Bose showed that plants react to touch, heat, chemicals and injury with electrical signals, much as animal nerves do. Many scientists of his day were doubtful, but later research proved him largely right.

Up, down and around

Roots always end up growing downwards, even if you plant a seed upside down. Special cells in the root tip contain heavy grains that settle at the bottom, telling the root which way gravity pulls.

Some plants move to the rhythm of the day. Many legumes fold their leaves at night, as if asleep. The telegraph plant, found in India, has small side leaflets that jerk around fast enough to see.

Can a plant learn?

In 2014, a team led by Monica Gagliano, working in Australia, dropped potted chhui-mui plants a short, harmless distance again and again. At first, the leaves folded every time. After a while, many plants stopped folding, as if they had learned the drop was harmless. They still folded when touched in other ways.

Some scientists say this shows a simple kind of learning. Others think it may be tiredness or something else, and want more experiments. Either way, plants turn out to be busier than they look, as their seeds show by travelling the world on wind, water and fur.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Sunflowers follow the Sun all day, every day, their whole lives.

Truth: Only young sunflower plants track the Sun. Once a sunflower blooms, its head stops turning and mostly faces east.

Try this

Put a potted bean or pea seedling on a windowsill and mark which way its tip leans. After two days, turn the pot halfway round and keep watching. Within a day or two the shoot bends back towards the light. Take a photo each morning to see the movement you could never see happening.
