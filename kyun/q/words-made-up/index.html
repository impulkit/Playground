Who makes up new words?

Dr Seuss gave us the earliest known use of "nerd", and a dictionary once printed a word that didn't exist for 13 years.

The short answer

You do. So does everyone else. English has no official office that approves new words. A word becomes real when enough people use it, for long enough, to mean the same thing.

Dictionary editors, called lexicographers, don't invent words. They are more like word detectives. They collect examples from books, newspapers, websites and speech, and watch how a word spreads. When it has clearly settled into the language, it goes into the dictionary. Big English dictionaries add hundreds of new words every year.

Some words have a famous inventor. Most have none. They just bubble up, often in playgrounds, games, songs and group chats, long before any grown-up writes them down.

Surprising but true

From 1934 to 1947, Webster's dictionary listed "dord" as a word meaning density. It was a mistake that nobody noticed for years.

Writers who made words

Lewis Carroll loved making words. In his poem "Jabberwocky", from 1871, he wrote "chortle", a mix of chuckle and snort, and "galumph", a clumsy gallop. Both are in ordinary dictionaries now. He even explained his trick: squashing two words into one, which he called a portmanteau, after a kind of suitcase with two halves. His other inventions fill Alice's Wonderland.

Dr Seuss gave us the earliest known printed use of "nerd", in the book If I Ran the Zoo in 1950. There a nerd was just an odd little creature. In 2003 the Oxford English Dictionary added "muggle" from the Harry Potter books, for a person who lacks a skill.

Shakespeare is the earliest known writer of words like "eyeball" and "lacklustre". But many words once credited to him have turned up in older writing.

Six ways to build a word

Most new words are made by a few simple tricks.

  • - Joining two words: sunflower, skateboard.
  • - Blending parts of words: brunch (breakfast and lunch), smog (smoke and fog).
  • - Borrowing from other languages. English took shampoo, bungalow, jungle and pyjamas from Indian languages, as you can see in words English borrowed.
  • - Shortening: bus comes from omnibus, and maths from mathematics.
  • - Using a name: the sandwich is named after an English earl.
  • - Using initials as a word: laser and scuba started as long phrases.

Brand names can turn into words too. In 2006 "google" entered the Oxford English Dictionary as a verb.

The word that never was

In 1934, Webster's New International Dictionary printed "dord", meaning density. Nobody had ever used it. A note saying "D or d", meaning the letter D in capital or small form could stand for density, got misread as a single word.

"Dord" sat in the dictionary for years until an editor noticed in 1939 that it had no history at all. It was finally removed in 1947. Lexicographers call this a ghost word. Some reference books now plant fake entries on purpose, nicknamed mountweazels, to catch copycats who steal their pages.

What is Aadhaar?The first Aadhaar number went to a woman in a small village in Maharashtra in 2010. Today well over 130 crore people have one.

New words from India

Indian English has its own favourites, like "prepone", the opposite of postpone, and "timepass". In 2017 the Oxford English Dictionary added dozens of Indian English words, including "jugaad", meaning a clever, make-do fix, and "dabba".

That same year, Oxford Dictionaries chose "Aadhaar" as its first Hindi Word of the Year. A government ID scheme had turned a Sanskrit-rooted word meaning foundation into something every family talked about.

Kids are word makers

Young people have always driven new slang. In 2023 Oxford picked "rizz", meaning charm, as its Word of the Year, a word spread mostly by teenagers online.

There is even a famous children's novel about this. In Frindle, by Andrew Clements, a boy calls a pen a "frindle" to annoy his teacher, and the word spreads across America. And the strangest part? Real slang moves almost exactly the way that book describes.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Shakespeare invented about 1,700 English words.

Truth: Shakespeare is the earliest known writer of many words, but researchers keep finding older examples by other writers. He did invent some, but far fewer than the famous number suggests.
