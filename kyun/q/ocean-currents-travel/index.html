How far can a message in a bottle travel?

In 1886 a sailor dropped a gin bottle off a German ship. A family walking on an Australian beach picked it up 132 years later.

The short answer

A bottle can travel thousands of kilometres, and some have crossed whole oceans. It all depends on the currents it falls into. A bottle doesn't sail. It drifts like a leaf on a stream, carried by ocean currents and pushed a little by the wind.

Some bottles wash up within weeks, a few kilometres away. Others spend years riding huge loops of water that circle each ocean. Many are never found at all. They sink, break on rocks or end up on empty shores where no one walks. The record holder spent 132 years between the throw and the find.

Surprising but true

The oldest known message in a bottle was part of a science experiment. German sailors threw thousands of bottles overboard to map the ocean's currents.

The bottle on Wedge Island

In January 2018, Tonya Illman was walking among the sand dunes near Wedge Island, north of Perth. She found an old, thick glass bottle half buried. Inside was a rolled-up, damp piece of paper.

The Western Australian Museum checked it carefully. The note was a printed form, filled in by hand and dated 12 June 1886. It came from a German sailing ship called the Paula, crossing the Indian Ocean. The German Naval Observatory had asked ships to throw bottles overboard, each with a form asking the finder to report where it landed. Over about 69 years, thousands of bottles were thrown, and a few hundred forms came back. This one was the last to be found.

Rivers inside the sea

Ocean currents are like rivers running through the sea. Winds push the surface water, and the turning of the Earth bends its path. Together they make giant loops called gyres, which circle the middle of each big ocean.

The Gulf Stream is one of the fastest currents. It runs up the east coast of the United States and across the Atlantic. In places it flows at up to about 9 km/h, faster than you can walk. A bottle caught in it could reach Europe within a year or so.

The Indian Ocean is unusual. In the north, its currents change direction with the monsoon. In summer they flow one way around India and in winter they turn back. Sailors trading between India, Arabia and East Africa used those seasonal flips for centuries.

Why does the monsoon come?Every year, the wind turns around. And the rain arrives.

The rubber duck voyage

In January 1992, a storm in the North Pacific knocked shipping containers off a cargo ship. One held about 29,000 plastic bath toys: yellow ducks, blue turtles, red beavers and green frogs.

The toys floated off in a crowd. Months later some washed up on the coast of Alaska. Others spent years drifting around the North Pacific gyre. An American oceanographer, Curtis Ebbesmeyer, asked beachcombers to report every toy they found. Their reports helped scientists test computer models of how currents move. The story became a children's book and made the ducks famous.

Messages without bottles

Today, scientists don't need to wait for strangers to find bottles. About 4,000 robot floats, part of a project called Argo, drift through the oceans as of 2025. Every ten days or so, each one sinks to about 2,000 m, measures temperature and saltiness, then rises and beams its data to a satellite. India runs some of them in the Indian Ocean.

Satellites also track buoys on the surface using GPS. In one day they send back more information than all the old bottle experiments put together.

Not such a good idea now

Throwing a bottle into the sea once felt romantic. Now we know how much rubbish ends up in the oceans, and a glass or plastic bottle is one more piece of it. Many beach-cleaning groups ask people to keep their messages on land.

The bottles that do turn up still tell a story. Each one is a record of a long, slow journey that nobody watched.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ocean currents are mostly caused by the tides.

Truth: Tides move water in and out along coasts, but the big ocean currents are driven mainly by wind at the surface. Deep down, slow currents are pushed by differences in how cold and salty the water is.

Try this

Fill a wide baking tray with water and sprinkle a little pepper or tiny bits of paper on top. Blow gently and steadily across one end with a straw. Watch the "sea" start to flow in a loop around the tray. Winds push real ocean currents in much the same way.
