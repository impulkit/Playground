What is Monument Valley?

Twice a year, near the equinoxes, the shadow of one giant red rock creeps across the desert and climbs up the side of its twin.

The short answer

Monument Valley is a stretch of red desert on the border of Arizona and Utah, in the southwestern USA. Huge rock towers rise from the flat sand, some about 300 m tall, as high as a 90-storey building. Geologists call the flat-topped ones mesas and buttes, and the thin ones spires.

The valley lies inside the Navajo Nation, the largest Native American reservation in the USA. The Navajo call it Tsé Biiʼ Ndzisgaii, which means roughly "valley of the rocks". It became famous around the world as the backdrop of old cowboy films, but for the Navajo it has always been home.

Surprising but true

Monument Valley isn't a national park. It belongs to the Navajo Nation, and Navajo families still live and herd sheep among its towers.

Built from ancient sand

The towers are made mostly of sandstone, rock formed from sand pressed and cemented together. Much of it was laid down about 270 million years ago, as dunes and river sand, long before the first dinosaurs. The sandstone is red because tiny bits of iron in it have rusted.

On top of many towers sits a thinner layer of harder rock. That cap acts like a lid. It protects the softer sandstone underneath from rain and wind, as described in the story of how rocks form.

From plateau to pillar

Millions of years ago, this whole area was a flat, high plateau. Rain, frost and wind began cutting cracks into it. Water widened the cracks into gullies, and the gullies grew into valleys.

What was left formed a kind of ladder. A large flat block is a mesa. When erosion shrinks a mesa until it is taller than it is wide, it is called a butte. When a butte wears down to a thin finger, it is a spire. One day, each spire will tumble too. The same slow forces of water and weather explain how cliffs erode everywhere.

Shadows, mittens and a totem pole

The most famous towers are the West and East Mitten Buttes. Seen from the right spot, each looks like a giant mitten, with a thumb sticking up. Near the spring and autumn equinoxes, the late-afternoon shadow of the West Mitten falls across the valley and climbs up the face of the East Mitten.

Another landmark, the Totem Pole, is a spire about 120 m high but only a few metres wide. Climbing it is banned. The Navajo Nation closed the valley's towers to climbers to protect the rock and places that matter to Navajo people. Even so, the Totem Pole appears in many photographs, standing beside a smaller spire nicknamed the Yei Bi Chei.

Hollywood's favourite desert

In 1939, director John Ford filmed Stagecoach here, starring John Wayne. He returned for many more westerns. For millions of people, Monument Valley became the picture of the American West. Many local Navajo people worked on the films as actors, guides and helpers.

Later films went there too. In Forrest Gump (1994), the hero ends his years-long run across America on a road with the towers behind him. That stretch of US Route 163 is now one of the most photographed roads in the country. Visitors often stand in the middle of it, which is dangerous because cars still use it.

A Navajo home

The Navajo Nation covers about 71,000 km² across Arizona, Utah and New Mexico. That is larger than the Indian state of Punjab. In 1958 the Navajo opened Monument Valley as a tribal park, which they still run.

Visitors can drive a dirt loop past many of the buttes. To go deeper into the valley, they must hire a Navajo guide. Some families still live there without mains electricity, herding sheep and weaving rugs. The Navajo language that you might hear in the valley once became an unbreakable military code, a story told in the code talkers.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Monument Valley's rocks were carved by one giant flood.

Truth: They were shaped slowly over millions of years. Rain, frost and wind wore away softer rock, while harder layers on top protected the towers beneath them. The process is still going on, a grain at a time.

Try this

Build a mini butte. Pack damp sand into a cup, turn it out on a tray, and press a flat stone or a coin on top. Pour water gently over it from a cup held above. Watch the unprotected sand wash away while the part under the stone survives, just like a butte with its hard cap.
