Is weight the same as mass?

On the Moon, you would weigh about one-sixth of what you weigh on Earth. But there would be exactly as much of you.

The short answer

In everyday life, people use "weight" and "mass" to mean the same thing. Scientists keep them apart.

Mass is how much matter, or stuff, something contains. It is measured in kilograms. Your mass is the same on Earth, on the Moon or floating in space.

Weight is how hard gravity pulls on that mass. It depends on where you are. On the Moon, gravity is about one-sixth as strong as on Earth, so you'd weigh about one-sixth as much. On Jupiter you'd weigh more than twice as much. Scientists measure weight as a force, in units called newtons.

So when you step on a scale and it says 35 kg, it is really measuring how hard you press down, then turning that into kilograms using Earth's gravity.

Surprising but true

Astronauts on the space station are not out of reach of gravity. Up there it is still about 90% as strong as on the ground. They float because they are falling around the Earth all the time.

The pull of a planet

Gravity depends on how much mass a planet has and how far you are from its centre. The Moon is much smaller than the Earth, so its pull is weaker.

When the Apollo astronauts walked on the Moon between 1969 and 1972, their spacesuits and backpacks had a mass of around 80 kg. On Earth that would have been a crushing load. On the Moon it felt like carrying about 13 kg. The astronauts bounced and hopped across the surface, and sometimes fell over because they misjudged how their bodies would move.

Their mass hadn't changed. A heavy suit was still hard to start moving and hard to stop, even if it felt light to hold up.

Floating in orbit

Astronauts on the International Space Station float around, so it seems as if they have no weight. But the station is only about 400 km up. Gravity there is still about 90% as strong as on the ground.

The station and everyone in it are falling towards the Earth all the time. They are also moving sideways at about 28,000 km/h, so fast that they keep falling around the planet instead of into it. Because everything falls together, nothing presses on anything else. That is why astronauts feel weightless.

Weighing without weight

Doctors on the space station still need to know if astronauts are losing or gaining mass. Ordinary scales don't work when everything is floating.

So NASA uses a device that measures mass instead. An astronaut holds on to a frame attached to a spring. The spring pushes them back and forth, and a computer measures how quickly they move. A bigger mass is harder to shake, so it moves more slowly. From that, the device works out the astronaut's mass in kilograms.

A kilogram made of metal

For 130 years, the kilogram was defined by a single metal cylinder about the size of a golf ball. It was made of platinum and iridium and kept under glass in a vault near Paris. People called it "Le Grand K". Countries kept official copies. India's is kept at the National Physical Laboratory in New Delhi.

The problem was that the cylinder could change. Comparisons over the years showed tiny differences between it and its copies, too small to see but enough to worry scientists.

On 20 May 2019, the kilogram was redefined using a fixed number from nature called the Planck constant. Now any well-equipped laboratory in the world can make a perfect kilogram without comparing it to a lump of metal. The kilogram became the last of the main metric units to be based on nature instead of an object.

Weight in everyday life

When a shopkeeper weighs out a kilo of rice, the scale really measures how hard the rice pulls down. Since everyone in a market is on the same planet, it doesn't matter. Weight and mass go up and down together.

If you took that bag of rice to the Moon, a spring scale would show only about 170 grams. A balance scale, which compares the rice with standard weights, would still show 1 kg, because gravity pulls on both sides equally.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Astronauts float because there is no gravity in space.

Truth: Gravity is everywhere in space. At the height of the space station, it is about 90% as strong as on Earth's surface. Astronauts float because they and the station are falling together around the Earth, moving sideways so fast that they keep missing it.

Try this

Hold a heavy book in one hand and a small ball in the other. Feel how much harder the book pulls down. That pull is weight. Now gently push each one across a smooth table. The book is also harder to get moving, because it has more mass. On the Moon the pull would be weaker, but the push would feel the same.
