How do huge steel ships float?

A steel nail sinks in a bucket of water. A steel ship about as long as four football fields floats. The secret is shape.

The short answer

When a ship sits in water, it pushes some water out of the way. The water pushes back. That upward push is called buoyancy, and it equals the weight of the water the ship has pushed aside. If the ship can push aside its own weight in water before it sinks too deep, it floats.

A solid lump of steel is about eight times heavier than the same amount of water, so it sinks straight away. A ship, though, is not a solid lump. Its steel is spread out into a huge, thin-walled bowl full of air. That bowl pushes aside a lot of water while weighing much less than that water would. Add up the steel, the air, the cargo and the crew, and the whole ship is lighter, for its size, than the sea. Engineers would say its average density is lower than water's.

Surprising but true

A ship floats a little lower in a river than in the sea, because fresh water is less dense than salty water and pushes up less. Painted marks on the side, called load lines, show how deep it may safely sit in each.

Archimedes in the bath

The rule behind this is called Archimedes' principle, after the Greek mathematician from Syracuse. A Roman writer, Vitruvius, told a famous story about him two centuries later. A king wanted to know if his new crown was pure gold or had cheaper silver mixed in. Archimedes, stepping into a full bath, saw water spill over the side. He realised he could measure the crown's volume by the water it pushed aside. Then, the story says, he ran through the streets shouting "Eureka!", meaning "I have found it!"

Historians doubt the bath story happened like that. But Archimedes did write a book on floating bodies, and the principle carries his name.

Giants of the sea

The largest container ships today are about 400 metres long, about as long as four American football fields laid end to end, or nearly 20 cricket pitches. They have room for over 20,000 standard 6-metre steel containers. In March 2021, one of them, the Ever Given, got stuck sideways in the Suez Canal in Egypt and blocked one of the world's busiest shipping routes for six days.

In 2022, India commissioned INS Vikrant, its first aircraft carrier designed and built in the country, at Cochin Shipyard in Kerala. It is 262 metres long and carries fighter jets on its deck, yet it floats as steadily as a much smaller boat.

Floating safely

Too much cargo and a ship sits dangerously low, where waves can wash over it. In the 1800s, many overloaded British ships sank with their crews. A member of parliament named Samuel Plimsoll campaigned for years until a law in 1876 required a mark painted on every British ship's side. Today load lines, often called Plimsoll lines, show the deepest a ship may safely sit, with separate marks for fresh water, salty sea, summer and winter.

Ships are also split inside into watertight compartments, so one hole doesn't flood the whole hull. When the Titanic hit an iceberg in 1912, too many of its compartments were opened to the sea at once, and it sank.

Sinking on purpose

A submarine uses the same rule in both directions. To dive, it lets seawater into special tanks, so it becomes heavier for its size and sinks. To rise, it blows the water out with compressed air, becomes lighter, and floats back up.

Ice floats for a related reason. When water freezes, it expands, becoming a little less dense than liquid water. That's why icebergs float with only about a tenth of their bulk above the waves, and why ships have long kept a careful lookout for them.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Heavy things sink and light things float.

Truth: What matters is how heavy something is for its size, compared with water. A 100,000-tonne ship floats because its hollow hull, full of air, is on average lighter than the same volume of water. A small pebble sinks.

Try this

Make two boats from equal sheets of aluminium foil, one flat like a raft and one with tall sides like a tub. Float them in a bucket or sink and add coins one at a time, spreading them out. Count how many each holds before it sinks. Then squash an empty sheet into a ball and see what it does.
