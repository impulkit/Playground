Who was Sacagawea?

A teenage Shoshone mother crossed the Rocky Mountains with a baby on her back, and helped one of America's most famous expeditions reach the Pacific.

The short answer

Sacagawea was a young Lemhi Shoshone woman who travelled with the Lewis and Clark expedition across North America between 1805 and 1806. She worked as an interpreter, helped the explorers buy horses from her own people, found food plants, and carried her baby son the whole way.

The expedition, called the Corps of Discovery, was sent by US President Thomas Jefferson in 1804 to explore the land west of the Mississippi River. Its goal was to find a route to the Pacific Ocean. About 30 men set out. Sacagawea, a teenager, was the only woman.

Surprising but true

When the expedition heard that a whale had washed up on the Pacific shore, Sacagawea insisted on going to see it. She had travelled thousands of kilometres and had never seen the ocean.

Taken from home

Sacagawea was born around 1788 among the Lemhi Shoshone, in the mountains of today's Idaho. When she was about 12, a raiding party of Hidatsa people captured her and took her far east, to villages along the Missouri River in what is now North Dakota.

There, a French-Canadian fur trader named Toussaint Charbonneau took her as one of his wives. In the winter of 1804, Lewis and Clark built a fort nearby and hired Charbonneau as an interpreter. They wanted Sacagawea too, because she spoke Shoshone, and the expedition would need horses from the Shoshone to cross the mountains.

In February 1805 she gave birth to a son, Jean Baptiste. Two months later, she set off with the expedition, carrying him on her back.

Saving the journals

In May 1805, a sudden wind tipped one of the boats on the Missouri River. The boat began filling with water. While the men panicked, Sacagawea calmly reached out and grabbed papers, instruments and medicine floating away. Among them were parts of the captains' precious journals.

A few days later, the captains named a river after her. They wrote her name many ways in their journals, as they did with many words.

The brother on the mountain

In August 1805, the expedition finally reached Shoshone country. When they met a group of Shoshone, Sacagawea began to dance with joy. Their leader, Cameahwait, turned out to be her own brother, whom she had not seen in years.

Thanks to her, the Shoshone sold the expedition horses and pointed out a way across the Rocky Mountains. Talking was slow. Sacagawea spoke Shoshone and Hidatsa. Charbonneau spoke Hidatsa and French. A soldier spoke French and English. Every sentence passed down a chain of languages.

Clark later wrote that a woman with a baby showed other Native nations that the group was not a war party. A war party would never travel with a mother and child.

Reaching the ocean

In November 1805, the expedition reached the Pacific coast, in today's Oregon and Washington. When they voted on where to spend the winter, both Sacagawea and York, an enslaved Black man in the group, had their say. That was decades before women or Black Americans could vote in US elections.

That winter, news came of a huge whale washed up on a beach. Clark planned to go and get some of its blubber. Sacagawea told him she had come a long way to see the great waters, and now a monstrous fish was there too, so she insisted on going. She saw the skeleton, stretched out on the sand.

What happened to her?

Most historians think Sacagawea died in about 1812 at a fur trading fort on the Missouri, still in her twenties. A clerk wrote down a death that seems to be hers. But some Shoshone tell a different story: that she lived to old age on a reservation in Wyoming and died in 1884.

Clark later paid for her son's schooling in St Louis. In 2000, the United States put a Native woman with a baby on its golden dollar coin in her honour. Nobody knows what she really looked like, so the artist used a modern Shoshone-Bannock woman as the model.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Sacagawea was the expedition's guide and led it all the way across America.

Truth: She was not hired as a guide and did not know most of the route. She was hired as an interpreter, and she did recognise landmarks in her home country, but her biggest help was in speaking with the Shoshone and showing the expedition came in peace.
