Why do we need friends?

A study that has followed the same group of people since 1938 found that good relationships predicted healthy, happy lives better than money or fame.

The short answer

Humans are social animals. For most of our history, people lived in small groups where everyone depended on each other for food, safety and care. Friends helped you survive, so our brains evolved to want company and to feel uneasy when we are alone too long.

Today, friends still matter for our health and happiness. They help us share good news, get through hard times, try new things and laugh. Research suggests that people with close relationships tend to be healthier and live longer. Long-term loneliness, on the other hand, has been linked to health problems.

The 80-year study

In 1938, researchers at Harvard University began following a group of young men, and later added their families. The Harvard Study of Adult Development has checked on them for more than 80 years, through jobs, marriages, wars and old age.

Its present director, psychiatrist Robert Waldinger, says the clearest finding is that people with warm, close relationships were happier and healthier as they aged. Good relationships predicted long, healthy lives better than wealth, fame or social class.

Loneliness and health

In 2023, the US Surgeon General, America's top public health official, issued a warning about loneliness. The report said long-term loneliness can raise the risk of heart disease, stroke and depression. It compared the health risk of serious social isolation to smoking up to 15 cigarettes a day, a figure drawn from a large review of earlier studies.

Feeling lonely sometimes is normal, especially after moving to a new school or city. If loneliness lasts a long time or feels heavy, talking to a parent, teacher or counsellor can help.

How friendships grow

Friendships take time. In one study, researcher Jeffrey Hall estimated it takes about 50 hours together to go from strangers to casual friends, and more than 200 hours to become close friends. Shared activities help: playing on the same team, working on a project, or simply sitting together at lunch every day.

British anthropologist Robin Dunbar argued that most people can keep up roughly 150 meaningful relationships, with only about five in the innermost circle. Other scientists dispute the exact number, but most agree that close friends are few.

Friends in the animal kingdom

Many animals form lasting bonds. Female elephants stay with family and friends for decades, and they greet each other with rumbles and trunk touches after time apart.

Chimpanzees groom friends and back each other up in arguments. Vampire bats, which can die if they go without blood for a couple of nights, share meals with hungry roost-mates. Researchers found they are more likely to help bats that once helped them.

Being a good friend

Friendships are not always easy. Friends argue, drift apart or change as they grow. Psychologists say the friendships that last usually involve trust, listening, keeping promises and saying sorry after a mistake. Kindness helps both sides.

Online friendships can be real and important too, though experts suggest balancing screen time with time spent together in person. A friend who laughs at your jokes, sits with you when you are sad and tells you the truth when you need it is worth more than a long list of contacts.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You need lots of friends to be happy.

Truth: Studies suggest a few close, trusted friends matter more than a large number of acquaintances. Being popular and feeling connected are not the same thing.

Try this

Draw a "friendship map". Put yourself in the middle, then draw circles around you, with closest friends in the first ring, good friends in the next, and people you are friendly with on the outside. Notice how few people are in the middle ring. Most people find just a handful.
