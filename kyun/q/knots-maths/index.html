Is there maths in knots?

Mathematicians have a whole branch of maths about knots, and in it the simplest knot of all is a loop with no knot in it.

The short answer

Yes, quite a lot. Knot theory is a branch of maths that studies knots and how they can be told apart. To a mathematician, a knot is a loop of string with its ends joined, so it can't be untied by pulling an end through.

The big question is simple to ask and hard to answer: are two tangled loops really the same knot, or different ones? Two knots count as the same if you can wiggle one into the other without cutting it. Mathematicians have found clever ways to sort and count knots, and their work is now used to study DNA, chemistry and even the shape of space.

Surprising but true

In four dimensions, every knot in a piece of string can be undone without cutting it. Knots can only exist in three-dimensional space.

The unknot and the trefoil

The simplest knot in knot theory is the unknot: a plain circle, with no knot at all. Any loop that can be wiggled into a circle is an unknot, however messy it looks.

The simplest real knot is the trefoil. Tie an overhand knot, the one you make before tying a bow, and then join the ends. You'll see the string crosses over itself three times. No amount of wiggling will turn it into a circle.

Next comes the figure-eight knot, with four crossings. After that the numbers explode. In 1998, three mathematicians used computers to list every prime knot, one that can't be split into simpler knots, with up to 16 crossings. They found 1,701,936.

Knots and atoms

Knot theory grew from a wrong idea. In the 1860s, the Scottish scientist Lord Kelvin suggested that atoms might be tiny knots in an invisible substance filling space. Different knots, he thought, could be different elements.

His friend Peter Guthrie Tait began drawing tables of knots to test the idea. The atom theory turned out to be false. But Tait's tables started a new branch of maths. In the 1920s, the German mathematician Kurt Reidemeister showed that any wiggle of a knot can be broken into three simple kinds of move, which made knots much easier to study.

Knots inside you

Inside each of your cells, DNA is packed into a space far smaller than a pinhead. If you stretched out the DNA from one cell, it would be about 2 m long. Packed that tightly, it gets tangled and even knotted.

Special proteins called topoisomerases fix this. They cut a strand, pass another strand through the gap and join it back together. Knot theory helps biologists understand how these proteins work, and some medicines work by blocking them in harmful bacteria.

Why earphones tangle

In 2007, two scientists in California tumbled pieces of string in a spinning box and counted how often knots formed. Short strings rarely knotted. Longer, floppy strings knotted often, sometimes within seconds. Once a string's end crosses over another part, a knot can form quickly.

This explains why wires stuffed in a pocket come out in a mess. Many tangles turn out to be unknots, though, which is why a bit of patient wriggling often frees them.

Knots in kolam and sailing

In Tamil Nadu, a kind of kolam called sikku kolam is drawn with lines that loop around a grid of dots without ever ending. The word sikku means a tangle or knot in Tamil. Mathematicians have studied these drawings because each one is a single, unbroken closed loop, a lot like the knot diagrams in a maths book. Patterns like these also show off the symmetry found in rangoli.

Sailors, climbers and Scouts care about knots for practical reasons. Some hold tight under load. Others are easy to untie. The difference between a reef knot and a granny knot is a single crossing, but one holds well and the other can slip.

Still unsolved

Knot theory still has open questions. There's no known quick method that can always tell whether a very tangled loop is secretly an unknot. It's one of many unsolved problems in maths. For a subject that starts with a piece of string, it goes surprisingly deep.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A granny knot is just as good as a reef knot.

Truth: They look almost the same, but they are different knots. A reef knot holds well for tying bandages and bundles. A granny knot can slip or jam. To mathematicians, they are two different combinations of the same trefoil knot.

Try this

Take a shoelace or a piece of string and tie a simple overhand knot. Now tape the two ends together so it's a closed loop. Try to make it a plain circle without untaping. You can't, because you've made a trefoil. Then make a messy loop that looks knotted but isn't, and ask a friend to tell which is which.
