Why do peacocks have such fancy tails?

A tail that big makes it harder to fly and easier to catch. So why have it?

The short answer

Peahens choose their mates. In the breeding season, a male raises his long train into a shimmering fan and shakes it, and females look closely before choosing. Over many generations, males with the most impressive displays had more chicks. So the genes for big, bright trains spread.

Charles Darwin called this sexual selection. It explains why some animals carry features that seem to make life harder, such as giant tails or antlers. The tail is a cost, but if it wins a mate, it is worth paying. When a peacock shakes its fan, it also makes a low rumble too deep for us to hear, and other peafowl seem to respond to it.

Surprising but true

Darwin once wrote that looking at a peacock's tail feather made him feel sick. It didn't fit his theory, until he worked it out.

Not really a tail

The famous fan is not made of tail feathers. It is a train of about 200 long feathers that grow from the bird's lower back, just above the real tail. The short, stiff tail feathers underneath prop the train up when it is raised.

A peacock grows a new train each year and sheds it after the breeding season. Even with a train up to about 1.5 m long, peacocks can fly. They make short, noisy flights up into trees to roost safely at night.

Colours made of light

The blue and green of a peacock's feathers are not mostly paint-like pigments. They come from tiny, regular structures inside the feather that reflect certain colours of light. That is why the colours shift as the bird moves, like a soap bubble. Scientists call this structural colour. It works differently from mixing paint, as you can see in how colours mix.

Some animals use colour to hide, like the tiger in long grass. The peacock does the opposite. Its whole display is made to be seen.

Darwin's headache

In 1860, Darwin wrote to a friend that the sight of a peacock's tail feather made him feel sick. His theory of evolution explained features that help animals survive. A tail like this seemed to do the opposite. A few years later he worked out that choosing mates could shape animals too.

Scientists still argue about the details. In 1991, a study in England found that peahens preferred males with more eyespots on their trains. But a seven-year study of peafowl in Japan, published in 2008, found no clear preference at all. Peahens may judge several things at once, including the sound and the shaking. Some dinosaurs may have shown off too, as explored in dinosaur colours.

A shiver and a rattle

A displaying peacock does more than stand still. He turns his back, then swings round to face the female. He shakes his raised train so fast that the feathers rattle with a soft, shimmering sound. In 2016, scientists filmed this with high-speed cameras. They found that the eyespots stay almost still while the rest of each feather vibrates around them.

Peacocks also have one of the loudest calls in India, a high "may-awe" that carries far. They call often in the breeding season, which in much of India comes with the monsoon. In Indian poems and songs, peacocks dance when dark rain clouds gather, an image that has inspired painters and poets for centuries.

India's national bird

The Indian peafowl was named India's national bird in 1963, and it is fully protected by law. Peacocks appear across Indian art and belief. The god Kartikeya, also called Murugan, rides a peacock, and Krishna is often shown with a peacock feather in his crown. Shah Jahan's jewelled Peacock Throne was named after it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Peahens lay eggs after drinking the tears of a peacock.

Truth: This is an old folk belief, but it isn't true. Peafowl mate like other birds, and the peahen then lays her eggs, usually three to six of them, in a scrape on the ground.

Try this

Look at a peacock feather, or a soap bubble, or the shiny side of an old CD. Tilt it slowly under a lamp and watch the colours change. No paint is moving. Light bouncing off tiny layers creates the colours, the same trick a peacock's feathers use.
