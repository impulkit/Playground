Is the Bermuda Triangle really mysterious?

Five navy planes vanished there in 1945, and so did the plane sent to find them. Yet the Bermuda Triangle doesn't even appear on official maps.

The short answer

No, not really. The Bermuda Triangle is a stretch of the Atlantic Ocean between Miami, the island of Bermuda and Puerto Rico. Stories say ships and planes vanish there without a trace. But when researchers checked the records, they found that disappearances there are no more common than in other busy parts of the ocean.

The real causes of accidents are ordinary, if dangerous: sudden storms, a fast and powerful current, heavy traffic and human mistakes. The "mystery" grew mostly from magazine articles and best-selling books.

Surprising but true

The US Coast Guard and the insurance market Lloyd's of London have both said that ships and planes are no more likely to go missing in the Bermuda Triangle than in other busy stretches of ocean.

How a legend was born

The name was coined in 1964 by a writer called Vincent Gaddis in a magazine article. In 1974, a book by Charles Berlitz called The Bermuda Triangle sold millions of copies. It suggested strange forces, from lost cities to aliens.

The triangle isn't marked on official charts. The US Board on Geographic Names doesn't recognise it. Different writers even draw it in different sizes, from about 1.3 million to nearly 4 million square kilometres. If you're allowed to stretch the borders, more accidents fall inside.

Flight 19

The most famous story happened on 5 December 1945. Five US Navy torpedo bombers, called Flight 19, took off from Florida on a training flight. The flight leader became confused, believing his compass was faulty and that they were over the wrong islands. The planes flew back and forth as the weather worsened and their fuel ran low.

They were never found. Most likely, they ran out of fuel and ditched into a rough, dark sea. A rescue flying boat sent after them also vanished. A passing ship reported seeing flames and an oily patch on the water. Planes of that type were known as "flying gas tanks", because fuel fumes could explode.

The librarian who checked

In the 1970s, an American librarian named Lawrence Kusche decided to look up every case himself. He read old newspapers, weather records and official reports.

He found that the stories had been copied from book to book, growing more dramatic each time. Ships said to vanish on calm days had sunk in storms. Some "Bermuda" losses happened in the Pacific, or near Ireland. A few could not be found in any shipping record at all. His 1975 book was titled The Bermuda Triangle Mystery: Solved.

What makes the sea dangerous there

The Gulf Stream runs through the area. It is one of the strongest currents on Earth, flowing north at up to about 9 km/h. It can push a drifting boat far off course and quickly carry away wreckage.

The region also gets sudden thunderstorms, waterspouts and, from June to November, hurricanes. The sea floor drops into some of the deepest trenches in the Atlantic, more than 8 km down, so wrecks can be very hard to find.

Add thousands of ships, cruise liners, fishing boats and small private planes, many flown by amateurs, and some accidents are certain.

What about the compass?

You may hear that compasses go haywire there. In parts of the region, a compass needle points close to true north, rather than magnetic north. That's not magic. There are lines like that elsewhere on Earth too, and sailors have known about them for centuries.

Another popular idea is that bubbles of methane gas from the sea floor could sink ships. Scientists have not found evidence that this happens in the Bermuda Triangle.

Why the story survives

A good mystery is more fun than a weather report. Once the legend was famous, any accident in the area got linked to it, while accidents elsewhere didn't. That makes the triangle seem deadlier than it is, a trap explained in how true numbers can mislead.

NOAA, the US agency that studies the oceans, says plainly that there is no evidence that disappearances there happen more often. Still, the sea does hold real puzzles. The strangest might be the Mary Celeste, found drifting in 1872 in good shape, with every single person gone.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ships and planes disappear in the Bermuda Triangle far more often than anywhere else.

Truth: Studies by insurers and the US Coast Guard found no extra losses there. It is one of the busiest stretches of ocean in the world, with sudden storms and a fast current, which explains the accidents that do happen.
