How do 3D glasses work?

A 3D film is really two films shown at once. The glasses make sure each of your eyes sees only one of them.

The short answer

Your eyes are about 6 cm apart, so each one sees the world from a slightly different spot. Your brain compares the two views and uses the small differences to work out how far away things are. This is called stereo vision.

A 3D film copies this trick. It is filmed with two cameras side by side, like a pair of eyes. In the cinema, both pictures are shown on the screen at the same time, overlapping. Without glasses, they look blurry and doubled.

The glasses are filters. Each lens lets through only the picture meant for that eye and blocks the other. Your brain receives two slightly different views, combines them as it always does, and the scene appears to have depth.

Surprising but true

India's first 3D feature film was a Malayalam children's film, My Dear Kuttichathan, released in 1984 and later dubbed into Hindi and other languages.

Two eyes, one picture

Hold up a pencil at arm's length and look at it with one eye, then the other. It jumps against the background. Close objects jump a lot, distant ones hardly at all. Your brain measures those jumps, in a fraction of a second, without you noticing.

In 1838, the English scientist Charles Wheatstone built a device called a stereoscope. It used mirrors to show a different drawing to each eye, and viewers saw a single scene with depth. Within a few decades, stereoscopes with pairs of photographs became a popular entertainment in Victorian homes in Britain and America.

Three ways to split the picture

The oldest kind of 3D glasses have one red lens and one blue or cyan lens. The two pictures are printed or shown in matching colours. The red lens blocks one picture, the blue lens blocks the other. They're cheap, but colours look odd.

Most cinemas now use polarised glasses. Light waves can wiggle in different directions. The projector sends one picture with light that twists one way and the other picture twisting the opposite way, and each lens of the glasses lets only one twist through. Colours look normal.

Some 3D televisions used battery-powered shutter glasses. Each lens darkens and clears many times a second, in time with the screen flashing between left and right pictures.

A Malayalam first

3D films have come and gone in waves. Hollywood had a 3D boom in the early 1950s, when studios hoped the novelty would pull families away from their new televisions. Another big wave came in 2009, when the film Avatar became a huge hit in 3D.

In India, the first 3D feature film was My Dear Kuttichathan, made in Malayalam and released in 1984. It told the story of a friendly little spirit and the children who free him. Audiences across South India lined up to wear the glasses. The film was later dubbed into Hindi and other languages and shown across the country.

Why some people can't see it

Not everyone sees 3D films the same way. A small share of people have weak stereo vision, often because their eyes didn't learn to work together properly in early childhood. For them, 3D films look flat or uncomfortable.

Some people also get tired eyes or headaches. In real life, your eyes point at and focus on the same spot. In a 3D film they have to point at objects that seem to float in front of the screen while still focusing on the screen itself. Virtual reality headsets face the same puzzle. If 3D always looks wrong to you, it's worth getting your eyes checked.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Watching 3D films damages children's eyes.

Truth: Eye doctors say there's no evidence that 3D films harm healthy eyes. Some people get tired eyes or headaches, which go away when they stop. Trouble seeing 3D can be a sign of an eye problem that's worth checking with a doctor.

Try this

Hold one finger up about 30 cm from your face, in front of a far-off door frame. Close your left eye, then your right, and switch quickly back and forth. Your finger seems to jump sideways, because each eye sees it from a slightly different spot. That difference is what 3D films copy.
