Why are the ruins of Hampi so famous?

Among giant boulders in Karnataka lie the stone bones of a city that once housed hundreds of thousands of people, with pillars that ring like music.

The short answer

Hampi is the ruin of Vijayanagara, the capital of one of India's greatest empires. For about 200 years, until 1565, it was one of the largest and richest cities in the world.

What makes Hampi special is how much survives and where it sits. Temples, palaces, markets, water tanks, stables and gateways are scattered across about 41 square km of strange, boulder-strewn hills beside the Tungabhadra river. You can walk down a ruined market street several hundred metres long and picture shoppers buying silk and spices. UNESCO listed Hampi as a World Heritage Site in 1986, and travellers from across the world come to explore it.

Surprising but true

The stone chariot at Hampi's Vittala temple appears on the back of India's fluorescent blue ₹50 note.

Boulders older than the dinosaurs

Hampi's landscape looks as though a giant tipped out a bag of marbles. Huge rounded boulders balance on hilltops, sometimes on tiny points.

They are granite, formed deep underground billions of years ago. Over enormous spans of time, the rock cracked into blocks, and rain and wind wore away their corners until they became rounded. The builders of Vijayanagara used this stone for almost everything, so the rocks of the hills and the walls of the city are the same.

A chariot made of stone

The most famous building at Hampi is the Vittala temple, begun in the 15th century and expanded under King Krishnadevaraya. In its courtyard stands a shrine carved in the shape of a temple chariot, complete with stone wheels.

It was built for Garuda, the eagle who carries the god Vishnu. Though it looks as if it could roll away, it is made of granite blocks fitted so neatly that the joins are easy to miss. Real temple chariots in south India are made of wood and pulled through the streets at festivals, so this one copies them in stone. The chariot has become a symbol of Karnataka. Since 2017 it has appeared on the back of India's ₹50 note, so millions of people carry a picture of Hampi in their pockets.

Pillars that sing

The Vittala temple's main hall has slender stone pillars clustered around thicker ones. When struck gently, many give clear musical notes, like a giant stone xylophone.

The sound depends on each pillar's shape, size and thickness, just as a short string gives a higher note than a long one. Tapping caused damage over the years, so visitors may no longer strike them. You can still stand in the hall and picture musicians and dancers performing there.

Not just temples

Hampi was a working city. There are elephant stables with domed rooms big enough for royal elephants, and the Lotus Mahal, a graceful pavilion with arches shaped like petals.

A huge stepped tank, built of neatly fitted green stone, once held water for ceremonies. Stone channels carried water across the royal area. The Virupaksha temple, whose history goes back before the empire, is still a busy place of worship, and its tall gateway towers over the old bazaar.

Monkeys, legends and the Ramayana

Across the river lies Anegundi, which many people identify with Kishkindha, the kingdom of the monkey heroes in the Ramayana. Anjanadri hill nearby is believed by many to be the birthplace of Hanuman, and pilgrims climb its 500-plus steps.

Real monkeys are everywhere at Hampi, leaping between ruins and snatching snacks from tourists. After 1565, they and the farmers had the city largely to themselves. Some villagers still live and farm among the ruins, growing bananas and sugarcane between the temples.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Battle of Talikota was fought in the streets of Hampi.

Truth: The battle was fought far to the north, near the Krishna river. After the army lost, the undefended city was looted over several months and then abandoned.
