What is Ranthambore?

In Ranthambore, tigers walk past the ruins of a thousand-year-old fort, and a temple inside it receives wedding invitations by post.

The short answer

Ranthambore is a tiger reserve and national park in eastern Rajasthan, about 160 km from Jaipur, where two ancient hill ranges, the Aravallis and the Vindhyas, meet. It is dry, rocky forest, dotted with lakes and crossed by old stone walls.

It's one of the best places in the world to see a wild tiger, because its tigers are used to safari jeeps and often walk about in daylight. Rising out of the forest is Ranthambore Fort, a huge hilltop fortress first built over a thousand years ago. Today the fort and the forest share the same land: temples and gateways above, tigers, leopards and crocodiles below.

Surprising but true

In 2008, tigers from Ranthambore were moved by helicopter to Sariska, another reserve in Rajasthan that had lost all of its own tigers to poachers. It was India's first tiger relocation of its kind.

A fort on a hill

The fort sits on a rocky hill about 200 m above the plains. Its walls run for several kilometres and enclose palaces, step-wells, temples and water tanks. It is believed to have been started in the 10th century by Chauhan Rajput rulers.

Its most famous moment came in 1301, when Sultan Alauddin Khalji of Delhi besieged it. The ruler, Hammir Dev Chauhan, held out for months before the fort fell. His story is still told in Rajasthani ballads.

In 2013, the fort became part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site, along with five other hill forts of Rajasthan. Inside, the Trinetra Ganesh temple receives wedding invitations from across India, sent by families hoping for the god's blessing.

From hunting ground to safe ground

For centuries, the forest was a private hunting ground of the maharajas of Jaipur. Kings, and later British officials and foreign royals, came to shoot tigers here.

In 1973, India launched Project Tiger to save its fast-shrinking tiger population, and Ranthambore became one of the first tiger reserves. It was made a national park in 1980. Several villages inside it were moved out, with the government offering land and money elsewhere, though some families felt they lost a great deal.

Under protection, the forest recovered. Much of the credit goes to forest officers such as Fateh Singh Rathore, who led the reserve for years, chased off poachers and was once badly beaten by angry villagers. Grass grew back where cattle had grazed it bare, and deer numbers rose. Tigers that had once hidden by day began to roam openly, and wildlife photographers started to arrive from around the world.

The queen of the lakes

Ranthambore's most famous tiger was a tigress called Machli, which means "fish" in Hindi, after a fish-shaped marking on her face. She ruled the area around the park's lakes for years and raised several litters of cubs.

In 2003, she was filmed fighting a mugger crocodile about 4 m long, and she won. Machli became one of the most photographed tigers on Earth. She died in 2016, aged about 19, a very old age for a wild tiger. Many of today's Ranthambore tigers are her descendants.

Tigers to spare

Ranthambore's success brought a new problem. Young tigers need territory of their own, and the reserve's limited habitat began to fill up. Some wandered into farmland and villages. By 2005, poachers had wiped out every tiger in Sariska, another Rajasthan reserve. From 2008, wildlife officials began moving Ranthambore tigers there, and later to other reserves.

Recent counts put Ranthambore's tigers at roughly 75 to 90, including cubs. India as a whole had about 3,700 wild tigers in its 2022 count, around three-quarters of all the world's wild tigers, from Ranthambore's dry hills to the mangroves of the Sundarbans.

Leopards, sloth bears, sambar and spotted deer, wild boar and hundreds of kinds of birds live in Ranthambore too. Most visitors come for one animal, though, and many leave with a story of a tiger strolling past a crumbling stone gate.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Tigers only hunt at night.

Truth: Tigers are often active at dusk, dawn and night, but they will hunt whenever they get a chance. In Ranthambore, where they are well protected, tigers have often been seen hunting deer by the lakes in broad daylight.

Try this

Make a "tiger ID card". Draw a tiger's face, then give it a unique pattern of stripes above the eyes. Swap with a friend and try to recognise each other's tigers. Forest guards and scientists identify real tigers the same way, because every tiger's stripes are different.
