What are Mughal miniature paintings?

Some Mughal painters used brushes so fine they were made from a few hairs of a squirrel's tail.

The short answer

Mughal miniatures are small, richly detailed paintings made for the Mughal emperors of India, mostly between the 1500s and 1700s. They were painted on paper with brushes and bright colours, often to illustrate books of history, poems and stories.

They were not meant to hang on a wall. An emperor would turn the pages of an album or a book, studying each picture up close. That is why the detail is so fine: a single painting can show every leaf on a tree, the pattern on a carpet and the whiskers on a cat.

Surprising but true

One of the best paintings of a living dodo was probably made in Jahangir's workshop in India, from a bird brought from Mauritius.

Painters who travelled from Persia

The story begins with the emperor Humayun. After losing his throne in the 1540s, he spent years in exile at the court of the Shah of Persia (modern Iran). There he admired the court painters, and he invited two of them, Mir Sayyid Ali and Abd al-Samad, to join him.

When Humayun won back his kingdom, these artists came to India. They trained Indian painters, and the styles mixed. Persian elegance met Indian colour and storytelling, and later even ideas from European prints brought by traders. The result was something new.

Akbar's giant workshop

Humayun's son Akbar became a great patron and turned painting into a big project. He set up a workshop, called a karkhana, where more than a hundred artists worked. Many were Hindus from Gujarat, Gwalior and elsewhere, working alongside painters with Persian training.

Their most famous task was the Hamzanama, the adventures of a hero called Amir Hamza. It had about 1,400 paintings on cloth, each roughly the size of a newspaper page, and took around fifteen years. Akbar, who struggled to read, liked to have stories read aloud while the pictures were held up. Only a small number of these pages survive today, scattered across museums in several countries.

What happened to the dodo?Less than a hundred years after sailors first saw a dodo, there were none left anywhere on Earth.

A naturalist emperor

Akbar's son Jahangir had a different passion. He loved animals, birds and flowers, and he kept a diary full of careful notes about them. He asked his painters to record creatures exactly as they looked.

His artist Ustad Mansur painted a zebra sent from Africa, a turkey from the Americas and many Indian birds. A painting of a dodo, a bird from the island of Mauritius, is linked to his workshop and is one of the few made from a living bird. Scientists still study it, because the dodo vanished soon after.

Squirrel hair and lapis blue

The tools were as fine as the paintings. Brushes were made from squirrel or kitten hair, sometimes trimmed to just a few hairs for the tiniest details. Paper was made from cloth fibres, then rubbed with a smooth stone until it was glossy enough for paint to glide across.

The colours, or pigments, came from minerals, plants and metals. The deep blue was ground from lapis lazuli, a stone brought from Afghanistan. Real gold was mixed into paint or laid on in thin sheets. Painters built up colour in thin layers and polished the surface between them. Some pages took weeks.

What came after

As the Mughal empire weakened in the 1700s, many painters moved to smaller royal courts in Rajasthan and the Himalayan hills. There, Rajput and Pahari painting grew from the same roots, with bolder colours and many scenes of Krishna. Mughal artists never used strict perspective the way European painters did. They often showed a courtyard from above and the people in it from the side, so you could see everything at once.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Miniature paintings are called that because the people in them are tiny.

Truth: The name comes from their small size and fine detail, meant to be held in the hands and looked at closely. Some Mughal paintings were actually quite large, like the Hamzanama pages, which are about 70 cm tall.

Try this

Make a mini painting no bigger than a playing card. Use the finest brush or sharpest coloured pencil you have and fill it with detail, adding a decorated border around the edge. Then look at it through a magnifying glass, the way people once studied Mughal albums up close.
