What did the Portuguese do in India?

The Portuguese were the first Europeans to reach India by sea, in 1498, and the last to leave. They held on to Goa until 1961.

The short answer

In 1498 the Portuguese explorer Vasco da Gama sailed around Africa and landed near Kozhikode (Calicut) in Kerala. Portugal wanted a sea route to buy spices directly, without going through the Arab and Venetian traders who controlled the old routes. Within a few years, Portuguese warships were trying to control trade along India's west coast.

They captured Goa in 1510 and built forts and trading posts from Gujarat to Sri Lanka. They brought Catholic missionaries, new crops and new words. Their rule was often harsh, especially towards people of other faiths. Goa stayed Portuguese for over 450 years, and Daman and Diu for about 400, until India took them in 1961.

Surprising but true

The Hindi words for key (chabi), bucket (balti), towel (tauliya) and pineapple (ananas) all came into Indian languages through Portuguese.

A route around Africa

For centuries, Indian pepper, cinnamon and cloves reached Europe through long chains of traders across the Arabian Sea, Egypt and the Mediterranean. Each middleman added to the price. Portugal, a small country on the edge of Europe, decided to go around them.

Vasco da Gama's four ships left Lisbon in 1497. With help from a local pilot who knew the monsoon winds, they crossed from East Africa to India in under four weeks. The ruler of Calicut, called the Zamorin, was not impressed by the cheap gifts da Gama offered. But the spices da Gama carried home were worth many times the cost of the voyage.

Guns, forts and passes

The Portuguese did not just trade. They used cannons on their ships to force their way. Afonso de Albuquerque, a Portuguese commander, seized Goa in 1510 and made it the capital of Portuguese Asia. He also captured Hormuz in the Persian Gulf and Malacca in Southeast Asia, to control key sea lanes.

At sea, they made Indian and Arab ships buy a pass, called a cartaz, to sail safely. Ships without one could be attacked. Local rulers such as the Zamorins of Calicut, whose admirals the Kunjali Marakkars fought the Portuguese at sea for nearly a century, resisted fiercely.

Why does Indian food use so many spices?Black pepper from Kerala was once so valuable that Roman ships sailed across the ocean to buy it with gold.

Churches and the Inquisition

Portuguese rule brought Catholic missionaries. Francis Xavier, a Spanish priest who worked in Goa and along the south Indian coast in the 1540s, is buried in the Basilica of Bom Jesus in Old Goa. The churches and convents of Old Goa are now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

There was also a darker side. From 1560, the Goa Inquisition, a church court, persecuted Hindus, Muslims, Jews and new converts accused of keeping old practices. Many temples were destroyed. Many Goan families moved away to keep their faith. The Inquisition was finally ended in 1812.

Chillies, cashews and pav

Portuguese ships carried plants from the Americas to India. Chillies, which now flavour almost every Indian kitchen, arrived this way in the 1500s. So did cashews, papayas, pineapples, guavas and potatoes. The cashew trees planted to hold Goa's soil now support a whole industry, including the local drink feni.

The Portuguese also taught bakers in Goa to make leavened bread. The soft bread roll called pav takes its name from the Portuguese pão. Mumbai's vada pav and pav bhaji owe their bread to that history. Portuguese-style dishes like vindaloo grew from Portuguese recipes mixed with Goan spices.

Words, names and an ending

Portuguese words slipped into Hindi, Marathi, Konkani, Bengali and other languages: chabi for key, almari for cupboard, balti for bucket, girja for church and mistri for a skilled worker. Many Goan, Mangalorean and Kerala Christian families still carry Portuguese surnames such as D'Souza, Fernandes and Pereira.

As other European powers grew, Portugal lost most of its Indian territories. By the 1900s it held only Goa, Daman, Diu and Dadra and Nagar Haveli. After independence, India asked Portugal to leave, but it refused. In December 1961, Indian troops entered Goa, and Portuguese rule ended within two days, a story told in Goa's liberation. Today Goa is India's smallest state by area, and its whitewashed churches, red-tiled houses and Konkani songs still carry traces of those 450 years.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The British were the first Europeans to rule part of India.

Truth: The Portuguese arrived first, in 1498, and controlled Goa from 1510, more than 100 years before the English East India Company set up its first factory. They also left last, in 1961.
