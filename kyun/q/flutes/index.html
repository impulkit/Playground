How does a flute make music?

The oldest flutes ever found were carved from bird bones in a German cave more than 35,000 years ago, and they still work on the same idea as a bamboo bansuri.

The short answer

A flute makes music by setting a column of air vibrating inside a tube. The player blows a thin stream of breath across a sharp edge, such as the rim of the mouth hole on a bansuri. The stream flips rapidly from one side of the edge to the other, hundreds of times a second.

Those flips push and pull on the air inside the tube, and the air starts to vibrate. How fast it vibrates depends on how long the column is. The finger holes let the player change that length, and so play different notes.

Surprising but true

A flute has no string, reed or skin that vibrates. The instrument's real "string" is the column of air inside the tube.

Why length matters

Sound is a wave of pushes travelling through air. In a tube, the waves bounce back from the open end and settle into a steady pattern. A long tube holds a long wave, which wobbles slowly and makes a low note. A short tube holds a short wave and a high note.

When you lift a finger, air escapes through that hole, and the tube acts as if it ends there. Open more holes and the "tube" gets shorter, so the note rises. Blow a little harder and the air can jump to a pattern with twice as many wobbles, playing the same note an octave higher. This is how a flute with only six holes can play more than two octaves.

Bones, bamboo and silver

The oldest flutes yet found come from caves in southwestern Germany. One was carved from the wing bone of a griffon vulture, with five finger holes, and is thought to be about 35,000 to 40,000 years old. People were making music on flutes long before they were farming or writing.

Since then, almost every culture has made some kind of flute. Japan has the bamboo shakuhachi. Native American peoples made flutes of cedar and river cane. Children in many US schools learn the recorder, which has a little channel that shapes the breath for you, much like a whistle.

The bansuri

In India, the flute is bound up with stories of Krishna, who is often shown playing one while cows and people gather to listen. The bansuri is made from a single piece of bamboo, with one hole for blowing and usually six or seven for fingers. Players hold it sideways.

For a long time it was mainly a folk instrument. In the mid-1900s, Pannalal Ghosh made longer, deeper bansuris and showed that the flute could play full ragas in Hindustani classical music. Later, Hariprasad Chaurasia carried its sound to concert halls around the world. In South India, the shorter Carnatic flute, often called the venu, was made famous by the brilliant T. R. Mahalingam, known as Mali. Both styles are part of the wider story of Carnatic and Hindustani music.

The metal flute

A Western concert flute is usually made of metal, often silver-plated. In the 1840s, a German flute maker named Theobald Boehm redesigned it. He used larger holes placed where the physics said they should go, and added a system of keys so fingers could close holes too big or far apart to reach. Most flutes in orchestras today still use his design.

It is still grouped with the woodwinds, because early flutes were wooden and because the way it makes sound, with breath split on an edge, is the same. A bansuri player and an orchestral flautist are doing the same thing with their breath, just with different tubes.

Breath and patience

Playing a flute well takes control. The player shapes the lips, aims the air at just the right angle and keeps it steady. Indian flautists also slide their fingers half-way across holes to bend notes smoothly, which gives the bansuri its singing, almost human sound.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The sound of a flute comes from the flute itself shaking.

Truth: The tube hardly vibrates at all. The sound comes from the air inside it, which wobbles back and forth when the player's breath is split on a sharp edge.

Try this

Line up four or five glass bottles and fill them with different amounts of water. Blow gently across the top of each one, as if you were cooling soup. The bottle with the most air inside, and least water, gives the lowest note. You have made a simple flute.
