What is a cooperative?

In 1959, seven women on a Mumbai rooftop borrowed ₹80 and started rolling papads. Today tens of thousands of women own and run Lijjat together.

The short answer

A cooperative, or co-op, is a business owned and run by the people who use it. Instead of investors owning it to earn a profit, the members own it together. They might be farmers selling milk, shoppers buying groceries, workers running a factory or neighbours sharing a bank.

Each member usually gets one vote, no matter how much money they have put in. Any surplus is either reinvested or shared among members, often according to how much each one used the co-op. A cooperative lets ordinary people do together what none of them could afford to do alone.

Surprising but true

The Associated Press, one of the world's biggest news agencies, is a cooperative owned by its member newspapers and broadcasters.

Twenty-eight weavers in Rochdale

In the 1840s, workers in the English mill town of Rochdale were poor and often cheated by shopkeepers. Flour was mixed with chalk, and scales were rigged. During the Industrial Revolution, many working families had no one to complain to.

So in 1844, a group of 28 men, many of them weavers, saved up and opened their own shop on Toad Lane. At first it sold only butter, sugar, flour, oatmeal and candles. They sold honest goods at fair weights, shared the profits among members, and gave each member one vote. Their rules became the model for cooperatives around the world.

India's co-op story

India passed its first cooperative law in 1904, mainly to set up credit societies so that farmers could borrow without falling into the hands of moneylenders. After independence, cooperatives spread into sugar mills, fertiliser, housing and banks. In 1950, farmers near Loni in Maharashtra opened one of the first cooperative sugar factories in the country. Hundreds followed across the state, and the farmers who ran them became powerful local leaders. The fertiliser maker IFFCO, founded in 1967, is also owned by thousands of farmer co-ops.

The most famous is the dairy cooperative movement started in Anand, Gujarat, in 1946, which grew into Amul. Milk co-ops later spread across India during Operation Flood and helped make India the world's largest milk producer. In 2021, the Government of India set up a separate Ministry of Cooperation to look after the sector.

How did India become the world's top milk producer?India makes about a quarter of the world's milk, and most of it comes from small farmers with just two or three animals each.

Seven women and a rooftop

In 1959, seven women living in a crowded building in Girgaum, Mumbai, wanted to earn a little money without leaving their homes. They borrowed ₹80 and began rolling papads on their terrace.

They set strict rules. Every papad had to taste the same. Every member was an owner, called a sister, and nobody was just an employee. The organisation, Shri Mahila Griha Udyog Lijjat Papad, grew branch by branch across India. Today it is run by tens of thousands of women, who still mostly roll papads at home and bring them to a centre each day.

Co-ops in America and beyond

In the USA, about one in three people belongs to a credit union, a bank owned by its customers. Cranberry farmers own Ocean Spray, and the news agency Associated Press is a cooperative of newspapers and broadcasters. Many rural American homes still get their electricity from co-ops formed in the 1930s, when private companies didn't think it was worth running wires to farms.

The International Cooperative Alliance says co-ops around the world have more than a billion members. The United Nations named 2012, and again 2025, the International Year of Cooperatives. Even Mumbai's dabbawalas share their earnings as partners rather than as staff of a single boss.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Cooperatives are small, old-fashioned groups that can't compete with big companies.

Truth: Some of the world's best-known brands are cooperatives. Amul in India, Ocean Spray cranberry juice in the USA and many large farm, banking and shopping groups are owned by their members.

Try this

Start a class book cooperative. Each member brings one or two books they've finished and puts them on a shared shelf. Members borrow freely and vote together, one person one vote, on rules like how long you can keep a book. See whether the shelf grows.
