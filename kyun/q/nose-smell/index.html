How does your nose smell?

In a town in Uttar Pradesh, perfume makers bottle the smell of the first monsoon rain, by baking clay and catching its scent.

The short answer

Smells are tiny invisible particles floating in the air. When you breathe in, some of them drift to a small patch high up inside your nose, about the size of a postage stamp. It's covered in millions of nerve cells with tiny hairs that poke into a layer of mucus.

Those hairs carry receptors, little docking stations that catch particular kinds of smell molecules. Humans have about 400 different kinds. When a molecule docks, the nerve cell fires a signal straight up into the brain.

Most smells are made of many different molecules at once. Your brain reads the pattern of which receptors fire, a bit like recognising a song from its notes, and names the smell.

Surprising but true

African elephants have about 2,000 genes for smell receptors, roughly five times as many as humans and twice as many as dogs.

A direct line to feelings

Signals from your nose go first to a part of the brain called the olfactory bulb. From there they pass close to the areas that handle emotions and memories. That's why a smell can bring back a memory so strongly. The scent of agarbatti, of a grandmother's kitchen, or of a new school book can take you back years in a single breath.

Other senses take a longer route through the brain before reaching those areas. Shops and hotels know this, and some pump gentle scents into their lobbies to put customers in a good mood.

The smell of rain

The earthy smell when rain hits dry ground has a name: petrichor. Two Australian scientists, Isabel Bear and Richard Thomas, coined it in 1964. Part of the smell comes from a chemical called geosmin, made by bacteria living in soil. Human noses can pick up geosmin even when only a tiny trace is in the air.

In Kannauj, in Uttar Pradesh, perfume makers have long made mitti attar, "earth perfume". They bake discs of clay from dried ponds and distil the scent, catching the smell of the first monsoon shower in a bottle.

Why does the monsoon come?Every year, the wind turns around. And the rain arrives.

Not as bad as we thought

People often say humans have a poor sense of smell. In 2017, an American neuroscientist named John McGann looked at the evidence and found this was mostly a myth from the 1800s. In tests, people can follow a scent trail across a field, a bit like a dog. We're especially good at some smells, like the scent of a banana.

Dogs are still far better at many smells. They have many more smell cells than we do, which is why they're trained to sniff out lost people and hidden things at airports. Elephants may be champions, with more smell genes than any animal tested.

More than smelling

Your nose also warms and moistens the air you breathe, and its hairs and mucus trap dust and germs. A sudden tickle can set off a sneeze, blasting out whatever is irritating it.

Smell keeps you safe too. Cooking gas has almost no smell of its own, so gas companies add a strong-smelling chemical. If there's a leak, your nose warns you.

Losing your sense of smell

A cold can block smell for a few days. Some people lose their sense of smell for longer, for example after certain infections such as COVID-19. Food then tastes flat, because flavour depends so much on smell. Doctors sometimes suggest "smell training", sniffing a few strong scents every day, and the nose's smell cells are replaced every month or two, which may help some people recover.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Humans have a terrible sense of smell compared with other animals.

Truth: Humans can smell some things as well as dogs can, and better than many animals. A neuroscientist reviewing the evidence in 2017 traced the idea back to the 1800s, when a French scientist assumed our small smell area meant a weak sense.

Try this

Ask an adult to help you fill five small cups with different smelly kitchen things, such as cardamom, coffee, lemon peel, mint and cinnamon. Cover each with a cloth. Close your eyes and sniff one at a time. Try to name each smell, then see if you can describe what each one reminds you of.
