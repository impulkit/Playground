How do ants work together?

No ant is in charge. Not even the queen. Yet a colony of a million ants can build, farm, fight and find food together.

The short answer

Ants work together by following simple rules and talking with smells. Each ant reacts to what is right around it: the scent of a trail, the touch of another ant, a crumb of food. No ant sees the big picture, and no ant gives orders.

But when thousands of ants follow the same simple rules, the colony as a whole acts cleverly. It finds the shortest path to food, builds nests with tunnels and chambers, and sends more workers wherever they're needed. Scientists sometimes call a colony a superorganism, because it behaves almost like one big animal made of many small bodies.

Surprising but true

A 2022 study estimated that about 20 quadrillion ants live on Earth, roughly 2.5 million for every person.

Talking in smells

Ants communicate mostly with chemicals called pheromones. They give off different ones for different messages. One means "food this way". Another means "danger, come and help". Ants also recognise their nestmates by smell, so a stranger from another colony is spotted instantly.

When a worker finds food, it heads home laying a trail of scent with the tip of its body. Other ants pick up the scent with their antennae and follow it. If they find food, they add their own scent on the way back. A good food source quickly gets a strong trail with hundreds of ants. When the food runs out, ants stop topping up the scent, and the trail fades within minutes.

Shortcuts without maps

This simple system finds short routes. Ants that happen to take a shorter path get back faster, so their trail gets more scent sooner. More ants choose it, and it becomes stronger still. Longer paths slowly lose out.

Computer scientists noticed. In the early 1990s, a researcher named Marco Dorigo wrote "ant colony" algorithms, computer programs in which imaginary ants leave digital scent. These programs are used to plan delivery routes and network traffic.

What is an algorithm?You follow algorithms every day, from brushing your teeth to finding a word in a dictionary. Computers just follow them faster.

Jobs in the colony

Most ants in a colony are female workers. Young workers often stay inside, caring for eggs and larvae. Older ones go outside to forage and defend, which is riskier. In some species, workers come in different sizes. Big-headed soldiers guard the nest, and tiny ones tend the young.

The queen's main job is laying eggs, sometimes for many years. Male ants live only a short time. Their role is to mate with new queens, which then fly off to start colonies of their own.

Builders and farmers

In India and southeast Asia, weaver ants make nests high in mango and other trees. Workers form a living chain, gripping each other's waists, to pull two leaves together. Then other workers hold larvae against the leaf edges. The larvae give out silk, and the ants use them like tubes of glue to stitch the leaves shut. Some people in eastern India eat these ants as a spicy chutney.

In the Americas, leafcutter ants carry pieces of leaves underground. They don't eat the leaves. They chew them up to grow a fungus, which they then eat, much like farmers growing a crop. Termites in Africa and Asia farm fungus too, though they are not close relatives of ants.

Living bridges and rafts

Army ants in Central and South America have no fixed nest. When they reach a gap, some workers link their bodies to make a bridge for the others to cross. Fire ants, during floods, grip each other to form a floating raft with the queen safe in the middle.

There are so many ants that their total weight is thought to be greater than that of all wild birds and wild mammals put together. They turn soil, spread seeds and clean up dead insects. Look under any stone in a garden and you're likely to find a colony at work.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The queen ant rules the colony and tells the other ants what to do.

Truth: The queen is the colony's mother, not its boss. She lays the eggs. The work of building, foraging and defending is organised by thousands of workers following simple rules and reacting to smells and touch.

Try this

On a balcony or in a garden, with an adult nearby, put a tiny pinch of sugar on one piece of paper and a pinch of salt on another, a little way from an ant trail. Check every ten minutes. Then gently wipe your finger across the trail and watch what the ants do when the scent is broken.
