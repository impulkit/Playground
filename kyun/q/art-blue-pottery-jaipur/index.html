What is Jaipur's blue pottery?

Jaipur's famous blue pottery isn't made from ordinary potter's clay. It is made mostly from crushed quartz stone and powdered glass.

The short answer

Jaipur blue pottery is a glazed craft famous for its bright cobalt blue and turquoise patterns of flowers, birds and swirling vines on a white background. Unlike almost every other kind of pottery, it isn't made from ordinary potter's clay, and the only clay in it is a little Multani mitti. The dough is a mix of quartz powder, powdered glass, Multani mitti (Fuller's earth), borax and plant gum.

Artisans shape this dough in moulds, paint it by hand and fire it at low heat. The result is light, shiny and stays cool to the touch, which made it popular for water pots in hot Rajasthan. Today you will see it as vases, plates, doorknobs, tiles and tiny beads, sold in Jaipur's markets and shops around the world.

Surprising but true

In the 1950s the craft had almost died out in Jaipur. An artist named Kripal Singh Shekhawat helped bring it back, with support from Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay and Rajmata Gayatri Devi.

A craft that travelled

The story begins with the shining blue-tiled mosques and palaces of Persia and Central Asia, where craftsmen perfected blue glazes over many centuries. The technique of making glazed tiles and pots travelled into India with Turkic and Mughal rulers. You can see similar blue tiles on old buildings in Lahore, Delhi and elsewhere in north India.

Jaipur's own tradition grew in the 1800s. Sawai Ram Singh II, who ruled Jaipur from 1835 to 1880, loved the craft and sent local artisans to learn it. He set up a school of art in the city that helped the craft grow.

How a pot is made

First, the ingredients are ground, mixed with water and kneaded into a stiff dough. Because there is no clay, it is hard to shape on a wheel. Instead, artisans roll it flat like a roti and press it into moulds, then fill the inside with ash or sand to support it while it dries.

Once dry, pieces are smoothed, coated in a white base layer and painted with brushes made of squirrel hair or goat hair. The blue comes from cobalt oxide, and the green from copper oxide. Finally, a glassy glaze is added and the piece is fired once in a kiln at about 800 °C, much cooler than the heat used for stoneware.

Nearly lost

By the middle of the 20th century, blue pottery had almost disappeared from Jaipur. Few artisans remained, and cheaper factory goods took over. Then the painter Kripal Singh Shekhawat, who had studied art in Shantiniketan and Japan, began working with the old techniques in the 1950s and 1960s.

He had important supporters. Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay, a freedom fighter who worked to revive Indian crafts, and Rajmata Gayatri Devi of Jaipur encouraged the effort. Shekhawat added new colours and designs and trained many younger artisans. He was awarded the Padma Shri in 1974.

Why it cracks

The glass and quartz give blue pottery its glassy shine, but they also make it delicate. Without clay to stretch and bend, the dough cracks easily as it dries, and some pieces break in the kiln. Artisans expect to lose a share of every batch.

That fragility helps explain why it's handmade in small workshops, not mass-produced in big factories. Each piece is slightly different, and many carry the potter's signature.

Blue pottery today

In 2008, Blue Pottery of Jaipur received a Geographical Indication tag, which protects the name for pottery made in the Jaipur region using the traditional method. Hundreds of artisans work in and around the city, many in the Kot Jewar area and in Jaipur's old city.

Designers now mix tradition with new ideas, from fridge magnets to light fittings. Visitors to Jaipur can watch artisans at work in their studios. And the bright blue on every piece connects to a much older story of colour, glaze and trade stretching back to ancient Persia.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Blue pottery is an ancient art that has been made in Jaipur for thousands of years.

Truth: The technique reached India from Persia and Central Asia over several centuries, and Jaipur's workshops date mainly from the 1800s. The craft nearly vanished in the 20th century and was revived in the 1950s and 1960s.
