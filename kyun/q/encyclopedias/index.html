How were encyclopedias made?

In 1408, more than 2,000 scholars in China finished an encyclopedia of over 11,000 volumes. Fewer than 4 in 100 survive today.

The short answer

An encyclopedia tries to gather what is known about many subjects in one place, arranged so you can find things. For most of history this was done by hand. Teams of scholars wrote, copied and checked thousands of entries, then sorted them by topic or alphabet. Printing made it possible to sell copies widely. Computers and the internet changed it again.

The idea is very old. Ancient Romans, Chinese emperors, Arab scholars and Indian kings all made collections of knowledge. The modern word comes from Greek words meaning something like "all-round learning".

Surprising but true

The Roman writer Pliny the Elder, who wrote one of the first encyclopedias, died in 79 CE after sailing towards Mount Vesuvius as it erupted.

Pliny and his 20,000 facts

Around 77 CE, a Roman officer called Pliny the Elder finished his Natural History in 37 books. He said it held 20,000 facts drawn from about 2,000 books. It covered stars, animals, plants, metals and medicines.

Some of it was wrong. Pliny believed in people with one giant foot they used as a sunshade. But it was copied for over a thousand years. Pliny commanded a fleet near Naples, and in 79 CE he sailed towards Mount Vesuvius during the eruption that buried Pompeii. He died on the shore.

Eleven thousand volumes

In 1403 the Chinese emperor Yongle ordered scholars to collect all knowledge into one work. By 1408 more than 2,000 people had produced the Yongle Encyclopedia: 11,095 handwritten volumes. Only the original and one later copy were ever made, because it was far too big to print.

Wars and fires destroyed most of it. Today only about 400 volumes survive, scattered across libraries in China, Europe and the USA.

Its makers did not write new essays. They copied whole books and passages from older works, sorted under topics. Because of that, the surviving volumes preserve pieces of books that are otherwise lost.

What happened to the Library of Alexandria?The ancient world's most famous library did not vanish in a single dramatic fire. It faded away over hundreds of years.

A king's guide to everything

In India, the Western Chalukya king Someshvara III wrote the Manasollasa in Sanskrit in about 1129. It is often called an encyclopedia. It covers ruling a kingdom, cooking, music, painting, horses, elephants, games and even how to enjoy a garden.

It is one of the best records of what people ate in medieval south India, including recipes for dishes that sound like early versions of idli and dosa.

Printed in parts

In France in the 1750s, Denis Diderot and Jean d'Alembert published the Encyclopédie in 28 volumes, with beautiful drawings of machines and crafts. Its ideas annoyed the king and the church, and it was banned more than once.

In Edinburgh, Scotland, the Encyclopaedia Britannica first came out in weekly parts from 1768 to 1771. It grew with each edition. Later editions invited well-known experts to write long articles. The famous 11th edition of 1910–11 had articles by scientists and explorers of the day, and some readers still collect it. By its last printing it filled 32 heavy volumes and took up a whole shelf. The last printed edition came out in 2010. Since then, Britannica has been published online.

Everyone writes

In 2001 Jimmy Wales and Larry Sanger started Wikipedia, an online encyclopedia anyone can edit. The English version passed 6 million articles in 2020. Volunteers check each other's work and argue on discussion pages about what counts as a reliable source. Wikipedia now exists in more than 300 languages, including Hindi, Tamil, Bengali and Santali.

Encyclopedias have always had to balance speed and accuracy. Printed ones were slow to update. Online ones can change in minutes, for better or worse. The best ones, old and new, show where each fact comes from.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Everything in a printed encyclopedia is always correct.

Truth: Encyclopedias are made by people, and people make mistakes. That is why editions are revised and why good encyclopedias, including Kyun, list their sources so readers can check.
