What are India's marine parks?

Off the coast of Tamil Nadu, a protected chain of small islands shelters coral reefs, sea turtles and one of India's last groups of dugongs, the gentle "sea cows".

The short answer

India's marine parks are protected areas in the sea, set up to keep coral reefs, seagrass beds, mangroves and sea creatures safe. They work like national parks on land, but much of what they protect is underwater.

India has a long coastline, more than 11,000 km including its islands by a 2024 measurement, and several marine national parks and sanctuaries. The best known are the Gulf of Kachchh in Gujarat, the Gulf of Mannar in Tamil Nadu and the Mahatma Gandhi Marine National Park in the Andaman Islands.

Inside them, rules limit fishing, mining and building. Some zones are open to visitors, who can snorkel, take glass-bottomed boat rides or walk on reefs at low tide.

Surprising but true

In the Gulf of Kachchh marine park, at low tide, the sea draws back so far that visitors can walk out across the reef and see corals, octopuses and sea anemones without getting more than their ankles wet.

The first: Gulf of Kachchh

In 1982 the Gulf of Kachchh, on Gujarat's coast, became India's first marine national park. It covers dozens of islands and reefs, along with mangrove forests and mudflats.

The water here is warm and full of nutrients, but also murky and salty, so its corals are tough. At low tide the sea pulls far back, and visitors at places such as Narara can walk across the exposed reef with a guide. Small octopuses, pufferfish, sea cucumbers and colourful sea slugs hide in the pools.

Gulf of Mannar and the dugongs

Between India and Sri Lanka lies the Gulf of Mannar. In 1986 a chain of 21 small islands off the Tamil Nadu coast, along with the shallow sea around them, became a marine national park. Two of those islands have since sunk below the waves, worn away after years of coral mining nearby.

Coral reefs ring the islands. Sea turtles, dolphins and hundreds of kinds of fish live here. So do some of India's last dugongs, large slow sea mammals related to elephants rather than whales. They graze on seagrass meadows on the sea floor.

Dugongs are now rare in India. In 2022 Tamil Nadu set up India's first Dugong Conservation Reserve in nearby Palk Bay, to protect the seagrass they depend on.

The Andaman reefs

The Andaman and Nicobar Islands have some of India's clearest water and richest coral reefs. The Mahatma Gandhi Marine National Park, near Wandoor on South Andaman, was declared in 1983. It protects 15 islands and their reefs.

Visitors take boats out to islands such as Jolly Buoy and Red Skin, where they can snorkel over branching corals and see parrotfish, clownfish and giant clams. To protect the reef, plastic bottles and bags are not allowed on Jolly Buoy island.

The Andamans also have Rani Jhansi Marine National Park, which protects mangroves, beaches and reefs around Ritchie's Archipelago.

Turtle beaches

Some marine sanctuaries protect beaches as much as water. At Gahirmatha in Odisha, hundreds of thousands of olive ridley sea turtles sometimes come ashore over a few nights to lay eggs. This mass nesting is called an arribada, a Spanish word meaning "arrival".

During the nesting season, fishing close to shore is banned, because turtles get caught in nets.

Protecting the sea is hard

Fences can't keep trawlers out of the sea. Marine parks rely on patrol boats, local rules and the help of fishing families who live alongside them.

Warming seas are a bigger threat. When the water stays too hot for too long, corals lose the tiny algae that live inside them and turn white. This bleaching has hit reefs in the Gulf of Mannar, Lakshadweep and the Andamans several times since 1998.

Other countries face the same challenge. Australia's Great Barrier Reef is a marine park too, and in the USA, Hawaii's Papahānaumokuākea is one of the largest protected ocean areas in the world. In both places, as in India, scientists are learning which reefs can recover.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Marine national parks are closed to everyone, so fishing families lose their living.

Truth: Most Indian marine parks allow visitors in some zones, and many local people still fish in the wider waters around them. The strictest rules usually cover the coral islands and reefs themselves. Balancing protection with fishers' needs is an ongoing debate.

Try this

Make a "rock pool" in a basin. Put sand and a few stones at the bottom, add water and small shells or toy sea creatures. Pour some water out slowly to copy low tide and see which creatures are left high and dry and which still sit in water. That is the daily life of animals on a tidal reef.
