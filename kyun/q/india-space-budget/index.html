How does ISRO do so much with so little?

India reached Mars on its first try for about ₹450 crore, less than it cost Hollywood to make the space film Gravity.

The short answer

In 2014, India's Mars Orbiter Mission, nicknamed Mangalyaan, slipped into orbit around Mars on the very first try. No Asian country had reached Mars orbit before. It cost about ₹450 crore, roughly $74 million at the time. The Hollywood film Gravity, about astronauts in trouble, cost about $100 million to make.

ISRO, the Indian Space Research Organisation, does this again and again. Its secret is a mix of things. Its engineers earn less than those in rich countries. It reuses designs from one mission to the next and builds very few spare test models. And it has a habit of solving problems with clever thinking rather than more money.

Surprising but true

India's Mars mission cost less per kilometre travelled than a ride in a Bengaluru autorickshaw.

Small money, big sky

Here are some numbers to feel. India's Department of Space got about ₹13,700 crore in the 2026–27 budget. That is roughly $1.5 billion. NASA, the US space agency, gets about $25 billion a year, about sixteen times more.

Yet ISRO has landed Chandrayaan-3 near the Moon's south pole (2023), sent a probe to study the Sun called Aditya-L1, and once launched 104 satellites on a single rocket, in February 2017. That was a world record until a SpaceX rocket carried 143 in 2021. Chandrayaan-3 cost about ₹615 crore, which is less than many big Bollywood and Hollywood films. Mangalyaan travelled about 67 crore km to reach Mars. Divide the cost by the distance and you get under ₹7 a kilometre, less than the meter charges in a Bengaluru autorickshaw.

Started on a bicycle

The habit of making do goes right back to the start. In 1963, India launched its first rocket from Thumba, a fishing village near Thiruvananthapuram. Scientists used a church as an office. Rocket parts were carried to the launch pad on bicycles, a scene that became a famous photograph.

Why did ISRO carry a rocket on a bicycle?India's space age began in 1963 in a fishing village in Kerala, with a church for a workshop and rocket parts wheeled to the launch pad on bicycles.

The man behind it, Vikram Sarabhai, believed space should help ordinary people: weather forecasts for farmers, television for villages, maps for planners. That purpose shaped how ISRO spends money even today.

How the savings work

Building a spacecraft usually means making several practice versions first, to test in shaking machines and heat chambers. ISRO often builds fewer of these, testing parts carefully instead. It reuses proven designs: Mangalyaan was based on a satellite frame ISRO had already flown many times.

Indian-made parts are cheaper than imported ones. Engineers' salaries are much lower than in the USA or Europe. And clever planning saves fuel, which saves money. Mangalyaan looped around Earth for weeks, stretching its orbit each time, before heading to Mars. A bigger, costlier rocket could have gone straight there.

Time was squeezed too. The Mars team built Mangalyaan in about 15 months, partly because Earth and Mars line up well for a cheap trip only once every 26 months or so. Miss the window and you wait two years. Working to a hard deadline left little room for expensive extras.

Is cheap always good?

Not everyone thinks the "low-cost" label is all good. Some Indian scientists say ISRO needs more money, not praise for spending less, to build bigger rockets and do more research. Smaller budgets can also mean slower progress: India's crewed mission, Gaganyaan, has taken years longer than first planned.

ISRO has had failures too. The Vikram lander of Chandrayaan-2 crashed in 2019. Engineers studied what went wrong, and four years later Chandrayaan-3 landed safely. Now India is working on rockets that can fly more than once, the same idea that has made reusable rockets the biggest money saver in spaceflight today.

Myth or fact?

Myth: ISRO's missions are cheap because they use poor-quality parts.

Truth: The savings come mostly from lower salaries and local costs, reusing tested designs, and building fewer test models. ISRO's rockets and spacecraft meet tough international standards, and many countries pay ISRO to launch their satellites.

Try this

Plan a pretend space mission with ₹100 of play money. List what you need (rocket, fuel, camera, team snacks) and give each a price. Then try to cut your costs by a third without dropping anything important. That is roughly the puzzle ISRO's engineers solve every day.
