Why do people move from villages to cities?

India's 2011 Census found that about 45 crore people, more than one in three, were living somewhere other than their previous home.

The short answer

People move from villages to cities mostly for work. Farming depends on rain and land, and a small family farm often cannot feed or employ everyone. Cities offer jobs in building sites, factories, shops, offices and homes, and they usually pay more.

Other reasons pull people too: better colleges, hospitals, or the chance to join relatives who have already moved. Experts call these "push" and "pull" factors. A bad harvest or debt pushes someone out of a village. A cousin with a job in Surat pulls them in. Often both happen at once.

India's 2011 Census counted about 45.6 crore internal migrants, people living somewhere other than the place they used to live. That was more than one in three Indians.

Surprising but true

Most people who move within India do not cross a state border, and for women the most common reason given for moving is marriage, not work.

Not always the journey you'd expect

The picture of a young man boarding a train to Mumbai is real, but it is only part of the story. Most migration in India happens within the same state. A large share is from village to village.

The biggest single reason for women is marriage, because many brides move to their husband's home. For men, work comes first. Some families migrate for a season, going to work in brick kilns or sugarcane fields for a few months and returning home for the rest of the year.

Long-distance moves often follow well-worn paths, from states like Uttar Pradesh and Bihar to big cities like Delhi and Mumbai. The railways carry many of these journeys, and some long-distance trains are packed with migrant workers around festivals.

How did railways come to India?India's first passenger train ran for about 34 km in 1853. Today Indian Railways makes close to 2 crore passenger journeys on a typical day.

Money sent home

Many migrants send part of their earnings back to their village. This money pays for school fees, medicines, a new roof or a well. Whole villages can change because of what relatives earn hundreds of kilometres away. In parts of Bihar, Uttar Pradesh and Odisha, money from city jobs is a big part of what keeps many households going. Migrants also carry ideas home: new recipes, new words, new ways of doing things.

City life is often hard at first. Rent is high, so many newcomers share small rooms or live in slums. Many work without contracts, so they can lose their jobs without warning.

That became painfully clear in 2020. When India went into its first COVID-19 lockdown, factories and building sites shut. With trains stopped, many thousands of migrant workers set out to walk or cycle home, some across several states. Special trains and buses were later arranged. The sight of families walking along highways made many people notice how much their cities depend on workers from far away.

America's Great Migration

The USA saw its own huge move. From the 1910s to 1970, about six million African Americans left the farms of the rural South for cities in the North and West, such as Chicago, Detroit and New York. They were escaping unfair laws, violence and poor pay, and seeking factory jobs.

Their move changed the country. It helped create the Harlem Renaissance in New York, spread jazz and blues, and built the strength of the civil rights movement.

A world moving to towns

Around 2007, according to the United Nations, more than half the people on Earth were living in towns and cities for the first time. India is still more rural than most: in 2011, only about 31% of Indians lived in urban areas. That share is growing, and planners expect India's cities to keep swelling for decades. Some villages are themselves turning into towns, as shops, colleges and roads arrive. The line between village and city is getting blurrier every year.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Most people who move in India go from a village straight to a big city in another state.

Truth: Most moves happen within the same state, and many are from one village to another, often for marriage. Long trips to big cities in other states are important but are a smaller share.
