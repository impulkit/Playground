Why are Russia and Ukraine at war?

Russia and Ukraine share a long history, related languages and many family ties. Yet since 2022 they have fought the biggest war in Europe in 80 years, and its effects reach Indian kitchens and petrol pumps.

The short answer

Russia and Ukraine are at war because Russia invaded Ukraine in February 2022. Russia says it acted to protect its security and Russian-speaking people, and to stop Ukraine joining the Western military alliance called NATO. Ukraine, and most countries in the United Nations, say Russia broke international law by attacking a free country.

Fighting had already begun in 2014, when Russia took control of Crimea. Peace talks have started and stopped many times, because the two sides disagree about land, security and what a fair peace looks like.

Surprising but true

When Ukraine became independent in 1991, it held the world's third-largest stock of nuclear weapons, left over from the Soviet Union. It gave them all up in the 1990s in return for promises that its borders would be respected.

How it works: the main questions

To understand the war, it helps to know what NATO is. NATO, the North Atlantic Treaty Organization, is a group of 32 countries in North America and Europe, set up in 1949. Its members promise that an attack on one will be treated as an attack on all. Several countries that were once part of the Soviet bloc joined it after the 1990s. Russia sees this as a danger at its door. Countries that joined say they wanted protection.

Now it helps to see what each side says it wants.

Russia's view. Russia's leaders say NATO, led by the USA and Western Europe, has moved closer and closer to Russia's borders since the 1990s, and that Ukraine joining it would be a threat. They also say many people in eastern Ukraine speak Russian and feel close to Russia. Russia now claims four Ukrainian regions as its own.

Ukraine's view. Ukraine says it is an independent country that has the right to choose its own government and friends. It says Russia's claims are an excuse to take its land, and that it cannot trust any deal without strong guarantees that Russia will not attack again.

The world's view. Many countries condemned the invasion and put sanctions on Russia, cutting trade and freezing money. Others, including India, China and Brazil, did not join the sanctions and called for talks.

It is a bit like two neighbours with a shared past, quarrelling over where the fence goes, who may visit whom, and whether one of them may join a powerful group of friends. Except here, the quarrel involves armies, cities and millions of people.

Where it came from

Russians and Ukrainians trace their history back to a medieval state called Kyivan Rus, centred on the city of Kyiv, more than 1,000 years ago. Later, most of Ukraine became part of the Russian Empire, and then of the Soviet Union.

When the Soviet Union broke up in 1991, Ukrainians voted overwhelmingly for independence. In the years that followed, Ukraine was pulled between closer ties with Russia and closer ties with Europe. This tug of war was partly a leftover of the Cold War between the West and the Soviet Union.

In 2014, after protests in Ukraine's capital forced out a president who favoured Russia, Russia took control of Crimea, a peninsula on the Black Sea. Fighting broke out in the eastern region called Donbas. On 24 February 2022, Russia launched a full invasion. In September 2022, it said it had made four regions part of Russia, though it did not control all of them. Most countries did not accept this.

India's story

India has old, friendly ties with Russia, which has sold India weapons and energy for decades. India also has growing ties with Ukraine, Europe and the USA. So India chose not to take sides in votes at the United Nations, while calling for peace and respect for every country's borders. In 2022, Prime Minister Narendra Modi told Russia's President Vladimir Putin that "today's era is not of war".

When the invasion began, about 22,500 Indians, most of them medical students, were living in Ukraine. Under Operation Ganga, India flew them home through neighbouring countries such as Poland, Romania, Hungary and Slovakia.

The war also touched Indian homes. Ukraine and Russia were big sellers of wheat and sunflower oil, so prices rose worldwide. Russia began selling oil cheaply, and India bought much more of it, as the story of where India gets its oil explains. That later caused friction with the USA.

The numbers that matter

  • - 24 February 2022: the day Russia's full-scale invasion began.
  • - 141: countries that voted at the UN in March 2022 to demand that Russia withdraw. India was among 35 that abstained.
  • - About 22,500: Indians brought home from Ukraine under Operation Ganga.
  • - Four: Ukrainian regions Russia claims, even though it does not fully control them.

What people argue about

The biggest argument is about what peace should look like. Russia wants Ukraine to give up the land it claims, stay out of NATO and accept limits on its army. Ukraine refuses to hand over land and wants strong security guarantees. Russia prefers one big, complete deal, while Ukraine wants a ceasefire first.

Other countries argue too. Some say the priority is to stop the fighting quickly, even with a hard compromise. Others say rewarding an invasion would encourage more wars. India has said the answer must come through dialogue and diplomacy.

Myths and mix-ups

"Russians and Ukrainians speak the same language." Russian and Ukrainian are separate, though related, languages. Many Ukrainians speak both.

"India supports one side." India has kept ties with both countries and repeatedly called for talks.

"A war far away does not affect India." It changed prices of oil, wheat, fertiliser and cooking oil in India.

What it means for you

The war is one reason petrol, cooking oil and fertiliser prices jumped in recent years. Many Indian families also have children studying abroad, often to become doctors, and some had to rush home from Ukraine. The war forced millions of Ukrainians to become refugees in other countries.

Understanding how borders are decided and why talks are so hard helps make sense of the news, and of why a peace deal far away could make a difference to prices at home.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The war is only about one piece of land.

Truth: Land is a big part of it, but the war is also about whether Ukraine can choose its own friends and alliances, how safe Russia and Europe feel, and history going back centuries.
