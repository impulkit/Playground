What was the Harlem Renaissance?

In the 1920s, one neighbourhood in New York became the centre of a burst of Black poetry, novels, painting and jazz that changed American culture.

The short answer

The Harlem Renaissance was a time, roughly from the end of the First World War to the mid-1930s, when Black American artists, writers and musicians created a huge amount of new work. Its home was Harlem, a neighbourhood in northern Manhattan in New York.

Renaissance means rebirth. People used the word because the movement felt like a new beginning for Black culture in America. Poets like Langston Hughes wrote about everyday Black life with pride. Jazz musicians packed the clubs. Thinkers argued that art could help win respect and equal rights.

Surprising but true

Langston Hughes wrote one of his most famous poems, "The Negro Speaks of Rivers", at about 18, while crossing the Mississippi on a train.

The Great Migration

The story starts in the American South. After slavery ended, Southern states passed unfair laws that kept Black people from voting and separated them in schools, trains and restaurants. Violence was common, and farm work paid very little.

From about 1910, millions of Black Americans moved north to cities such as Chicago, Detroit and New York, looking for factory work and more freedom. Historians call this the Great Migration. Between 1910 and 1970, about six million people made the journey, often by train, carrying their music, food and stories with them. Harlem, with its fine brownstone houses, became the biggest Black neighbourhood in the country. People came from the South and from Caribbean islands such as Jamaica, bringing new voices and ideas.

Words on fire

Writers were at the heart of the movement. Langston Hughes wrote poems in the rhythm of jazz and the blues. Zora Neale Hurston travelled the South collecting folk tales and later wrote the novel Their Eyes Were Watching God (1937). Claude McKay, born in Jamaica, wrote fierce poems against injustice.

In 1925 the philosopher Alain Locke published The New Negro, a collection of stories, poems and essays. Negro was the word commonly used then. The "New Negro" was a person who refused to accept second-class treatment.

Music and the stage

Harlem's nights belonged to jazz. Duke Ellington led his orchestra at the Cotton Club. Louis Armstrong's trumpet and Bessie Smith's voice filled theatres and record players. Dances like the Lindy Hop were born in Harlem's ballrooms, especially the Savoy, where Black and white dancers could dance together.

Painters joined in too. Aaron Douglas created bold murals using silhouettes and African designs. Photographer James Van Der Zee recorded Harlem families and weddings, and his pictures are now a priceless record of the time.

Thinkers and magazines

W. E. B. Du Bois edited The Crisis, the magazine of the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People. It published many young writers for the first time. Du Bois was interested in struggles beyond America too. His 1928 novel Dark Princess tells of an alliance between a Black American man and an Indian princess working against empire.

How it faded

The Great Depression that began in 1929 hit Harlem very hard. Money for books, plays and nightclubs dried up, and many people lost their jobs. A riot in 1935, sparked by anger over poverty and unfair treatment, is often seen as the end of the era.

Its influence never faded, though. The writers and musicians of Harlem inspired the civil rights movement of the 1950s and 1960s, and later poets such as Maya Angelou. The Apollo Theater, which began hosting Black performers in 1934, still runs its famous Amateur Night today.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Harlem Renaissance was mainly about music.

Truth: Jazz was a big part of it, but writers, poets, painters, photographers, thinkers and actors were just as important. It was a movement across many arts at once.

Try this

Choose a short poem by Langston Hughes, such as "Dreams" or "April Rain Song", with an adult's help. Read it aloud twice, once slowly and once with a jazz beat tapped on the table. Notice how the rhythm changes the feeling.
