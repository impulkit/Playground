What are India's classical dances?

A dancer's hands can become a lotus, a deer or a bow in a single breath, using a language over 2,000 years old.

The short answer

India's classical dances are old, highly trained styles that tell stories with the whole body. The Sangeet Natak Akademi, the national academy for music, dance and drama, recognises eight. They are Bharatanatyam from Tamil Nadu, Kathak from the north, Kathakali and Mohiniyattam from Kerala, Kuchipudi from Andhra Pradesh, Odissi from Odisha, Manipuri from Manipur and Sattriya from Assam.

Each looks and sounds different. But they share an idea from an ancient book, the Natya Shastra: dance should move the audience by showing emotions, not just steps. A dancer's eyes, eyebrows and fingers matter as much as the feet.

Surprising but true

Most of these dances were nearly lost about a hundred years ago, and were rebuilt for the stage by dancers and teachers in the 1920s and 1930s.

A very old rulebook

The Natya Shastra is said to have been written by a sage called Bharata, somewhere between about 200 BCE and 200 CE. Nobody is sure of the date, and it may be the work of several authors. It covers acting, music, make-up, stage building and dance in great detail.

The book describes how a performance creates rasa, a kind of flavour of feeling in the audience, such as love, courage, laughter or fear. It lists 108 karanas, key movements of dance. Centuries later, sculptors carved these poses into temple walls in Tamil Nadu, and dancers today still study them.

Hands that speak

Classical dances split into three parts. Nritta is pure dance: footwork and shapes that show rhythm and skill. Nritya adds expression, with the face and hands acting out the words of a song. Natya is full drama, with a dancer playing characters.

The hand gestures are called mudras. One shape can mean many things depending on how it moves. A flat hand with fingers together, called pataka, can become a flag, a forest, a wave or a door. Watching a skilled dancer is a bit like reading a book in a language you are slowly learning.

What is the Ramayana?A prince, a princess, a demon king and an army of monkeys. The Ramayana has been retold for more than 2,000 years, from Indian villages to Thai palaces.

Eight dances, many places

The styles grew in different places, often linked to temples, royal courts and village festivals. Kathakali, from Kerala, uses huge skirts, painted green faces and eye movements that can be seen from far away. Traditionally, its performances lasted all night.

Odissi, from Odisha, uses curved, sculpture-like poses. You can see similar figures carved on the Sun Temple at Konark. Manipuri is soft and flowing, often telling stories of Krishna. Sattriya was created in Assam's monasteries more than 500 years ago, and joined the official list only in 2000.

Nearly lost, then reborn

In the late 1800s and early 1900s, many of these traditions were in trouble. Royal patrons lost their wealth, and some reformers attacked temple dancing. Some states made laws against temple dancing in the decades around independence.

Dancers and teachers fought to keep the art alive. In 1930, the poet Vallathol Narayana Menon set up Kerala Kalamandalam to teach Kathakali. In 1936, Rukmini Devi Arundale founded Kalakshetra in Chennai to train Bharatanatyam dancers. People still debate how much the old traditions changed in this rebirth, and who was left out.

Stories that never get old

Many dances take their stories from the Ramayana and the Mahabharata, and from poems about gods such as Krishna and Shiva. Others tell of nature, seasons or love.

Today, children across India, the USA and the wider world learn these dances. In American cities, it is common to see a young dancer's arangetram, a first full solo performance, in a school hall on a Saturday. An art once taught only in temples and palaces now travels in suitcases full of ankle bells.

Myth or fact?

Myth: India's classical dances have been performed in exactly the same way for 2,000 years.

Truth: Their roots are very old, but the dances you see on stage today were shaped, and in some cases rebuilt, in the last two hundred years. Each generation of teachers has changed them.

Try this

Try two mudras. For pataka, hold your hand flat with fingers together and thumb bent in, like a flag. For alapadma, spread your fingers wide and curve them, like an open lotus. Make up a short story using only these two shapes and your face, and see if a friend can follow it.
