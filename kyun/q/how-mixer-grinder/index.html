How does a mixer grinder work?

The blades in a kitchen mixer can spin around 18,000 times a minute, fast enough to turn hard turmeric and dry chillies into powder in seconds.

The short answer

A mixer grinder is an electric motor with a spinning shaft sticking out of the top. You put a steel jar on it. Inside the jar, sharp blades sit on a short spindle. When the jar locks onto the base, a plastic or rubber coupler connects the motor's shaft to the blade spindle. Switch on, and the motor spins the blades at around 18,000 turns a minute.

At that speed, the tip of the blade moves faster than a car on a highway. It smashes into whatever is in the jar, chopping it into smaller bits. The jar's shape and the swirling food keep pushing everything back down onto the blades until it becomes a paste, a powder or a smooth chutney.

Surprising but true

India's famous mixie was invented in Mumbai in the 1960s because a German blender burned out trying to grind Indian spices.

The motor: magnets and copper

Almost all kitchen mixers use a universal motor. Inside is a ring of coiled copper wire and a spinning part called the rotor, also wrapped in wire. When electricity flows through the coils, they become electromagnets. The magnetic push and pull between them turns the rotor, round and round, as long as the power is on. It works because of the way magnets attract and repel.

Two small blocks of carbon, called brushes, press against the spinning rotor to feed it current. They wear down slowly and make the faint burning smell of an old mixer. These motors are light, cheap and spin extremely fast, which is exactly what grinding needs. The same kind of motor drives most electric drills, hair dryers and vacuum cleaners, which is why they all whine with a similar high note.

Blades, jars and the vortex

Different jars do different jobs. A small chutney jar has short blades close to the bottom, so a spoonful of coconut doesn't escape them. A big liquid jar has blades bent up and down, which throw liquid outwards and create a whirlpool, or vortex. The vortex pulls food from the top back down into the blades.

Many Indian jars have bumps or ribs on the inside wall. These break up the swirl so food doesn't just spin around in a ring, untouched. Dry grinding works best in short bursts, because hard spices like turmeric are tough on the motor and the blades.

Why India needed its own mixie

Blenders were invented in the USA in the 1920s and 1930s for milkshakes and drinks. They were built for soft fruit and ice, not for grinding urad dal for dosa and idli batter or roasted spices for garam masala.

In the early 1960s in Mumbai, an engineer named Satya Prakash Mathur watched his wife's imported German blender struggle with Indian cooking and eventually burn out. In 1963 he started a company to build a tougher motor and stronger jars designed for Indian kitchens. His brand, Sumeet, became so common that "mixie" turned into an everyday Indian word.

Keeping it safe

Grinding can overheat a motor. Most mixers have an overload protector, a small switch that cuts power when the motor gets too hot. You press a button underneath to reset it after it cools. The jar must lock in place before the motor will run well, and the lid must be held down, because hot liquids can push it off.

A mixer is also a good lesson in speed. The motor turns the blades directly, with no slowing down at all, unlike a bicycle where gears change the speed. Hand-turned grinders of the past took an hour to do what a mixie does in a minute, and the grinding stone your grandmother used still sits in many Indian homes, quietly waiting.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A mixer grinder crushes food by pressing it, like a stone grinder.

Truth: A mixer mostly cuts and smashes. Its blades hit food at high speed, chopping it into smaller and smaller pieces. A traditional wet grinder or a stone sil batta works by slowly rubbing and pressing, which is why some cooks say idli batter is softer from a stone grinder.

Try this

Make a whirlpool like the one in a mixer jar. Fill a clear bottle two-thirds with water, add a few drops of food colouring or a pinch of glitter, close the lid tightly and swirl it fast in circles, then stop. Watch the funnel form in the middle. Inside a mixer jar, the blades make the same vortex, which keeps pulling food down onto the blades.
