What is the Line of Actual Control?

India and China share one of the longest borders in the world, yet the two countries have never agreed on exactly where it runs. Soldiers patrol a line that exists mostly in their minds and on their own maps.

The short answer

The Line of Actual Control, or LAC, is the line that separates land controlled by India from land controlled by China. It runs for about 3,488 km through the Himalayas, from Ladakh in the north to Arunachal Pradesh in the east.

It is not an official border that both countries have agreed on. It is simply where each side's control actually stops. Because India and China see the line differently in many places, their soldiers sometimes meet face to face.

Surprising but true

Along much of the Line of Actual Control, there is no fence, no wall and no painted line. Soldiers from both sides follow patrol routes through mountains and valleys based on where each country believes the line lies.

How it works

An agreed border is like a line both neighbours have signed on the same map, with markers on the ground. The LAC is different. Each country has its own map, and in some areas the two maps do not match.

Imagine two families sharing a big field with no fence. Each family believes the boundary stone is in a different spot. Every day, someone from each family walks to where they think their land ends. In the middle, there is a strip both think is theirs. Most days they nod and walk away. Sometimes they argue.

The LAC is split into three parts:

  • - Western sector: Ladakh, where the Galwan Valley and Pangong Lake are.
  • - Middle sector: along Himachal Pradesh and Uttarakhand.
  • - Eastern sector: along Sikkim and Arunachal Pradesh, two of India's northeastern states.

To avoid fights, India and China have signed agreements. They promised to keep peace, limit troops, warn each other about exercises, and not use guns near the line. Commanders meet at set points to sort out problems. When patrols meet, they are supposed to stop, show banners asking the other side to go back, and return to base.

Where it came from

In 1914, British India, Tibet and China met at a conference in Shimla. British official Henry McMahon drew a line in the east, now called the McMahon Line. Tibet agreed, but China did not sign. China still does not accept it.

After India became independent in 1947 and China took control of Tibet in 1950, the two countries disagreed about large areas, including Aksai Chin in the west and what is now Arunachal Pradesh in the east. In 1962, they fought a short war. After it, each side stayed where it was, and that line of control became the LAC.

In 1993, India and China signed an agreement to keep peace along the line, followed by more agreements in 1996, 2005 and 2013. For many years, there were few serious clashes.

India's story

Tensions returned in the 2010s. In 2017, Indian and Chinese troops faced off for 73 days at Doklam, near Bhutan. In June 2020, soldiers clashed in the Galwan Valley in Ladakh, and soldiers on both sides lost their lives. It was the worst fighting in more than 40 years, and relations froze. Both countries rushed tens of thousands of extra troops to the mountains, where winter nights can fall far below freezing. Talks between generals went on for years, one friction point at a time.

In October 2024, the two countries agreed to pull back troops from the last points of tension in Ladakh. After that, direct flights between the countries returned, and Indian pilgrims could again make the pilgrimage to Kailash Mansarovar in Tibet.

But the eastern sector stayed tense. China calls Arunachal Pradesh "South Tibet" and has given its own names to places there. India says Arunachal Pradesh is, and always will be, part of India. In 2026, officials confirmed new face-offs there.

India has been building roads, bridges and tunnels near the LAC so soldiers and supplies can move faster. The Sela Tunnel in Arunachal, opened in 2024, lets traffic reach Tawang even in heavy snow. The Indo-Tibetan Border Police, raised in 1962, guards much of the line, alongside the Army.

The numbers that matter

  • - About 3,488 km: the LAC's length by India's count, longer than the distance from Kashmir to Kanyakumari. China gives a much shorter figure.
  • - 1962: the year of the India-China war, after which the LAC took shape.
  • - 73 days: how long the Doklam standoff lasted in 2017.
  • - Over 100 billion dollars: the value of trade between India and China each year in recent years. The neighbours argue at the border but trade a huge amount.

What people argue about

People in India broadly agree that the border must be protected. The debate is over how to deal with China. The government says it uses talks and military strength together, and that peace on the border is needed for normal ties. Opposition leaders have asked the government to share more information about what is happening on the ground.

There is also debate about trade. Some say India buys too many goods from China, like electronics parts and solar panels, and should make more at home. Others say cheap parts help Indian factories grow and keep prices low.

Myths and mix-ups

"The LAC and the LoC are the same." The LoC, or Line of Control, divides the parts of Jammu and Kashmir held by India and Pakistan. The LAC is with China.

"There is a wall along the LAC." It runs over some of the world's highest mountains, glaciers and rivers. Most of it has no fence at all.

"The Himalayas make a clear natural border." Mountain ridges, rivers and passes can be read in different ways, which is part of why the two sides disagree.

What it means for you

What happens on the LAC affects soldiers and their families, people living in border villages, and pilgrims hoping to visit holy places in Tibet. Calm on the border also helps trade, flights and business between the world's two most populous countries, India and China.

Learning how borders are decided helps make sense of the news. A territory dispute is often less about one hill and more about history, maps and trust. A treaty that both sides accept is what turns a line of control into a true border.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Line of Actual Control is a proper international border marked with fences.

Truth: The LAC is a working line, not an agreed border. Most of it runs through high mountains with no fence or marker, and India and China each have their own idea of where it lies.
