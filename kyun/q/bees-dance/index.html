How do bees tell each other where flowers are?

They dance. And the dance is a map.

The short answer

A honeybee that finds flowers comes home and does a "waggle dance" on the honeycomb.

The direction of the waggle shows the direction of the flowers compared with the Sun. The length of the waggle shows how far.

Karl von Frisch figured this out and won a Nobel Prize in 1973. He shared it with two other scientists who studied animal behaviour.

Surprising but true

India's giant rock bees dance on open combs hanging from cliffs and buildings.

Reading the dance

The dancer runs in a short straight line, shaking her body from side to side very fast. Then she loops back round to the start, first to the right, then to the left, tracing a figure of eight. Other bees crowd around her in the dark, feeling her movements with their antennae.

The straight "waggle run" is the message. Inside the hive, the comb hangs upright. Straight up stands for the direction of the Sun. If the flowers are 40 degrees to the left of the Sun, she waggles 40 degrees to the left of straight up. The longer the waggle lasts, the further away the food. In many hives, a waggle of about one second means roughly a kilometre of flying, though each colony's "dialect" is a little different.

She also carries the scent of the flowers on her body, and shares tastes of the nectar. So the recruits know what to look for and what it smells like.

A scientist with a box of bees

Karl von Frisch began studying bees in Austria in the early 1900s. He trained them to visit dishes of sugar water, marked each bee with a dot of paint, and watched through glass-sided hives. He noticed that when one bee found a rich dish, her nestmates soon arrived even if he moved it far away.

Over many years of patient experiments, he showed that the dance carried both direction and distance. Many scientists at first doubted that an insect could share such detailed information. Later researchers built a tiny robot bee that danced in a hive. Many real bees followed its dance and searched in the right direction and at the right distance.

India's wild honeybees

India is home to several wild honeybee species. Three are especially common. The giant rock bee builds a single huge comb in the open, hanging from cliffs, tall trees and buildings. With no walls, its dancers perform right on the comb's surface in daylight, on the curtain of bees.

The Asian hive bee nests in hollow trees and walls and is kept in wooden boxes by beekeepers. The tiny dwarf honeybee builds a palm-sized comb wrapped around a twig. It dances on the flat top of its comb, pointing straight at the food, without needing to turn the Sun into "up".

Choosing a new home

Dancing isn't only for flowers. When a colony grows too big, half the bees leave with the old queen and hang in a cluster on a branch. Scout bees fly off to inspect possible homes, then dance to describe them. The better the site, the more energetically they dance.

The American scientist Thomas Seeley spent years watching these swarms. He found that scouts gradually come to agree as weak sites lose dancers. When enough scouts are dancing for one site, the whole swarm takes off together. A cloud of insects makes a group decision without any leader.

Why it matters

Bees pollinate many of the fruits, vegetables and nuts we eat, from mangoes and apples to almonds and mustard. Some plants, like figs, rely on other insects instead. Each worker visits many flowers on a single trip, her wings beating over 200 times a second, collecting nectar to make honey and carrying pollen from bloom to bloom. Their dance helps the colony send its workers to the best flowers quickly, wasting as little flying as possible. To store all that honey, they build wax cells in a near-perfect hexagon pattern.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Every bee in the hive is busy collecting nectar from flowers.

Truth: Only the older worker bees go out to gather food. Younger workers clean cells, feed the young, build comb and guard the entrance. The queen lays eggs, and male bees, called drones, don't collect food at all.

Try this

Play the bee dance game. Hide a small object somewhere in a room or playground. Then, without words or pointing, show a friend where it is. Walk in a straight line facing the direction they should go, and wiggle for longer if it's far away. Can they find it from your dance alone?
