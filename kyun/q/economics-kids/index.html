What is economics?

Economics is the study of choices, and you make dozens of economic choices before school, starting with whether to get out of bed.

The short answer

Economics is the study of how people, families, companies and countries decide what to do with things that are limited. There is only so much money, so much land, so much water and so many hours in a day. Wanting is easy. Having enough of everything is impossible.

So everyone must choose. Should a family spend its savings on a scooter or on school fees? Should a farmer grow rice or cotton? Should a government build a new road or a new hospital? Economists study how those choices are made, what happens because of them, and whether there might be a better way.

Surprising but true

One of the most famous examples in economics is about pins. In 1776, Adam Smith showed that ten workers splitting the job into small steps could make thousands of pins a day, far more than if each worked alone.

The price of saying yes

The most useful idea in economics is called opportunity cost. Every time you choose something, you give up the next best thing you could have had instead.

If you spend your last ₹100 on a book, the cost is the cricket ball you didn't buy. If you spend Saturday at a friend's house, the cost is the film you skipped. The idea works for whole countries too. Money spent on army tanks is money not spent on schools. Seeing that hidden cost helps people choose more carefully.

Small and large

Economists often split their subject into two halves. Microeconomics looks closely at single choices: why a shop sets a price, why a family buys one brand of soap and not another, why a worker takes one job. Ideas such as supply and demand belong here.

Macroeconomics zooms out to a whole country. It asks why prices rise over time, called inflation, why some years have more jobs than others, and how much a government should borrow. It also measures the size of whole economies, usually with GDP, the total value of everything a country produces in a year. That is how we know the USA and China have the two largest. Central banks, like the Reserve Bank of India and the US Federal Reserve, use macroeconomics to set interest rates.

Economists also study choices that look odd. Why do people buy things they don't need just because there's a sale? Why do they keep paying for a gym they never visit? A newer branch called behavioural economics mixes economics with psychology to explain why real people don't always act the way a textbook says they should.

What is game theory?The best way to win at rock-paper-scissors, according to mathematics, is to stop trying to be clever and choose at random.

Old ideas, new names

People thought about these questions long before the word economics existed. The Arthashastra, an ancient Indian book on running a kingdom, is linked to Chanakya, adviser to the Mauryan emperor. It discusses taxes, trade, prices and workers' wages. Scholars still argue about exactly when it was written and by whom.

In Scotland in 1776, Adam Smith wrote The Wealth of Nations. He described a pin workshop where each worker did one small step, and together they made thousands of pins a day. He also argued that when people trade freely, each looking after their own interest, the whole society can end up better off.

Why economists argue

Economics is about people, and people are hard to predict. Two economists can look at the same numbers and disagree about what to do. Should a government spend more in hard times, or save? Do high taxes on the rich help everyone, or slow things down? These arguments are often in the news.

Some economists study things that seem far from money. The Indian economist Amartya Sen won the Nobel Prize in economics in 1998. Part of his work showed that famines can happen even when a country has enough food, because poor people cannot afford to buy it. That idea changed how governments respond to hunger, and it began with a simple question about who gets what.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Economics is only about money and rich people.

Truth: Economics is about choosing when you can't have everything. That includes money, but also time, food, water, land and even attention.

Try this

Give yourself 10 imaginary coins and write down five things you want this week, each with a price in coins. You can't afford all of them. Choose, then write next to each thing you dropped what it would have given you. That list is your opportunity cost.
