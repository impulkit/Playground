Why are wheels round?

A square wheel would bump you up and down at every turn. A circle's secret is that its middle never moves up or down.

The short answer

A wheel turns around an axle, a rod through its middle. On a circle, every point on the edge is exactly the same distance from the centre. That distance is called the radius.

So as a round wheel rolls, the axle stays at the same height above the road the whole time. The cart, car or bicycle glides along without bouncing.

Try that with a square wheel. Its corners are further from the centre than the middle of its sides. As it turns, the axle would rise onto each corner, then drop again, thump, thump, thump. Every other flat-sided shape has the same problem, only smaller.

Surprising but true

A circle is not the only shape that rolls with the same width all the way round. A rounded triangle called a Reuleaux triangle does it too.

The magic number inside every circle

Measure the distance around any circle, then measure straight across it. The first number is always about 3.14 times the second. This number is called pi, and it is the same for a bangle, a bicycle wheel or the rim of a stadium.

That means one turn of a wheel moves it forward about 3.14 times its own width. A bicycle wheel about 70 cm across travels roughly 2.2 metres every time it goes round. A bicycle's computer uses exactly this idea to count your distance from how many times the wheel turns.

Rolling without a circle

Here is something that surprises even grown-ups. Draw an equal-sided triangle. Now, with a compass point on each corner in turn, draw a curve joining the other two corners. You get a fat, rounded triangle called a Reuleaux triangle, after a German engineer, Franz Reuleaux, who studied it in the 1800s.

It has the same width whichever way you measure it. Lay a board on top of a few of them and it rolls along perfectly flat. The British 20p and 50p coins use a seven-sided version of the same idea, which helps vending machines measure them.

So why not use them as wheels? Because the centre of a Reuleaux triangle moves up and down as it rolls. You can't fix an axle to it. Only a circle keeps its centre still.

Who invented the wheel?The wheel came surprisingly late. People were farming, making pots and building towns for thousands of years before anyone rolled a cart.

The first wheels

Wheels seem obvious now, but people lived for a very long time without them. Wheeled carts appear in the Middle East and Europe a little over 5,000 years ago. The oldest wooden wheel yet found, from the Ljubljana Marshes in Slovenia, is about 5,000 years old.

In the Indus Valley cities of what is now Pakistan and north-west India, archaeologists have dug up little clay toy carts with round wheels, made about 4,500 years ago. Children were pushing them around long before anyone wrote a book about geometry.

A wheel needs more than a round shape, though. It needs a smooth axle, a strong hole in the middle and a road that isn't too rough. Getting all of those right at once is part of why the wheel took so long to appear.

Circles that hold things up

Round shapes are good at more than rolling. A circle encloses the most space for a given length of edge, which is why water tanks, pipes and cans are often round. A round pipe also spreads the push of the water inside evenly in every direction, so there are no weak corners to burst.

And then there are manhole covers. Because a circle is the same width all the way round, a round cover can never be tipped down its own hole, however you turn it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A circle is the only shape that rolls with the same width all the way round.

Truth: Many shapes have this "constant width". The simplest after the circle is the Reuleaux triangle, a triangle with bulging sides. It rolls smoothly between two flat boards, but its centre wobbles, so it can't spin on an axle.

Try this

Lay two round pencils on a table and rest a book on them. Push the book and it rolls smoothly. Now try the same with two pencils that have six flat sides, or with a few square erasers. Notice how the book bumps up and down when the rollers aren't round.
