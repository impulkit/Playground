Where did humans come from?

About 3.6 million years ago, three upright walkers left footprints in wet volcanic ash in Tanzania. The prints are still there.

The short answer

Humans evolved in Africa. Over millions of years, a branch of the ape family slowly changed into the many kinds of early humans that scientists call hominins. Only one of those kinds survives today: us, Homo sapiens.

The oldest fossils of our own species, found at Jebel Irhoud in Morocco, are about 300,000 years old. From Africa, groups of modern humans spread across the world, reaching Asia, including India, Australia, Europe and finally the Americas. This idea, built from fossils, stone tools and DNA, is often called the "Out of Africa" story.

Surprising but true

Most people whose families come from outside Africa carry a small amount of Neanderthal DNA, often around 1–2%, from ancestors who met and had children with Neanderthals tens of thousands of years ago.

Standing up

Our family split from the line leading to chimpanzees roughly 6–7 million years ago. One of the first big changes was walking upright on two legs.

At Laetoli in Tanzania, the team of the British scientist Mary Leakey found a trail of footprints in 1978. They were made about 3.6 million years ago by upright walkers crossing damp volcanic ash, which then hardened. The prints look surprisingly like ours.

In 1974, in Ethiopia, scientists found much of the skeleton of a small female about 3.2 million years old. They nicknamed her Lucy, after a Beatles song playing in their camp. Lucy walked upright but had a small brain, about the size of a chimpanzee's.

Tools, fire and bigger brains

Over the next few million years, hominin brains grew larger. By at least 2.6 million years ago, and perhaps earlier, some were making sharp stone tools, marking the start of the Stone Age. Around 2 million years ago, a species called Homo erectus appeared. It had long legs, used fire at some point in its history, and was the first hominin known to leave Africa, reaching as far as China and Indonesia.

Many kinds of humans once shared the planet. Neanderthals lived in Europe and western Asia. A group called the Denisovans lived in Asia. In 1982, a fossil skull of an early human was found at Hathnora in the Narmada valley in Madhya Pradesh, one of the few such fossils from India.

Who made the very first paintings?Long before paper, pencils or paintbrushes, people were painting on rock. Some of their pictures are more than 50,000 years old.

Meeting the cousins

When modern humans spread out of Africa, they met Neanderthals and Denisovans. DNA shows that they sometimes had children together. The Swedish scientist Svante Pääbo won the 2022 Nobel Prize for reading the DNA of Neanderthals from ancient bones.

Today most people with ancestry outside Africa carry a little Neanderthal DNA, and some groups in Asia and Oceania carry Denisovan DNA too. The Neanderthals themselves died out about 40,000 years ago.

How scientists piece it together

The evidence comes from several directions. Fossils show how bodies changed. Layers of volcanic ash around them can be dated using the slow, steady decay of radioactive elements. Stone tools and hearths show what people did. DNA from living people works like a family tree: groups that split apart long ago have built up more differences. When all these clues point the same way, scientists grow more confident. When they clash, a new debate begins.

Reaching India and the Americas

Modern humans had reached South Asia at least 50,000–70,000 years ago. Stone tools found at Jwalapuram in Andhra Pradesh may be even older, though scientists argue about who made them. Today, India has some of the greatest genetic variety outside Africa, a sign of a long and rich human history.

People reached the Americas much later, probably crossing from Siberia into Alaska. Footprints at White Sands in New Mexico may be over 20,000 years old, though some scientists question those dates.

Along the way, humans created cave paintings, music and stories. Genes carry traces of this whole journey in every person alive today.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Humans evolved from monkeys or chimpanzees.

Truth: Humans did not descend from any living ape. We share common ancestors with them. Chimpanzees are our closest living relatives, like cousins rather than grandparents.
