How do opinion polls work?

In 1936 an American magazine asked over 2 million people who would win the election and got it badly wrong, while a pollster asking far fewer people got it right.

The short answer

An opinion poll asks a small group of people a question and uses their answers to estimate what a much bigger group thinks. Before an election, pollsters might phone or visit 1,000 to 2,000 voters and ask whom they plan to vote for.

The trick is choosing that small group well. If the sample is picked at random, so that every kind of voter has a fair chance of being asked, the result usually lands close to the real answer. If it is picked carelessly, even millions of replies can mislead.

Surprising but true

A well-chosen sample of about 1,000 people can describe the views of a whole country to within about 3 percentage points, whether the country has 3 crore people or 140 crore.

Tasting the soup

Think of a big pot of dal. You don't need to drink the whole pot to know if it needs salt. One spoonful is enough, as long as the dal has been stirred well.

A poll works the same way. Random sampling is the stirring. It makes sure the spoonful holds a little of everything: young and old, city and village, rich and poor. That is why a poll of 1,000 people can work for a country as big as India. The size of the pot hardly matters. What matters is the stir.

The poll that got it wrong

In 1936, an American magazine called the Literary Digest mailed out about 10 million ballots asking who would win the presidential election. Around 2.4 million came back, and the magazine predicted a big win for Alf Landon.

Franklin D. Roosevelt won by a landslide. The magazine had found its names in phone books and car registration lists, and in the 1930s many poorer Americans had neither a phone nor a car. A young pollster called George Gallup asked far fewer people, but chose them more carefully, and got the winner right.

Plus or minus

Every poll comes with a margin of error. For a random sample of 1,000 people, it is about 3 percentage points. So if a poll says 52% support a candidate, the true figure is probably somewhere between 49% and 55%.

That is why a close race is so hard to call. If two candidates are 2 points apart, the poll can't really say who is ahead. Pollsters also adjust, or "weight", their results. If too few young people answered, each young person's answer counts a little more, so the sample matches the population. This part of the work is based on probability, and it is where good and bad polls often differ.

Exit polls on voting day

An exit poll asks people how they voted just after they leave the polling station. In India, the Election Commission does not allow exit poll results to be published until the last phase of voting is over, so that they can't sway people still waiting to vote. In the USA, TV networks usually wait until polls close in a state before reporting.

Exit polls are not always right. In India's 2024 general election, most exit polls predicted a much larger win for the ruling alliance than it actually got. In the USA in 2016, several state polls underestimated support for Donald Trump.

Why polls still matter

Elections are just one use for polls. Governments, scientists and companies use the same methods to find out how many children have been vaccinated, what people think of a new law or which snack flavour to sell. A good survey asks clear questions in a fair order. A question like "Don't you agree that homework is too long?" pushes people towards one answer, and a careful pollster would never ask it that way. Even the order of questions matters. Ask about crime first, and people may rate their police differently on the next question.

Myth or fact?

Myth: To know what a country thinks, you have to ask a large part of the country.

Truth: What matters most is that the people asked are chosen fairly, so every group has a fair chance of being included. A huge but lopsided sample can be far worse than a small, random one.

Try this

Put 30 red and 70 blue counters, beads or paper slips in a bag. Without looking, pull out 10, count the reds, and put them back. Do it five times. Your guesses will wobble around 3 reds out of 10, and the wobble is exactly what pollsters call the margin of error.
