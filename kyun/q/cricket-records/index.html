What are cricket's most famous records?

One batter needed just four runs to finish with a Test average of 100. He was bowled second ball for nought.

The short answer

Cricket is a game of numbers, and fans remember records the way other people remember birthdays. The most famous include Don Bradman's Test batting average of 99.94, Brian Lara's 400 not out, Sachin Tendulkar's 100 international centuries and Muttiah Muralitharan's 800 Test wickets.

Some records feel almost impossible to beat. Others fall every few years as the game changes. One-day and T20 cricket, with their fast scoring, have produced a flood of new ones. That is part of the fun: a record set today might be broken by a teenager who is practising right now.

Surprising but true

The first double century in a one-day international was scored by a woman, Australia's Belinda Clark, in 1997, thirteen years before any man did it.

The magic of 99.94

Don Bradman of Australia played 52 Tests between 1928 and 1948. He scored 6,996 runs and was out 70 times, giving a batting average of 99.94. Most great batters average about 50 to 60. Bradman's number is so far ahead that statisticians say no record in any major sport is quite like it.

He needed just four runs in his last innings to reach 100. The crowd at The Oval stood and cheered him, the England players gave him three cheers, and he was bowled second ball for nought. Some people think the emotion of the moment got to him.

Big scores

The highest score in a Test innings belongs to Brian Lara of the West Indies. He made 375 against England in 1994, lost the record to Matthew Hayden's 380 in 2003, and won it back with 400 not out in Antigua in 2004. Lara also holds the highest first-class score: 501 not out for Warwickshire in 1994.

In one-day internationals, Rohit Sharma hit 264 against Sri Lanka at Eden Gardens, Kolkata, in 2014. He is also the only player with three ODI double centuries. The first ODI double hundred came from Australia's Belinda Clark in 1997. Sachin Tendulkar became the first man to do it, in 2010.

What does average mean?In the 1950s, the US Air Force measured about 4,000 pilots to design an "average" cockpit. Not one pilot was average.

All ten wickets

A team has ten wickets in an innings, and taking all of them alone is extremely rare in Tests. Only three bowlers have done it. Jim Laker of England took 10 for 53 against Australia in 1956, and 19 wickets in the match. Anil Kumble took 10 for 74 against Pakistan in Delhi in 1999.

The third story is the strangest. In December 2021, Ajaz Patel took all ten Indian wickets for New Zealand at the Wankhede Stadium in Mumbai. Ajaz had been born in Mumbai and moved to New Zealand as a child, so he did it in his own birth city.

The long-haul records

Sachin Tendulkar played 200 Tests, scored 15,921 Test runs and made 100 international centuries, all records. In 2023 Virat Kohli passed Sachin's 49 one-day hundreds with his 50th, in a World Cup semi-final in the same Mumbai stadium. Sachin was watching.

Muttiah Muralitharan of Sri Lanka took exactly 800 Test wickets, the last with the final ball he bowled in Tests, in 2010. The fastest bowlers rarely play as long, because bowling fast is hard on the body.

Sixes and more

Six sixes in an over has been hit in international cricket only a handful of times. Yuvraj Singh did it against England's Stuart Broad at the 2007 T20 World Cup in Durban. The first to do it in any first-class match was Garfield Sobers, in 1968.

Records keep changing, so cricket websites update them daily. Some numbers, like Bradman's, may never be beaten. That is why fans still argue about them in buses, trains and school canteens.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Records in cricket are just about batting.

Truth: Some of the most famous records belong to bowlers, like Jim Laker taking 19 wickets in one Test in 1956, or Anil Kumble and Ajaz Patel taking all ten in an innings.
