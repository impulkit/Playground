What was the Bronze Age?

About 3,300 years ago, a ship sank off the coast of Turkey carrying ten tonnes of copper and one tonne of tin: the recipe for bronze.

The short answer

The Bronze Age was the long stretch of time when the most important material for tools and weapons was bronze. Bronze is an alloy, a mixture of metals: mostly copper with a little tin. It is much harder than pure copper and holds a sharp edge.

In West Asia, the Bronze Age began around 3300 BCE and lasted about 2,000 years. It came after the Stone Age and before the Iron Age. During it, people built great cities, invented writing and set up trade routes across seas and deserts.

Surprising but true

Mesopotamia had no tin mines at all, yet it became a great centre of bronze-making, using tin carried in from far away.

A recipe that changed everything

People had worked copper for thousands of years before bronze. But copper is soft. A copper axe bends and blunts quickly. Somewhere in West Asia, metalworkers learned that melting in some tin made a far tougher metal.

Bronze could be poured into moulds of clay or stone, so the same shape could be made again and again. Farmers got better sickles and ploughs. Carpenters got chisels and saws. Armies got swords, spearheads and helmets. Bronze also made beautiful things: mirrors, bells, jewellery and statues.

In China, bronze-workers of the Shang dynasty, more than 3,000 years ago, cast huge ritual vessels for food and wine, some heavier than a small car. In Europe, people buried bronze swords and shields in rivers and bogs, perhaps as gifts to their gods. Because bronze was valuable, old tools were often melted down and recast, so the objects that survive are only a small share of what was made.

How did Chola artists cast bronze gods?Every Chola bronze began as a figure of beeswax, and every mould was smashed to get the statue out, so no two are exactly alike.

Bronze in the Indus cities

The cities of Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, in today's Pakistan and India, were a Bronze Age civilisation. Their people made copper and bronze tools, fish-hooks, razors and pots. The most famous object is a small bronze figure called the Dancing Girl, about 4,500 years old and only 10.5 cm tall. She stands with one hand on her hip, wearing a stack of bangles on her arm.

She was made by the lost-wax method. A model is shaped in wax, covered in clay and heated, so the wax melts away and leaves a hollow mould. Craftspeople making dhokra figures in Chhattisgarh, Odisha and West Bengal still use a version of this method today.

Tin, the rare ingredient

Copper is found in many places, but tin is not. Mesopotamia had no tin of its own. Traders brought it from far away, possibly from Central Asia. Indus cities traded with Mesopotamia too, sending beads, cotton and other goods by sea along the Harappan trade routes.

In 1982, a sponge diver spotted strange metal "biscuits" on the seabed off Uluburun in southern Turkey. They turned out to be the cargo of a ship that sank around 1300 BCE. On board were about ten tonnes of copper ingots and one tonne of tin, almost exactly the right mix for bronze. There were also ebony, ivory, glass and a gold scarab from Egypt. It is one of the richest ancient wrecks ever found.

How it ended

Around 1200 BCE, many kingdoms around the eastern Mediterranean fell apart within a few decades. Cities were burned, trade routes broke and writing vanished from some regions. Historians still argue about why. Drought, earthquakes, raiders called the "Sea Peoples" and rebellions are all suspects, and it was probably several at once.

When trade broke down, tin became even harder to get. Iron ore, on the other hand, is found almost everywhere. Smiths who learned to work it no longer had to wait for ships from far away. Slowly, iron took over, and the Bronze Age gave way to a new one.

Where the names came from

The idea of a Stone Age, Bronze Age and Iron Age came from Christian Jürgensen Thomsen, a Danish museum curator. In the early 1800s he sorted his museum's objects by what they were made of, and in 1836 he published the idea. The labels stuck and are still used, even though the real story is messier than three neat boxes.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Bronze Age started at the same moment all over the world.

Truth: Bronze-making began in West Asia around 3300 BCE, reached Britain around 2500 BCE, and never really happened in some places, which went from stone tools almost straight to iron. The "ages" are labels historians use, not dates on a world calendar.

Try this

Collect five metal things at home, such as a spoon, a key, a coin, a safety pin and a paper clip. Ask a grown-up what each is made of, and look up which are alloys (mixtures of metals). Brass, for example, is copper mixed with zinc, a close cousin of bronze.
