Do people eat seaweed?

The dark sheet around a sushi roll is seaweed, and Japan's nori farmers owe their harvest to a British scientist.

The short answer

Yes, people have eaten seaweed for thousands of years, especially in East Asia. In Japan, Korea and China it is part of daily meals: wrapped around rice, floated in soup, or tossed in salads. Coastal people in Wales, Ireland, Hawaii and Chile have long eaten it too.

Most people in India and the United States eat seaweed without knowing it. Thickeners made from seaweed go into ice cream, chocolate milk, jelly sweets and even toothpaste. And the jelly called China grass, used in Indian puddings and falooda, is made from red seaweed. Seaweed is a kind of algae, simple living things that make food from sunlight.

Surprising but true

The jelly in some falooda and Indian puddings, often called China grass, is agar, made from red seaweed.

Nori and the Mother of the Sea

The best-known seaweed food is nori, the paper-thin sheets wrapped around sushi. Nori is made by chopping a red seaweed, spreading it on frames and drying it, a bit like making handmade paper.

For centuries Japanese farmers grew it on nets in shallow bays, but nobody knew where the young seaweed came from each season. When harvests failed in the late 1940s, the industry was in trouble. Then in 1949 a British scientist, Kathleen Drew-Baker, found that the seaweed spends part of its life as tiny threads living inside old seashells. Japanese scientists used her work to grow baby nori in tanks. The harvest recovered. Today a monument stands to her in Uto, in southern Japan, and she is remembered there as the "Mother of the Sea".

Soup, salad and laverbread

In Korea, seaweed soup called miyeok-guk is eaten on birthdays and by mothers after a baby is born. Korean gim, toasted with sesame oil and salt, is a popular snack. In Japan, a big brown seaweed called kombu is simmered to make dashi, the savoury stock behind many dishes.

Far from Asia, people in Wales boil a seaweed called laver for hours into a dark paste called laverbread. It is fried and eaten with breakfast.

What are kelp forests?Off the coast of California grow underwater forests taller than a 10-storey building, made of seaweed.

India's seaweed divers

Along the coast of Tamil Nadu, near Rameswaram and the Gulf of Mannar, women have collected seaweed for generations. Many dive from small boats, holding their breath, and pull seaweed from rocks on the seabed. Much of it is sold to factories that make agar and other products.

India also farms seaweed. Farmers tie pieces of seaweed to ropes hung from bamboo rafts in the sea, and harvest them after about six weeks. The government has been encouraging this as a way for fishing villages to earn more. In 2022 China grew far more farmed seaweed than any other country, according to the UN's Food and Agriculture Organization.

Hidden in your food

Several seaweeds give us gels. Agar, from red seaweeds, sets firmly and is used in sweets and in laboratories, where scientists grow bacteria on it. Carrageenan helps keep chocolate milk smooth. Alginate, from brown kelp, thickens sauces and ice cream.

If you read a food label and see one of these names, you are eating a little bit of the ocean.

Fast-growing and filling

Seaweed doesn't need fresh water, fertiliser or farmland. Giant kelp, which grows in kelp forests off California, can grow more than 30 cm in a single day. For this reason, scientists are studying seaweed as a food that could help feed more people without clearing land.

Seaweed is rich in minerals, especially iodine, which the body needs in small amounts. It can contain too much iodine if eaten in large amounts, so in places where it is eaten every day, people tend to eat small portions. A sheet of nori on your rice is fine. A whole bowl of kelp every meal would not be.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Seaweed is a plant that grows in the sea.

Truth: Seaweeds are algae. They make food from sunlight like plants, but they have no true roots, stems, leaves or flowers. The holdfast at the bottom only grips a rock, and the seaweed takes in water and nutrients all over its surface.

Try this

Look at the ingredient labels on ice cream, jelly, chocolate milk and toothpaste at home. Hunt for the words agar, carrageenan or alginate, or the codes E406, E407 and E401. Every one of those comes from seaweed. Make a list of how many you find.
