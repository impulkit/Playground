What makes Kolkata special?

Kolkata has India's oldest metro, a giant bridge held together by rivets instead of bolts, and a festival so grand that UNESCO honoured it.

The short answer

Kolkata sits on the Hooghly River in West Bengal. For more than a century it was the capital of British India and one of the biggest cities in the British Empire. That past left grand buildings, India's first metro and trams that rattled through the streets for 150 years.

But what people love most about Kolkata is its spirit. It is a city of books, poetry, films, football, sweets and long arguments over tea. During Durga Puja, the whole city turns into an open-air art gallery. Today around 1.5 crore people live in its wider metropolitan area.

Surprising but true

Howrah Bridge was built during the Second World War and holds together without a single nut or bolt.

Three villages and a trading post

In 1690, Job Charnock of the East India Company set up a trading post beside three villages: Sutanuti, Gobindapur and Kalikata. The last probably gave the city its name. The British built Fort William and the town grew around it. Calcutta became the capital of British India in 1772.

Merchants, clerks and labourers poured in from across Bengal and beyond. In 1911 the British moved the capital to Delhi, but Calcutta stayed India's largest city for decades more. Its name was officially changed to Kolkata in 2001.

A bridge with no bolts

The Hooghly is wide and busy with boats, so the city needed a bridge that would not block them. Howrah Bridge, opened in 1943, is a cantilever bridge: two huge steel arms reach out from the banks and meet in the middle, with no pillars in the river at all. It is about 705 m long.

The steel is held together by rivets, not nuts and bolts. Today the bridge carries a huge stream of cars, buses, handcarts and people on foot every day.

How do bridges hold up so much weight?Every bridge is a tug-of-war between two forces, a push and a pull. Good engineers decide which parts get which.

City of ideas

In the 1800s, Calcutta became a centre of new thinking, a period later called the Bengali Renaissance. Reformers such as Raja Ram Mohan Roy and Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar campaigned for women's education and against cruel customs. Colleges, newspapers and printing presses spread new ideas fast.

Rabindranath Tagore, born in Kolkata in 1861, became the first Asian to win the Nobel Prize in Literature, in 1913. He wrote the national anthems of both India and Bangladesh. Later, the film-maker Satyajit Ray made films here that are studied around the world, and Mother Teresa ran her mission for the poor in the city.

The festival city

Every autumn, during Durga Puja, Kolkata's neighbourhoods build pandals, temporary halls made of bamboo and cloth. Some are shaped like temples, ships or castles. Inside stand clay idols of the goddess Durga, made by craftspeople in the area called Kumartuli. Millions of people walk from pandal to pandal late into the night. In 2021, UNESCO added Durga Puja in Kolkata to its list of the world's intangible cultural heritage.

Trams, metro and sweets

Kolkata's horse-drawn trams began in 1873 and electric trams in 1902. Only a few routes are left now, but it is the only city in India that still runs them. In 1984, the city opened India's first underground metro. Its yellow Ambassador taxis became a symbol of the city.

Kolkata is also famous for sweets like rosogolla and mishti doi, sweet curd set in clay pots. South of the city, the river meets the sea in the Sundarbans, a mangrove forest where tigers swim between islands.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Kolkata was founded by Job Charnock in 1690.

Truth: Charnock set up an East India Company trading post there in 1690, but the villages of Sutanuti, Gobindapur and Kalikata were already busy places. In 2003 the Calcutta High Court ruled that he should not be called the city's founder.

Try this

Test a cantilever. Put two equal piles of books a ruler's length apart. Lay one ruler on each pile so it sticks out over the gap, and press books on the back ends to hold them down. Can the two rulers meet in the middle and hold a small eraser? That is how Howrah Bridge's two arms reach across the river.
