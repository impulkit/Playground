What is the Tour de France?

For three weeks every July, about 180 riders pedal roughly 3,400 kilometres around France, over mountains higher than any in Britain.

The short answer

The Tour de France is the most famous bicycle race in the world. Every July, about 22 teams of riders race around France, and sometimes into neighbouring countries, for three weeks. The race is split into 21 day-long stages, with two rest days. Altogether riders cover roughly 3,300 to 3,500 kilometres, including long climbs in the Alps and the Pyrenees.

Each rider's times are added up. The rider with the lowest total time wears the yellow jersey, the maillot jaune, and whoever holds it at the end wins. The race usually finishes on the Champs-Élysées in Paris.

Surprising but true

The leader's yellow jersey is yellow because the newspaper that started the race, L'Auto, was printed on yellow paper.

A race to sell newspapers

The Tour was invented in 1903 by a French sports newspaper, L'Auto, to beat a rival paper. Its editor, Henri Desgrange, hoped readers would buy the paper every day to follow the riders. It worked.

Early riders rode heavy bikes with no gears on rough, unlit roads, sometimes through the night. Some cheated by catching trains. In 1919 the leader was given a yellow jersey, matching the colour of L'Auto's pages, so spectators could spot him.

Jerseys of many colours

Besides yellow, there are other prizes. The green jersey goes to the best sprinter, who collects points for fast finishes. The white jersey with red polka dots goes to the King of the Mountains, the best climber. The plain white jersey is for the best young rider, aged 25 or under.

Teams work together. Most riders, called domestiques, help their team leader by fetching water bottles, sheltering him from wind and setting a fast pace on climbs.

Riding in the peloton

The main group of riders is called the peloton. Inside it, riders save energy because those in front block the wind. This is called drafting. A rider sheltered in the middle may use much less effort than one alone at the front.

That is why riders who break away often get caught before the finish. Pushing alone against the air, a form of friction called drag, wears them out. Teams take turns at the front, then drop back to rest.

The route changes every year and is announced each October. Some years the race starts in another country. The 2024 Tour began in Italy and, because Paris was busy with the Olympics, finished in Nice instead. Millions of people line the roads for free, and some camp on mountain passes for days to get a good spot.

Mountains and food

Mountain stages are the hardest. The climb up Alpe d'Huez has 21 hairpin bends. Mont Ventoux is a bare, windy mountain in Provence. Some passes in the Alps rise above 2,000 metres, higher than Ben Nevis, Britain's tallest peak, at 1,345 metres.

Riders can burn about 5,000 to 6,000 kilocalories on a mountain day, more than twice what most adults eat. They eat rice cakes, gels and energy bars while riding, grabbing bags of food from helpers at the roadside.

Champions and cheating

Five riders have won the Tour five times: Jacques Anquetil, Eddy Merckx, Bernard Hinault, Miguel Induráin and, in 2026, Tadej Pogačar of Slovenia. The American Lance Armstrong won seven times, but all seven titles were taken away in 2012 after it was proved he had used banned drugs. Cheating with drugs, called doping, damaged the race badly for years.

In 1989 another American, Greg LeMond, won by just 8 seconds after 3,285 kilometres, the closest finish ever. In recent years Pogačar and Jonas Vingegaard of Denmark have battled for the yellow jersey. A women's Tour de France Femmes began in 2022.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The rider who wins the most stages wins the Tour.

Truth: The overall winner is the rider with the lowest total time across all stages. A rider can win the Tour without winning a single stage, and fast sprinters can win several stages without coming close overall.

Try this

On a windy day, walk into the wind holding a large piece of cardboard flat in front of you, then turn it sideways. Feel how much harder the flat side pushes back. Cyclists crouch low and ride in groups to cut down that push from the air.
