Why does the sea go in and out every day?

The Moon is pulling on the oceans. Right now.

The short answer

The Moon's gravity pulls on everything on Earth, including the oceans. It pulls hardest on the side of Earth facing it, so the water there piles up into a bulge. On the far side, the Moon pulls the water least, and a second bulge forms there too.

As Earth spins, each coast passes through both bulges. When your beach is inside a bulge, the sea creeps up the sand. That is high tide. When it is between bulges, the water slides back. That is low tide. Most coasts get two of each every day.

The Sun pulls too, but less than half as strongly, because it is so much further away. When the Sun and Moon line up, they team up for bigger tides. The pull even stretches the solid ground. The rock under your feet rises and falls by a few tens of centimetres every day, too slowly and smoothly for you to notice.

Surprising but true

The Moon pulls on you too. And on the solid ground, which rises a little under your feet each day.

Why the tides come later each day

High tides don't come at the same time each day. They arrive about 50 minutes later. That is because while Earth spins, the Moon keeps moving along its path around us. Earth has to turn a little extra to catch up, so the gap between high tides is about 12 hours and 25 minutes.

Fishermen, sailors and port workers live by these times. In India, the Survey of India prints tide tables for ports such as Mumbai, Kolkata and Kandla, so ships know when there is enough water to sail.

Spring tides and neap tides

At full moon and new moon, the Sun, Earth and Moon sit roughly in a line. Their pulls add together, so high tides are higher and low tides are lower. These are called spring tides. You can see how the Moon's shape changes in moon phases.

At half moon, the Sun and Moon pull at right angles. Their pulls partly cancel out, and the difference between high and low tide is smallest. These gentle tides are called neap tides.

Where the tides are biggest

In the open ocean, the tide only rises by about a metre. But in long, narrow bays, the water gets squeezed and piles up much higher. The Bay of Fundy in Canada has the biggest tides on Earth, up to about 16 m, as tall as a five-storey building.

India's biggest tides are in the Gulf of Khambhat in Gujarat, where the sea can rise and fall by several metres. Nearby, at Lothal, people of the Indus civilisation built a large brick basin over 4,000 years ago. Some archaeologists think it was a dock that let boats in on the high tide. Others think it held water. They still disagree.

In some rivers, the rising tide rushes upstream as a single wave called a tidal bore. The Hooghly near Kolkata has one, and so does the Qiantang River in China.

A forest that floods twice a day

In the Sundarbans, the tide floods the mangrove forest twice a day and drains it again. The trees grow breathing roots that poke up out of the mud, and even the tigers there are strong swimmers.

The tides are slowly changing our planet too. Their drag acts like a gentle brake on Earth's spin, making each day longer by about two thousandths of a second every hundred years. At the same time the Moon is drifting away from us, about 4 cm a year, roughly as fast as your fingernails grow.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Spring tides only happen in springtime.

Truth: Spring tides have nothing to do with the season. They are the extra-big tides that come twice a month, at full moon and new moon, when the water seems to "spring" up higher.
