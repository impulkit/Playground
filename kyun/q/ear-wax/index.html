What is ear wax for?

Ear wax isn't dirt. It's a sticky, slightly acidic shield your ears make on purpose, and it slowly carries itself out on a moving belt of skin.

The short answer

Your ears make wax to protect themselves. The narrow tunnel from the outer ear to the eardrum, called the ear canal, is lined with delicate skin. Ear wax coats that skin and keeps it from drying out and cracking.

The wax is sticky, so it traps dust, tiny bits of grit and even small insects before they get near the eardrum. It's also slightly acidic and contains substances that slow the growth of bacteria and fungi. Doctors call it cerumen.

Best of all, the ear cleans itself. Old wax moves slowly out of the canal on its own and falls away, often without you ever noticing. Some people make more wax than others, and that's normal too.

Surprising but true

Whether your ear wax is wet and sticky or dry and flaky depends mostly on a single gene, the same one that affects how much your armpits smell.

How it's made

Ear wax is a mix. Glands in the outer third of the ear canal make oily and waxy substances. These blend with dead skin cells and a few hairs. The result can be pale yellow, orange, brown or grey.

The inner part of the canal, near the eardrum, has no wax glands at all. So wax you find deep inside usually got pushed there by something, often a cotton bud.

The skin conveyor belt

The skin in your ear canal does something unusual. Instead of just flaking off where it is, it grows slowly outwards, from the eardrum towards the opening, at roughly the speed your fingernails grow.

As it moves, it carries old wax along like a slow conveyor belt. Jaw movements when you chew and talk help shift it too. By the time wax reaches the outer ear, it's dry and ready to wash away in the shower.

That's why most people never need to clean inside their ears. For a few, especially older adults or people who wear hearing aids, wax can build up and block the ear. Then a doctor or nurse can soften it with drops or wash it out gently.

Wet or dry? Your genes decide

There are two main types of ear wax. Wet wax is sticky and honey-brown. Dry wax is crumbly and grey. In 2006, Japanese scientists found that the type depends mainly on one gene, called ABCC11.

Which type you have often reflects where your ancestors lived. Most people with roots in Africa or Europe have wet wax. Dry wax is far more common in East Asia, in places such as Korea, China and Japan. In India, both types are found.

The same gene affects certain sweat glands, so people with dry ear wax usually have less smelly armpits. One small change in your genes shows up in two very different places.

A whale's diary

Ear wax tells scientists stories about animals too. Big whales build up wax in their ear canals throughout their lives, in layers, a bit like rings in a tree trunk. When a whale dies and washes ashore, researchers can cut the wax plug open and count the layers.

In 2013, scientists studying one blue whale's earplug, about 25 cm long, read a record of its whole life. The layers showed when it became an adult, how stressed it was and which pollutants it had swallowed, year by year.

Leave it alone

Doctors around the world give the same simple advice. Don't put anything smaller than your elbow in your ear, an old joke that still makes the point. That includes cotton buds, hairpins and keys.

"Ear candling", where a hollow candle is lit in the ear, doesn't remove wax and can burn the face and ear. If your ear hurts, feels blocked or you suddenly can't hear well, tell an adult and see a doctor.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ear wax means your ears are dirty and need cleaning out with cotton buds.

Truth: Ear wax is a normal, healthy coating that protects the ear. Cotton buds often push wax deeper and can scratch the canal or even hurt the eardrum. Washing the outer ear is enough for most people. If ears feel blocked, a doctor can help.

Try this

Put a dot of washable ink on the back of your hand and watch it over several days. It slowly fades as your skin sheds. The skin in your ear canal sheds too, but it also creeps outwards, carrying wax towards the opening, which is why ears mostly clean themselves.
