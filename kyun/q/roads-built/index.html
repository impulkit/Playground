How are roads built?

A good road is like a layered sandwich. The black top you see is only a thin slice of it.

The short answer

Building a road starts with the ground. Workers scrape away soft soil and plants, then flatten and squash the earth underneath with heavy rollers until it is firm. This is called the subgrade.

On top go layers of crushed stone, coarse at the bottom and finer above. Each layer is spread and rolled hard, so the stones lock together and spread the weight of traffic over a wide area. Finally comes the surface. Most roads are topped with asphalt, small stones mixed with hot, sticky black bitumen. It is spread by a paving machine and pressed smooth by rollers while it is still hot. Some roads are topped with concrete instead. Concrete roads cost more to build but can last much longer, so they are often used on busy highways and expressways.

The top is shaped slightly higher in the middle than at the edges, so rain runs off into drains.

Surprising but true

In the 1880s and 1890s, cyclists were among the loudest voices demanding smooth paved roads in America, years before cars became common.

The Romans and Grand Trunk Road

People have built roads for thousands of years. The Romans laid more than 80,000 kilometres of paved roads across their empire, with deep layers of stones and a curved top for drainage. Some Roman roads are still in use.

In South Asia, a great road has run across the north for well over 2,000 years. In the 1500s, the ruler Sher Shah Suri rebuilt it with rest houses and trees along the way. Under British rule it became known as the Grand Trunk Road. Today, parts of it run from Bangladesh through India and Pakistan into Afghanistan.

McAdam's stones and tar

In the early 1800s, the Scottish engineer John McAdam had a cheap new idea. Instead of big stones, he used small, sharp broken stones packed in layers, which locked together under passing wheels. The road was higher in the middle so water drained away. These became known as macadam roads.

Horses and carriages kicked up clouds of dust on them, though. In 1902, the British engineer Edgar Hooley patented a way of binding the stones with tar. He called it tarmac, short for tar macadam. Modern roads usually use bitumen, made from crude oil, instead of tar.

How was the bicycle invented?The first bicycle, built in Germany in 1817, had no pedals. You sat on it and pushed along the road with your feet.

Bikes, cars and potholes

In the USA, it wasn't drivers who first demanded better roads. In the 1880s and 1890s, cyclists formed clubs that campaigned for smooth paved roads, in what became known as the Good Roads Movement. When cars arrived soon after, the roads were partly ready for them.

Every road eventually wears out. Water seeps through cracks and softens the layers below. Traffic pounds the weak spot, and a pothole opens. In cold places like the northern USA, water freezes and expands inside cracks, breaking roads apart each winter. In India, heavy monsoon rains do similar damage.

Roads made from rubbish

In the early 2000s, Rajagopalan Vasudevan, a chemistry professor in Madurai, Tamil Nadu, found a use for waste plastic bags. Shredded and melted into hot bitumen, the plastic helped bind the stones and keep water out. His method has since been used to build thousands of kilometres of roads in India.

Engineers elsewhere are trying old car tyres, crushed glass and recycled asphalt scraped up from worn-out roads. India now has one of the largest road networks in the world, over 60 lakh kilometres, and finding cheap, tough materials to build and fix it is a big task.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Potholes are caused only by heavy trucks.

Truth: Trucks do add stress, but water is the real villain. It seeps under the surface, weakens the layers below and, in cold places, freezes and cracks the road from inside. Good drainage is one of the most important parts of any road.

Try this

Build a road in a clear plastic cup. Put a layer of soil at the bottom and press it down, then a layer of small pebbles, then a layer of sand, pressing each one firm. Slowly pour a little water on top and watch where it goes. Try another cup with only soil and compare.
