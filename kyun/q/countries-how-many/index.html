How many countries are there?

Ask five people how many countries there are and you might get five answers. Most people say 195, but it isn't that simple.

The short answer

The most common answer is 195. That is the 193 member countries of the United Nations, plus two "observer states" that take part in the UN but cannot vote: the Holy See, which runs Vatican City, and Palestine.

But the real answer depends on who is counting. Some places have their own government, borders and passports, yet are not UN members, because other countries disagree about their status. Different lists can give anything from about 193 to about 200.

So a better question might be: what makes a place a country? Usually it needs land with clear borders, people who live there, a government that runs it, and other countries willing to treat it as a country.

Surprising but true

Vatican City, the world's smallest country, is about 0.44 km², smaller than many school and college campuses. It sits entirely inside the city of Rome.

Becoming a country

The number of countries has changed a lot. When the UN began in 1945, it had 51 members, including India, which joined before it was even independent. As colonies in Africa and Asia won freedom in the 1950s and 1960s, membership grew fast.

In 1991 the Soviet Union broke up into 15 separate countries, including Russia, Ukraine and Kazakhstan. The newest UN member is South Sudan, which split from Sudan in 2011 after its people voted for independence.

The tricky cases

Some places are recognised by many countries but not all. Kosovo declared independence from Serbia in 2008. Over 100 countries recognise it, but others, including India, do not.

Taiwan has its own government, army and elections. China says it is part of China, and most countries, including India and the USA, do not officially recognise it as a separate country. Places like these are why the count is never settled. Each case is tied up with history and politics, and governments disagree strongly.

Who decides where one country ends and another begins?You can't see borders from space. People decided them, sometimes in a few weeks, sometimes over centuries.

Not quite countries

Some places look like countries but belong to another. Greenland, the world's largest island, runs most of its own affairs but is part of the Kingdom of Denmark. Puerto Rico and Guam are territories of the USA. Hong Kong has its own money and sports teams but is part of China.

Then there are the four nations of the United Kingdom: England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland. People there often call them countries, and they have their own football teams. But together they make up one member of the UN. Whether you call a place a country can depend on whether you are talking about law, sport or everyday life.

Giants and tiddlers

Countries come in wildly different sizes. Russia is the largest, about 1.7 crore km², so wide that it spans 11 time zones. India is the seventh largest.

At the other end, Vatican City is about 0.44 km², roughly the size of a large park. Monaco, on the French coast, is about 2 km². All its people could fit inside one big cricket stadium.

Neighbours and oddities

China and Russia each touch 14 other countries, more than anyone else. Some countries are completely surrounded by just one other: Lesotho sits entirely inside South Africa, and San Marino and Vatican City inside Italy.

Liechtenstein and Uzbekistan are "doubly landlocked". To reach the sea from either one, you must cross at least two other countries.

Counting in sport

Sports bodies count differently again. FIFA, which runs world football, has more than 200 members, because England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland each play separately. The Olympics welcomes teams from more than 200 places too. For a sports fan, the world has more countries than the UN says.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Everyone agrees on exactly how many countries there are.

Truth: There is no single list. The UN has 193 members, but some places, such as Kosovo and Taiwan, run their own affairs and are recognised as countries by some governments and not others.

Try this

Open an atlas or a printed world map and count the countries on one continent, such as Africa or South America. Ask a friend to count the same continent without comparing. If your numbers differ, look closely at where you disagreed. Those spots are often the tricky cases that experts argue about too.
