Why is Mawsynram the wettest place on Earth?

A village in Meghalaya gets so much rain each year that, if none of it drained away, it would be deeper than a three-storey building.

The short answer

Mawsynram is a village in the Khasi Hills of Meghalaya, in north-east India. It gets about 11,800 mm of rain in an average year. Most lists of world records call it the wettest place on Earth.

The reason is geography. Each summer, the monsoon winds blow north from the Bay of Bengal, full of moisture picked up over the warm sea. They cross the flat plains of Bangladesh, where nothing stops them. Then they slam into the steep southern edge of the Khasi Hills. The air is forced upwards, cools, and drops its water as rain, again and again. Mawsynram sits in just the right spot to catch it.

Surprising but true

Cherrapunji, about 15 km from Mawsynram, still holds the record for the most rain in a single year, about 26,000 mm between August 1860 and July 1861.

Mountains that squeeze clouds

When moist air rises, it cools. Cool air can't hold as much water vapour as warm air, so the vapour condenses into droplets and forms clouds. When the droplets grow heavy enough, they fall. You can follow this in clouds and rain.

The Khasi Hills rise sharply from the plains to about 1,400 m in only a short distance. The monsoon winds have nowhere to go but up, and the air is wrung out like a wet towel. Rain can fall for days without stopping. Some valleys and slopes funnel the clouds even more, which is why a few villages get far more rain than others just a short drive away.

Why does the monsoon come?Every year, the wind turns around. And the rain arrives.

Mawsynram and Cherrapunji

Cherrapunji, also called Sohra, is about 15 km east of Mawsynram. For many years it was known as the wettest place on Earth, and it still holds two world records. Between August 1860 and July 1861, it received about 26,000 mm of rain, the most ever recorded in a single year. In July 1861 alone it got about 9,300 mm.

In long-term averages, though, Mawsynram usually edges ahead. Lloró, a town in the rainforest of Colombia, has also claimed the title, but its records are not as complete. So the crown is shared and argued over, which is why many books call Mawsynram and Cherrapunji the two wettest places on Earth.

Living with endless rain

People in the Khasi Hills have adapted cleverly. During the heaviest rains, some farmers wear a "knup", a full-body shield woven from bamboo and leaves, shaped like a turtle shell. It keeps the rain off while leaving both hands free for work.

Houses have sloping roofs, and some have tin roofs lined with grass to soften the drumming of rain. People dry clothes over fires indoors, and markets keep running whatever the weather.

Bridges that grow

The Khasi and Jaintia people grow bridges. They guide the roots of the Indian rubber tree, a cousin of the banyan, across streams, using bamboo frames to direct them. Over 15 years or more, the roots thicken and twist together into a strong, living bridge.

Some bridges are thought to be centuries old, and they get stronger as they grow. A famous "double-decker" bridge at Nongriat has two levels of roots, one above the other. Unlike wooden bridges, which rot in the constant damp, living bridges love the rain.

Too wet, then too dry

Strangely, Cherrapunji and nearby towns can run short of drinking water in winter. Most of the rain falls in just a few months. The steep slopes and thin soil, worn away by years of tree cutting, let water rush straight downhill into Bangladesh instead of soaking in.

Communities are now planting trees, building tanks and protecting springs. In the water cycle, where water stays can matter as much as how much falls.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Cherrapunji is the wettest place on Earth.

Truth: Cherrapunji holds records for the most rain in one year and one month, but in most long-term averages nearby Mawsynram gets a little more. Both are among the very wettest places on the planet.

Try this

Put a straight-sided jar outside before rain is forecast, away from roofs and trees. Measure how many millimetres collect in one day with a ruler. Then work out how many days of rain like that it would take to reach Mawsynram's 11,800 mm.
