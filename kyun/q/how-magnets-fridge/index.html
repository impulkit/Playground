How do fridge magnets stick?

A floppy fridge magnet is secretly striped, with north and south poles side by side, which is why it grips on one face and barely on the other.

The short answer

A fridge door is wrapped in a thin skin of steel, and steel is mostly iron. Iron is one of the few materials a magnet can really pull on. When you press a magnet against the door, it tugs on the iron, and that tug is strong enough to hold up the magnet and a sheet of paper or two.

The flat, bendy magnets that come free with pizza menus are cleverer than they look. They are made of a powder called ferrite, a kind of iron oxide (close cousin of rust), mixed into rubber. A factory then magnetises them in thin stripes, north, south, north, south. That pattern pushes most of the pull out of the back, so the front hardly sticks at all.

Surprising but true

Rub the backs of two flexible fridge magnets together and they slide in little jerks, clicking over each other's hidden stripes like a zip.

Tiny compass needles inside the iron

Inside a piece of iron are billions of microscopic regions called domains. Each one behaves like a tiny compass needle. Normally they point every which way and cancel out, so a nail isn't magnetic. Bring a magnet close and many of those needles swing round to line up with it. For a moment the steel door becomes a weak magnet itself, pointing the opposite way, and opposites attract.

That is why the pull vanishes when you take the magnet away. The domains relax and go back to pointing anywhere. A steel spoon behaves the same way, and so does a paper clip. Aluminium cans, copper wires and gold rings don't have domains that line up like this, so magnets ignore them.

How strong is a fridge magnet?

A typical fridge magnet's field is around 100 times stronger than Earth's own magnetic field, the one that turns a compass. That sounds big until you meet the magnets inside a hospital MRI scanner, which are hundreds of times stronger again and can yank a metal chair across a room.

Fridge magnets are weak on purpose. A tiny neodymium magnet, the silver kind in earbuds, can pinch your skin if two of them snap together. Most fridge magnets would struggle to hold a thick school notebook, which is fine, because their job is a shopping list.

The magnet you never noticed

Every fridge already has a long magnet built in. Look at the soft rubber strip around the door. Hidden inside is a flexible magnetic strip that pulls the door tight against the steel frame and keeps cold air in.

There is a serious story behind it. Old fridges in the USA shut with metal latches that could only be opened from outside. Children playing hide-and-seek sometimes climbed into old fridges dumped in yards and got trapped. In 1956, the US Refrigerator Safety Act required doors that could be pushed open from the inside. Magnetic seals solved it neatly. A child can push the door open, yet it still closes tightly enough to keep milk cold.

Why some fridges won't hold a magnet

If you have tried to stick a holiday souvenir to a shiny new fridge and it slid straight down, you weren't imagining it. Many modern fridges use stainless steel. Some types of stainless steel contain a lot of nickel and chromium, arranged in a way that stops the domains forming. They look like steel but are almost invisible to a magnet.

Manufacturers sometimes add a magnetic layer to stainless doors because so many families complained. In India and the USA alike, the fridge door has quietly become the family noticeboard. Timetables, menus, drawings and wedding cards all hang there, held up by iron powder and rubber.

And the strangest part? A compass needle is just a skinny magnet like the one on your refrigerator. It swings north because it is feeling a far bigger magnet, made by swirling molten iron thousands of kilometres under your feet: Earth itself.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Magnets stick to all metals.

Truth: Magnets pull strongly only on a few metals, mainly iron, nickel, cobalt and most steels. Aluminium, copper, gold and many stainless steels don't grab a fridge magnet at all.

Try this

Take two old flexible fridge magnets, the flat advertising kind. Put them back to back and slide one across the other slowly, first in one direction, then turned 90 degrees. In one direction you will feel bumps and clicks, which are the hidden stripes catching on each other. In the other direction it slides smoothly.
