Why are bananas curved?

A banana starts life pointing down, then slowly bends to point up at the sky, and that turn is what gives it its curve.

The short answer

Bananas are curved because they change direction as they grow. A banana bunch hangs from a heavy stalk that droops downwards, so at first the young fruits point at the ground.

Then something happens. Each banana starts to grow away from gravity and towards the sunlight, curling upwards. Botanists call this negative geotropism, which means growing against the pull of gravity. The side facing down grows faster than the side facing up, so the fruit bends into its familiar arc.

Surprising but true

The banana plant isn't a tree. Its "trunk" is made of tightly rolled leaf bases, and it is really a giant herb.

Plants that turn

Plants can't walk, but they move all the time, just very slowly. Roots grow down, following gravity. Shoots grow up, against it. Sunflowers turn their heads, and climbing plants twist around poles.

Can plants move?Touch a chhui-mui leaf and it folds shut in seconds. Plants move all the time; most are just too slow for us to notice.

Plants sense gravity using tiny grains of starch that settle at the bottom of special cells, like sand in a jar. A hormone then builds up on one side and makes those cells grow longer. That's the same pull you can read about in why things fall.

A tree that isn't

A banana plant can grow taller than a two-storey house, but it has no wood. What looks like a trunk is called a pseudostem, meaning "false stem". It is made of the bases of huge leaves, rolled tightly around each other like a newspaper.

The real stem stays underground. Once a plant has produced a bunch, its pseudostem dies and is cut down. New shoots, called suckers, grow from the base and carry on. Farmers plant these suckers instead of seeds. A single bunch can hold well over a hundred bananas, arranged in rows called hands, and each banana is called a finger.

Seedless clones

Wild bananas are full of hard, black seeds, as big as peppercorns, with little flesh. Thousands of years ago, farmers in New Guinea and Southeast Asia found rare plants with almost no seeds and spread them by planting suckers.

So the bananas in a shop are clones, plants that are genetically almost identical. That makes them easy to grow, but risky. In the 1950s, a soil fungus called Panama disease destroyed most plantations of the Gros Michel, then the world's favourite export banana. Growers switched to the Cavendish. Now a new form of the same disease is attacking Cavendish farms in Asia, Africa and Latin America.

India's bananas

India grows more bananas than any other country, roughly a quarter of the world's crop. Most are eaten in India. There are dozens of local kinds: the small, sweet elaichi banana, the red banana, and the large nendran of Kerala, fried into banana chips.

Almost every part of the plant gets used. Raw bananas go into curries, the purple flower becomes a vegetable dish, and the stem's core is cooked too. Banana leaves serve as plates, especially at festive meals such as the Onam feast, as you can read in Onam and Pongal.

A slightly radioactive snack

Bananas are rich in potassium, a mineral your nerves and muscles need. A tiny fraction of all natural potassium is a radioactive form, so bananas are very slightly radioactive. So are you, for the same reason.

The amount is far too small to harm anyone. Scientists sometimes joke about a "banana equivalent dose" to explain how small everyday radiation is.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Bananas grow on trees.

Truth: The banana plant has no wood. Its tall stem is made of leaf bases wrapped tightly around one another. After it fruits once, that stem dies and a new shoot grows from the base.

Try this

Put a green banana in a closed paper bag with a ripe apple or ripe banana. Leave another green banana on its own. Check them each day. The bagged banana should ripen faster, because ripe fruit gives off a gas called ethylene that tells other fruit to ripen.
