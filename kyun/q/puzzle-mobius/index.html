What is a Möbius strip?

Take a strip of paper, give it half a twist and tape the ends. You now hold a shape with only one side and one edge.

The short answer

A Möbius strip is a loop with a half-twist. You make one by taking a strip of paper, flipping one end over and joining it to the other.

That small twist changes everything. A normal loop, like a bangle or a rubber band, has two sides, inside and outside, and two edges. A Möbius strip has just one side and one edge. Run your finger along its surface and you will travel over what looked like both the "front" and "back" before arriving where you started. It is pronounced "MUR-bee-us" or "MOE-bee-us".

Surprising but true

The recycling symbol printed on bottles and boxes, with its three chasing arrows, is drawn as a Möbius strip.

Two discoveries in one year

In 1858, two German mathematicians found this shape at about the same time, without knowing of each other. Johann Listing seems to have found it a few weeks earlier. But August Möbius described it more fully, and his work became better known, so the strip carries his name.

They were studying a new kind of maths called topology. It asks what stays the same about a shape when you bend, stretch or squash it, as long as you don't cut or glue it. To a topologist, a coffee mug and a doughnut count as the same shape, because each has exactly one hole.

The ant test

Picture an ant walking along the middle of a paper loop. On a normal loop, it stays on the outside forever. On a Möbius strip, after one lap it finds itself on what looked like the underside. After two laps, it is back where it started.

The Dutch artist M. C. Escher drew exactly this in 1963. His print Möbius Strip II shows nine red ants marching round and round a Möbius strip, never reaching an edge. You can find more of his mind-bending pictures in Escher's maths art.

How did M.C. Escher use maths in art?An artist who struggled with maths at school ended up drawing staircases that climb forever and lizards that crawl out of the page, and mathematicians fell in love with his work.

Cutting surprises

Cut a Möbius strip down the middle and you might expect two loops. Instead, you get one loop, twice as long, with two full twists. It now has two sides.

Cut a strip one-third of the way in from the edge, and something even stranger happens. You get two loops linked together like chain links. One is a new, thinner Möbius strip. The other is a longer two-sided loop. Many maths teachers love this trick because almost nobody predicts it.

You can keep going. Give a strip a full twist instead of a half, then cut it down the middle. This time you really do get two loops, but they come out linked together. Three half-twists and a cut give you a single loop tied in a knot.

Möbius strips at work

Long ago, some factories used belts to carry power from an engine to machines. A plain belt wears out on one side only. A belt with a half-twist wears evenly on its one surface, so it can last twice as long. The same idea has been used for conveyor belts and old typewriter ribbons.

In 1970, a college student in the USA named Gary Anderson won a design contest with a symbol of three arrows folded into a loop. It is a Möbius strip, and it became the worldwide symbol for recycling.

Rings, roller coasters and molecules

Artists and builders keep returning to this shape. There are Möbius roller coasters, Möbius sculptures and even buildings shaped like the strip. Chemists have made molecules joined in a Möbius loop.

Some mathematicians enjoy an even weirder cousin, the Klein bottle. It is a bottle whose inside and outside are the same surface, though a true one can't be built in our three-dimensional world. Shapes that look possible but cannot exist have their own story in impossible shapes.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A Möbius strip is just a twisted ring with two sides, like any loop.

Truth: An ordinary loop has an inside and an outside. A Möbius strip has only one surface. An ant could walk over every part of it and return to its starting point without ever crossing an edge.

Try this

Make a Möbius strip from a strip of paper about 3 cm wide and 30 cm long. Give one end half a twist and tape it. Draw a line along the middle without lifting your pencil until you meet the start. Then cut along your line, and after that, try another strip cut one-third of the way in from the edge.
