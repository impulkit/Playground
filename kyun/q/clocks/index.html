How does a clock keep time?

Every clock, from a sundial to an atomic clock, has two parts: something that repeats steadily, and something that counts.

The short answer

To keep time, a clock needs something that repeats at a steady beat, like a heart. That might be the Sun crossing the sky, a swinging pendulum, a buzzing crystal or the tiny vibrations of an atom. Each beat must take exactly the same time as the last.

The second thing a clock needs is a counter. Gears, a computer chip or a person keep track of how many beats have gone by and turn them into seconds, minutes and hours on a dial or screen. The steadier the beat, the better the clock. A good pendulum clock might drift a few seconds a week. The best atomic clocks would take millions of years to drift by one second.

Surprising but true

The quartz crystal inside an ordinary wristwatch vibrates 32,768 times every second.

Shadows and water

The oldest clocks used the Sun. A sundial has a pointer that casts a shadow, and the shadow moves around the dial as the Sun crosses the sky. In the 1720s and 1730s, in Jaipur, Maharaja Sawai Jai Singh II built the Jantar Mantar, a set of giant stone instruments. Its largest sundial, the Samrat Yantra, is about 27 metres tall. Its shadow moves fast enough for you to watch it creep across the stone.

Sundials don't work at night or on cloudy days. So people in ancient Egypt, Babylon, China and India also used water clocks. Water dripped steadily out of one bowl, or into another, and marks showed how much time had passed.

The swinging weight

Around 1600, Galileo noticed that a pendulum of a given length takes the same time for every swing, whether the swing is big or small. In 1656, the Dutch scientist Christiaan Huygens used this to build the first pendulum clock. It was far more accurate than anything before it.

Inside a pendulum clock, a part called the escapement lets the gears turn one tooth at a time with each swing. It also gives the pendulum a tiny push to keep it going. That steady tick-tock sound is the escapement at work. Grandfather clocks in old homes in Kolkata or Boston still run on the same idea.

The buzzing crystal

Most watches and wall clocks today use quartz, a mineral found in ordinary sand. When electricity from a battery passes through a tiny quartz crystal shaped like a tuning fork, it vibrates very steadily, 32,768 times every second.

That number is not random. Halve it 15 times and you get exactly 1. A chip inside the watch does that halving and sends one pulse a second to move the hands or change the screen. Quartz watches arrived in 1969 and soon became cheap enough for almost everyone.

Atoms keep the best time

The most accurate clocks use atoms. Atoms of the metal caesium absorb a particular kind of radio wave at an extremely precise frequency. Since 1967, one second has been officially defined as 9,192,631,770 of these vibrations.

Atomic clocks do far more than tell you when school starts. GPS satellites carry them, and your phone works out where you are by comparing their time signals. An error of just a millionth of a second could put you hundreds of metres off on a map. India's own NavIC satellites also carry atomic clocks, and the National Physical Laboratory in New Delhi keeps the country's official time.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Big Ben is the name of the famous clock tower in London.

Truth: Big Ben is the nickname of the huge bell inside the tower. The tower itself was called the Clock Tower and was renamed the Elizabeth Tower in 2012.

Try this

Tie a key or a metal washer to the end of a piece of string about 50 cm long. Hold the other end still and let it swing. Count the swings in 30 seconds using a phone timer. Now shorten the string to 25 cm and count again. Then try a bigger swing with the long string and see if the count changes.
