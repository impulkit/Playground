Why is Rome called the Eternal City?

Rome's people were calling it eternal over 2,000 years ago. They had a point. It is still a capital, and a Roman aqueduct still feeds one of its fountains.

The short answer

Rome has been called the Eternal City since ancient times. The Roman poet Tibullus used the phrase in the first century BCE, and Romans came to believe their city would last forever. In a way, it has. Rome was the centre of a great empire, then the centre of the Catholic Church, and today it is the capital of Italy.

Walk through Rome and you see every age at once. A 2,000-year-old temple stands beside a café. Ancient columns poke out of modern buildings. About 2.8 million people live in the city today, among ruins older than most countries.

Surprising but true

The Pantheon's concrete dome, built about 1,900 years ago, is still the largest dome of unreinforced concrete in the world.

Wolves and seven hills

Legend says twin boys, Romulus and Remus, were left beside the River Tiber and fed by a she-wolf. Romulus founded Rome on 21 April 753 BCE, and Romans still celebrate the city's birthday on that date. Archaeologists have found the remains of huts on the Palatine Hill from around that time and even earlier.

Rome grew across seven hills beside the Tiber. Over centuries, it became the capital of an empire that stretched from Britain to Egypt. Roads paved with stone linked it all, which is where the saying "all roads lead to Rome" comes from.

Built to last

The Colosseum opened in 80 CE. It could hold about 50,000 people, who came to watch gladiators and wild animal fights. Earthquakes and stone robbers took parts of it away, but most of its outer walls still stand. Visitors can see the tunnels underneath, where animals and fighters waited.

The Pantheon, rebuilt around 125 CE under the emperor Hadrian, has a concrete dome about 43 m across. At the top is a round opening, the oculus, about 9 m wide. When it rains, water falls into the building and drains away through small holes in the floor. Roman concrete was made with volcanic ash, and scientists are still studying why it lasts so well.

How do Roman numerals work?The Colosseum in Rome still has numbers carved over its arches, so that 50,000 Roman spectators could find the right gate.

Water from the hills

The ancient Romans built aqueducts, channels that carried fresh water downhill from springs many kilometres away. The Aqua Virgo, finished in 19 BCE, still carries water to Rome today. It feeds the Trevi Fountain, finished in 1762.

Visitors throw coins over their shoulders into the fountain, because tradition says it means you will return to Rome. More than a million euros are collected each year, and the money goes to a charity that helps people in need.

A country inside a city

Inside Rome lies Vatican City, the smallest country in the world, only about 0.44 km². It is the home of the Pope, the leader of the Catholic Church. St Peter's Basilica, one of the largest churches in the world, rises over a huge square.

In the nearby Sistine Chapel, Michelangelo painted the ceiling between 1508 and 1512, mostly standing on scaffolding with his head tilted back. He also designed the great dome of St Peter's.

Layers upon layers

Because people have lived in Rome for so long, the city stands on layers of its own past. When builders dig a new metro line, they often find ancient houses, streets or burial places. Work can stop for months while archaeologists study them. One new station even displays objects found during digging, so travellers can see Rome's history on their way to work.

Roman influence spreads far beyond the city. Roman numerals still appear on clocks and in film credits, Latin words fill English, and many legal ideas began in Roman law.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The emperor Nero played the fiddle while Rome burned in 64 CE.

Truth: The fiddle had not been invented yet. The Roman historian Tacitus wrote that Nero was away in the town of Antium when the fire started and came back to help organise relief. The story of him singing during the fire was a rumour even then.
