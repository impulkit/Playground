Why do chillies turn red?

A green chilli and a red chilli can come from the same plant. The red one is just older, and it's dressed up for birds.

The short answer

Chillies turn red because they are ripening. A young chilli is green because it is full of chlorophyll, the same green pigment leaves use to catch sunlight. As the fruit matures, the chlorophyll breaks down. At the same time the chilli builds up red and orange pigments called carotenoids.

So a green chilli is an unripe chilli. Leave it on the plant for a few more weeks and it will usually turn red, or yellow, orange, purple or even brown, depending on the variety. In nature, that colour change is a signal. It tells hungry birds: "the seeds inside are ready now."

Surprising but true

Birds can't feel the burn of chillies at all, so they happily eat the hottest ones and spread the seeds far and wide.

The pigments behind the paint

Two pigments do most of the work in red chillies. They are called capsanthin and capsorubin, and they are found almost only in peppers. They belong to the same family as the orange pigment in carrots and the pink in flamingo feathers.

These pigments are so strong that chilli powder is used as a natural food colour around the world. Paprika, made from mild red peppers, colours sausages, cheese and crisps. In India, Kashmiri chilli powder gives rogan josh its deep red without making it too hot.

A fruit made for birds

Why would a plant make its fruit spicy? The heat comes from capsaicin, which makes mammals' mouths feel like they are burning. That keeps mice, squirrels and deer away. They chew seeds and destroy them.

Birds are different. Their heat sensors ignore capsaicin, so a sparrow can eat the hottest chilli with no fuss. Birds swallow the seeds whole, fly off and drop them far away, often with a little fertiliser. In a 2001 study of wild chillies in Arizona, scientists found that rodents destroyed the seeds they ate, while seeds that passed through birds still sprouted. The bright red colour works like an advert aimed at birds, who see red very well.

Why does chilli feel hot?Chilli does not actually burn you. It tricks the same nerves that warn you about a hot stove.

How chillies came to India

It's hard to picture Indian food without chillies, but they are newcomers. Chillies come from the Americas, where people in Mexico grew them at least 6,000 years ago. Portuguese ships carried them to Goa in the 1500s. Before that, Indian cooks used black pepper, ginger and long pepper for heat.

Chillies spread fast. They grew easily, dried well and were cheaper than pepper. Today India grows more dried chillies than any other country, more than a third of the world's crop in 2022. Guntur in Andhra Pradesh is one of the biggest chilli markets anywhere. In the busy season, trucks piled high with red sacks queue outside its market yard, and the air makes visitors sneeze. Indians also eat a lot of what they grow, so most of the crop never leaves the country.

Picked for colour

Some chillies are grown mainly for their colour. Byadgi chillies from Karnataka are wrinkled, deep red and fairly mild. Factories extract their red oil, called oleoresin, for lipsticks and nail polish as well as food.

Farmers spread red chillies on rooftops and fields to dry in the sun. Seen from above, whole villages in parts of Andhra Pradesh and Karnataka turn scarlet in the harvest season. Sunlight slowly fades the colour, though, so the best chillies are dried quickly and kept in the dark.

Same trick, different plants

Plenty of fruits change colour when ripe, from mangoes to tomatoes. Leaves do something similar in autumn in cold countries, when their chlorophyll fades and hidden yellow and red pigments show through. The chilli's trick is the hot taste that keeps the wrong animals away, while the colour waves the right ones in.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Red chillies are always hotter than green chillies.

Truth: Heat depends mostly on the variety, not the colour. A Kashmiri red chilli is mild, while some small green chillies are fiery. A chilli can get a little hotter as it ripens, but colour alone won't tell you.

Try this

Ask an adult to buy two green chillies and keep one on a sunny windowsill for a week or two. Watch it change colour each day and draw the changes. Don't rub your eyes after touching chillies, and wash your hands with soap.
