Why is Tamil called one of the world's oldest living languages?

A Tamil schoolchild today can read lines of poetry composed about 2,000 years ago. Few languages on Earth can say that.

The short answer

Tamil has been spoken and written without a break for more than 2,000 years. Its oldest poems, known as Sangam literature, were composed roughly 2,000 years ago, and schoolchildren in Tamil Nadu still read some of them today. Its oldest surviving grammar, the Tolkappiyam, may be almost as old.

Many ancient languages, such as Latin or ancient Egyptian, survive mainly in books or have changed beyond recognition. Tamil is still the everyday language of about 7 crore people in India, and of millions more in Sri Lanka, Singapore, Malaysia and beyond.

In 2004, the Government of India named Tamil its first "classical language", a title for languages with a very long, independent literary tradition.

Surprising but true

The English word catamaran comes from Tamil kattumaram, meaning "tied logs".

A family of its own

Tamil belongs to the Dravidian family, along with Telugu, Kannada and Malayalam. These languages are not descended from Sanskrit, which belongs to a different family, Indo-European. Over the centuries the two families have borrowed many words from each other, but their grammar and everyday core words are separate.

Tamil builds words by sticking pieces together, one after another. Veedu means house, and veettukku means "to the house". Add more pieces and a single long word can say what English needs a whole phrase for. Linguists call this agglutination, from a Latin word meaning "to glue".

Poems from the Sangam age

Sangam poems are short and full of nature. Poets sorted their world into five landscapes: mountains, forests, farmland, seashore and dry wasteland. Each landscape had its own flowers, animals, gods and feelings. A poem set in the mountains was about lovers meeting. One set by the seashore was about waiting and longing.

Another group of poems, called puram, praised kings, battles and generosity. These verses give historians a picture of trade, food and daily life in early south India. Later came the Thirukkural, 1,330 short couplets of advice on life, still quoted in Tamil homes and speeches and painted inside government buses.

Writing on pots and rocks

The oldest Tamil writing is in a script called Tamil-Brahmi, carved in caves and scratched on pottery. Many inscriptions date from at least the 2nd century BCE. At Keezhadi, near Madurai, archaeologists have found broken pots marked with Tamil-Brahmi letters. The Tamil Nadu archaeology department has argued that some are even older than that. Other scholars want more tests before agreeing.

Tamil-Brahmi words have also turned up far from home, on pottery found at Berenike, an ancient port on the Red Sea coast of Egypt. Tamil merchants traded pepper and gems across the ocean, long before the Chola kings sent their ships to Southeast Asia.

Still changing, still alive

A living language keeps changing, and Tamil is no exception. Spoken Tamil differs quite a lot from the formal written style, so children learn both. Chennai Tamil, Madurai Tamil and Sri Lankan Tamil each sound different. Tamil films, songs and social media spread new slang every year.

Tamil has given a few words to English too. Catamaran comes from kattumaram, "tied logs", a kind of boat still used by fishing families along the coast near Chennai. In Singapore, Tamil is one of four official languages, printed on signs beside English, Malay and Chinese. Its oldest poems and its newest film songs are written with the same letters.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Tamil comes from Sanskrit.

Truth: Tamil belongs to the Dravidian language family, while Sanskrit is Indo-European. The two have borrowed many words from each other, but their grammar and core vocabulary have separate roots.

Try this

Ask a Tamil-speaking friend or relative to teach you to say and write வணக்கம் (vanakkam), meaning hello. Copy it carefully three times. Then ask them for a word their grandparents used that young people don't say any more, and find out why it changed.
