What is bhangra?

Bhangra started as farmers celebrating the wheat harvest in Punjab. Today it fills dance floors in Birmingham, Toronto and New York.

The short answer

Bhangra is a lively folk dance and music from Punjab, the region shared by north-west India and Pakistan. It is driven by the deep, bouncing beat of the dhol, a big double-sided drum. Dancers raise their arms high, bounce their shoulders, hop, kick and spin, often shouting "balle balle" or "hoi" in time with the music.

It began as a celebration of the harvest, especially at Baisakhi in April, when the wheat crop is gathered. Today bhangra is danced at weddings, festivals, college competitions and parties, and it has become one of South Asia's best-known dances worldwide. Its music has blended with pop, reggae and hip-hop to make a sound that is played in clubs from London to Los Angeles.

Surprising but true

Some of the most famous bhangra music was not made in Punjab at all but by Punjabi families in Britain in the 1980s.

A harvest dance

For centuries, farmers in Punjab worked hard through the winter to grow wheat. When the golden crop was ready in spring, villages celebrated. Men danced in a circle around a dhol player, while someone sang short, funny or proud verses called boliyan about village life, love and farming.

Many steps copy farm work, such as sowing seeds or swinging a sickle. Women had their own dance, giddha, with clapping, teasing songs and graceful movements. Today women and girls dance bhangra too, and many teams are mixed.

The dhol

The drum is the heart of bhangra. A dhol is a barrel-shaped wooden drum with skin on both ends, hung from the player's shoulder. One side is struck with a thick, curved stick for a deep boom. The other is hit with a thin cane for a sharp crack.

Other instruments join in. The tumbi is a one-string instrument with a bright, twanging sound. The algoza is a pair of wooden flutes played together, and the chimta is a pair of long iron tongs with jingles, which is clapped and shaken.

After Partition

In 1947, Punjab was divided between India and Pakistan during Partition. Millions of people were forced from their homes. In the years that followed, Punjabis on both sides kept their music and dance alive as a way of holding on to their culture.

In Indian Punjab, colleges and dance teams developed a new, stage-ready bhangra in the 1950s and 1960s. They combined steps from several regional dances, such as jhummar and luddi. They also added bright matching costumes: a kurta, a wrapped cloth called a tehmat, a waistcoat and a turban with a fan-shaped turla on top. This is the style most people now think of as bhangra.

Bhangra goes global

Punjabis who moved to Britain in the 1960s and 1970s took their music with them. In the 1980s, bands in Birmingham and Southall mixed traditional bhangra songs with drum machines, synthesisers and electric guitars. Young British Asians danced to it at daytime parties, and a new sound was born.

In 1998, Panjabi MC released "Mundian To Bach Ke", which mixed bhangra with a TV theme tune. It became a huge hit across Europe a few years later, and an American rapper made a remix. That same year in India, Daler Mehndi's "Tunak Tunak Tun" became famous across the country and, later, on the internet.

On campuses and at weddings

Today, university bhangra teams in the United States, Canada and Britain compete in big inter-college competitions. At Indian weddings, almost every family has someone who will start a bhangra the moment the dhol begins. The beat that once marked the end of a farming year now marks celebrations of every kind.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The bhangra seen at weddings and on stage is exactly the old village harvest dance.

Truth: Bhangra has changed a great deal. After 1947, dance teams combined steps from several Punjabi folk dances and added costumes and choreography for stage shows. British and American DJs later mixed bhangra with pop and hip-hop.

Try this

Make a dhol beat on a cardboard box or an upturned bucket. Hit one side with your palm for a deep "dhum" and tap the other with a pencil for a sharp "ta". Try the pattern dhum, ta-ta, dhum, ta and see if someone at home starts lifting their shoulders.
