What is life like in the hills of Himachal?

A little toy train climbs to Shimla through 102 tunnels, and the apples on Himachal's slopes trace back to one American who arrived there in 1904.

The short answer

Himachal Pradesh is a mountain state in northern India, stretching from green, forested foothills up to snowy peaks and the high, dry valleys of Lahaul and Spiti. Most people live in small towns and villages on slopes, often in houses of stone and wood with slate roofs that shed the snow.

Life follows the seasons and the steep land. Families grow apples, plums and potatoes on terraces cut into the hills. Roads twist in hairpin bends. Winter brings snow to many places, while summer brings visitors escaping the heat of the plains. Many villages have their own local deity, and festivals like Kullu Dussehra fill whole valleys with music.

Surprising but true

Himachal's famous apple orchards owe a lot to Samuel Stokes, an American who settled near Shimla and planted Red Delicious apple trees from the US in 1916.

The summer capital

The British found Shimla's cool air a relief from the scorching plains. From 1864, the government of British India moved there every summer, carrying files, clerks and officials up the mountain. Shimla filled with churches, mock-Tudor buildings and a long promenade called the Mall.

To get there more easily, they built the Kalka–Shimla Railway, which opened in 1903. Its tiny trains run on narrow tracks about 96 km through 102 tunnels and over hundreds of bridges, climbing well over a kilometre in height. In 2008, it was added to UNESCO's list of the Mountain Railways of India, alongside the Darjeeling and Nilgiri lines.

An American and his apples

Apples are Himachal's most famous crop, and their story has an unexpected hero. Samuel Stokes, a young American, came to the hills in 1904 to work with people affected by leprosy. He stayed, married a local woman and settled at Kotgarh, near Shimla.

In 1916, he planted Red Delicious apple trees brought from the US. They grew well in the cool climate, and he encouraged neighbouring farmers to plant them too. Stokes later joined India's freedom struggle and took the name Satyanand. Today, apple growing supports many thousands of families across the state.

Up in the cold desert

North of the high Rohtang Pass lie Lahaul and Spiti, where the land changes completely. Very little rain gets over the mountains, so the valleys are dry, rocky and brown. Spiti looks more like Ladakh or Tibet than the green hills near Shimla.

Villages there are some of the highest lived-in places in India. Old Buddhist monasteries, such as Key and Tabo, cling to the hillsides. Tabo's monastery was founded in 996 and still holds ancient paintings. Snow leopards, ibex and blue sheep live on the high slopes. Farmers here grow barley and green peas in the short summer, watering their fields with channels of melted snow.

For centuries, the Rohtang Pass closed with snow for months each winter. In 2020, the Atal Tunnel opened beneath it, about 9 km long, so Lahaul now stays connected all year.

A hill state's worries

Steep slopes and heavy monsoon rain make landslides a real danger. In recent years, floods and slides have washed away roads, bridges and buildings, especially in 2023. Scientists say that building on fragile slopes, cutting forests and changing rainfall patterns all play a part.

Dharamshala, another hill town, has a different story. Since 1960, the Dalai Lama, the Tibetan Buddhist leader, has lived in McLeod Ganj just above it, and many Tibetans have settled there. On a clear day, visitors can watch monks debating in a courtyard while the snowy Dhauladhar range glows behind them.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The hill states of India are cold everywhere all year.

Truth: Himachal ranges from warm valleys about 350 m above sea level to icy passes above 5,000 m. Lower towns can be hot in summer, while high places like Spiti stay cold and dry most of the year.
