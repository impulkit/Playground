What are stone forests?

In southern China, grey rock pillars taller than houses crowd together like trees, and in Rajasthan's desert lie tree trunks that have turned to stone.

The short answer

"Stone forest" is a name for two quite different wonders. The first kind has no trees at all. It is a crowd of tall rock pillars, carved by rain until they stand like trunks in a wood. The most famous is Shilin, which simply means "stone forest", near Kunming in China.

The second kind was once a real forest. Its fallen trees were buried, and water full of minerals slowly turned them into stone. These petrified forests, as they are called, lie in places like Arizona in the USA and the Thar Desert near Jaisalmer in Rajasthan.

Surprising but true

In Madagascar's Tsingy, the rock pinnacles are so sharp that the local name roughly means "the place where you cannot walk barefoot".

Rain with teeth

Limestone is made mostly of the shells and skeletons of sea creatures that piled up on old seabeds. It is tough, but it has one weakness. Rain mixes with a little carbon dioxide from the air and soil and becomes a very weak acid, about as weak as fizzy water. Limestone slowly dissolves in it.

Over millions of years, rain trickled into cracks and made them wider and deeper. The rock between the cracks was left standing. A landscape shaped this way is called karst, and the same process digs out huge caves underground. At Shilin, some pillars rise as high as a 10-storey building, and the forest spreads over about 350 square km.

China's maze and Madagascar's knives

Shilin's limestone formed on a seabed about 270 million years ago. Local Yi people tell the story of Ashima, a girl turned to stone, and point to one pillar said to be her. Walking paths wind through slots so narrow that two people cannot pass.

Madagascar has a sharper version called the Tsingy. Here the rain has cut the limestone into blades with edges like broken glass. Rangers clip visitors onto steel cables and rope bridges. Lemurs leap between the pinnacles, and some plants and animals live only there, which is one reason UNESCO protected it in 1990.

The Tsingy has a hidden basement too. Beneath the blades, water has carved caves and tunnels, and in places whole sections of roof have collapsed. That leaves deep green canyons with their own small forests, cut off from the rest of the island. Scientists exploring them keep finding species that nobody had described before, from tiny frogs to new kinds of lemur.

Why are Madagascar's animals found nowhere else?Madagascar has been cut off for so long that about nine in ten of its plants and animals live nowhere else.

Trees that turned to rock

In a petrified forest, the trees really were trees. About 225 million years ago, in what is now Arizona, rivers knocked down giant conifers and buried them in mud and volcanic ash. Groundwater rich in silica, the stuff of sand and glass, seeped in. Bit by bit, it filled every cell and replaced the wood.

The result is stone that keeps tree rings and bark, sometimes stained red, yellow and purple by iron and other minerals. Some logs at Petrified Forest National Park are longer than a school bus. US President Theodore Roosevelt protected the area in 1906, after visitors kept carting off pieces as souvenirs.

India's stone trees

At Akal, about 17 km from Jaisalmer, lies a fossil wood park with tree trunks around 180 million years old. They grew when this hot, dry desert sat near a warm, swampy coast. The Geological Survey of India lists the site as a National Geological Monument. In Tamil Nadu, near Tiruvakkarai, more fossil trees lie scattered in the fields.

So the Thar was once a land of tall trees by the sea. That sounds impossible until you learn how the Thar Desert formed, and how ordinary rocks can hold the shells of animals that died long before the first dinosaurs. And the strangest part? The same slow swap of minerals that turned these trunks to stone also turned dinosaur bones into fossils.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Petrified wood is just very old wood that has dried out and gone hard.

Truth: It is really stone. Minerals carried by water slowly filled and replaced the wood, cell by cell, until the log became rock that keeps the shape of rings and bark.

Try this

Stack a small tower of sugar cubes on a plate. Use a spoon or dropper to drip water slowly onto one spot at the top and watch channels and gaps form. Rain does the same thing to limestone, only millions of times more slowly.
