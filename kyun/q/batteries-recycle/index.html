What happens to old batteries?

The heavy battery in a car is one of the most recycled products on Earth, while the tiny one in a phone often ends up in the wrong bin and can start a fire.

The short answer

It depends on the battery. Big lead batteries from cars and inverters are usually collected and melted down, and the lead goes straight into new batteries. Lithium-ion batteries, the kind in phones, laptops and electric cars, can be taken apart for their valuable metals, though many are still lost.

Small batteries, like the AA cells in a remote control, often end up in household rubbish. That wastes metal, and some kinds can leak harmful chemicals into soil. Worse, lithium batteries thrown in the bin can catch fire when rubbish trucks crush them. In many countries, firefighters now see this as a growing problem.

Tiny button batteries, found in watches, car keys and toys, carry another danger. If a small child swallows one, it can burn the inside of the throat within about two hours. Doctors say anyone who sees a child swallow one should get medical help straight away. That's one more reason to keep old batteries in a closed box, out of reach.

Surprising but true

In the United States, about 99% of the lead in old car batteries is recycled, a better rate than for drink cans or paper.

Car batteries, the success story

A car battery is mostly lead plates sitting in acid. Lead is poisonous, but it is also easy to recycle. It can be melted and reused again and again without getting worse.

When someone buys a new car battery, the shop usually takes the old one back. Recyclers break the case, drain and treat the acid, melt the lead and make new plates. In the United States, about 99% of the lead from old batteries is recovered this way. In India, much of the work has long been done by small, informal workshops, where workers often handle lead without proper protection. New rules aim to move more of it into safe, licensed plants.

Taking apart a lithium battery

Inside a phone or car battery are thin sheets coated with lithium, cobalt, nickel, manganese and copper. These metals are mined in only a few places and are expensive, so there is money in getting them back.

First the batteries are fully discharged, so they can't spark. Then machines shred them. The plastic and metal casings are sorted out, leaving a dark powder that recyclers call black mass. Chemicals dissolve the black mass, and each metal is pulled out separately to be made into new batteries. Some plants use furnaces instead, melting everything and separating the metals afterwards. Recycling also eases pressure on mining. Much of the world's cobalt comes from the Democratic Republic of the Congo, where some mines have been found using child labour.

A second life

Electric car batteries slowly hold less charge. After about ten years, a car's battery may only hold 70 to 80 percent of what it once did. That might be too little for long drives, but it is plenty for sitting still.

So some old car batteries get a second job. Companies link them together to store electricity from solar panels during the day and release it at night. Only when they are completely worn out do they go for recycling.

Who is responsible?

In 2022, India brought in rules saying that companies selling batteries must also collect and recycle a share of them. The USA, the European Union and other places have similar laws, and Europe now asks new batteries to contain some recycled metal.

Rules only work if old batteries reach the right place, though. Most of them still sit in drawers, mixed with other e-waste such as old phones and chargers. Many shops and municipal collection points take used batteries. The safest home habit is a box kept away from heat, and a trip to drop them off when it fills up.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A dead battery is empty, so it's safe to throw in the rubbish.

Truth: Most "dead" batteries still hold some charge. Crushed in a rubbish truck, a lithium battery can spark and start a fire, so they should go to a collection point instead.

Try this

Go on a battery hunt at home with an adult. Count every device that runs on batteries, from clocks to toothbrushes. Then make a "dead battery box" from an old container and keep it away from heat. Never open, crush or bite a battery, and if you find a coin-shaped button battery loose, give it straight to an adult.
