What are grasslands, and why do they matter?

Wheat, rice, maize, millet, sugarcane and bamboo are all grasses. Most of what humans eat comes from the same family as a lawn.

The short answer

Grasslands are large open areas where grasses are the main plants, with few or no trees. They grow where it is too dry for thick forest but not dry enough to be a desert. Fire, grazing animals and long dry seasons also stop trees from taking over.

Grasslands go by different names around the world. North America has prairies, South America has pampas, Central Asia has steppes, and Africa has savannas, the grasslands with scattered trees where lions and zebras live. Southern Africa calls its grasslands veld. India has several kinds too, from the Banni grasslands of Kutch to the tall wet grasses of the terai at the foot of the Himalaya.

Surprising but true

Grass grows from the bottom, not the tip. That is why a cow can chew a blade down to a stub and it grows straight back, while a nibbled tree branch can't.

Built to be eaten

Most plants grow from the tips of their stems. Grass is different. Its growing point sits low, near the soil, and each blade is pushed up from below. When a deer bites off the top, the plant just keeps growing.

Many grasses also spread through underground stems called rhizomes, and their roots can reach deep into the soil. When a fire sweeps across a grassland, the tops burn, but the roots and growing points survive underground. Within weeks of rain, fresh green shoots appear.

That toughness means grasslands can feed huge herds. In Africa's Serengeti, more than a million wildebeest follow the rains across the grass each year.

The grasses we eat

Grass doesn't only feed cows and wildebeest. Rice, wheat, maize, barley, oats and millets like ragi and bajra are all grasses whose seeds people learned to grow and harvest thousands of years ago. Sugarcane is a giant grass, and bamboo is the biggest grass of all.

Some of the world's richest farmland was once grassland. The deep, dark soils of the American prairies and the steppes of Ukraine built up over thousands of years from grass roots growing and rotting.

India's forgotten grasslands

India's grasslands have often been overlooked. For many years, some official land maps listed open grasslands as "wasteland", and some were planted with trees or turned into farms. Yet they are home to animals found nowhere else.

The great Indian bustard, one of the heaviest flying birds, lives in the dry grasslands of Rajasthan and Gujarat. Only about 150 are left in the wild, by recent estimates. Blackbuck, Indian wolves, foxes, florican birds and many insects need open grass too. In 2022, India brought cheetahs from Africa to Kuno National Park in Madhya Pradesh, which has stretches of open grassland and woodland.

Herds that nearly vanished

North America's prairies once held tens of millions of bison, perhaps 30 to 60 million. In the 1800s, hunters shot them in huge numbers, for hides, for sport, and to starve the Native American nations who depended on them. By the late 1880s, fewer than a thousand were left.

Conservation work slowly brought bison back, and today hundreds of thousands live in North America, mostly on ranches, with smaller wild herds in parks like Yellowstone. Grazing bison help prairies by trimming grass and churning up soil, much as wild herds do elsewhere.

Why grasslands matter

Grasslands cover a big share of Earth's land, by some counts close to two-fifths if you include savannas and shrublands. They store huge amounts of carbon in their roots and soil, sometimes more safely than forests, since fire burns the grass but not the underground store.

They are also disappearing fast, ploughed for crops, covered by buildings or overgrazed. Scientists say protecting them means treating them as valuable places in their own right, not as empty land waiting to become something else.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Grasslands are empty wasteland that would be better off planted with trees or turned into farms.

Truth: Natural grasslands are full of life, from bustards and blackbuck to wolves, foxes and countless insects. Planting trees on them can wipe out the animals that need open land. Their deep roots also store carbon and hold soil in place.

Try this

Sprinkle wheat or ragi seeds on wet cotton in a saucer and keep it moist near a window. When the shoots are a few centimetres tall, pinch the tops off half of them with your fingers. Watch over the next few days whether the pinched shoots keep growing.
