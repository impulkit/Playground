What is Saturn's moon Titan like?

Titan has rain, rivers, lakes and seas, just like Earth, except they are made of liquid methane at about −180 °C.

The short answer

Titan is Saturn's largest moon and the second-largest moon in the solar system, after Jupiter's Ganymede. It is even bigger than the planet Mercury.

It is the only moon with a thick atmosphere, and the only place besides Earth known to have stable liquid on its surface. But Titan is extremely cold, about −180 °C. There, methane and ethane, which are gases on Earth, form clouds, fall as rain, carve rivers and fill lakes and seas. Water is frozen solid and acts like rock.

Below its icy crust, scientists think Titan also hides an ocean of salty liquid water.

Surprising but true

Titan's air is so thick and its gravity so weak that a person wearing wings strapped to their arms could, in theory, fly.

An orange, hazy world

Titan's air is mostly nitrogen, like Earth's, but at ground level it is about four times denser than the air you breathe. The pressure at the surface is about one and a half times what you feel on Earth, similar to being a few metres underwater in a swimming pool.

Sunlight breaks up methane high in the air and builds new, more complicated chemicals. These form a thick orange smog that hides the surface. When the Voyager spacecraft flew past in 1980, its cameras saw only a fuzzy orange ball.

Landing on Titan

In 2004, the Cassini spacecraft, built by NASA, the European Space Agency and Italy, arrived at Saturn. It carried a European probe named Huygens, after the Dutch astronomer Christiaan Huygens, who discovered Titan in 1655.

On 14 January 2005, Huygens parachuted down through the haze for more than two hours. It landed on a damp plain scattered with rounded pebbles of water ice, like stones in a dry riverbed. It is still the most distant landing ever made by a spacecraft.

Cassini kept studying Titan for 13 years. Its radar could see through the haze and found lakes and seas near the north pole. The biggest, Kraken Mare, is larger than the Caspian Sea, the biggest lake on Earth.

Rain made of gas

On Earth, water evaporates from the sea, forms clouds and falls as rain in the water cycle. On Titan, methane does the same job. Cassini watched clouds form and saw dark patches appear after storms, where the ground had become wet.

The rain falls slowly. With low gravity and thick air, raindrops on Titan drift down at about walking speed, and they can grow larger than raindrops on Earth.

Titan's lakes hold far more methane and ethane than all of Earth's known oil and gas reserves. Dunes made of dark grains of carbon-rich material wrap around its middle, like the sand seas of the Sahara.

Could anything live there?

Titan has many of the chemicals that may have come before life on Earth. Some scientists wonder whether strange forms of life could use liquid methane instead of water, though nobody knows if that is possible. The hidden ocean of water beneath the crust is another place where life might exist.

To find out more, NASA is building Dragonfly, a car-sized flying machine with eight rotors, like a giant drone. It is planned to launch in 2028 and reach Titan in the 2030s. It will fly from place to place, landing to sniff and sample the ground, on a world where flying is easier than walking. Titan's gravity is only about a seventh of Earth's. Some scientists have worked out that, in air that thick, a person with wings strapped to their arms could flap their way off the ground, if they could survive the cold.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Titan's seas are full of water.

Truth: At about −180 °C, water is frozen as hard as rock. Titan's lakes and seas are filled with liquid methane and ethane, the gases we burn in stoves and lighters on Earth.
