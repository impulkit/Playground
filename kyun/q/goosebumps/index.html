Why do we get goosebumps?

Goosebumps are your body trying to fluff up a fur coat you no longer have.

The short answer

At the base of almost every hair on your body is a tiny muscle, thinner than a thread. When you're cold or scared, your nervous system tells these muscles to tighten. Each one pulls its hair upright and pushes up a small bump of skin around it.

Thousands of bumps appear at once, and your skin looks like a plucked goose, which is how goosebumps got their name. In furry animals, raised hair traps a layer of warm air like a puffy jacket, or makes the animal look bigger to an enemy. Our body hair is too thin to do much of either, so goosebumps are mostly a leftover from our hairier ancestors.

Surprising but true

Humans have about as many hair follicles on their bodies as chimpanzees do. Our hairs are just so thin and short that we look bare.

A coat that isn't there

Watch a sparrow on a cold winter morning. It puffs up its feathers into a round ball, trapping air close to its body. Mammals do the same with fur. The warm, still air acts as a blanket.

Our ancestors were covered in hair, and the reflex helped them stay warm. Over hundreds of thousands of years, humans became less hairy, perhaps to sweat and cool down better while running in hot places. The tiny muscles stayed. So when you step out of a bath into a cold room, your body still tries to fluff up a coat it doesn't have.

Looking bigger

The second job of raised hair is to look scary. A frightened cat arches its back and puffs up its tail. A chimpanzee's hair stands on end before a fight. Porcupines raise their sharp quills, which are really thick, stiff hairs.

The same alarm system runs in you. When you get a fright, your body releases adrenaline, which gets you ready to run or fight. It speeds up your heart and also triggers goosebumps. That's why a scary story told in the dark can make the hair on your arms and neck stand up, as part of the same burst of stress.

Why does music sometimes give you goosebumps?A song builds, builds, builds... and suddenly you shiver.

Chills from music

Many people get goosebumps from things that aren't cold or scary at all. A soaring song, a stirring national anthem or a moving scene in a film can do it. Scientists call this feeling frisson, a French word for a shiver.

Brain scans show that when music gives people chills, the reward areas of the brain light up, the same ones that respond to good food. Nobody knows exactly why the alarm system gets switched on too. One idea is that a sudden surprise in the music, like a high note or a new voice, briefly sets off the same reaction as a surprise in real life. Not everyone gets music chills, and that's normal.

A hidden job

For a long time, scientists thought the goosebump muscles were almost useless in humans. In 2020, a team at Harvard University found a hidden extra job, at least in mice. The nerve that tightens the muscle also sends signals to the cells that grow new hair. Long cold spells made the mice grow thicker fur.

So the same wiring that raises a hair in the cold may also help grow a warmer coat over weeks. Humans can't grow a winter coat, but the muscles and nerves around our hairs may still help keep the follicles working. The bumps you get on a chilly morning may be doing a little more than they seem.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Humans have lost most of the hair follicles our ape relatives have.

Truth: We have roughly the same number of hair follicles as chimpanzees. Most of our body hairs are simply very fine and short, so the follicles are still there and can still give us goosebumps.

Try this

Hold an ice cube in a plastic bag against your forearm for a few seconds and watch the skin closely with a magnifying glass. You may see tiny bumps rise and the fine hairs stand up. Then play a piece of music that gives you chills and see if it happens again.
