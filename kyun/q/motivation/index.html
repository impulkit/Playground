Why do we feel motivated, and why does it disappear?

Children who were promised a prize for drawing later drew less in their free time than children who got nothing.

The short answer

Motivation is the push that gets you to do something. It comes from your brain's reward system, a set of connected areas deep in the brain that predict which actions will pay off. When your brain expects something good, it releases a chemical called dopamine, and you feel the pull to go and get it.

Psychologists split motivation into two kinds. Intrinsic motivation comes from inside: you play football because it's fun. Extrinsic motivation comes from outside: you tidy your room because you'll get pocket money. Both are real, and most of what we do mixes the two. A cricketer may play for the joy of hitting a clean cover drive and also for a place in the team.

Motivation fades when the brain stops expecting a reward, or when the reward feels too far away.

Surprising but true

Dopamine, often called the "pleasure chemical", seems to be more about wanting things than about enjoying them.

Wanting is not the same as liking

For years, people called dopamine the pleasure chemical. Then, in the 1990s, researchers at the University of Michigan found something odd. Rats whose dopamine was blocked still made happy faces when they tasted sugar. They just wouldn't make any effort to get it.

So dopamine seems to be about wanting, not liking. It rises when you spot a chance of a reward, which is why the moment before opening a present can feel more exciting than the present itself. Video games use this cleverly, with surprise prizes and levels that are always almost finished.

The drawing experiment

In 1973, psychologists at Stanford University in California watched nursery children who loved drawing with markers. They split them into groups. One group was promised a "Good Player" certificate for drawing. Another drew with no reward. A third got a surprise certificate afterwards.

A week or two later, the markers were put out during free play. The children who had expected a certificate spent less time drawing than the others. The researchers argued the reward had changed how the children saw drawing: from play to work. Psychologists still argue about how strong this effect is. Rewards for boring jobs seem to do little harm, and praise for effort can help. Still, the study changed how many schools think about prizes.

What keeps people going

Two American psychologists, Edward Deci and Richard Ryan, spent decades studying what makes motivation last. They concluded people need three things. They need some choice in what they do. They need to feel they're getting better at it. And they need to feel connected to other people.

Athletes often describe this. The javelin thrower Neeraj Chopra, who won India's first Olympic gold in athletics in 2021, began training as a teenager to get fitter. He kept going for years because he loved getting better. Coaches in sports science now talk about small, clear goals that let athletes feel progress every week.

When motivation runs out

Motivation isn't a fixed amount you either have or don't. It drops when you're tired, hungry or stressed, and when a goal seems impossibly far off. Breaking a big task into small steps gives the brain more chances to feel it's winning. Ticking off a list, or colouring in a chart, turns invisible progress into something the brain can see.

Waiting to feel motivated before starting doesn't always work, either. Often starting comes first, and the motivation follows once your brain sees some progress. Over time, things you do regularly can become habits, which need much less motivation at all.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Dopamine is the brain's pleasure chemical.

Truth: Research suggests dopamine is mostly about wanting, expecting and chasing rewards, and about learning what's worth doing. The good feeling of actually enjoying something seems to involve other brain chemicals as well.

Try this

Make a "why" list for something you want to get better at, such as a sport, an instrument or a language. Write down the reasons that come from you, like "it feels good when I get it right", and the ones that come from outside, like prizes or marks. Which list is longer?
