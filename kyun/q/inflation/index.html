Why do prices go up?

In Germany in 1923, prices rose so fast that people were paid twice a day and ran to the shops before their money lost its value.

The short answer

Prices go up when there is more money chasing goods than there are goods to buy, or when it costs more to make and move things. This general rise in prices over time is called inflation.

A little inflation is normal and even expected. If prices rise by 4% in a year, something that cost ₹100 last year costs about ₹104 now. The money in your pocket buys a bit less. India's central bank, the Reserve Bank of India, tries to keep inflation near 4%, and anywhere between 2% and 6% counts as on target. America's Federal Reserve aims for 2%. Prices that rise far faster than that can hurt families, especially poorer ones, who spend most of their money on food and fuel.

Surprising but true

Zimbabwe printed a 100-trillion-dollar banknote in 2009, and it was still not enough to buy much.

Three main causes

The first cause is demand. When people have more money to spend and shops can't keep up, sellers raise prices. A good harvest year has the opposite effect: plenty of vegetables, lower prices.

The second is cost. If fuel becomes expensive, everything carried by truck costs more, from tomatoes to cement. A poor monsoon can push up food prices across India.

The third is money itself. If a government prints large amounts of new money without more goods being made, each rupee or dollar is worth less. That is why central banks, not politicians, usually control how much money is created.

What was the Great Depression?In October 1929 share prices in New York crashed. Within three years, about one in four American workers had no job, and the trouble had spread around the globe.

When prices go wild

Very fast inflation is called hyperinflation. In Germany in 1923, after the First World War, the government printed huge amounts of money. Prices doubled every few days. By the end of the year, a loaf of bread cost billions of marks. Families saw their life savings vanish.

In Zimbabwe, prices rose so fast in 2008 that early the next year the country printed a note worth 100 trillion Zimbabwean dollars. Soon after, it gave up its own currency and used foreign money instead. These cases are rare, but they show why governments take inflation seriously.

How inflation is measured

Every month, government statisticians check the prices of a long list of things a typical household buys: rice, milk, cooking gas, rent, school fees, haircuts, bus fares. This "basket" gives a consumer price index. Comparing it with the same month a year earlier gives the inflation rate as a percentage.

The basket is an average, so it won't match every family. A household that eats lots of vegetables feels a tomato price spike more than one that doesn't. In India, the National Statistics Office publishes these figures every month. In the USA, the Bureau of Labor Statistics does the same job.

Sometimes prices rise in disguise. A company keeps the price of a biscuit packet the same but quietly puts fewer biscuits inside. People have nicknamed this shrinkflation, and it is one reason to check the weight printed on a packet.

How central banks fight it

The main tool is the interest rate. When inflation is too high, the central bank raises the rate at which banks borrow. Loans become more expensive, so people and businesses borrow and spend less, and price rises slow down.

After the COVID-19 pandemic, prices jumped around the world as supply chains broke down and fuel became expensive. US inflation hit about 9% in June 2022, its highest in about four decades. Central banks raised interest rates sharply, and inflation gradually came down. It showed how much a few percentage points can change what ends up in a family's shopping bag, and how the interest on loans is tied to the price of bread.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Inflation means everything gets more expensive at the same rate.

Truth: Inflation is an average. In the same year, vegetables might jump in price while phones or televisions get cheaper. Your own family's costs may rise faster or slower than the official figure.

Try this

Ask a grandparent or older relative what things cost when they were your age, such as a cinema ticket, a bus ride or a cup of tea. Write down today's price next to each. Work out how many times more expensive each one has become, and which rose the most.
