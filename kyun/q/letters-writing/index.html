Why do people still write letters?

In 1928 Jawaharlal Nehru wrote letters to his ten-year-old daughter Indira about stars, fossils and ancient empires. They became a book still in print today.

The short answer

Most messages today are texts, emails and voice notes. Yet people still write letters by hand. A letter is slow and takes effort, and that is exactly why it feels special. You can hold it, keep it in a drawer and read it again years later.

People also write letters for practical reasons. Courts, banks and governments still send official letters. Many children write to Santa or to their favourite authors. And for soldiers, prisoners and people in remote places, a letter can still be the best way to hear from home.

Surprising but true

India has more post offices than any other country, well over 1.5 lakh, and most of them are in villages.

A father's letters

In the summer of 1928, Jawaharlal Nehru was in Allahabad while his daughter Indira was at school in Mussoorie. He wrote her long letters about how the Earth was formed, how early humans lived and how languages grew.

The next year they were printed as Letters from a Father to His Daughter. Indira grew up to become India's prime minister, and the book is still read by children. Nehru wrote to her from prison too, and those letters became a much longer history of the world.

How a letter travels

Before 1840, the person receiving a letter usually paid for it. Then Britain introduced the Penny Black, the first sticky postage stamp. The sender paid in advance, and posting a letter became cheap and simple.

Today a letter goes into a postbox, gets collected and sorted, and travels by van, train or plane. In India the six-digit PIN code, introduced in 1972, helps sorters send it to the right post office. Each digit has a job. The first stands for one of India's postal regions, the next two narrow it to a smaller zone and a sorting district, and the last three point to the delivery post office. The United States started its five-digit ZIP codes in 1963. India Post runs well over 1.5 lakh post offices, from big city buildings to tiny rooms in Himalayan villages.

Slow on purpose

An email arrives in seconds. A letter takes days. Some people think that wait is part of the charm. Writing slowly also changes what you say. You choose your words more carefully when you can't press delete.

Some scientists have studied whether writing by hand helps you remember better than typing. Several studies suggest it does, perhaps because handwriting makes you slow down and think about each idea. Researchers are still arguing about how big the effect is.

Letters that changed history

Many famous letters are now treasured in museums. Abraham Lincoln wrote letters that people still quote. In 1939 Albert Einstein signed a letter warning the US president about atomic weapons. Anne Frank, hiding from the Nazis, wrote her diary partly as letters to an imaginary friend called Kitty.

Historians love letters because they show what ordinary people thought at the time, in their own handwriting and ink. A soldier's note home or a farmer's letter to a son in the city can tell us more than an official report.

Pen pals and postcards

A pen pal is a friend you get to know by writing. Some schools in India and the United States link classes so pupils can swap letters across oceans. Many people collect stamps or postcards from the places they visit. Stamp collecting, called philately, can teach you a surprising amount of history and geography.

Writing a letter needs little: paper, a pen, an envelope and a stamp. An inland letter card in India still costs only a few rupees to send anywhere in the country. For that price, someone far away gets a piece of paper that you once held in your hands.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Nobody uses the post any more, so post offices are disappearing.

Truth: Fewer personal letters are sent than before, but post offices now carry huge numbers of parcels from online shopping. India Post also runs savings accounts and a bank for millions of people.
