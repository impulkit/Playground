Why is Spanish spoken in so many countries?

A language from one corner of Europe is now the first language of about 500 million people, and most live in the Americas.

The short answer

Spanish spread mostly through empire. Starting in 1492, Spain sent ships, soldiers, priests and settlers across the Atlantic. Over the next three centuries, it ruled most of South America, Central America, Mexico, much of the Caribbean and large parts of what is now the USA. It also ruled the Philippines in Asia.

Spanish became the language of government, church, schools and trade in all those places. When the colonies became independent countries in the 1800s, they kept the language. Today Spanish is an official language in about 20 countries and is spoken as a first language by about 500 million people, more than any other language except Mandarin Chinese.

Surprising but true

Thousands of Spanish words come from Arabic, because much of Spain was ruled by Arabic-speaking Muslim kingdoms for hundreds of years. Even the Spanish word for "hopefully", ojalá, comes from an Arabic phrase.

From Roman soldiers to Spanish kings

Spanish began as the everyday Latin spoken by people in Roman Spain. After the Roman Empire fell, that Latin slowly changed into several local languages, including Castilian, from the kingdom of Castile in central Spain. Castilian is what most people now call Spanish.

From 711, much of Spain was ruled by Muslim kingdoms, a region known as al-Andalus, for nearly 800 years in some places. Arabic left thousands of words in Spanish, from aceite (olive oil) to almohada (pillow). Many words starting with "al-" come from the Arabic word for "the".

In 1492, the last of these kingdoms, Granada, fell to the Spanish monarchs. In the same year, they paid for Columbus to sail west. That same year, a scholar named Antonio de Nebrija published one of the first grammar books of Spanish, and he told the queen that language had always been a companion of empire.

What did Columbus really find?Christopher Columbus set out to reach India and China. He died still believing he had reached Asia.

An empire, and what it cost

Spanish conquerors defeated the Aztec and Inca empires in the 1500s. Diseases brought from Europe, such as smallpox, killed huge numbers of people in the Americas, who had no resistance to them. Many Indigenous people were forced to work in mines and on farms.

Spanish was imposed as the language of power, but Indigenous languages did not vanish. Millions of people still speak Quechua in Peru and Bolivia, Guaraní in Paraguay and Nahuatl and Mayan languages in Mexico. Spanish itself borrowed words from them: "chocolate", "tomato" and "avocado" all reached English through Spanish from Nahuatl, the Aztec language.

The USA's second language

Spanish arrived in North America before English did. Spanish explorers founded St. Augustine, in Florida, in 1565, and it is the oldest continuously settled European town in the mainland USA. California, Texas, New Mexico and Arizona were once part of Spain's empire and then of Mexico, which today has more Spanish speakers than any other country.

Today more than 40 million people in the USA speak Spanish at home, more than any other language except English. In Puerto Rico, a US territory, Spanish is the main language. English has borrowed plenty of words from Spanish, including canyon, patio, rodeo, mosquito and tornado.

One language, many accents

Spanish sounds different from place to place. In much of Spain, the letter "z" is said like the "th" in "thin". In Latin America, it is said like "s". Mexicans, Argentinians and Cubans each have their own slang and rhythm. A bus is a camión in Mexico, a guagua in Cuba and a colectivo in Argentina. A popcorn seller in Madrid and one in Bogotá might not even use the same word for popcorn.

Even so, a Spanish speaker from Madrid can talk with one from Lima or Los Angeles without much trouble. The Real Academia Española, founded in 1713, works with academies in other Spanish-speaking countries to keep a shared dictionary. The language even has its own letter, ñ, as in España.

Myth or fact?

Myth: People in Brazil speak Spanish.

Truth: Brazilians speak Portuguese, a close cousin of Spanish. A Spanish speaker and a Portuguese speaker can often understand bits of each other, but they are different languages.
