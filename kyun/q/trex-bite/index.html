How strong was T. rex's bite?

Strong enough to crunch through bone, and we have the fossil poo to prove it.

The short answer

We can't measure a dinosaur's bite directly. So scientists build computer models of the skull and jaw muscles, and test those models on living animals, like crocodiles and birds, to make sure they work.

The models suggest T. rex bit with a force of roughly 35,000 to 57,000 newtons. That is like the weight of three or more small cars pressing down on one spot. And T. rex fossil poo contains crushed bone, which shows it really did crunch bones.

Surprising but true

T. rex had the strongest bite of any land animal we know of.

Measuring a bite

A newton is a unit of force. Holding a 100-gram chocolate bar takes about 1 newton. A grown-up's back teeth can press with a few hundred newtons.

For living animals, scientists measure bites directly. Gregory Erickson and his team held padded force sensors between the jaws of crocodiles and alligators. A large saltwater crocodile bit with about 16,000 newtons, the strongest bite measured in any living animal.

Building a virtual T. rex

To estimate T. rex's bite, Karl Bates and Peter Falkingham built a 3D computer model of the skull of a large adult in 2012. They added jaw muscles, using muscle scars on the bones and comparisons with crocodiles and birds. Then they let the model "bite".

Other teams have used different methods and got slightly different answers. All of them put T. rex well above any living animal, and above any other known land animal.

Teeth like bananas

A big T. rex had about 60 teeth. The longest, including the root hidden in the jaw, could be about 30 cm long, roughly the size of a large banana. They were thick and rounded rather than thin and blade-like, with tiny saw-edges, called serrations.

Thin teeth would snap on bone. Thick ones could punch in and crush. Like most reptiles, T. rex kept growing new teeth all its life, so a broken one was soon replaced. Your own teeth only get one replacement set.

Proof in the poo

In 1998, Karen Chin and her colleagues described a giant fossil dropping, or coprolite, from Saskatchewan in Canada. It was about 44 cm long and packed with bits of bone from a young plant-eating dinosaur. Only a T. rex was big enough to have left it.

Bones with T. rex tooth marks tell the same story. Some Triceratops hips and horns carry deep punctures and scrapes from its teeth.

Bone for dinner

Why bite into bone at all? Bones hold minerals and fatty marrow, a rich food. Few reptiles alive today chew their food. Most swallow chunks whole. T. rex seems to have bitten bones again and again, more like a hyena or a wolf does.

That habit may have let a big T. rex get the most out of every carcass, including ones other hunters had already picked over.

Why the jaw was so strong

A jaw works like a lever, with a hinge at the back. T. rex had a wide skull at the back, leaving room for huge jaw muscles. Its skull bones were also tightly braced, so they didn't bend much when biting.

Strong bite force on thick, sharp teeth produced enormous pressure at the tooth tips. One study estimated that pressure was high enough to shatter bone, much as a crocodile's bite does today.

Myth or fact?

Myth: T. rex swallowed its prey whole, like a snake.

Truth: Fossil bones with T. rex tooth marks, and fossil poo full of crushed bone, show it bit and tore off chunks, crunching bone as it ate.

Try this

Feel the lever in your own jaw. Bite gently on a folded cloth with your front teeth, then with your back teeth. The back teeth press harder because they're closer to the hinge of your jaw, like a nutcracker. Don't bite anything hard.
