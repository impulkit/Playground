What is a Kashmiri wazwan?

At a Kashmiri wedding, four guests share one huge copper plate, and course after course of meat keeps arriving for hours.

The short answer

A wazwan is a grand Kashmiri feast, served mainly at weddings and big celebrations. It has many courses, mostly of mutton, cooked slowly by specialist chefs called wazas. The grandest feasts are said to have up to 36 dishes, though many have fewer.

Guests sit on the floor in groups of four and share a large copper plate called a trami, heaped with rice. Servers bring one dish after another: kebabs, ribs, meatballs in red gravy, chicken and more. The feast ends with gushtaba, soft meatballs in a white yogurt gravy, followed by a cup of kahwa, a green tea flavoured with saffron and spices.

Surprising but true

The bright red colour of many wazwan dishes comes from mild Kashmiri chillies and the dried flowers of a plant called mawal, not from fiery spice.

The wazas

The word "waza" means a cook, and "wan" is often said to mean a shop or the cook's spread of dishes. The head chef is called the vasta waza. Wazas are usually men from families who have cooked for generations, learning recipes by watching rather than from books.

Kashmiri tradition says the wazas' ancestors came from Central Asia centuries ago, possibly from around Samarkand. Kashmir lay near routes connected to the Silk Road, so cooks, traders and ideas did travel. Historians can't confirm the exact story, but Central Asian and Persian influences are clear in the food.

Cooking through the night

Wazwan cooking often starts the evening before a wedding. Cooks set up large copper pots over wood fires outdoors. For dishes like rista and gushtaba, mutton is pounded on a flat stone with wooden mallets until it becomes a smooth paste. The rhythmic thumping of the mallets is a familiar sound before Kashmiri weddings.

The meatballs are shaped by hand and simmered for hours. Fat from the meat is mixed in so they stay soft.

A feast in order

A wazwan follows an order. Early dishes include tabak maaz, ribs simmered in milk and spices, then fried crisp. Seekh kebabs and roasted chicken may follow.

Then come the famous curries. Rista is meatballs in a fiery-looking red gravy. Rogan josh is mutton in a rich red sauce. Its colour comes from mild Kashmiri chillies and the dried flowers of a plant called mawal, not from strong heat. Yakhni is mutton cooked in a yogurt gravy flavoured with fennel and dried mint.

Gushtaba arrives last, the grand finale of the meat courses. Its pale, creamy gravy is a calm ending after so many red dishes.

Spices and saffron

Wazwan cooking uses whole spices like cardamom, cloves, cinnamon and black cumin, plus dried ginger powder and fennel. Many dishes use little or no tomato.

Kashmir is famous for saffron, grown on the plateaus around Pampore near Srinagar. A few threads flavour the rice in some dishes and the kahwa served at the end. Each saffron flower gives only three red threads, so it's precious.

Other Kashmiri kitchens

The wazwan is mainly a tradition of Kashmiri Muslims. Kashmiri Pandits, the Hindu community of the valley, have their own celebrated cooking, which traditionally avoids onion and garlic and uses asafoetida, fennel and dried ginger instead. Their rogan josh is just as famous, made with yogurt for its rich gravy.

Both communities share a love of rice, lotus stem (called nadru), and haak, a leafy green cooked with mustard oil. Despite the troubles Kashmir has faced, food remains a shared pride, and a cup of kahwa is offered to almost every guest.

A feast to share

Sharing a trami matters. Four people eat from one plate, and the host watches to make sure every guest is served. Leftovers are often packed up and taken home in a cloth or container, which is considered polite, not greedy.

Wazwan has spread to restaurants in Srinagar and beyond, and you can now order a small version in Delhi or even abroad. But many Kashmiris say the real thing still needs a wedding, a crowd and a waza who has been up all night by the fire.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Every wazwan has exactly 36 courses.

Truth: Thirty-six is a traditional number often quoted for the grandest feasts. Many wazwans have fewer dishes, depending on the family and the occasion.

Try this

Make a "feast plan" for a family celebration. List the dishes you'd serve in order, from light starters to the final sweet, the way a wazwan builds from kebabs to gushtaba. Then ask an older relative what was served at a wedding they remember.
