Where did ludo come from?

Ludo is a simplified version of pachisi, an old Indian game once played with cowrie shells instead of dice.

The short answer

Ludo comes from pachisi, a board game played in India for hundreds of years. Pachisi uses a cross-shaped board, often sewn from cloth, with four arms of squares and a home in the middle. Each player races four pieces around the board and back home, knocking rivals back to the start.

In the 1800s British people in India took the game home. In 1896 a simpler version was patented in England as ludo, Latin for "I play". It had a printed board, coloured counters and a cube-shaped die. The same game also crossed to America as Parcheesi. Today ludo is played on phones by millions of people, many of whom have never seen a cloth board.

Surprising but true

The word pachisi comes from the Hindi word for 25, the highest score you could throw with the cowrie shells.

Shells, not dice

In traditional pachisi, players throw six or seven cowrie shells. Each shell can land with its open slit facing up or down. You count the open ones to get your move, and certain throws give bonus turns. The best throw was 25, or pachisi.

Shells make some numbers more likely than others, which you can work out with probability. A cube die is simpler, because each number from one to six has the same chance. That may be one reason the English version switched.

A related game, chaupar, uses long dice. A famous dice game in the Mahabharata, where the Pandavas lose their kingdom, is often linked to games like this.

The emperor's courtyard

At Fatehpur Sikri, the city Emperor Akbar built near Agra in the late 1500s, a courtyard floor is laid out like a giant pachisi board. A popular story says Akbar sat in the middle and played using real people as pieces, dressed in the right colours. There is no firm record that he did, and some historians think the pattern may be decoration. It remains one of the most repeated stories about the game.

What is clear is that pachisi was played across India by royalty and ordinary families alike, on embroidered cloth, on boards scratched into stone or simply drawn in the dust. It was often played in teams of two, with partners sitting opposite each other, and a cloth board could be rolled up with its cone-shaped wooden pieces and carried anywhere.

Ludo goes global

Once it reached Britain, ludo became a standard family game. Versions spread around the world under new names. Germany has Mensch ärgere Dich nicht, "Man, don't get annoyed". Spain has parchís. Like snakes and ladders, another game with Indian roots, it is often among the first board games a child learns.

The rules change a little from home to home. In most versions you need a six to bring a piece out of its starting yard, and a six gives you another turn. Squares marked with a star are often safe, so nobody can capture you there. In the USA, Parcheesi was sold as "The Royal Game of India", and a later English game called Sorry! uses cards instead of dice for the same race-and-capture idea.

Back home on a phone

In 2016 a small Indian company launched an app called Ludo King. It grew slowly until 2020, when COVID-19 lockdowns kept families apart. Suddenly grandparents, cousins and friends were playing ludo together through their phones, from Delhi to Dallas.

The app became one of the most downloaded games in India. A game that left India on cloth boards and came back on screens had, in a way, returned home. The rules are much the same, and so is the groan when someone's piece gets sent back to the start just before reaching home.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ludo was invented in England.

Truth: An English version was patented in 1896, but it was based on pachisi, which Indians had played for centuries. The English game simplified the board and used a cube die.

Try this

Play ludo with a twist. Instead of a die, throw five coins and count the heads, giving 0 to 5 moves. Play a game this way, then a game with a normal die. Talk about which felt fairer and why some numbers came up more often with the coins.
