What was the French Revolution?

When crowds stormed the Bastille prison in Paris on 14 July 1789, they found only seven prisoners inside. The attack still began a revolution.

The short answer

The French Revolution was a period of upheaval in France from 1789 to 1799. Ordinary people, lawyers and some nobles rose against an unfair system in which the king held most power and nobles and clergy paid few taxes.

In ten years, the revolutionaries ended the monarchy, executed the king, wrote a famous declaration of rights and tried out new ideas about government. The revolution also brought violence, war and a period of fear called the Terror. In 1799 the general Napoleon Bonaparte seized power. Its slogan, "Liberty, equality, fraternity", is still France's motto.

Surprising but true

The metre, now used by most of the world, was created during the French Revolution as a new measure for everyone.

Why France exploded

In the 1780s, French society was divided into three "estates". The clergy were the First Estate, and nobles the Second. Everyone else, about 97 per cent of people, from rich merchants to poor peasants, made up the Third Estate. The Third Estate paid most of the taxes.

The government was nearly bankrupt, partly because France had spent heavily helping the Americans win the American Revolution. In 1788 bad harvests made bread, the main food of the poor, very expensive. Meanwhile, writers of the Enlightenment had spread ideas that power should come from the people, not from kings chosen by God.

The Bastille falls

In May 1789, King Louis XVI called a meeting of the three estates to fix the money crisis. The Third Estate's members demanded more say. When they found themselves locked out of their meeting hall, they gathered in an indoor tennis court and swore not to break up until France had a constitution.

On 14 July, crowds in Paris, fearing the king would send troops, stormed the Bastille, an old fortress-prison, looking for gunpowder. It held only seven prisoners. But it was a hated symbol of royal power, and its fall shocked Europe. France celebrates 14 July as its national day.

How was the length of a metre decided?In the 1790s, two French astronomers spent seven years measuring a slice of the Earth, just to decide how long a metre should be.

Rights for citizens

In August 1789, the new National Assembly issued the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen. It said that people are born and remain free and equal in rights, and that power belongs to the nation.

Not everyone was included. In 1791 the writer Olympe de Gouges published her own Declaration of the Rights of Woman, arguing that women deserved the same rights. Enslaved people in French colonies were also left out at first, until the enslaved people of Saint-Domingue rose up in the Haitian Revolution. In 1794 France abolished slavery in its colonies, though Napoleon later brought it back.

The Terror

Europe's kings feared the revolution would spread. War broke out in 1792. Accused of plotting with France's enemies, Louis XVI lost his throne, and in January 1793 he was executed by guillotine.

A group led by Maximilien Robespierre then took control. In 1793–94, during the Terror, they arrested people suspected of opposing the revolution. Around 17,000 people were officially sentenced to death, and many more died in prison or in fighting inside France. In July 1794 Robespierre himself was overthrown and executed.

A new measure for everyone

Some changes from these years still shape daily life. The revolutionaries wanted measurements that were the same everywhere and based on nature. They defined the metre as one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator. Over the next two centuries, the metric system spread around the world, including to India.

After the Terror, a weak government struggled on, until Napoleon took power in 1799. The ideas of the revolution, that citizens have rights and that power should come from the people, inspired movements far beyond France, including later freedom struggles in Latin America and Asia.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Queen Marie Antoinette said "Let them eat cake" when told the poor had no bread.

Truth: There is no evidence she ever said it. A similar line appears in the writings of Jean-Jacques Rousseau, about an unnamed "great princess", written when Marie Antoinette was still a child in Austria.

Try this

Measure your room twice, once in "feet" using your own foot, heel to toe, and once with a metre ruler or tape. Compare your foot-count with a friend's or a parent's for the same room. You'll see why the revolutionaries wanted one standard unit for everybody.
