How do starlings fly in giant flocks?

Tens of thousands of starlings swirl across the evening sky like smoke, and almost never crash. Each bird watches only about seven of its neighbours.

The short answer

Starlings fly in giant flocks, called murmurations, by following simple rules. Each bird keeps an eye on about six or seven of its closest neighbours. It matches their speed and direction, stays close, but not too close. When one bird turns, its neighbours turn, and the change ripples across the flock in a split second.

There's no leader. Yet the whole flock can twist, split and come back together like a single shape-shifting cloud. Scientists think murmurations help protect the birds from falcons and hawks, and help them gather before roosting for the night.

Surprising but true

A 2008 study in Rome found that each starling keeps track of about six or seven nearby birds, no matter how tightly or loosely the flock is packed.

A sky full of starlings

Murmurations happen mostly on autumn and winter evenings, just before sunset. Starlings fly in from the fields where they've been feeding all day and gather above a roost, such as a reedbed, a woodland or even a pier.

A flock might have a few thousand birds, or hundreds of thousands. Some have been estimated at more than a million. In Rome, enormous flocks swirl over the city in winter, and in parts of Britain, crowds gather just to watch.

India gets its own show. Each winter, rosy starlings arrive from Central Asia, and their flocks swirl over fields and wetlands in states like Rajasthan and Gujarat.

Seven birds at a time

How does a bird keep up in a flock of thousands? In 2008, Italian scientists filmed starling flocks over Rome with several cameras at once. They used computers to work out the position of every bird in 3D.

They found that each starling paid attention to about six or seven neighbours, not to everyone nearby. It didn't matter if the flock was packed tight or spread out. The number stayed about the same. This makes the flock stable, even when it stretches thin.

A ripple across the sky

When a starling at the edge spots danger, it swerves. Its neighbours copy it, and their neighbours copy them. That wave of turning can sweep across a flock of hundreds of birds in about half a second.

Scientists found that a change in direction travels through a starling flock almost without fading, as if the flock were one big body. It's a bit like a Mexican wave in a cricket stadium, only in three dimensions and much faster.

Why gather?

The most popular explanation is safety. A peregrine falcon hunting a starling needs to pick out one bird. In a swirling crowd, that's very hard. The more birds, the less chance any one of them gets caught.

Murmurations may also help birds stay warm together at night, swap information about good feeding places, or simply call more starlings to the roost. Scientists still debate how much each reason matters.

Computers learned from birds

In 1986, an American computer scientist, Craig Reynolds, made a program called "Boids". Each pretend bird followed three simple rules: avoid bumping, match your neighbours' direction, and stay close to the group.

On screen, they flocked just like real birds. The same idea was used for the swarms of bats in the 1992 film Batman Returns, and is now used for crowds and herds in films and video games. It shows how simple algorithms, followed by many individuals, can create complex patterns in nature.

Starlings in America

Starlings aren't native to North America. In 1890, a man named Eugene Schieffelin released about 60 starlings in New York's Central Park, and more the next year. Today there are tens of millions across the continent.

Some say he wanted every bird mentioned by Shakespeare to live in America, but historians have found little proof of that story. What's certain is that starlings spread fast. Their flocks follow rules that other animals share too, from fish swimming in schools to ants working together without a boss.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A flock of starlings follows a leader bird.

Truth: There's no leader. Each bird just reacts to a handful of its neighbours. A turn can start anywhere, and ripple through the whole flock faster than any single bird could signal it.

Try this

Play a flocking game with at least eight friends in a playground. Everyone silently picks three people to watch and tries to stay the same distance from them while walking around. Nobody leads. Watch how the group starts moving together without anyone giving orders.
