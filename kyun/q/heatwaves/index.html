Why are heatwaves getting worse?

A summer heatwave that might have come once in a lifetime a century ago now arrives far more often, and scientists can measure why.

The short answer

A heatwave is a spell of days that are much hotter than normal for a place and time of year. Heatwaves have always happened. What has changed is how often they come and how hot they get.

The main reason is climate change. Burning coal, oil and gas adds carbon dioxide to the air, which traps extra heat. By the 2010s, Earth's average temperature had risen by about 1.1 °C since the late 1800s, according to the IPCC, the United Nations' panel of climate scientists. That sounds small. But when the whole curve of temperatures shifts upwards, the hottest days get hotter and far more common.

Other things add to it. Cities trap heat, dry soil warms faster, and natural patterns like El Niño can push some years higher still.

Surprising but true

In June 2021, a village in Canada called Lytton reached 49.6 °C, hotter than any temperature ever recorded in Las Vegas, and burned down in a wildfire the next day.

How a small shift makes a big difference

Picture a year's daily temperatures as a hill-shaped graph. Most days sit in the middle, and very hot days are rare, out in the thin tail on the right. Now nudge the whole hill one step to the right. The middle barely looks different, but the tail of extreme heat grows much fatter. Days that used to be rare become regular, and new records become possible.

Scientists can now test single events. Groups such as World Weather Attribution compare the real world with computer models of a world without human warming. For many recent heatwaves, they found the heat would have been almost impossible without climate change.

Trapped by a dome

Heatwaves often start when a large area of high pressure settles over a region and stays put. Air inside it sinks, warms and blocks clouds, so the sun beats down day after day. Meteorologists call it a heat dome. In June 2021, a heat dome over western Canada pushed the village of Lytton to 49.6 °C, the highest temperature ever measured in that country. A wildfire swept through the village the next day.

Dry ground makes it worse. Normally, the Sun's energy is partly used to evaporate water from soil and plants, which cools them. When the ground is parched, more of that energy simply heats the air. In India, heatwaves usually strike from April to June, before the monsoon rains arrive. On the plains, the India Meteorological Department declares a heatwave when the temperature reaches at least 40 °C and is well above normal, or hits 45 °C whatever the normal. In May 2016, Phalodi in Rajasthan reached 51.0 °C, the highest temperature ever recorded in India.

Heat and the human body

Your body stays near 37 °C by sweating. As sweat evaporates, it carries heat away from the skin. But in very humid air, sweat can't evaporate well, so you can't cool down properly. That is why hot, sticky weather can be more dangerous than dry heat at the same temperature.

Doctors warn that heatstroke can be deadly. Older people, babies, outdoor workers and people without cool homes face the most danger. On very hot days, drinking water often, staying in shade and resting in the hottest hours all help. If you feel dizzy or sick in the heat, tell an adult straight away.

Cities fighting back

In 2010, a severe heatwave struck Ahmedabad and many people died. In 2013, the city launched a heat action plan, one of the first in South Asia. It sends out early warnings, keeps parks and water points open, trains health workers and helps people paint roofs white or light colours to reflect sunlight. Many other Indian cities and states now have similar plans.

In the USA, cities like Phoenix have opened cooling centres and planted shade trees. Air conditioning saves lives, but it also uses a lot of electricity and pumps heat outdoors, so planners are also looking at trees, water and smarter buildings to keep streets cooler.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A few hot summers prove nothing, because the weather has always gone up and down.

Truth: Weather does vary, but scientists look at decades of records worldwide. Hot extremes have become more frequent and more intense since the 1950s, and the IPCC says human-caused warming is the main driver.

Try this

On a sunny day, place two identical thermometers or two cups of water in the sun, one on a dark surface and one on a white cloth. After an hour, ask an adult to help you compare the readings. This is why some cities paint roofs white to keep homes cooler.
