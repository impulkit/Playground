Why does milk help after spicy food?

Gulping water after a fiery green chilli just spreads the burn around your mouth. A glass of milk or a spoon of curd can wash it away.

The short answer

Chillies reached India from the Americas about 500 years ago, and Indian cooks soon made them their own. Their burn comes from a chemical called capsaicin. It doesn't mix with water, but it does dissolve in fat and oil. So water mostly sloshes it around your mouth.

Milk works better for two reasons. It contains fat, which can dissolve some capsaicin. And it contains a protein called casein, which acts a bit like soap. Casein grabs the oily capsaicin molecules, pulls them away from the nerve endings on your tongue and lets you swallow them. That is why curd, raita, lassi and buttermilk sit beside spicy meals all over India.

Surprising but true

In 2021, the scientist David Julius shared a Nobel Prize partly for using the chemical in chillies to discover how our nerves sense heat.

A burn that isn't real

Your mouth has tiny sensors on its nerves that warn you about dangerous heat, like a too-hot cup of chai. Capsaicin fits into these sensors like a key in a lock and switches them on.

Your brain gets the message "fire!" even though nothing is hot. Your face may go red, your eyes may water and your nose may run. Some people even start to sweat, because the body tries to cool down from a heat that doesn't exist.

In the 1990s, the American scientist David Julius used capsaicin to find the exact sensor, called TRPV1. That discovery, about how we feel heat and touch, earned him a share of the Nobel Prize in 2021.

Why water fails

Capsaicin is an oily molecule. Like oil and water in a salad dressing, it won't blend with water. A gulp of water washes over it and spreads it to fresh parts of your mouth, which can make the burn feel worse.

Ice-cold water can numb things for a few seconds, but the burn comes back once the water warms up. Fizzy drinks can make it worse, because the bubbles add their own prickle.

What milk does

Milk is a mix of water, fat, sugar and proteins. Casein forms tiny clusters that are attracted to oily things. They surround capsaicin and drag it off the sensors.

A study at Penn State University in the USA tested several drinks after people sipped a spicy liquid. Milk worked best. Skimmed milk worked about as well as whole milk, which suggests the protein matters as much as the fat. Sugary drinks helped a bit too.

Curd, yoghurt, paneer and even a spoon of ghee can help in the same way. In South India, many meals end with curd rice, a cool, creamy finish after spicy dishes.

How hot can it get?

Chilli heat is measured in Scoville heat units. A bell pepper scores zero. A typical green chilli in an Indian kitchen may score a few thousand to tens of thousands.

The bhut jolokia, or ghost pepper, grown in Assam, Nagaland and Manipur, scores around 1 million. In 2007 it was named the world's hottest chilli. Since then, plant breeders in the USA have grown even hotter ones. As of 2023, the record belongs to Pepper X, at about 2.69 million units. Eating these can be risky, and people have ended up in hospital.

Birds don't feel it

Mammals feel the burn of capsaicin, but birds don't. Their heat sensors are built slightly differently. A bird can eat the hottest chilli without blinking.

Scientists think this helps the chilli plant. Mammals chew and crush the seeds, but birds swallow them whole and drop them far away, ready to grow. Birds carry chilli seeds across forests and hills, one of many ways they spread plants, which is easier to understand once you see how birds fly.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Spicy food burns because it is actually damaging or heating your mouth.

Truth: Capsaicin tricks your nerves. It switches on the same sensors that detect real heat, so your brain thinks your mouth is on fire. No real burn is happening, and the mouth's temperature doesn't change.

Try this

See why water fails. Put a few drops of cooking oil in a small glass of water and stir hard. The oil won't mix in. Now add a spoon of milk and stir again. Capsaicin acts like that oil, and milk helps break it up and carry it away.
