How does your brain work?

Your brain weighs about as much as a bag of sugar, yet it burns about a fifth of all the energy your body uses.

The short answer

Your brain is made of about 86 billion nerve cells, called neurons. Each one is a long, thin cell with branches that reach out to other neurons. Together they form a web with trillions of connections.

Neurons talk using electricity and chemicals. A tiny electrical signal races along a neuron. When it reaches the end, the neuron squirts out chemicals that cross a gap and switch on the next cell. Every thought, feeling and movement is a pattern of these signals flickering through the web.

Your brain gets information from your senses, compares it with what it has learned, and sends out instructions, all in a fraction of a second.

Surprising but true

The brain has no pain sensors of its own, so surgeons can operate on it while some patients are awake and talking.

Three main parts

The biggest part is the cerebrum, the wrinkled dome on top. It handles thinking, language, seeing, planning and deciding. Its wrinkles fold a large sheet of brain into a small skull, like scrunching a big dupatta into a small bag.

Tucked underneath at the back is the cerebellum. It keeps your balance and smooths your movements, so you can ride a bicycle or bowl a cricket ball without thinking about every muscle.

The brainstem joins the brain to the spinal cord. It runs the jobs you never think about, such as breathing, heartbeat and swallowing.

Left controls right

The cerebrum is split into two halves. Oddly, the left half controls the right side of your body, and the right half controls the left. The nerves cross over on their way down.

You may have heard that "left-brained" people are logical and "right-brained" people are creative. Scientists have found no good evidence for this. The two halves do have some special jobs, but they work as a team, linked by a thick bridge of about 20 crore nerve fibres.

Faster than a train

Some nerve signals travel more than 100 metres a second, quicker than India's fastest trains. That's how a reflex can jerk your hand off a hot pan before you even feel the pain. The signal goes to your spinal cord and straight back to your muscles, and the brain is told afterwards.

The signals are real electricity, though far weaker than the current in a wall socket. Doctors can measure them with sensors on the scalp. The wavy lines they record, called brain waves, change when you are awake, drowsy or deeply asleep.

What is artificial intelligence?In 1997, a computer beat the world chess champion. Today AI can chat, draw and translate, yet it can still make mistakes a child wouldn't.

A brain that rebuilds itself

Every time you practise something, like a tabla rhythm or a long division sum, connections between certain neurons get stronger. Ones you don't use can weaken. Scientists call this ability to change plasticity. It's why practice works, and why children pick up new languages so easily.

Computers use electricity too, but they work very differently. A laptop does one step after another at enormous speed. Your brain is much slower per step but does billions of things at the same time, using about as much power as a dim light bulb.

The man with the iron rod

In 1848, a railway worker in Vermont named Phineas Gage survived a terrible accident. An iron rod shot through the front of his head. He recovered and could walk and talk, but people who knew him said his personality changed. Doctors studied him for years. His case helped show that different parts of the brain do different jobs, and that the front of the brain helps control how we behave.

Myth or fact?

Myth: We only use 10% of our brains.

Truth: Brain scans show activity all over the brain, though not every part is busy at once. Even simple jobs like catching a ball use many areas together. Damage to almost any part of the brain affects something.

Try this

Ask a friend to hold a ruler upright, with the zero end just above your open thumb and finger. Without warning, they let go, and you catch it as fast as you can. The centimetre mark where you caught it shows your reaction time. Try ten catches and see if your brain gets faster with practice.
