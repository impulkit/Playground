What do kids carry to school around the world?

In Japan, a child's first school bag can cost as much as a new bicycle, and it's built to last all six years of primary school.

The short answer

Open a school bag in Chennai, Chicago, Tokyo or Nairobi and you'll find a lot of the same things: books, a pencil box, a water bottle, something to eat. But the bags themselves, and how heavy they are, can be very different.

Some children carry hard leather boxes on their backs. Some pull wheeled trolleys like tiny airline passengers. Some carry a light cloth bag with a single slate. And some carry so much that governments have made rules about it, including India's School Bag Policy of 2020.

Surprising but true

Japan's hard leather school bag, the randoseru, takes its name from "ransel", a Dutch word for a soldier's backpack.

The six-year bag of Japan

In Japan, almost every primary school child wears a randoseru. It is a stiff, box-shaped backpack, usually made of leather or tough synthetic leather. Families often buy one just before the first year of school, and it is expected to last all six years. A good one can cost around as much as a bicycle.

The idea came from the Netherlands. In the 1800s, Japan borrowed a Dutch soldier's backpack called a ransel, and the name drifted into randoseru. The hard shape protects books, and some families say it can even cushion a child who falls backwards. Plenty of Japanese adults keep their old randoseru as a souvenir, and some send them to children in other countries.

The great Indian bag problem

In India, school bags have long been famous for being heavy. Textbooks, notebooks for every subject, a steel tiffin, a water bottle, sometimes sports kit: it adds up. Surveys in Indian cities have found young children carrying bags that weighed a quarter or more of their own body weight.

So in 2020, India's Ministry of Education issued a School Bag Policy. It says a bag should weigh about 10% of a child's body weight. Schools are asked to keep lockers, plan timetables so fewer books come each day, and give no homework to Classes 1 and 2. Some schools now weigh bags at the gate.

Lunch is its own story. In Mumbai, many office workers don't even carry their lunch: dabbawalas bring it to them. Children mostly still carry their own.

How do Mumbai's dabbawalas deliver lunches?Every working day, thousands of men in white caps carry home-cooked lunches across a city of 2 crore people, and almost nothing goes missing.

Wheels, slates and backpacks

In parts of Europe and China, many children drag wheeled bags. They save shoulders but bump down stairs, and some schools have banned them to stop children tripping in corridors.

In the United States, the zipped nylon backpack rules. It became common in the 1970s and 1980s, when companies began selling light outdoor packs to students. Doctors there often suggest a bag should be under 10–20% of body weight, worn on both shoulders, with the heaviest things packed closest to the back.

In many villages around the world, children still carry very little. A cloth bag, a notebook, perhaps a slate and chalk. A slate weighs little, and the same one can be wiped and used for years, which makes it one of the cheapest school tools ever made.

What the bag says about school

What children carry tells you about how their school works. Lots of books means lots of subjects taught from textbooks. Lockers mean the school expects things to stay there. A tablet in the bag means digital lessons.

Even the water bottle tells a story. Not long ago, many children drank from a school tap or a shared pot. Now the plastic or steel water bottle is in nearly every bag on Earth. And the strangest part is how much all that kit weighs in the end, which comes down to the simple maths of weight and mass.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Carrying a heavy school bag gives children a permanently curved spine.

Truth: Studies have not shown that heavy bags cause scoliosis, a sideways curve of the spine. Heavy or badly worn bags can still cause back and shoulder pain, so lighter bags carried on both shoulders are better.

Try this

Weigh yourself on a bathroom scale, then weigh yourself again holding your packed school bag. The difference is the weight of your bag. Divide it by your own weight and multiply by 100 to find the percentage. Is it under 10? If not, see what you can leave at home.
