How do surfers ride waves?

At Nazaré in Portugal, a surfer rode a wave about 26 metres high, taller than an eight-storey building, which was still the Guinness world record in 2025.

The short answer

Surfers ride waves by sliding down the steep front of a wave as it moves towards the shore. A surfer lies on the board and paddles hard to match the wave's speed. As the wave lifts the back of the board, gravity pulls the surfer down its slope. The surfer pops up to standing and steers along the wave's face, staying just ahead of the part that is breaking.

It works a bit like sledding down a hill that keeps moving forward and building itself again in front of you. Surfing began among Polynesian peoples, especially in Hawaii, centuries ago. Today it is an Olympic sport, and there are surfers on beaches from California to Kerala.

Surprising but true

The surfing event at the Paris 2024 Olympics was held in Tahiti, about 15,000 kilometres from Paris, the farthest any Olympic medal event has been from its host city.

Where waves come from

Most surfing waves are made by wind. When wind blows over the sea for a long time and a long distance, it builds ripples into rolling waves called swell. Storms can send swell thousands of kilometres across the ocean. A wave that breaks on a beach in Hawaii may have started in a storm near Antarctica days earlier.

In deep water, a wave is mostly energy passing through. As it reaches shallower water near the shore, its bottom drags on the seabed and slows. The top keeps going, the wave gets steeper and taller, and finally it tips over and breaks.

Riding the slope

A surfboard floats because it is light and wide. To catch a wave, a surfer has to be moving at nearly the wave's speed. Paddling builds that speed. Then the steep face of the wave tilts the board downhill, and the surfer starts sliding.

Good surfers don't go straight down. They angle across the face, staying in the "pocket" just ahead of the curling lip. On big hollow waves, they can ride inside the curl itself, called the barrel or tube, before shooting out of the end.

Sliding on waves in old Hawaii

Polynesian peoples were surfing long before Europeans arrived. In Hawaii it was called he'e nalu, wave sliding. Chiefs rode long, heavy boards made of wood, some more than 4 metres long. Ordinary people rode shorter boards. Surfing contests were part of festivals.

In 1778 and 1779, officers on Captain James Cook's voyage described Hawaiians riding waves and were amazed. In the early 1900s, the Hawaiian swimmer Duke Kahanamoku, an Olympic champion, showed off surfing in Australia and California and helped spread it worldwide. Bored surfers in California later put wheels on boards, and skateboarding was born.

The biggest waves on Earth

At Nazaré, on Portugal's coast, a deep underwater canyon funnels ocean swell straight at the shore, making some of the biggest waves anyone surfs. In October 2020, the German surfer Sebastian Steudtner rode one measured at 26.21 metres. Guinness World Records lists it as the largest wave ever surfed. That same year, Brazil's Maya Gabeira set the women's record there, at about 22.4 metres.

Waves that big move too fast to paddle into. So surfers are towed in by a partner on a jet ski.

Surfing at the Olympics and in India

Surfing first appeared at the Olympics at Tokyo 2020, held in 2021 because of the pandemic. For Paris 2024, the surfing was held at Teahupo'o in Tahiti, a French territory in the Pacific, famous for its thick, heavy waves breaking over a shallow reef.

In India, surfing grew from the 2000s, with surf schools along the coasts of Karnataka, Kerala and Tamil Nadu. And the strangest part? The fastest, most powerful waves of all, tsunamis, are almost impossible to surf. In deep water they are barely a ripple, and near land they arrive as a rising flood.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Surfers ride the water as it rushes to the beach.

Truth: In open water, a wave is energy moving through the sea, and the water itself mostly goes round in circles. Surfers ride the moving slope of the wave as it breaks, sliding down it while it travels forward.

Try this

In a bathtub or a long tray of water, push one end gently to make a wave. Float a flat piece of plastic or a sponge in its path. Then try again, giving the sponge a small push forward just as the wave arrives. See which time the wave carries it furthest.
