What is Angkor Wat?

The largest religious monument on Earth was built for the Hindu god Vishnu, and it stands not in India but in Cambodia.

The short answer

Angkor Wat is a vast stone temple in northern Cambodia, near the town of Siem Reap. It was built in the early 1100s by King Suryavarman II of the Khmer Empire, as a temple to the Hindu god Vishnu. Over time, it became a Buddhist temple, and it still is one today.

It is the largest religious monument in the world. Its outer walls and moat enclose an area of about 160 hectares, roughly the size of 200 football fields. In the middle rise five towers shaped like lotus buds. They are meant to stand for Mount Meru, the home of the gods in Hindu and Buddhist belief. Angkor Wat is so important to Cambodians that it appears on the national flag.

Surprising but true

One carved wall at Angkor Wat shows the churning of the ocean of milk, a story from Hindu texts, and it is about 49 m long.

A temple shaped like the universe

The whole design is a model of the universe as described in Hindu texts. The central tower is Mount Meru. The four smaller towers are surrounding peaks. The walls stand for the mountains at the edge of the world, and the wide moat for the ocean beyond.

Unusually, Angkor Wat faces west, while most Khmer temples face east. Scholars have suggested reasons. West is the direction linked with Vishnu, and some think the temple was also meant as the king's tomb.

Stories in stone

Long galleries around the temple are covered in carvings called bas-reliefs. One shows the churning of the ocean of milk, a story told in Hindu texts. Gods and demons pull on a giant serpent wrapped around a mountain, churning the ocean to make the nectar of immortality.

Other walls show scenes from the Mahabharata and the Ramayana, the Battle of Kurukshetra and the battle of Lanka, along with parades of the king's army. Hundreds of graceful female figures, called apsaras, are carved on the walls too, each with a different hairstyle or jewellery.

Building with water

The temple is built of sandstone blocks brought from a quarry in the Kulen hills, about 40 km away. Experts think the blocks were moved partly by canals and rafts, then pulled on land.

Angkor Wat was only one part of a huge city. The Khmer built giant reservoirs, called barays, and canals to store and spread water for rice fields. In 2012, and again in 2015, scientists flew over the area with lasers, a method called lidar, which can see through trees to the ground. They found roads, ponds and neighbourhoods hidden under the forest, showing that greater Angkor was one of the largest cities of its time. Archaeology has rarely had a better tool.

Ideas from India

Merchants, priests and travellers carried Hindu and Buddhist ideas from India across South-East Asia over many centuries. Khmer kings used Sanskrit names and titles, and built temples to Vishnu and Shiva in their own style.

India's link to the temple continued in modern times. From 1986 to 1993, the Archaeological Survey of India worked on cleaning and repairing parts of Angkor Wat, though some of its cleaning methods were later criticised. Many countries now help to look after Angkor together.

After the empire

The Khmer capital moved south in the 1400s, and many of Angkor's temples were swallowed by forest, as told in lost cities. At the nearby temple of Ta Prohm, giant tree roots still grip the stones.

Angkor Wat itself was never abandoned. Today it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and crowds gather before dawn to watch the Sun rise behind its towers, reflected in the pools in front.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Angkor Wat was lost in the jungle until a French explorer found it in 1860.

Truth: Cambodians always knew about it, and Buddhist monks kept using it. The French naturalist Henri Mouhot wrote about it, which made it famous in Europe, but he did not discover it.
