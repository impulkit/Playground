What are the loudest animals?

A sperm whale's click is one of the loudest sounds made by any animal, a shrimp's snap makes a flash of heat as hot as the Sun's surface, and a tiny water bug sings louder than a train.

The short answer

The loudest animal ever measured is the sperm whale. Its clicks can reach about 230 decibels underwater, among the loudest sounds made by any living thing. Sperm whales use them like sonar to find squid in the dark deep sea.

On land and in the air, the champions include the white bellbird of the Amazon, howler monkeys, lions and some cicadas. And the smallest show-offs are wild too. A snapping shrimp makes a bang that boils water. A water bug the size of a rice grain sings louder than a busy road.

Surprising but true

A water bug about 2 mm long, smaller than a grain of rice, sings at about 99 decibels by rubbing a tiny body part against its belly.

Decibels: a quick guide

Sound is measured in decibels, or dB. The scale jumps fast. Every extra 10 dB means about ten times more sound energy. A quiet library is about 30 dB, normal talking about 60 dB, and a loud rock concert about 110 dB. Sounds above about 120 dB can hurt your ears.

There's a catch. Underwater decibels use a different starting point from those in air, so a sound of 230 dB in the sea isn't the same as 230 dB in a room. Scientists subtract roughly 60 dB to compare them. Even so, a sperm whale's click is extraordinarily powerful.

Giants of the deep

A sperm whale's head holds a huge organ filled with oil, which focuses its clicks into a beam. The clicks bounce off squid and come back as echoes, a bit like bats use sound to hunt insects.

Blue whales are close behind. Their deep moans can travel hundreds of kilometres through the ocean. Some are so low that human ears can barely hear them. The ocean carries sound much better than air, which is why whales can talk across such distances.

The shrimp with a sonic gun

Pistol shrimps, also called snapping shrimps, are only a few centimetres long. One claw is much bigger than the other. When it snaps shut, it shoots out a jet of water so fast that it forms a tiny bubble.

When the bubble collapses, it makes a crack of about 200 dB underwater and, for a split second, heats up to about 4,700°C, close to the temperature of the Sun's surface. The shock can stun small fish. Colonies of these shrimps are so noisy that submarines in the Second World War used their crackle to hide.

The loudest bird

In 2019, scientists recording birds in the Brazilian Amazon measured the song of the male white bellbird at about 125 dB. That's louder than a chainsaw close up. The male sings his loudest note right at a female perched beside him.

Nobody is sure how she avoids hurting her ears. The bird is only about the size of a pigeon, yet its thick belly muscles squeeze out a note as loud as a pneumatic drill.

Monkeys, lions and bugs

Howler monkeys of Central and South America have a special hollow bone in the throat that makes their calls boom. A group's dawn chorus can be heard about 5 km away through the forest.

A lion's roar reaches about 114 dB close up and can carry up to 8 km on a still night. In India, the lions of Gir roar to tell other prides where their territory ends.

Some cicadas reach over 100 dB, loud enough to drown out conversation in a forest. And the water boatman Micronecta scholtzi, a bug about 2 mm long, sings at about 99 dB by rubbing a tiny part of its body against its belly. Luckily for us, most of its sound stays underwater.

Why be so loud?

Loud sounds help animals find mates, warn rivals, hunt in the dark or stay in touch across long distances. In thick forests or the deep sea, where you can't see far, sound is the best way to reach someone.

But some of the most important animal sounds are ones we can't hear at all. Elephants rumble so low that the sound travels through the ground for kilometres, and other elephants may pick it up through their feet, as you'll find in how elephants talk.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The blue whale, the biggest animal, is also the loudest.

Truth: Blue whale calls are very loud, but the clicks of sperm whales are measured as even louder. Size helps, but the loudest sounds come from animals built to make sharp bursts of sound.
