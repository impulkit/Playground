How are cities planned?

Some of the world's oldest planned grids of straight streets were laid out about 4,500 years ago, in cities of the Indus Valley.

The short answer

Most cities grow on their own, one house and lane at a time. Planning is the job of deciding ahead of time where things should go: homes, roads, schools, parks, markets, factories, water pipes and drains.

City planners study maps, count how many people live in each area and guess how many will come in the next twenty years. Then they draw a master plan. It says which land is for housing, which is for shops and offices, and where the next bus route, hospital or metro line should be built.

Some cities are planned from the very first brick. Most are planned in patches, long after people have already moved in. That is much harder, a bit like rearranging the furniture while the whole family is still sitting on it.

Surprising but true

Adding more lanes to a crowded road often fills up with new traffic within a few years, so planners now treat buses, trains and footpaths as traffic cures too.

Grids, drains and the Indus

Planning is old. In the Indus Valley cities, such as Mohenjo-daro, streets ran in straight lines that met at right angles. Covered brick drains ran along them, carrying waste water away from homes. That was about 4,500 years ago.

Jaipur, founded in 1727 by Maharaja Sawai Jai Singh II, was laid out in rectangular blocks with wide markets, guided by old Indian ideas about building. In the USA, the French-born engineer Pierre L'Enfant drew up the plan for Washington, D.C. in 1791, with broad avenues that fan out from the Capitol. In 1811, New York decided that most of Manhattan would become a giant grid of numbered streets.

A city drawn from scratch

After Partition in 1947, Punjab lost its old capital, Lahore, to Pakistan. India decided to build a new one. The Swiss-French architect Le Corbusier took charge in the early 1950s, and Chandigarh was the result.

The city is divided into sectors, each about 800 m by 1,200 m. Every sector was meant to be a small neighbourhood with its own shops, school and green spaces, so daily needs were within walking distance. Fast roads run around the sectors, not through them. Chandigarh was also planned with large green belts, a long park, Leisure Valley, and a man-made lake, Sukhna, at its edge. Today it is often ranked among the cleanest and greenest cities in India, though it has grown far beyond the number of people it was first planned for.

The hidden half of a plan

Much of a city's plan is invisible. Pipes bring in clean water. Sewers carry dirty water out. Cables carry power. Planners also decide how tall buildings can be and how far apart they must stand, so light and air reach the street. These rules are called zoning. They can also keep noisy, smoky factories away from homes and schools. New York passed one of the first big zoning laws in 1916, after tall towers began blocking out the sun.

When a city grows faster than its plan, the gaps show. Homes go up without drains. Lakes are filled in and then flood. Bengaluru, for example, has struggled with flooding partly because many of its old lakes and the channels between them were built over.

Planning for people, not cars

For much of the 20th century, many planners designed cities around the car. Wide roads and flyovers came first. But wider roads tend to attract more cars, and traffic jams return.

Many cities now try a different approach. Delhi, Mumbai and Bengaluru have built metro lines. Some streets get wider footpaths and cycle lanes. The idea is simple: when homes, schools and jobs are close together and easy to reach on foot or by train, fewer people need to sit in traffic at all.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Building more roads always cures traffic jams.

Truth: New lanes often attract more drivers, and the jams come back. Good public transport, footpaths, cycle lanes and homes close to jobs tend to work better over time.
