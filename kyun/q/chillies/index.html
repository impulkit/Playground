How did chillies come to India?

Indian food without chillies seems impossible, yet for most of India's history there wasn't a single chilli plant in the country.

The short answer

Chillies are from the Americas. People in what is now Mexico were growing them more than 6,000 years ago. When Christopher Columbus reached the Caribbean in 1492, he tasted them and took them back to Spain.

Within a few decades, Portuguese ships carried chillies around Africa to their trading base in Goa, on India's west coast. The plant grew easily in Indian soil and was cheap to grow. In less than a century, it had spread across the country and changed Indian cooking for good.

Surprising but true

Birds can't feel the burn of chillies at all, so they happily eat them and spread the seeds far and wide.

Before the chilli

Indian food was spicy long before chillies arrived. The heat came from black pepper, long pepper (called pippali), ginger and mustard. Pepper was expensive, though, because so much of it was sold abroad.

So when Europeans saw this new fruit, they called it a "pepper" too, because it burned the tongue. That is why chillies are still called peppers in the USA and Britain, even though they belong to a completely different plant family. In several Indian languages the chilli's name borrowed from the word for pepper: in Hindi, kali mirch is black pepper and lal mirch is red chilli.

How a chilli travelled

The Portuguese reached India in 1498 and took over Goa in 1510. Their ships brought several American crops, including chillies, cashews and papaya. Farmers found that chillies grew quickly, produced lots of fruit, and could be dried and stored for a year.

A plant that gave you pepper-like heat for almost nothing was hard to resist. Chillies spread inland along trade routes. By the 1600s they were growing in many parts of India. Today India grows more chillies than any other country, and Guntur in Andhra Pradesh has one of the largest chilli markets in Asia.

What makes Goa different?Goa became part of India in 1961, fourteen years after independence. Before that, Portugal ruled it for about 450 years, and you can still see it in the churches, houses and food.

The chemistry of the burn

The heat of a chilli comes from a chemical called capsaicin. It doesn't actually burn you. It fits into the same sensors in your mouth that detect real heat, so your brain gets a false fire alarm.

In 1912, an American chemist named Wilbur Scoville invented a test to measure chilli heat. Tasters sipped chilli extract mixed with more and more sugar water until they could no longer feel the burn. The Scoville scale is still used, though labs now measure capsaicin with machines.

India's own fire

In 2007, a chilli from Northeast India called bhut jolokia, or ghost pepper, was named the hottest chilli in the world by Guinness World Records. It is grown in Assam, Nagaland and Manipur, and is hundreds of times hotter than a typical green chilli. Newer hybrids bred in the USA have since beaten it.

Not every Indian chilli is fiery. Kashmiri chillies are mild and give rogan josh its deep red colour. Byadgi chillies from Karnataka are prized for colour too. Many cooks use one kind for heat and another for colour in the same dish.

Back where it started

Chillies are still at the heart of Mexican cooking, from mild poblanos to smoky chipotles. Thai, Korean and Sichuan Chinese food, all famous for their heat, also got their chillies after 1500. In just 500 years, a plant from one corner of the world became the most widely grown spice on Earth.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The seeds are the hottest part of a chilli.

Truth: Most of the capsaicin is made in the pale, spongy ribs inside the chilli, which hold the seeds. Seeds taste hot because they touch those ribs.

Try this

Look at the labels on spice packets and ready-made masalas in your kitchen. Count how many list chilli as an ingredient. Then draw a world map and trace the chilli's route from Mexico to Spain, around Africa to Goa, and on to your own home.
