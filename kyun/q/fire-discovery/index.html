How did people learn to use fire?

Long before anyone could light a fire, early humans probably carried flames home from lightning strikes and worked hard never to let them go out.

The short answer

Nobody invented fire. It was already there, in grass fires lit by lightning and in smouldering logs after a storm. Early humans first learned to use it: carrying a burning branch, feeding it with wood and keeping it alive. Much later, they learned to start a fire whenever they wanted.

The evidence comes from charcoal, ash, burnt bones and stones changed by heat, found in caves and old campsites. The oldest strong clues are about a million years old, from a cave in South Africa. By around 400,000 years ago, fire shows up at many sites in Africa, Europe and Asia.

Surprising but true

Some of the oldest strong evidence of people using fire comes from deep inside Wonderwerk Cave in South Africa, where ash and burnt bone are about a million years old.

Clues in the ash

Finding ancient fire is tricky. A wildfire can scorch bones and soil too. So scientists look for signs that a fire burned in one spot, again and again, inside a shelter where lightning could not reach.

At Wonderwerk Cave, researchers found fine ash and burnt bone fragments about 30 metres inside the cave, layered as if from small fires. At Gesher Benot Ya'aqov in Israel, burnt seeds, wood and flint about 790,000 years old cluster in patches, like old hearths. Some claims of even older fires exist, but many scientists are not convinced.

Rubbing and striking

Starting a fire takes skill. One way uses friction: spinning a hard stick fast against a softer piece of wood until the dust gets hot enough to glow. Tiny embers are then dropped into dry grass and blown into flame. A bow, like a small archer's bow, makes the spinning easier.

The other way strikes certain stones together, such as flint against a mineral called pyrite, to throw sparks onto dry tinder. Some flint tools left by Neanderthals in France, about 50,000 years old, have marks that may come from exactly this.

What is fire?Fire isn't a thing at all. It's something happening, a fast chemical reaction that gives off heat and light.

What fire changed

Fire kept people warm and let them live in colder lands. It scared away animals at night. Its light stretched the day, giving hours for talking, making tools and telling stories.

It also changed food. Cooking softens meat and roots, kills many germs and makes food easier to digest. The scientist Richard Wrangham has argued that cooking gave early humans so much extra energy that it helped their brains grow larger. Others think brains began growing before regular fire use, so the idea is still debated.

People also used fire as a tool. They hardened the tips of wooden spears, and in South Africa, at least 160,000 years ago, people heated stone to make it easier to chip into sharp blades.

Fire in stories and belief

Because fire mattered so much, many cultures tell stories about it. In Greek myths, Prometheus steals fire from the gods and gives it to humans. Many Native American stories tell of an animal, such as a spider or a coyote, carrying fire to people.

In India, fire is sacred in Hindu worship. The very first hymn of the Rigveda, one of the oldest texts in any Indian language, praises Agni, the god of fire. Today, fire still sits at the centre of Hindu weddings, Parsi fire temples and the oil lamps of Diwali.

Matches arrive

For most of history, starting a fire was slow work, so people kept embers glowing or borrowed fire from neighbours. Friction matches were invented in England in 1826. Safety matches, which light only when struck on the box, followed in Sweden in the 1840s and 1850s. A trick that once took minutes of hard work now takes one flick of the wrist.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Fire was discovered in one moment by one clever person.

Truth: Early humans probably used fire from wildfires and lightning for a very long time before they could start one themselves. Learning to control and make fire likely happened slowly, and in more than one place.

Try this

Rub your palms together fast for ten seconds and feel how warm they get. Now try it slowly. That heat comes from friction, the same effect people used to light fires by spinning a stick against wood. Only an adult should ever try real fire-making, and only outdoors.
