Why is Silicon Valley famous?

Fewer than a hundred years ago, the valley south of San Francisco was famous for apricot and plum orchards. Now it's home to Apple, Google and Nvidia.

The short answer

Silicon Valley is the nickname for the area around the southern end of San Francisco Bay in California. It is famous because many of the world's biggest technology companies started or are based there, including Apple, Google, Meta, Intel and Nvidia.

The name comes from silicon, the material used to make computer chips. From the late 1950s, so many chip companies grew up in the area that a journalist, Don Hoefler, used the phrase "Silicon Valley USA" in a 1971 trade newspaper series. The name stuck, and the region became a symbol of new ideas, risky bets and enormous fortunes.

Surprising but true

There is no town called Silicon Valley on any map. The name is a nickname, first made popular by a journalist in 1971.

From orchards to electronics

Until the mid-1900s, the Santa Clara Valley was farmland, covered with fruit trees. People called it "the Valley of Heart's Delight". Every spring, millions of apricot, plum and cherry trees blossomed, and canneries packed fruit for the whole country.

Stanford University, in Palo Alto, helped change that. An engineering professor, Frederick Terman, encouraged his students to start companies nearby instead of moving east. Two of them, Bill Hewlett and David Packard, began in a garage in 1939. In 1951 Stanford opened an industrial park on its land and rented space to technology firms.

The traitorous eight

In 1956 William Shockley, a co-inventor of the transistor, set up a company near Stanford to make silicon parts. He hired brilliant young scientists but was very hard to work for. In 1957 eight of them quit together and started their own company, Fairchild Semiconductor. Shockley reportedly called them traitors, and the nickname "the traitorous eight" stuck.

Fairchild was hugely successful. Many of its workers left to start companies of their own, and those workers' workers did the same. Two of the eight, Robert Noyce and Gordon Moore, founded Intel in 1968. Dozens of firms trace their roots back to Fairchild, a family tree that spread across the valley.

Money for risky ideas

Silicon Valley also became the home of venture capital: investors who put money into young companies in return for a share. Many of their offices sit along one road, Sand Hill Road in Menlo Park. They expect most of their bets to fail, but one big success can pay for all the rest.

That attitude helps. In Silicon Valley, failing at a startup is often seen as experience rather than shame. Founders who fail can try again, and engineers move freely from one company to the next, carrying ideas with them.

People from everywhere

The valley has drawn engineers from around the world. Many come from India, China, Taiwan and Vietnam. Indian Americans now lead some of the biggest companies there, and many Indian engineers return home to start companies in Bengaluru and Hyderabad. It's part of a much bigger story of the Indian diaspora, and of why so many tech leaders come from India.

Problems in paradise

Success has costs. House prices in the valley are among the highest in the United States, so teachers, nurses and bus drivers often can't afford to live near their work. Traffic is heavy, and the gap between rich and poor is wide. Critics have also pointed out that tech companies there have hired too few women and too few Black and Latino workers.

Other places now copy the model. Bengaluru is often called "the Silicon Valley of India", and cities from Tel Aviv to Shenzhen compete for the same engineers. The fruit trees, meanwhile, are mostly gone, though a few orchards are kept as parks to remember the valley's sweeter past.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Silicon Valley is a city, and silicon is mined there.

Truth: It's a nickname for a group of towns and cities around the southern end of San Francisco Bay, including San Jose and Palo Alto. The "silicon" refers to the chips first made there, not to any mine.
