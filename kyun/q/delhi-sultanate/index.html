What was the Delhi Sultanate?

For 320 years, five different families ruled north India from Delhi, and one sultan tried to move the whole capital 1,100 km south.

The short answer

The Delhi Sultanate was a series of Muslim kingdoms that ruled much of north India from Delhi between 1206 and 1526. Its rulers were called sultans.

Five dynasties held power one after another: the Mamluks (sometimes called the Slave dynasty), the Khaljis, the Tughlaqs, the Sayyids and the Lodis. At its largest, under the Khaljis and the Tughlaqs, the sultanate reached deep into south India. The period brought new styles of building with arches and domes, new kinds of music and poetry, and a new mixed language that grew into Hindi and Urdu. It ended in 1526, when Babur defeated the last sultan at Panipat and founded the Mughal Empire.

Surprising but true

The Delhi Sultanate had a woman ruler, Razia Sultan, who ruled from 1236 to 1240 and rode out in front of her army.

A slave who became a king

The first sultan, Qutb-ud-din Aibak, began life as an enslaved soldier. In Central Asia at that time, slaves were often trained as soldiers and officials, and some rose very high. Aibak became a general for the ruler Muhammad of Ghor, and took charge of his Indian lands.

Around 1199, Aibak began building the Qutb Minar in Delhi, a tower of red and buff sandstone. His successor, Iltutmish, finished it. At about 73 metres, it is as tall as a 24-storey building, and it is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Razia, the sultan who was a woman

Iltutmish chose his daughter Razia to rule after him, saying she was more able than his sons. In 1236 she became sultan, the only woman to rule from Delhi's throne in this era.

Razia dressed in a cloak and turban, held court in public and rode an elephant at the head of her army. Many nobles disliked taking orders from a woman. After about four years she was overthrown and killed. Her story has inspired books, plays and a film.

How did Urdu develop?Urdu was born in the markets and army camps of north India, where Persian, Arabic and Turkish words met the everyday speech of Delhi.

Market rules and Mongol armies

Alauddin Khalji, who ruled from 1296 to 1316, faced repeated invasions by Mongol armies from Central Asia. To pay a large standing army, he fixed the prices of grain, cloth and horses in Delhi's markets, and punished shopkeepers who cheated on weights.

His armies also marched south and brought back great wealth. Court historians praised his control, while others recorded that people found his rule harsh. He was one of the few rulers of the time to keep the Mongols out of India.

A sultan with big ideas

Muhammad bin Tughlaq, who ruled from 1325 to 1351, was clever and well read, yet many of his plans failed. In 1327 he ordered the capital moved from Delhi to Daulatabad in the Deccan, about 1,100 km away. Many people suffered on the march, and the court later returned.

He also issued copper and brass token coins to stand in for silver, an idea a bit like modern money. But they were easy to forge, and the plan collapsed. The Moroccan traveller Ibn Battuta worked for him as a judge and described his generosity and his sudden anger.

Pillars, poets and the end

The next sultan, Firoz Shah Tughlaq, built canals, gardens and towns. He also had two of Ashoka's ancient stone pillars carried to Delhi, wrapped in silk cotton and moved on a huge cart.

The poet and musician Amir Khusrau lived in this era. He wrote in Persian and in Hindavi, an early form of Hindi and Urdu, and he is linked to the growth of qawwali singing. In 1398, the Central Asian conqueror Timur sacked Delhi, and the sultanate never fully recovered. In 1526, Babur's guns and cavalry defeated Ibrahim Lodi at Panipat, and a new empire began.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Delhi Sultanate was one family that ruled for 300 years.

Truth: It was five separate dynasties, some lasting only a few decades. Power often changed hands through rebellions and murders at court.
