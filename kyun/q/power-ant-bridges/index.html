How do ants build bridges with their bodies?

In the rainforests of Central and South America, army ants grab each other's legs and turn themselves into living bridges, so the rest of the colony can march straight across a gap.

The short answer

When marching army ants reach a gap, such as a space between two leaves or a dip in a branch, one ant stops at the edge and stretches across. Another walks onto it and grips on, then another. Each ant hooks its claws onto its neighbours' legs and bodies. Within seconds, there is a living bridge, and the rest of the column streams across.

No ant is in charge. Each one follows simple rules. If it feels other ants walking over it, it holds still. If the traffic stops, it lets go and rejoins the march. When the column has passed, the bridge melts away, ant by ant, as each one climbs off and follows the others.

Surprising but true

An army ant bridge can slide sideways while the ants are still walking over it, moving to a better spot to shorten the route.

Ants that never stay home

Army ants of the genus Eciton live in the rainforests of Central and South America. A single colony can hold hundreds of thousands of ants, sometimes more than a million. Unlike most ants, they have no permanent nest. They march through the forest in long raiding columns, catching insects, spiders and other small animals.

Army ants are almost blind, and many kinds can't see at all. They follow each other by smell, laying chemical trails called pheromones as they go. Speed matters. A raid can move about 20 metres an hour and sweep across a strip of forest floor as wide as a road. A gap in the path costs time, so plugging it with bodies is worth it, even though the ants in the bridge can't hunt.

A bridge that thinks for itself

In 2015, scientists in Panama tested army ants on a track with a V-shaped gap. The ants built a bridge near the point of the V, where the gap was small. Then, over several minutes, the bridge slowly moved down the V, towards the open end, making the route shorter.

But it stopped partway. Building further out would have needed too many ants in the bridge, and those ants could not carry food. The bridge settled where shortcuts and lost workers balanced out. No ant can see the whole bridge, and most army ants can barely see at all. Yet together they solved a trade-off that engineers would need a calculator for.

Other living structures

Army ants are not the only builders. When their nests flood, fire ants link their legs and float as a raft. The ants' waxy, hairy bodies trap air bubbles, so a raft of thousands can drift on water for days.

Weaver ants, which live in India, Southeast Asia and Australia, form chains to pull two leaves together. One ant grabs the edge of a leaf, another grabs its waist, and so on, until the chain can tug the leaf into place. Then other workers hold their larvae like glue sticks, which ooze silk to stitch the leaves into a nest. In parts of Odisha, people cook these red ants and their eggs into a sharp, sour chutney called kai chutney.

Ants and robot designers

Engineers study ant bridges for ideas. Swarms of small robots that join up to cross gaps or climb walls could help in disasters, reaching places too dangerous for people. Each robot would follow simple rules, like an ant, instead of waiting for orders from a central computer.

The same idea, many simple parts making one clever whole, turns up in bird flocks and fish schools. It also turns up in the way humans build things. Engineers have spent centuries learning to make bridges stay up with steel and concrete. Army ants do it with their own bodies, and then walk away, leaving no trace.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ant colonies work because the queen gives orders to everyone.

Truth: The queen's job is mostly to lay eggs. Nobody gives orders. Ants respond to smells, touch and what nearby ants are doing, and the colony's clever behaviour comes from all those small choices adding up.

Try this

Watch ants solve a problem. Put a few grains of sugar on a piece of paper near an ant trail outside, with an adult's permission. Once ants find it, lay a pencil or a twig across their path as a small barrier. Watch whether they go around, climb over or find a new route, and time how long it takes.
