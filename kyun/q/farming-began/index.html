How did farming begin?

The ancestor of corn was a skinny wild grass with a few rock-hard seeds. It took thousands of years of farmers' choices to turn it into a cob.

The short answer

For hundreds of thousands of years, people lived by hunting and gathering. Then, starting around 11,500 years ago, some groups in the Middle East began to plant seeds of wild grasses and keep herds of goats and sheep. Over many generations, they chose the best plants and the calmest animals, and those slowly changed. This is called domestication.

Farming was not invented once. It began in at least half a dozen regions, at different times, with different crops. Within a few thousand years, farmers had settled villages, food stores and far more children than hunters could feed. That set the stage for towns and cities.

Surprising but true

Farming was invented separately in several parts of the world, including the Middle East, China, Mexico, South America, New Guinea and Africa, by people who never met.

The Fertile Crescent

The first known farmers lived in an arc of land from today's Israel and Jordan, up through Syria and Turkey and down to Iraq and Iran. Historians call it the Fertile Crescent. Here grew wild wheat, barley, lentils and peas.

Wild wheat has a problem for people: when ripe, the seeds fall off and scatter. A few plants, by chance, held on to their seeds. When people harvested with sickles, those were the ones they carried home and planted. Over time, fields filled with wheat that waited to be picked.

Animals changed too. Goats and sheep were tamed here, and cattle soon after. Dogs had joined people even earlier, while everyone was still hunting. Taming an animal took many generations of keeping the gentlest ones and letting them breed, until the herd behaved very differently from its wild cousins.

Farmers around the world

In China, people began growing rice along the Yangtze River and millet along the Yellow River. In Mexico, they turned teosinte into maize, and later grew beans and squash beside it. In the Andes of South America, they grew potatoes and kept llamas. In New Guinea, they grew taro and bananas. In Africa, they grew sorghum and some kinds of millets.

In South Asia, one of the oldest farming sites is Mehrgarh, in the hills of Balochistan, now in Pakistan. About 9,000 years ago, people there grew barley and wheat and kept humped zebu cattle, the kind still seen across India today. Their descendants helped build the Indus cities.

Why start farming?

Farming is hard work. So why did people choose it? Nobody knows for sure. As the last ice age ended, the climate grew warmer and wetter, and wild grains became plentiful in some places. People may have settled near these grains, grown in number, and then needed to plant more to feed everyone.

Some groups built big stone monuments before they farmed. At Göbekli Tepe in Turkey, people carved huge stone pillars about 11,000 years ago. Gatherings at such places may have needed a lot of food, which could have pushed people to grow it.

A mixed blessing

Farming fed more people, but life was not always better. Skeletons show that many early farmers were shorter than hunters before them, and had more tooth decay and disease. Living close to animals and to each other spread germs. A failed harvest could mean famine.

The writer Jared Diamond once called farming "the worst mistake in the history of the human race". Many historians disagree, pointing to what farming made possible: food stores, trades, writing and cities. Today about 8 billion people depend on the crops those first farmers chose, grown on fields that still need good soil and steady rain.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Farming was invented in one place and spread from there to the rest of the world.

Truth: Farming began independently in several regions, each with its own local plants and animals. Wheat and goats came from the Middle East, rice and millet from China, maize and beans from Mexico, potatoes from the Andes.

Try this

Soak a few dried moong or chana in water overnight, then place them on wet cotton in a saucer near a window. Keep them damp and check every day. Pick the strongest sprouts and plant them in a pot of soil. You are doing what the first farmers did, choosing which plants get to grow.
