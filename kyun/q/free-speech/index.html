What is free speech?

In 1965, a 13-year-old girl in Iowa wore a black armband to school to protest a war. Her case reached the US Supreme Court, and she won.

The short answer

Free speech is the right to say what you think, write it, draw it or share it, without being punished by the government. It covers speeches, books, newspapers, films, art, protests and posts online. It also includes the right to criticise those in power.

Democracies treat it as basic, because people can't choose their leaders well without hearing different ideas. But no country allows every kind of speech. Threats, fraud and lies that harm someone's reputation are limited almost everywhere. The big arguments are about where else to draw the line.

Surprising but true

In 2015, India's Supreme Court struck down a law that had let police arrest people for "offensive" posts online, saying it was too vague and could silence almost anyone.

Two constitutions, two styles

The US Constitution's First Amendment, added in 1791, says Congress shall make "no law" limiting freedom of speech or of the press. American courts read this very strongly. Even hateful speech is usually protected, unless it is meant to cause immediate violence.

India's Constitution guarantees freedom of speech and expression in Article 19. But it also allows "reasonable restrictions" for reasons such as public order, decency, the security of the state and friendly relations with other countries. Courts decide what counts as reasonable. Many other democracies, like Germany, ban certain kinds of hate speech outright.

India's limits were debated from the very start. In 1950, the Supreme Court sided with a magazine editor whose paper had been banned from Madras state. The next year, Parliament changed the Constitution in its first-ever amendment, adding public order and friendly relations with other countries to the list of allowed limits. At the world level, Article 19 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, agreed in 1948, says everyone has the right to "seek, receive and impart information and ideas".

A student and an armband

In December 1965, Mary Beth Tinker, aged 13, and a few other students in Des Moines, Iowa, wore black armbands to school to mourn those killed in the Vietnam War. They were suspended. Her family went to court.

In 1969 the US Supreme Court ruled in their favour. Students, it said, do not "shed their constitutional rights to freedom of speech or expression at the schoolhouse gate". Schools can still stop speech that seriously disrupts lessons, but they can't silence a quiet protest just because they dislike it.

What makes a speech famous?Abraham Lincoln's most famous speech took about two minutes. The man who spoke before him talked for two hours, and almost nobody remembers his words.

Speech online

In India, a section of the Information Technology Act once allowed arrests for online posts that were "grossly offensive" or caused "annoyance". People were arrested for social media comments, including two young women in Maharashtra in 2012 over a Facebook post and a "like".

In 2015, in a case brought by law student Shreya Singhal, the Supreme Court struck the section down. It ruled that the words were so vague that they could punish almost any opinion. Today, arguments continue about how social media companies should handle false or hateful posts without silencing ordinary users.

Why it matters

Free speech protects unpopular ideas, because popular ones rarely need protecting. Many causes we now see as obviously right, like votes for women and the end of slavery, began as opinions most people disliked.

It also lets citizens find out what governments are doing. A free press, using journalism and records, can uncover mistakes and wrongdoing. Where speech is tightly controlled, only one side can be heard, and propaganda fills the gap.

Free speech works best alongside listening. You can disagree strongly with someone and still defend their right to speak.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Free speech means you can say anything, anywhere, with no consequences.

Truth: Free speech protects you from being punished by the government for your views, within limits. It does not stop others from disagreeing, and most countries still ban things like threats, fraud and lies that damage someone's reputation.

Try this

Hold a friendly class debate on a small question, like whether homework should be banned on weekends. The rule is that before anyone replies, they must first repeat the other person's point fairly. Notice how it changes the argument.
