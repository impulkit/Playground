What is Vedic maths?

With one trick from a 1965 book, you can square 85 in your head in about two seconds, and the answer is 7,225.

The short answer

Vedic maths is a set of mental calculation tricks described in a book called Vedic Mathematics, published in 1965. Its author, Bharati Krishna Tirtha, was a Hindu religious leader and scholar who was also trained in mathematics. The book gives 16 short rules, called sutras, for doing sums quickly in your head, such as multiplying large numbers or finding squares.

The tricks are real and often clever. Many schools and tuition classes in India and abroad teach them. But the name causes debate, because the rules do not appear in the ancient Vedas. India does have a long and genuine history of mathematics, from early geometry to zero, so the two are worth telling apart.

Surprising but true

The book that started Vedic maths was published in 1965, five years after its author died.

The man and the book

Bharati Krishna Tirtha was born in 1884 and studied Sanskrit, philosophy, mathematics and science. In 1925 he became the Shankaracharya of the Govardhan Matha in Puri, Odisha, one of the most important religious posts in Hinduism.

He said he had rebuilt the sutras after years of study and meditation, from an appendix of the Atharva Veda. He said he wrote 16 volumes explaining them, but the manuscripts were lost. Near the end of his life he wrote one introductory volume from memory. It was published in 1965, five years after he died.

How the tricks work

Each sutra is a short Sanskrit phrase. One of the best known is Ekadhikena Purvena, "by one more than the previous one". It helps with squaring numbers that end in 5. For 65 × 65, take the 6, multiply it by one more, 7, to get 42, then add 25 at the end. The answer is 4,225.

Another sutra, "all from 9 and the last from 10", makes subtracting from 1,000 easy. For 1,000 − 357, take each digit from 9, except the last, which you take from 10. That gives 6, 4 and 3, so the answer is 643.

A rule called "vertically and crosswise" multiplies two-digit numbers in one line. These are fun, and they work because of ordinary algebra. Once children have a firm grip on their times tables, the tricks can speed things up further.

What is Sanskrit?About 2,400 years ago, a scholar named Panini described the whole of Sanskrit grammar in about 4,000 short rules. Linguists still study his work.

Where the debate lies

The Vedas are among the oldest religious texts in the world, composed in the Vedic age roughly 3,000 years ago and passed down by memory. Scholars who know them well have not found Tirtha's sutras in them. The language of the sutras also looks much later than Vedic Sanskrit.

In 1993 the Indian mathematician S. G. Dani wrote a well-known critique. He argued the tricks were useful but had nothing to do with the Vedas, and that calling them Vedic could mislead students about India's real mathematical history. Supporters reply that the tricks still help children enjoy maths, whatever their source. Even the general editor's note in the 1965 book says the sutras are not found in the known versions of the Atharva Veda.

India's real ancient maths

The Vedic period did produce real mathematics. The Shulba Sutras, written roughly 2,500 to 2,800 years ago, give rules for building fire altars of exact shapes and sizes. They include a rule about the sides of right-angled triangles, the idea now often credited to Pythagoras, along with a very good approximation for the square root of 2.

Later Indian mathematicians, such as Aryabhata and Brahmagupta, made discoveries that changed the world. Their work on place value, zero and algebra was carried by scholars to the Arab world and on to Europe. Like the abacus, Vedic maths is a useful tool for mental sums. It is a modern tool, built by a gifted scholar, and India's ancient maths has plenty of true stories of its own.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The 16 sutras of Vedic maths are written in the ancient Vedas.

Truth: No known Vedic text contains them. Bharati Krishna Tirtha said they came from an appendix of the Atharva Veda, but nobody else has found it. Most historians think he composed or put together the sutras himself in the 20th century, while the Vedas date back around 3,000 years.

Use the "ends in 5" trick to square 15, 25, 35, 55 and 95 in your head. Multiply the first digit by one more than itself, then stick 25 on the end. Check each answer with a calculator or by long multiplication. Then try to work out why the trick always works.
