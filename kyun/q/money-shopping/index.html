How do shops decide prices?

The price on a packet of biscuits is a mix of costs, competition, clever psychology and, in India, a number the law says nobody may go above.

The short answer

A shop starts with what something costs it: the price it paid the supplier, plus rent, electricity, wages and transport. Then it adds a bit extra as profit. That is the basic price.

But the shop also watches what other shops charge, how many people want the item and how much it has in stock. If lots of people want something and there is little of it, the price goes up. If nobody is buying, the price drops. Shops also use small tricks, like ending prices in 99, to make things feel cheaper. In India, the law adds one more rule: never above the MRP.

Surprising but true

In India it is against the law for a shop to sell a packaged product for more than the MRP printed on it.

Cost plus a little more

Every item on a shelf carries hidden costs. A packet of chips includes the potatoes, oil, packaging, factory, advertising, truck ride and the shopkeeper's share. Each business along the way adds its own profit.

Shops usually work out a markup, a percentage added on top of cost. Small, everyday things like bread or milk often have small markups, because shops sell lots of them. Fancy items, like a designer bag, may have much bigger ones.

What does percent mean?If a price goes up by 50 per cent and then down by 50 per cent, it ends up cheaper than when it started.

Supply and demand

Prices shift when supply or demand changes. In 2023, heavy rains and heat damaged tomato crops in parts of India, and in some cities tomatoes crossed ₹200 a kilo for a while. Families switched to other recipes until the new harvest arrived.

In the United States, the price of eggs jumped in 2022 and 2023 when bird flu killed millions of hens. When supply fell, people competed for fewer eggs, and the price rose. When supply recovered, prices came down again.

The MRP rule

In India, every packaged product must show an MRP, or maximum retail price, which includes all taxes. Shops may sell for less, but not more. This rule protects shoppers in places where they cannot easily compare prices.

In the United States, there is no MRP. Price tags usually leave out sales tax, which is added at the till and varies from state to state. Visitors from India are often surprised when a $10 shirt costs more than $10 at the counter. Taxes are part of every price, whether you see them or not.

Bargaining and fixed prices

In many Indian markets, from Sarojini Nagar in Delhi to small weekly haats, bargaining is normal. The seller starts high, the buyer offers low, and they meet somewhere in the middle. Supermarkets and big stores usually have fixed prices printed on labels.

Fixed price tags only became common in the late 1800s, when big department stores in cities like New York and Paris wanted to serve many customers quickly. Before that, haggling was common almost everywhere.

Prices that change by the minute

Some prices change constantly. Airline tickets, hotel rooms and taxi apps use computer programs that raise prices when demand is high and lower them when it is quiet. A cab ride in Mumbai during a downpour may cost much more than the same ride on a dry afternoon.

Shops also use "loss leaders": they sell a few popular items very cheaply, even at a loss, to bring people in, hoping they will buy other things too.

Being a smart shopper

Understanding prices helps you spot a real bargain. Check the price per kilo or per litre, not just the price on the packet, and remember that a "buy one get one free" deal only helps if you need two. Paying by phone with UPI or by card makes spending easy, which is one reason it helps to stop and do the maths first.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A higher price always means better quality.

Truth: Price depends on costs, brand, demand and competition as well as quality. Studies often find that cheaper store brands are very similar to famous brands. Comparing ingredients and reviews tells you more than the price tag.

Try this

Find two sizes of the same product in a shop or your kitchen, like a small and a large packet of biscuits. Divide each price by its weight to find the price per 100 g. Which is the better deal? Sometimes the big packet is not cheaper.
