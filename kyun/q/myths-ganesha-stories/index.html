What are the favourite stories about Ganesha?

In one famous story, Ganesha broke off his own tusk so he could keep writing the Mahabharata without stopping.

The short answer

Ganesha, the elephant-headed god, is one of the best-loved figures in Hindu stories. He is the remover of obstacles and the god people pray to before starting anything new, from a school exam to a wedding. He is also clever, kind and fond of sweets, especially the steamed dumplings called modak.

Children grow up with his stories. They learn how he got his elephant head and how he won a race around the world without leaving home. They hear why he has a broken tusk, and why he once cursed the Moon. They appear in temple carvings, comic books, cartoons and grandparents' bedtime tales, and get told loudest during Ganesh Chaturthi.

Surprising but true

Different old texts tell different stories of how Ganesha got his elephant head, and Hindus have happily kept more than one version.

The boy at the door

In the best-known version, the goddess Parvati made a boy from turmeric paste and brought him to life. She told him to guard the door while she bathed and let no one in.

When Shiva, her husband, came home, the boy blocked his way. They fought, and Shiva cut off the boy's head. Parvati was heartbroken and furious. To calm her, Shiva sent his followers to bring the head of the first creature they found. They returned with an elephant's head, and Shiva brought the boy back to life as Ganesha, making him leader of his followers.

Other Puranas tell it differently. In one, the planet god Shani glances at the baby and the head is lost. Indians have kept several versions side by side for centuries.

The race around the world

Shiva and Parvati once had a special fruit, sometimes said to be a mango, and two sons who both wanted it. They set a contest: whoever went around the world three times first would win.

Kartikeya leapt onto his peacock and flew off. Ganesha, with his slow mouse, didn't even try. He walked three times around his parents instead. "You are my whole world," he said. When Kartikeya returned, the fruit was already Ganesha's. In Tamil Nadu, this story is linked to Kartikeya, called Murugan, going off to live on the hill at Palani.

The broken tusk

Ganesha is usually shown with one tusk broken. One story explains it this way. The sage Vyasa wanted to dictate the Mahabharata, the longest poem in the world, and asked Ganesha to write it down. Ganesha agreed on one condition: Vyasa must never stop. Vyasa added his own condition: Ganesha must understand every verse before writing it.

As they raced along, Ganesha's pen broke. Rather than stop, he snapped off his own tusk and kept writing with it. Other stories say he lost the tusk in a fight, but the writing story is the favourite of students.

Why not to look at the Moon

One night, after a big feast of modaks, Ganesha was riding his mouse home with a full belly. The mouse tripped over a snake, Ganesha tumbled, and his belly burst open with sweets everywhere. The Moon, watching, laughed.

Angry, Ganesha cursed the Moon to fade away. The Moon begged forgiveness, so Ganesha softened the curse: it would shrink and grow each month. Some people say this is why the Moon has phases. It's also why, in many homes, people avoid looking at the Moon on the night of Ganesh Chaturthi.

A god for everyone

In 1893, the freedom fighter Bal Gangadhar Tilak helped turn Ganesh Chaturthi in Pune from a family festival into a huge public one, bringing people together. Today giant idols fill Mumbai's streets. Ganesha is honoured in Nepal, Thailand, Indonesia and even Japan, where a form of him is called Kangiten.

He is a god who likes sweets, rides a mouse and wins with brains, not muscle. His neighbour in many temples is the strong and loyal Hanuman.

Myth or fact?

Myth: There is only one true story of how Ganesha got his elephant head.

Truth: Different Puranas, the old story collections, give different versions. In the best-known one, Shiva cuts off the boy's head and replaces it with an elephant's. In another, the god Shani's gaze causes the loss. Different regions keep different tellings.
