Can planes fly on electricity?

In 2015 a plane powered only by sunlight landed in Ahmedabad and Varanasi on its way around the world, without burning a drop of fuel.

The short answer

Yes, planes can fly on electricity, but so far only small ones over short distances.

An electric plane uses batteries to power electric motors, which turn propellers. Electric motors are quiet, simple and cheap to run, and they don't give off exhaust. Several small two-seater electric planes already fly, and one, the Pipistrel Velis Electro from Slovenia, became the first fully electric plane to be type certified, in 2020, for pilot training.

The big problem is weight. Batteries store far less energy than the same weight of jet fuel. That makes it very hard to build an electric plane big enough to carry many people across a country, let alone an ocean.

Surprising but true

A kilogram of jet fuel holds roughly 40 to 50 times as much energy as a kilogram of today's best aircraft batteries.

The battery problem

A kilogram of jet fuel holds about 12,000 watt-hours of energy. A kilogram of good lithium battery holds about 250 to 300. That is a gap of roughly 40 to 50 times. Electric motors waste much less energy than jet engines, which narrows the gap, but fuel still comes out far ahead, by something like 15 to 20 times.

That matters hugely in the air, because a plane has to lift everything it carries. A jet airliner flying from Delhi to New York carries well over 100 tonnes of fuel. A battery with the same usable energy would weigh far too much to get off the ground.

There's another catch. A fuel-burning plane gets lighter as it burns fuel, so landing is easier. An electric plane lands carrying the full weight of its empty batteries.

Flying on sunlight

One of the most famous electric planes didn't need a charging point at all. Solar Impulse 2, a Swiss plane, had over 17,000 solar cells spread across wings wider than a jumbo jet's. By day they powered four motors and charged batteries. At night the batteries kept it flying.

Between 2015 and 2016 it flew right around the world in stages, carrying one pilot at a time. In March 2015 it stopped in Ahmedabad and Varanasi. One leg, from Japan to Hawaii, lasted almost five days and nights without landing.

The plane was very light and very slow, often around 70 km/h, slower than a car on a highway. It proved what sunlight could do, but no airline could carry passengers that way.

Small planes, short hops

Where electric planes make most sense is short trips with few passengers. Training flights usually last under an hour and return to the same airfield, which suits batteries well. Flying schools in Europe now use electric trainers, which are cheap to run and much quieter than old piston planes.

Other companies are testing small electric planes for short routes, such as hops between islands or nearby cities. In 2019 a Canadian airline, Harbour Air, flew a small seaplane with an electric motor in Vancouver. In 2022 an American nine-seat electric plane called Alice made its first flight.

Air taxis that take off straight up, sometimes called flying cars, also run on batteries and are designed for trips across a city.

Mixing fuel and electricity

For bigger planes, many engineers think the answer is a mix. Hybrid planes could use electric motors for take-off, when the most power is needed, and fuel engines for cruising. Others are testing hydrogen, which can power fuel cells that make electricity on board.

Flying produces a few percent of the gases warming the planet, and that share is growing as more people fly. That is why governments and companies are spending money on cleaner aircraft to slow climate change. For now, though, if you board a plane from Mumbai to London, it will run on kerosene for years to come.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Electric planes can't fly yet. They are only an idea.

Truth: Small electric planes are already flying, training pilots and making short hops, especially in Europe. What doesn't exist yet is a battery-powered airliner that can carry hundreds of people a long way.

Try this

Make two paper planes the same way. Tape a coin to the nose of one. Throw both the same way several times and measure how far each flies. The extra weight is like a heavy battery, and it shows why designers fight for every gram.
