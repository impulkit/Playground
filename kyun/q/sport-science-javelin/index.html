How does a javelin fly so far?

In 1984 an athlete threw a javelin so far that officials redesigned it, because the next throw might have landed on runners at the other end of the stadium.

The short answer

A javelin flies far for three reasons: it's thrown very fast, at the right angle, and its long, thin shape lets it glide a little. Top men release the spear at around 28 to 30 metres a second, roughly 100 km/h. To get that speed, they sprint up a runway, then slam on the brakes with their front leg. Their whole body's momentum then whips through the throwing arm, like the crack of a whip.

Once in the air, a javelin isn't just falling. It behaves a bit like a narrow aeroplane wing. Air flowing past it gives it a little lift, keeping it up a little longer. Throwers have to get everything right: the speed, the angle of release and the angle of the tip.

Surprising but true

A top javelin thrower releases the spear at around 100 km/h, about as fast as a car on a highway.

Run, block, whip

The run-up is usually about 30 metres. In the last few strides, the thrower turns sideways and pulls the javelin back to full stretch. The final step is a hard block with the front leg, which stops the hips. Energy then travels upwards: hips, chest, shoulder, elbow, wrist and finally fingers, each moving faster than the last.

This chain is the same one cricket fast bowlers and baseball pitchers use. It puts huge strain on elbows and shoulders, which is why javelin throwers often fight injuries.

Why not 45 degrees?

For a ball thrown from the ground with no air, 45 degrees gives the longest throw. A javelin is different. It's released from about two metres up, above the thrower's head, which favours a lower angle. It also glides, and a flatter flight uses that better.

Most elite throws leave the hand at about 32 to 36 degrees. The tip should point almost the same way the javelin is moving. If the nose points too high, air pushes it up and it stalls, dropping short.

The throw that changed the javelin

In 1984 Uwe Hohn of East Germany threw the old men's javelin 104.80 metres. It was too far for safety. Stadiums have a running track wrapped around the field, and javelins were starting to fly dangerously far. Old javelins also often landed flat, so judges argued about where they had hit.

In 1986 the rules changed. The new men's javelin had its balance point moved about 4 centimetres towards the front. Now the nose dips earlier, the javelin flies a shorter, steeper path, and it sticks into the ground tip first. Distances dropped sharply overnight. The women's javelin was redesigned in the same way in 1999. Hohn's 104.80 metres will stand for ever as the record for the old design.

India's golden arm

On 7 August 2021, at the delayed Tokyo Olympics, Neeraj Chopra threw 87.58 metres to win gold. It was independent India's first Olympic gold in athletics. India's athletics federation now marks 7 August as National Javelin Day. Chopra also won the World Championships in 2023 and Olympic silver in Paris in 2024, where Pakistan's Arshad Nadeem won gold with an Olympic record of 92.97 metres. In May 2025, Chopra finally crossed 90 metres, with 90.23 metres in Doha.

A very old sport

People have thrown spears for hunting for hundreds of thousands of years. The ancient Greeks made javelin throwing part of the pentathlon at the ancient Olympics. They wrapped a leather strap around the shaft and held its loop with their fingers. As the spear left the hand, the strap unwound and set it spinning, which kept it steady, like the spin on a rifle bullet.

And the strangest part? Changing the balance point of a javelin works just like moving a paperclip on a paper plane: shift the weight forward and the nose dives sooner.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The javelin flies like a stone, so only arm strength matters.

Truth: A javelin is shaped to glide. The angle it's released at, the angle of its tip and where its weight sits all change the distance. Technique matters as much as muscle.

Try this

Make paper darts and test launch angles. Throw the same dart five times each at a low angle, a medium angle and a high angle, keeping your throw equally strong. Measure each landing spot with a tape or your steps. Which angle wins? Then try moving a paperclip from the nose to the middle, and watch how its flight changes.
