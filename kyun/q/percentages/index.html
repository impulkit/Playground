What does percent mean?

If a price goes up by 50 per cent and then down by 50 per cent, it ends up cheaper than when it started.

The short answer

Per cent means "out of a hundred". The words come from Latin, per centum, meaning "for each hundred". So 25 per cent, written 25%, means 25 out of every 100, which is the same as a quarter.

Percentages make it easy to compare things of different sizes. Scoring 45 out of 50 in one test and 72 out of 80 in another is hard to compare at a glance. Turn both into percentages and you get 90% each. That is why percentages appear on exam results, phone batteries, sale signs, weather forecasts and food packets.

Surprising but true

The % sign grew out of a shorthand that Italian merchants wrote for "per cento" in the 1400s, which slowly squashed into a little circle, a line and another circle.

Out of a hundred

A percentage is really a fraction with 100 on the bottom. 50% is 50/100, which is a half. 10% is 10/100, or a tenth. 1% is one hundredth.

To find a percentage of something, change it to a fraction or a decimal and multiply. Ten per cent is easiest: just divide by ten. So 10% of 350 rupees is 35 rupees. Twenty per cent is double that, and 5% is half of it. With these tricks, you can work out most discounts in your head.

Old taxes and new symbols

The idea is very old. The Roman emperor Augustus put a tax of one hundredth on goods sold at auction. Merchants in medieval Italy worked out interest on loans "per cento", for every hundred.

When writing by hand, they shortened "per cento" in different ways. Historians have traced how one shorthand, a "p" followed by a small looped mark, slowly changed over a couple of centuries into the sign % that we use now. Taxes and trade have been driving maths for a long time.

Why do people pay taxes?In 1696, England taxed windows. People bricked theirs up to pay less, and you can still see the blank walls on old houses.

Percentages all around

Your phone battery shows a percentage so you can tell at a glance how much charge is left. A weather forecast that says a 70% chance of rain means that on days with conditions like these, it rained about 7 times out of 10.

Percentages describe our planet and our bodies too. About 71% of the Earth's surface is covered by ocean. Roughly 60% of an adult's body weight is water, which is one reason you need to drink water every day. In India, board exam results are given as percentages. Many American schools turn percentages into letter grades, so 90% and above often earns an A.

Tricky percentages

Percentages can fool people. Suppose a shirt costs 1,000 rupees. The price rises by 10%, to 1,100. Then it falls by 10%. Ten per cent of 1,100 is 110, so the new price is 990 rupees. The rise and fall were the same percentage, but they were taken from different amounts.

Advertisers know that big percentages sound impressive. "Now with 50% more!" might mean a chocolate bar went from 20 grams to 30 grams. Newspapers sometimes report that a risk has "doubled", a 100% increase, when it went from 1 in a million to 2 in a million. Both are true, but the second way sounds much scarier.

More than 100 per cent

Can you have more than 100%? Yes, when you compare with a starting amount. If a tree grows from 2 metres to 5 metres, its height has increased by 150%. But you cannot score 110% in a test marked out of 100, and nobody can really give "110 per cent effort". There is only so much of you to give.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If something goes up by 50 per cent and then down by 50 per cent, it is back where it started.

Truth: The second change is worked out from a bigger number. If 100 rupees rises by 50 per cent, it becomes 150. A 50 per cent fall from 150 takes off 75, leaving 75 rupees, which is less than you began with.

Try this

Ask 20 people a simple question, like "Do you prefer mangoes or bananas?" Count the answers. Since 20 times 5 is 100, multiply each count by 5 to get a percentage. If 13 people chose mangoes, that is 65 per cent. Draw your results as a bar chart.
