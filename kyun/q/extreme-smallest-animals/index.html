What are the smallest animals?

A frog that fits on your fingernail with room to spare, a bird lighter than a one-rupee coin, and a wasp smaller than some single cells.

The short answer

The smallest animal with a backbone is a frog. The Brazilian flea toad, found in the forests of north-east Brazil, has males about 7 mm long. A whole adult frog could sit on your little fingernail with space left over.

Without a backbone, animals get smaller still. Fairyflies are wasps so tiny that some could pass through the eye of a needle. The male of one species is about 0.14 mm long, smaller than a full stop on this page. Every one of these creatures has a brain, muscles, a gut and a way to have babies, all packed into a speck.

Surprising but true

The males of one fairyfly wasp are about 0.14 mm long, smaller than some amoebas, which are single cells.

Tiny frogs and fish

In 2012, scientists in Papua New Guinea found a frog about 7.7 mm long and crowned it the smallest vertebrate. Then in 2024, careful measurements of the Brazilian flea toad, described in 2011, came in even smaller.

India has miniature frogs too. In 2017, researchers described several new night frogs from the Western Ghats, some about 12 mm long, small enough to sit on a thumbnail. They had been missed for years because their calls sounded like insects.

Some of the smallest fish, from the peat swamps of Sumatra, are under 1 cm long when fully grown.

The smallest bird and mammals

The bee hummingbird lives only in Cuba. It is about 5 to 6 cm long, including the beak, and weighs about 2 grams. That is less than a one-rupee coin. Its nest is the size of a bottle cap and its eggs are as small as coffee beans.

For mammals, there are two winners. The Etruscan shrew, found from Europe to South and Southeast Asia, weighs about 2 grams. The bumblebee bat of Thailand and Myanmar has a body about 3 cm long, and its wings stretch only about 17 cm from tip to tip. Both must eat almost all the time, because small bodies lose heat very fast.

The Etruscan shrew's heart can beat over 1,000 times a minute, and it may eat close to twice its own weight in insects every day. If a ten-year-old ate like that, they would need to munch through dozens of kilograms of food between breakfast and bedtime.

A chameleon on a fingertip

In 2021, scientists described Brookesia nana, a chameleon from northern Madagascar. The male's body is about 13.5 mm long, and about 22 mm with its tail. It could balance on the tip of your finger.

It lives in leaf litter on the forest floor and climbs low stems at night to sleep. Unlike larger chameleons, it is a dull brown and doesn't put on colourful displays.

Smaller than a cell

Fairyflies lay their eggs inside the eggs of other insects. Their larvae grow up in that tiny space. To save room, one kind has nerve cells that lose their nucleus, the cell's control centre, as the wasp grows up. Their wings are thin stalks fringed with hairs, like feathers.

At that size, air feels thick, a bit like honey does to us. The hairs let them "row" through it.

Smaller still are myxozoans, parasites distantly related to jellyfish. Some are made of just a few cells and are invisible without a microscope.

Why be small?

Being tiny lets an animal live in places others can't: inside an egg, between leaves, in a crack of bark. Small animals need little food overall, though they eat a lot for their size.

But small has limits. A body can only shrink so far before there is no room for a heart, eyes and eggs. Shrews and hummingbirds sit near that edge, burning energy so fast that a few hours without food can kill them. The flip side of tiny is mighty: some of the smallest creatures are also the strongest for their size.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The smallest animals are all insects.

Truth: Some of the tiniest are insects, but frogs, fish, chameleons, shrews, bats and birds all have miniature record holders. Even smaller are some parasite animals related to jellyfish, made of just a few cells.
