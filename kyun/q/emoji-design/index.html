Who designs emojis?

One of the first famous sets of emoji was drawn in Japan in 1999, as 176 tiny pictures each just 12 dots wide and 12 dots tall.

The short answer

Emojis are designed in two stages. First, a group called the Unicode Consortium decides which emojis exist. It gives each one a code number and a short description, such as "face with tears of joy". Anyone can send in a proposal for a new emoji, but only a small number are accepted each year.

Then companies such as Apple, Google, Samsung and Microsoft hire artists to draw their own version of each one. That's why the same emoji can look slightly different on different phones. The code says what the emoji is. The company decides exactly how it looks.

Surprising but true

The word "emoji" has nothing to do with "emotion". It comes from Japanese "e" (picture) and "moji" (character).

Tiny pictures from Japan

In the late 1990s, Japanese mobile phones could send short messages, but text alone could seem cold or unclear. In 1999, a designer named Shigetaka Kurita, working for the phone company NTT Docomo, drew a set of 176 small symbols: weather, hearts, faces, food and transport. Each was squeezed into a grid of just 12 by 12 pixels.

His set was not the very first. Another Japanese company had released a smaller set two years earlier. But Kurita's emojis became famous, and in 2016 the Museum of Modern Art in New York added them to its collection. The cute, simple style fits a wider love of cartoon images in Japan.

Making them work everywhere

At first, emojis from one Japanese phone company often showed up as nonsense on another company's phones. The fix came from Unicode, a system that gives every letter and symbol in the world's writing systems its own number. In 2010, hundreds of emojis were added to Unicode, so phones and computers everywhere could agree on them.

When Apple added an emoji keyboard to iPhones worldwide in 2011, emojis took off. By 2015, Oxford Dictionaries chose the "face with tears of joy" emoji as its Word of the Year. There are now almost 4,000 emojis, counting different skin tones and combinations.

How to get a new emoji

Anyone can write a proposal to Unicode. It must show that many people would use the emoji, that it can be recognised at a small size, and that it isn't already covered by another one. Proposals usually include sample drawings and evidence, such as how often people search for the word online.

A committee reviews them, and the process can take a year or two. Many ideas are turned down. Some that succeed come from ordinary people. A teenager in Saudi Arabia helped push for the hijab emoji, which arrived in 2017.

Emojis from India

For years, emoji lists had little that felt Indian. That changed with the 2019 update, which added a diya lamp, a sari, an auto rickshaw and a Hindu temple. The Sikh khanda symbol was approved in 2022 and reached most phones in 2023. Emojis for many Indian festivals and foods are still missing, and people continue to send proposals.

Skin tone choices arrived in 2015. They let people pick from five shades for faces and hands, based on a scale doctors use to describe skin types.

Same code, different face

Because companies draw their own versions, small differences can change the meaning. One "grimacing face" once looked like a nervous grin on one phone and an angry snarl on another. In 2016, Apple changed its pistol emoji into a green water gun, and other companies followed over the next few years.

Emoji designers work a bit like people who design fonts. They must keep the meaning clear while giving each set a style of its own. It is strange to think that, like ancient hieroglyphs, small pictures have become part of how billions of people write every day.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The word "emoji" comes from "emotion".

Truth: It is a Japanese word made from "e", meaning picture, and "moji", meaning written character. The similarity to "emotion" is a coincidence, though a handy one.
