What does a farmer's day look like in India?

Before most of India wakes up, millions of farmers are already milking cows, checking fields and watching the sky for rain.

The short answer

A farmer's day in India usually starts before sunrise. There are cows or buffaloes to milk, animals to feed and fields to check. Then come the jobs of the season: ploughing, sowing, weeding, watering or harvesting. Many farmers work until dark, with a break in the hottest part of the afternoon.

India has around 14 crore farm holdings, most of them small. About 43% of India's workers earn their living mainly from farming. Their lives follow the monsoon, the market and the soil, and no two days are quite the same.

Surprising but true

An average farm in the USA is roughly 185 hectares. An average farm in India is about 1 hectare, a little bigger than a football field.

Before the sun comes up

Many farming families are up by 4 or 5 in the morning. Milking comes first, because cows need milking at regular times. The milk is carried to a village dairy cooperative or sold to neighbours. Thanks to the White Revolution, India now produces more milk than any other country, much of it from small farms with just two or three animals.

Next, someone takes the animals out to graze or brings in fodder. Children often help before school, carrying grass or water. Breakfast might be roti with a bit of jaggery and chai, or leftover rice with curd.

In the hot months, many farmers work early and late and rest at midday, when temperatures in north India can pass 45 °C. Evening brings more chores: bringing animals home, cutting fodder for tomorrow and checking the water channels.

The rhythm of the seasons

Indian farming has two main seasons. Kharif crops, such as rice, cotton and maize, are sown when the monsoon rains arrive in June or July and harvested in autumn. Rabi crops, such as wheat, mustard and gram, are sown in winter and harvested in spring.

When the rains come late or too heavy, whole harvests can fail. That is why farmers watch the sky and listen closely to weather forecasts. Many now get alerts and advice on their phones in their own language, including tips on when to spray, sow or harvest.

How did India grow enough food?In the 1960s India depended on shiploads of foreign wheat. Within about a decade it was growing enough to feed itself.

Small farms, big work

The average Indian farm is about 1 hectare, a little bigger than a football field. In the USA, the average farm is close to 185 hectares, more than 170 times bigger. American farms use giant tractors and combine harvesters. Many Indian farmers still use bullocks, small tractors hired by the hour, or their own hands.

On a small farm, every family member often helps. Women do a large share of the work, from sowing and weeding to harvesting and caring for animals, yet they own only a small share of farmland. Some families also run a small side business, like poultry, beekeeping or a shop, to earn extra money.

Selling and saving

When the harvest is in, farmers take crops to a local market called a mandi. Prices can rise or fall from week to week. For some crops, the government buys at a fixed minimum support price, which gives farmers a safety net.

Farming is risky. Debts, droughts, pests and falling prices can hit hard. The government gives eligible farmers ₹6,000 a year through a scheme called PM-KISAN, and offers crop insurance. Farmers also form cooperatives so they can buy seeds and sell crops together.

Modern tools are spreading. Some farmers use drones to spray crops, soil tests to choose fertiliser and apps to check prices before going to market. These are part of smart farming. India learnt in the 1960s how better seeds and water could change everything, during the Green Revolution. And the food on your plate tonight, from rice to dal, has travelled a long road from a farmer's field, a journey told in farm to plate.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Farmers only work at harvest time and rest the rest of the year.

Truth: Farm work never really stops. Between ploughing, sowing, weeding, watering, caring for animals, fixing tools and selling crops, most farmers work every day of the year.

Try this

Plant a few methi or mustard seeds in a cup of soil and keep a "farm diary". Each day, write the date, the weather, how much water you gave and what changed. After two weeks, read it back and notice how much a farmer must watch and wait.
