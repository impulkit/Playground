Why does India's east coast get more cyclones?

The Bay of Bengal is smaller than the Arabian Sea, yet over the past century it has brewed about four cyclones for every one in the west.

The short answer

A cyclone is a giant spinning storm that feeds on warm sea water. India has seas on both sides, but the Bay of Bengal, to the east, produces far more cyclones than the Arabian Sea to the west. Over the past century, the ratio has been roughly four to one.

Several things make the Bay a better cyclone factory. Its surface water is usually warmer. Rivers like the Ganga and Brahmaputra pour in huge amounts of fresh water, which floats on top and keeps the warm layer from mixing with cold water below. And the Bay receives leftover storms that drift in from the Pacific Ocean.

The shape of the coast makes things worse. The Bay narrows towards Bangladesh and West Bengal, and its shallow edges can pile sea water into a dangerous wall called a storm surge.

Surprising but true

Some cyclones that hit Odisha or Andhra Pradesh began as storms over the Pacific Ocean, crossed Southeast Asia, and grew again over the Bay of Bengal.

How a cyclone is born

Cyclones and hurricanes are the same kind of storm with different names. They start over warm sea water, usually above about 26 °C. Warm, moist air rises, cools and forms towering clouds. As the water vapour turns to rain, it releases heat, which makes the air rise even faster.

Air rushes in at the bottom to replace it. Because the Earth spins, that incoming air starts to turn. In the Northern Hemisphere it spirals anticlockwise. When the winds reach about 62 km/h, the India Meteorological Department calls the system a cyclonic storm and gives it a name.

Why the Bay wins

The Arabian Sea is cooler for several reasons. Strong winds near Somalia and Oman stir up cold water from the deep. Dry, dusty air blows off the deserts of Arabia. Both make storms harder to grow.

The Bay of Bengal is different. A thin layer of fresh river water sits on top of the salty sea. It traps warmth near the surface, so a passing storm finds a deep supply of heat to feed on. The Bay also gets help from the east. Typhoons from the South China Sea sometimes cross Myanmar or Thailand, weaken over land, then strengthen again once they reach the warm water.

India's cyclone seasons come either side of the monsoon: April to May, and October to December.

From disaster to preparedness

The Bay has seen terrible storms. In 1970, the Bhola cyclone struck what is now Bangladesh and killed hundreds of thousands of people. In 1999, a super cyclone hit Odisha, killing about 10,000.

After that, Odisha built hundreds of cyclone shelters and trained volunteers in villages. Satellites and better computer models made weather forecasts far more accurate. When Cyclone Phailin arrived in 2013, around a million people were moved to safety. Fewer than 50 are thought to have died. In 2019, the state did it again for Cyclone Fani.

Nature helps too. Thick mangrove forests, like the Sundarbans, slow down storm surges and protect villages behind them.

Is the Arabian Sea catching up?

In recent years, the Arabian Sea has produced some strong cyclones, such as Tauktae in 2021 and Biparjoy in 2023. Its surface waters have been warming, and several studies have found that strong cyclones there have become more frequent. Scientists are still working out how much of this is part of a long-term trend and how much is natural variation.

The names come from a list agreed by 13 countries around the region, including India, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Thailand and Oman. Each country suggests names, and they are used in turn.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The eye of a cyclone means the storm is over.

Truth: The eye is a calm patch in the middle. When it passes, the strongest winds return from the other side, often blowing in the opposite direction. People should stay sheltered until officials say it is safe.

Try this

Fill a large round bowl with water and stir it fast in one direction with a spoon, then take the spoon out. Drop in a few bits of paper or tea leaves and watch them spiral towards the centre. A cyclone's winds spiral inwards around a calm eye in a similar way.
