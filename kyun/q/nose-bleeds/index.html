Why do noses bleed?

Just inside the front of your nose, several tiny arteries meet in one small patch, and that single spot causes most nosebleeds.

The short answer

The inside of your nose is lined with thin, damp skin called mucous membrane. Just under it lies a thick web of tiny blood vessels. Their job is to warm and wet the air you breathe before it reaches your lungs, so they sit very close to the surface.

That closeness is the problem. When the lining dries out and cracks, gets scratched by a fingernail or takes a knock, a vessel can break. Because the vessels are packed so tightly, even a small break can drip for several minutes.

Most nosebleeds look far worse than they are. A teaspoon of blood spread across a tissue seems like a lot. Almost all nosebleeds stop on their own, or with a few minutes of steady pressure.

Surprising but true

Tipping your head back during a nosebleed is the wrong move. The blood simply runs down your throat instead.

One busy corner

Doctors have two names for the trouble spot. The American surgeon James Little described it in 1879, and the German doctor Wilhelm Kiesselbach in 1884, so it is called Little's area or Kiesselbach's area. It sits on the septum, the wall of cartilage that splits your nose into two tunnels, about a fingertip inside. Here, branches of several arteries meet and join up.

About nine out of ten nosebleeds start in this patch. They are called anterior, or front, nosebleeds. They usually come from one nostril and are easy to stop, because you can squeeze the spot from outside.

A much rarer kind starts deep at the back of the nose. These are more common in older adults, can bleed heavily and need a doctor.

Dry air and busy fingers

Nosebleeds have seasons. In the northern USA they rise in winter, when heaters run all day and indoor air turns dry. In north India, the hot, dusty weeks before the monsoon do the same, and so can a night spent under a chilly air conditioner. Dry lining cracks like mud at the edge of a pond in May.

Then there's picking. It's the most common cause in children, and doctors are not shy about saying so. A fingernail scrapes the lining, a scab forms, it itches, and it gets picked again. Colds and allergies add to the damage, because sneezing and hard blowing keep the lining sore.

Children between about 2 and 10 get nosebleeds most often. Many simply grow out of them.

What to do when it happens

Sit down and lean slightly forward. Pinch the soft part of your nose, below the bony bridge, firmly with a thumb and finger. Breathe through your mouth and keep pinching for a full 10 minutes by the clock, without letting go to check. Spit out any blood that reaches your mouth.

Many people were taught to tip their head back. That only sends blood down the throat, which can make you cough or feel sick. Lying flat does the same. A cold pack on the bridge of the nose may help a little, but pressure does the real work.

Once it stops, try not to blow your nose or bend over for a few hours, so the fresh clot can hold. The first aid rules are short enough to learn in a minute.

When to tell an adult

Most nosebleeds are a nuisance and nothing more. But tell a parent or doctor if one lasts longer than 20 minutes of steady pinching, or follows a hard blow to the head. The same goes for nosebleeds that keep coming back week after week. A doctor can seal a troublesome vessel in a few minutes, a treatment called cautery.

Noses on mountains

Climbers know nosebleeds well. High up, the air is thin, cold and very dry, and every breath pulls water out of the nose. Trekkers heading to Everest Base Camp, at over 5,000 metres, often carry a small pot of petroleum jelly to dab inside their nostrils. At home, a bowl of water near a heater does a similar job. For most nosebleeds the fix is that simple: keep the lining damp and leave it alone.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You should tilt your head back to stop a nosebleed.

Truth: Leaning back sends blood down the throat, which can make you cough or feel sick. Doctors say to sit up, lean slightly forward and pinch the soft part of the nose.

Try this

Breathe out gently through your nose onto a cold steel spoon or a mirror. The mist that forms is water your nose and airways added to the air. Now breathe the same way on a hot, dry afternoon, or next to a heater, and notice how much drier your nose feels afterwards.
