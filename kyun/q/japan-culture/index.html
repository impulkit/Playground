What is daily life like in Japan?

In most Japanese schools there are no cleaners. After lunch, the students sweep, mop and scrub their own classrooms, corridors and even toilets.

The short answer

Daily life in Japan mixes old customs with very modern technology. A child might take a high-speed train to visit grandparents, then take off their shoes at the door of a house with paper sliding screens. People bow to greet each other, say thank you before and after meals, and keep public places tidy.

About 12.4 crore people live in Japan, a chain of islands in the Pacific. Most live in crowded cities on the coast, because much of the land is steep mountains. Sharing small spaces has shaped many habits, from quiet trains to careful sorting of rubbish.

Surprising but true

Curry and rice is one of the most popular home meals in Japan. It arrived about 150 years ago, by way of Britain, not straight from India.

Shoes off, please

Almost every Japanese home has a genkan, a small entrance area slightly lower than the rest of the house. You take off your outdoor shoes there and step up into the house in socks or slippers. Many schools, temples and even some restaurants do the same.

Some rooms have floors of tatami, thick mats of woven straw. Rooms are often measured by how many tatami mats fit, rather than in square metres. In many homes, people sleep on futons, thick mattresses that are folded away in a cupboard during the day.

School days

Japanese children often walk to school in small groups, even from age six, wearing yellow hats so drivers can see them. Many carry a sturdy leather backpack called a randoseru. At school, students take turns serving lunch and clean their own classrooms each day, a habit called o-soji.

Children learn three writing systems: hiragana and katakana, which stand for sounds, and kanji, characters that came from China. By the end of school, students learn about 2,000 kanji. Many also learn origami, folding paper into cranes and other shapes.

Itadakimasu

Before eating, people say "itadakimasu", roughly "I humbly receive". Meals are eaten with chopsticks, and a typical home meal might be rice, miso soup, fish and pickled vegetables. Parents often pack a bento, a lunch box with small portions neatly arranged.

Curry rice, or kare raisu, is a family favourite. It came to Japan in the late 1800s through the British, who had learned about curry in India, and the Japanese navy made it a regular meal. Japanese curry is thick, mild and slightly sweet.

Faith, seasons and festivals

Most Japanese people follow customs from both Shinto, Japan's own religion with its many shrines, and Buddhism. Buddhism came from India by way of China and Korea. In 752, an Indian monk named Bodhisena helped lead the ceremony to open the giant Buddha statue at Todai-ji temple in Nara.

The seasons matter a great deal. In spring, families picnic under blooming cherry trees, a custom called hanami. On Children's Day, 5 May, families fly carp-shaped streamers called koinobori. Carp swim upstream, so they stand for strength. Poets even capture the seasons in haiku, very short poems.

What is a haiku?The most famous haiku in the world is about a frog jumping into a pond. That's it, and people have been talking about it for more than 300 years.

Hot baths and an ageing country

Japanese people love a deep hot bath, called ofuro. You wash and rinse yourself outside the tub first, then soak in clean hot water. Japan's volcanoes heat thousands of natural hot springs, called onsen.

Japan has one of the longest life expectancies in the world. Almost three in ten people are aged 65 or over, the highest share of any large country. This brings challenges for ageing families and for finding enough workers, and it is one reason Japan builds robots to help care for older people.

Myth or fact?

Myth: People in Japan eat sushi every day.

Truth: Sushi is popular, but many families save it for treats and special occasions. Everyday meals are more likely to be rice, miso soup, grilled fish, noodles or curry rice.

Try this

Write a haiku. A traditional Japanese haiku has three lines of 5, 7 and 5 syllables, and often mentions a season. Try one about something you saw today, counting the syllables on your fingers. The pattern is five, then seven, then five.
