What is Miami like?

In this sunny Florida city you hear more Spanish than English, and the sea sometimes bubbles up through the streets.

The short answer

Miami is a big city near the southern tip of Florida, the sunny peninsula that pokes down from the United States towards the Caribbean. It is warm all year, with a wet, stormy summer and a dry winter that brings tourists from colder states.

Miami is also one of the most Latin American cities in the USA. Since the 1960s, people from Cuba, Venezuela, Colombia, Haiti and many other countries have made it home, and most people in the city speak Spanish at home. It is famous for its beaches, its pastel buildings and its swampy wild neighbour, the Everglades. And because it sits so low, it is one of the American cities most worried about the rising sea.

Surprising but true

Parts of Miami Beach flood on bright, cloudless days, because very high tides push seawater up through drains and the holey limestone under the streets.

A city built by a railway

In the early 1890s, Miami was little more than a trading post. A widow named Julia Tuttle owned land by the Miami River and wanted a city there. She persuaded the businessman Henry Flagler to bring his railway south. The trains arrived in 1896 and the city was founded the same year. Julia Tuttle is often called the mother of Miami.

The railway brought builders, hotels and holidaymakers. In the 1920s and 1930s, Miami Beach filled with Art Deco hotels painted pink, mint and lemon, with curved corners and porthole windows that look a little like ships.

Little Havana

In 1959, a revolution in Cuba brought Fidel Castro to power. Over the next years, hundreds of thousands of Cubans fled to the USA, and many settled in Miami, only about 370 km from Havana across the sea.

The neighbourhood where many settled became known as Little Havana. On its main street, Calle Ocho, old men play dominoes in a park, cafés sell tiny cups of very sweet, very strong coffee, and cigar makers roll tobacco by hand. Later waves of immigrants from across Latin America and the Caribbean have made Miami a meeting point for the whole region.

Why is the sea level rising?The sea has risen by more than 20 cm since 1880, and it is rising faster now than at any time in thousands of years.

Living with the sea

Much of Miami is only about 2 metres above sea level. During the highest tides of the year, called king tides, water can bubble out of drains and cover streets even when no rain falls. In Miami Beach, the city has raised some roads and installed pumps to push the water back out.

The rock under South Florida makes this hard. It is limestone, full of tiny holes, so seawater soaks through the ground rather than over a wall. Scientists expect sea level rise to make these floods more frequent in the coming decades. Miami also sits in the path of hurricanes. Hurricane Andrew in 1992 flattened tens of thousands of homes south of the city.

The river of grass

West of Miami lies the Everglades, a vast, shallow sheet of water flowing south through sawgrass taller than a child. Everglades National Park was set up in 1947 to protect part of it. The US National Park Service says it is the only place in the world where alligators and crocodiles live side by side.

Canals and farms have drained much of the old Everglades, and engineers are now trying to restore the natural flow. Miami gets most of its drinking water from the ground beneath this wetland, so keeping it wet matters for more than wading birds. It keeps the taps running.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Everglades is just a big, still swamp.

Truth: The Everglades is a very wide, very shallow river. Its water flows slowly south through sawgrass towards the sea, which is why the writer Marjory Stoneman Douglas called it a "river of grass".

Try this

Put a dry kitchen sponge in a shallow dish and stand a toy house on top. Slowly pour water into the dish, not onto the sponge. Watch how the water creeps up from below, just as seawater rises through the rock under Miami.
