What is the Golden Temple?

The holiest place of the Sikhs has four doors, one on each side, so that anyone from any direction can walk in.

The short answer

The Golden Temple is the holiest gurdwara, or Sikh place of worship, in the world. Its proper name is Harmandir Sahib, "the temple of God". It stands in the middle of a large pool in the city of Amritsar, in Punjab, and its upper floors are covered in gold.

Inside, the Sikh holy book, the Guru Granth Sahib, is read aloud and sung from early morning until night. The temple is open to people of every religion. Millions visit each year. Many also come to eat at its langar, a free kitchen where anyone can sit down for a meal.

Surprising but true

The temple's free kitchen serves tens of thousands of meals every day, cooked and served mostly by volunteers.

A pool and a temple

The fourth Sikh Guru, Guru Ram Das, began digging a pool here in the 1570s. It was called Amrit Sarovar, "the pool of nectar", and the town around it became Amritsar.

His son, Guru Arjan, the fifth Guru, built the temple in the middle of the pool. Tradition says the Sufi saint Mian Mir of Lahore was invited to lay the foundation stone in 1588, though historians debate whether that happened. In 1604 Guru Arjan placed the first version of the Sikh scripture, the Adi Granth, inside.

The temple sits lower than the land around it, so visitors walk down steps to reach it. This is often explained as a lesson in humility.

Four doors, one welcome

The temple has a door on each of its four sides. The Sikh Gurus wanted to show that God is everywhere and welcomes everyone, from every direction.

A long marble causeway leads across the water to the temple itself. Visitors cover their heads, take off their shoes and wash their feet before entering. Inside, singers called ragis perform hymns from the Guru Granth Sahib, accompanied by harmonium and tabla.

How it got its gold

The temple was attacked and badly damaged several times in the 1700s, including by the Afghan ruler Ahmad Shah Durrani in 1762. Sikhs rebuilt it each time.

In the early 1800s, Maharaja Ranjit Singh, who ruled Punjab, paid for the upper floors to be covered with gilded copper and decorated with marble and fine inlay work. That's when visitors began calling it the Golden Temple. The gold is still cleaned and restored by skilled craftspeople.

The great free kitchen

Next to the temple is the langar hall. Every day, people sit together on long mats on the floor and are served dal, roti, a vegetable dish and kheer. Everyone eats the same food, whether rich or poor, Sikh or not.

The kitchen serves tens of thousands of meals a day, and on festival days the numbers are often reported to pass a lakh. Most of the work is done by volunteers, who chop vegetables, roll rotis, serve food and wash plates. Machines help make rotis now, but huge numbers are still rolled by hand. The idea of langar comes from Guru Nanak, the first Guru, who taught that sharing food breaks down barriers between people.

A place of history and of pain

The complex also includes the Akal Takht, the highest seat of Sikh authority. In 1984, during an Indian Army operation against armed militants who had taken position inside the complex, the Akal Takht was badly damaged and many people died. The event still causes deep pain for many Sikhs, and the Akal Takht was later rebuilt.

A day at the temple

Every morning before dawn, the Guru Granth Sahib is carried from its resting place to the temple in a decorated palanquin. Late at night it is carried back. Between those times, the hymns never stop.

At night the temple's gold reflects in the still water of the pool, and the Punjabi cooks next door are already getting tomorrow's dal ready.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You have to be a Sikh to enter the Golden Temple.

Truth: The temple is open to everyone, of any faith or none. Visitors only need to cover their heads, remove their shoes, wash their feet and follow simple rules like not smoking or drinking alcohol.

Try this

Hold a mini langar at home. Everyone sits on the floor in a row, whatever their age, and is served the same simple meal by someone else. Afterwards, everyone helps clean up. Talk about how it felt to eat side by side instead of at the head of a table.
