Why are South Indian films so big?

In Chennai and Hyderabad, fans have been known to pour milk over giant cutouts of their favourite stars on the morning a new film opens.

The short answer

"South Indian cinema" usually means films in four languages: Telugu, Tamil, Malayalam and Kannada. Each has its own industry, based mainly in Hyderabad, Chennai, Kochi and Bengaluru. Together they make hundreds of films every year, and their fans are some of the most devoted anywhere.

In the last decade, these films have also crossed borders. Dubbed versions of hits such as Baahubali, KGF and RRR filled cinemas across north India and abroad. In 2023 the song "Naatu Naatu" from the Telugu film RRR won the Oscar for Best Original Song, the first for a song from an Indian film.

Surprising but true

Three famous South Indian film stars became chief ministers of their states, M. G. Ramachandran, N. T. Rama Rao and J. Jayalalithaa.

Four industries, four styles

Telugu films are known for grand action, big sets and huge dance numbers. Tamil cinema mixes mass entertainers with sharp, small films about everyday life. Malayalam cinema, from Kerala, is often praised for realistic stories made on small budgets. Kannada cinema grew fast after the KGF films became national hits.

Each industry has a nickname built on Hollywood. Tollywood is Telugu, Kollywood comes from Kodambakkam in Chennai, Mollywood is Malayalam, and Sandalwood is Kannada, after the sandalwood trees of Karnataka.

From Madras to Hyderabad

For much of the 1900s, Madras, now Chennai, was the film capital of the south. Telugu, Tamil, Kannada and Malayalam films were all made in its studios, such as AVM and Vijaya Vauhini. Composers like A. R. Rahman later began their careers there.

Over time, each industry moved closer to its own audience. Telugu filmmaking shifted to Hyderabad, where Ramoji Film City opened in 1996. It covers about 800 hectares, more than a thousand football pitches put together, and holds the Guinness World Record for the largest film studio complex.

Why is Tamil called one of the world's oldest living languages?A Tamil schoolchild today can read lines of poetry composed about 2,000 years ago. Few languages on Earth can say that.

Stars, fans and politics

In many southern states, a film star can be treated almost like a hero from legend. Fan clubs organise celebrations, decorate cinemas and put up cutouts several storeys high. On a big release day, some cinemas open at dawn.

That fame has carried some stars into politics. M. G. Ramachandran, a Tamil film hero, became Chief Minister of Tamil Nadu in 1977. N. T. Rama Rao, who often played gods in Telugu films, became Chief Minister of Andhra Pradesh in 1983. J. Jayalalithaa, a well-known actress, later led Tamil Nadu several times.

Why the whole country watches now

For years, Hindi-speaking audiences mostly saw South Indian films as dubbed versions on TV. Those dubbed films gathered loyal fans in villages and towns of the north. When big-budget Telugu and Kannada films were released in Hindi in cinemas, a ready audience was waiting. Dubbing did much of the work.

Streaming services helped too. Viewers anywhere could watch Malayalam thrillers or Tamil dramas with subtitles at home. Some film writers also say South Indian films kept faith with grand, emotional stories at a time when many Hindi films were aiming at city audiences.

Not just in India

Southern films have long had fans outside India. Tamil films are popular in Sri Lanka, Malaysia and Singapore, where many Tamil speakers live. A 1995 Tamil film, Muthu, starring Rajinikanth, became a surprise hit in Japan.

Telugu and Tamil films now open on the same day in the USA, the UK, the Gulf and Australia, often with special early shows for fans. Some of those fans have never been to India, but they know every word of the songs.

Myth or fact?

Myth: South Indian cinema is one film industry.

Truth: It is several separate industries working in different languages, each with its own stars, studios and audiences. A Malayalam film and a Telugu film can be as different as a Spanish film and a French one.

Try this

Watch the same short song from a South Indian film in two languages, if your family has a streaming service. Listen for what changes and what stays the same. Notice whether the singer, the lyrics or the actors' lips give the dubbing away.
