What are nebulae?

On a dark winter night you can see a nebula with your bare eyes. It is the fuzzy middle "star" in the sword of Orion, a glowing cloud about 1,300 light-years away where new stars are being born.

The short answer

A nebula is a giant cloud of gas and dust floating in space. Most of the gas is hydrogen and helium, the two lightest elements, with tiny specks of dust made of carbon, silicon and ice. Some nebulae are so big that light takes dozens of years to cross them.

Nebulae come in two main stories. Some are places where stars are born: gravity slowly pulls the gas into clumps that heat up and start to shine. Others are what is left when stars die, puffed off gently or blasted out in a supernova. The word nebula is Latin for mist, and the plural is nebulae.

Surprising but true

Although nebulae look thick and solid in photos, many are thinner than the best vacuums people can make in laboratories on Earth.

Glowing, shining and dark

Astronomers sort nebulae by how they look. Emission nebulae glow on their own. Hot young stars inside them flood the gas with energy, and the hydrogen gives off a deep pinkish-red light. The Orion Nebula is one.

Reflection nebulae don't glow. Their dust simply bounces back light from nearby stars, and because tiny grains scatter blue light best, they usually look blue. Dark nebulae are thick dust clouds that block the light behind them. The Horsehead Nebula in Orion is a dark cloud shaped like a chess knight against a glowing background.

Many nebulae mix all three. The Orion Nebula has glowing gas, bluish dust and dark lanes of thick cloud, all lit up by a tight group of young stars at its heart called the Trapezium.

Nurseries for stars

Stars form in the coldest, densest corners of nebulae. A clump of gas starts to shrink under its own gravity, gets hotter in the middle and eventually starts fusing hydrogen. Our own Sun formed this way about 4.6 billion years ago, from a cloud that has long since drifted away.

The most famous picture of a star nursery is the Pillars of Creation, three tall columns of gas and dust in the Eagle Nebula. The Hubble Space Telescope photographed them in 1995. In 2022 the Webb telescope looked again in infrared light, which passes through dust, and revealed baby stars glowing inside the pillars. The tallest pillar is about four light-years high.

Clouds left by dying stars

When a star like our Sun runs low on fuel, it swells up and gently puffs off its outer layers. The glowing shell that spreads out is called a planetary nebula. The name is a mistake: in the 1780s the astronomer William Herschel thought their round, greenish discs looked like planets through his telescope.

Bigger stars die violently. In 1054, astronomers in China recorded a "guest star" bright enough to see in daylight for weeks. Where it shone, telescopes now show the Crab Nebula, a tangled cloud of gas still rushing outwards. Elements made inside dead stars, such as carbon, oxygen and iron, spread through space this way and end up in new stars, new planets and even in you.

Not quite what they seem

For a long time, any fuzzy patch in the sky was called a nebula. In the 1700s, the French comet-hunter Charles Messier listed about a hundred of them so he wouldn't mistake them for comets. Some turned out to be clusters of stars, and some were whole other galaxies, like Andromeda, once called the Andromeda Nebula.

Nebulae look solid but are astonishingly thin. A thimbleful of air on Earth holds more atoms than a box of nebula many times bigger than your house. They glow brightly only because they are so enormous. The light you see from the Orion Nebula set out about 1,300 years ago, long before the Mughal emperors. Light from the Eagle Nebula left before the pyramids of Egypt were built.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The colours in nebula photos are exactly what you would see through a telescope.

Truth: Through a backyard telescope most nebulae look like pale grey or greenish smudges, because our eyes see few colours in dim light. Space photos collect light for a long time, and many add colours to show different gases or light our eyes can't see.

Try this

Shine a torch through a glass of water with a few drops of milk stirred in. From the side, the glass glows bluish. Look straight through it at the torch and the light looks orange. Dust in space scatters starlight in a similar way, which is why some nebulae look blue.
