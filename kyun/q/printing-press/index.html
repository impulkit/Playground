How did the printing press change the world?

Before the printing press, a single book could take a scribe months to copy by hand. Within fifty years of Gutenberg, Europe had printed millions.

The short answer

The printing press made books cheap and quick to copy. Before it, most books in Europe were copied by hand, letter by letter, often by monks. A single Bible could take a scribe more than a year.

Around 1450, in the German city of Mainz, Johannes Gutenberg put together a system that changed this. He cast small metal letters that could be arranged into pages, inked, printed and then rearranged for the next page. He used an oil-based ink that stuck well to metal and a screw press to push paper down evenly. A small team could now print hundreds of copies of a page in a day.

Surprising but true

Printing with movable type was invented in China about 400 years before Gutenberg, and the oldest surviving book printed with metal type comes from Korea.

Letters you can move

Gutenberg's key idea was a mould for casting letters quickly from a mix of lead, tin and another metal called antimony. Every "a" came out the same size and shape. Workers called compositors picked letters from cases and lined them up backwards, so they would print the right way round.

The famous Gutenberg Bible was finished around 1455. About 180 copies were printed, some on paper and some on animal skin called vellum. Fewer than 50 survive today, and one of them is at the Library of Congress in Washington, D.C.

Who invented paper?The first people to make paper weren't humans. Wasps have been chewing wood into paper nests for millions of years.

Printing in Asia first

Gutenberg was far from the first printer. In China, people printed whole pages from carved wooden blocks more than a thousand years ago. The oldest printed book with a date on it is a Buddhist text called the Diamond Sutra, from 868 CE. It was found in a cave at Dunhuang and is now in the British Library in London.

In the 1040s, a Chinese craftsman named Bi Sheng made movable type out of baked clay. Korea later cast metal type. A Korean Buddhist book called the Jikji, printed in 1377, is the oldest surviving book printed with movable metal type. In ancient China, though, movable type was less handy than in Europe, because Chinese writing uses thousands of different characters. Many Chinese printers stuck with carved blocks.

Ideas on the move

In Europe, with an alphabet of only a few dozen letters, movable type spread fast. By 1500, printing shops were working in more than two hundred towns and cities, and millions of books had been printed.

Cheaper books meant more people learned to read. Scientists could share drawings and results exactly, instead of relying on copies full of copying mistakes. Ideas of the Renaissance moved quickly between countries. In 1517, Martin Luther's arguments against the Church were printed and spread across Germany in weeks, helping to start the Protestant Reformation. Pamphlets, maps and later newspapers followed. Spelling began to settle down too, because printers wanted the same words to look the same in every copy. Gutenberg himself made little money. He lost control of his workshop to his business partner after a court case over unpaid loans.

The press reaches India and America

In 1556, Jesuit missionaries set up India's first printing press in Goa. For centuries afterwards, printing in Indian scripts was slow to develop, partly because those scripts have many joined letters that need extra pieces of type. Tamil was one of the first Indian languages printed in its own script, in the 1570s.

In North America, the first press in the English colonies began work in Cambridge, Massachusetts, in 1639. A year later it printed the Bay Psalm Book, the first book printed in what became the United States.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Johannes Gutenberg invented printing.

Truth: Printing from carved wooden blocks was used in China for centuries before him, and Bi Sheng made movable clay type in China in the 1040s. Gutenberg's achievement was a system of metal type, oil-based ink and a press that made printing fast and cheap in Europe.

Try this

Use a set of letter stamps, or ask an adult to cut a few letters out of thick card for you. Arrange the letters backwards to spell your name, ink them with a stamp pad and press onto paper. Notice that you must set the letters in mirror order. Printers did this for every page.
