How do spelling bee champions spell?

In 2026, a 14-year-old from California won the Scripps National Spelling Bee after spelling 32 words correctly in 90 seconds, a new record.

The short answer

Top spellers don't memorise every word. They learn how words are built. English has borrowed words from Latin, Greek, French, German, Hindi, Japanese and hundreds of other languages, and each language brings its own spelling habits. A champion who hears an unfamiliar word asks for its meaning, its language of origin and an example sentence. Then they use what they know about roots and patterns to work it out.

That is why spellers at the Scripps National Spelling Bee in the USA are allowed to ask those questions on stage. Behind every winning word are years of practice, thousands of flashcards and a lot of patient parents and coaches.

Surprising but true

In 2019, the Scripps National Spelling Bee ran out of hard enough words and ended with eight co-champions.

Spelling like a detective

Say the word sounds like "fy-LOG-uh-nee" and comes from Greek. A good speller knows Greek words often use "ph" for f and "y" for a short i. They might know the root phylo, meaning a tribe or group, as in "phylum". So they try phylogeny, and they are right.

Champions study Latin roots like aqua, water, and scribe, write. They learn that French words often hide silent letters, like the "t" in "ballet", and that Dutch words can use "oo" and "aa". English spelling looks chaotic, but much of it makes sense once you know where a word came from.

The big bee

The Scripps National Spelling Bee began in 1925 with nine spellers. The first champion, Frank Neuhauser, 11, won with "gladiolus", a kind of flower. Today, more than 200 spellers aged 15 and under reach the finals near Washington DC, after winning school and regional bees. The bee is shown live on American television.

The final rounds can be strange. In 2026, two spellers were still level when time ran out, so the bee used a spell-off. Shrey Parikh, 14, from California, spelled 32 words in 90 seconds, beating the old record. His winning word was "cashaw", a type of plant.

Eight champions at once

In 2019, eight spellers kept getting every word right deep into the night. The organisers had planned only so many rounds and felt the words left in their list wouldn't challenge them. So all eight were named co-champions, nicknamed the Octochamps. Since then, the bee has added tie-breaking spell-offs.

The bee has also added vocabulary questions, where spellers choose a word's meaning, so knowing what words mean now matters as much as spelling them.

Why so many Indian-American winners

In 1985, Balu Natarajan, from Illinois, became the first Indian-American to win. Since 1999, children of Indian heritage have won most of the titles, including the 2025 champion, Faizan Zaki from Texas.

People suggest several reasons. Many Indian-American families put strong value on study and competitions. A network of smaller bees, run by community groups, lets children practise for years. Older siblings coach younger ones. And success inspires others: each champion makes the next generation believe they can win too.

Spelling in India

India has its own spelling bees too, in English and in Indian languages. Some schools run them in Hindi, where the spelling of a word is usually much closer to its sound than in English.

English spelling is hard partly because English borrows so freely, including from India. Words like "bungalow", "shampoo", "jungle" and "pyjamas" all came from Indian languages. To spell those at a bee, it helps to know their history, which is one more reason the stories behind borrowed words are worth learning. And the reason English sticks to messy old spellings at all lies in its history in dictionaries, which froze many of them hundreds of years ago.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Spelling bee champions just memorise the whole dictionary.

Truth: No one can memorise the hundreds of thousands of words in a big dictionary. Champions learn many lists, but mostly they learn roots, word origins and spelling patterns, so they can work out words they have never seen.

Try this

Make a spelling detective chart. Pick five tricky words, such as rhythm, pharaoh, bouquet, pizza and kindergarten. Look each one up in a dictionary to find its language of origin, and write down any pattern you notice, like "rh" or "ph" in Greek words. Then test a friend and give them the origin as a clue.
