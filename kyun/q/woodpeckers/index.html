Why don't woodpeckers get headaches?

A woodpecker can hammer a tree up to about 20 times a second. For years, people thought its skull worked like a built-in crash helmet. A 2022 study said no.

The short answer

Woodpeckers smash their beaks into wood thousands of times a day, yet seem to stay perfectly healthy. For a long time, scientists thought their heads must be packed with shock absorbers, such as spongy bone and a tongue that wraps around the skull like a seatbelt.

In 2022, a team led by Sam Van Wassenbergh in Belgium filmed woodpeckers with high-speed cameras and came to a surprising conclusion. Their heads don't cushion the blows much at all. The skull works like a stiff hammer. The main reason woodpeckers don't hurt themselves may be that their brains are tiny, and small brains can take much bigger jolts than ours before getting injured.

Surprising but true

In some woodpeckers, the tongue is so long that, when pulled back, it wraps around the back of the skull and is anchored near the nostrils.

Why peck at all?

Woodpeckers peck for three main reasons. The first is food. They chip into bark to reach beetle larvae and ants, then hook them out with their tongues.

The second is housing. Most woodpeckers carve a new nest hole every year, often in dead or rotting wood. Old holes become homes for owls, parrots, squirrels and other animals that can't dig their own nests.

The third is talking. Woodpeckers drum fast bursts on hollow branches to claim territory and attract mates. Some discover that a metal gutter or a lamppost makes a much louder noise, to the annoyance of anyone trying to sleep.

The helmet idea, and the hammer

Woodpecker skulls do have some special features. Parts of the bone are spongy. The brain fits tightly in the skull, leaving little room to slosh about. Engineers studied these features, hoping to design better helmets and protective cases for electronics.

Van Wassenbergh's team tracked the beak and the eye of black, pileated and great spotted woodpeckers frame by frame. If the head absorbed shock, the eye would keep moving slightly after the beak stopped. It didn't. Beak and head stopped together, like the head of a hammer. A padded head, the scientists argued, would waste energy and make pecking feeble.

They then calculated the forces on a woodpecker's brain. For its small size, they found it stayed below the level likely to cause concussion, unless the bird pecked far harder than it normally does.

Still a puzzle

Not everyone thinks the story is finished. In 2018, researchers in the USA looked inside preserved woodpecker brains and found build-ups of a protein called tau. In people, too much tau is linked to brain damage from repeated head hits, like in some boxers and footballers. But the scientists noted that the protein might play a protective role in woodpeckers. Nobody has yet tested whether the birds suffer any harm from it, so the question is open.

Built for the job

Everything else about a woodpecker helps it work on tree trunks. Its feet have two toes pointing forward and two back, for a strong grip. Its stiff tail feathers press against the bark like a third leg. A see-through inner eyelid snaps shut just before each strike, keeping flying wood chips out of its eyes.

The tongue is the strangest part. In many species it is long, sticky or barbed at the tip, and when pulled in it curls around the back of the skull. In some species it loops right over the top of the head and is anchored near the nostrils.

India has over 30 kinds of woodpecker. The black-rumped flameback, with a golden back and red crest, is common even in city parks. In the USA, acorn woodpeckers drill thousands of small holes in a single tree and push an acorn into each one, storing food for winter.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A woodpecker's skull is full of spongy padding that soaks up every blow, like a crash helmet.

Truth: That was the common explanation for years, and it inspired some helmet designs. A 2022 study of high-speed videos found the head hardly cushions the impact at all. Small brain size seems to matter more.

Try this

Tap a pencil gently on a wooden table, first holding it loosely, then holding it firmly. Listen to which tap sounds sharper and feel which one stings your fingers more. A firm grip passes more force into the wood, which is the trick a woodpecker uses.
