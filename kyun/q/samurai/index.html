Who were the samurai?

For centuries Japan's samurai were famous as sword fighters, but the first samurai were mostly archers on horseback.

The short answer

The samurai were Japan's warrior class. They first rose to power in the 12th century and remained an elite group until the 1870s.

At first, samurai were armed servants of noble families and fought mainly as mounted archers. In 1185, after a long war between two great clans, Minamoto no Yoritomo set up a military government led by a shogun, a supreme general. Emperors stayed on the throne, but for nearly 700 years shoguns and samurai held the real power. Samurai served lords called daimyo and were expected to be loyal, brave and disciplined. In peaceful times they became officials, teachers and scholars. In the 1870s, Japan's new government abolished their special status.

Surprising but true

Japan's most famous haiku poet, Matsuo Basho, was born into a low-ranking samurai family.

The way of the bow and horse

Early samurai prized archery above all. Their long bows were taller than a person, made of bamboo and wood glued in layers. A warrior was expected to shoot while riding at full gallop.

Their armour was made of small lacquered plates laced together with silk cords, colourful and flexible. Swords were weapons for close fighting. The famous curved katana, made from carefully folded and hardened steel, became the symbol of the samurai later, and craftsmen still forge them today.

Storms against the Mongols

In 1274 and 1281, the Mongol rulers of China sent huge fleets to invade Japan. Samurai fought them on the beaches of Kyushu.

Both times, storms wrecked many Mongol ships. Japanese people called these storms kamikaze, "divine winds". The battles also showed that Mongol tactics, with group attacks and exploding bombs, were very different from samurai styles of fighting.

Guns and castles

In the 1500s, Japan broke into a long civil war, with lords fighting each other. In 1543, Portuguese sailors landed on the island of Tanegashima with matchlock guns. Japanese smiths copied them quickly.

At the Battle of Nagashino in 1575, the warlord Oda Nobunaga's gunners fired in turns from behind wooden fences and cut down a charging cavalry army. Lords built tall castles with white plaster walls and stone bases. The most famous is Himeji Castle, now a UNESCO World Heritage Site, nicknamed the White Heron for its graceful shape. Japan's castles had a lot in common with the castles of Europe, though they looked very different.

Warriors in a time of peace

In 1603, Tokugawa Ieyasu became shogun and brought the wars to an end. His family ruled for over 250 years from Edo, the city now called Tokyo.

Samurai still wore two swords as a mark of rank, but few ever fought a battle. They worked as government officials, ran schools and wrote books. The poet Matsuo Basho, master of the haiku, was born into a samurai family. Many samurai practised Zen meditation, part of a Buddhist tradition that reached Japan from India by way of China.

The end of the samurai

In 1853, American warships arrived and forced Japan to open its ports. Japan then modernised fast. In 1868, power returned to the emperor.

The new government built a modern army of ordinary conscripts and abolished the special status of samurai. In 1876, wearing swords in public was banned. Some samurai rebelled, but they were defeated. The idea of a samurai code called bushido became famous worldwide around 1900, largely through a book in English by Nitobe Inazo, long after the samurai themselves had gone.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Samurai always fought with swords, in honourable one-on-one duels.

Truth: Early samurai fought mainly with bows from horseback, and later armies used spears and guns in large formations. Battles could be just as messy and tricky as anywhere else.
