How does a jet engine work?

Inside a jet engine, the metal blades spin in gas hotter than the temperature at which that metal would melt. Tiny holes of cooling air keep them alive.

The short answer

A jet engine pulls air in at the front, squeezes it, burns fuel in it, and blasts it out of the back. Engineers sum it up as "suck, squeeze, bang, blow".

The hot gas rushing backwards pushes the engine, and the plane, forwards. This follows a rule described by Isaac Newton: every push has an equal push in the opposite direction. On the way out, the hot gas also spins a set of blades called a turbine, which drives the compressor at the front. So the engine keeps itself running for as long as it has fuel.

Surprising but true

The fan at the front of the biggest airliner engines is about 3.4 metres across, wider than an autorickshaw is long.

Suck, squeeze, bang, blow

Suck. At the front, a large fan draws in air, up to more than a tonne of it every second at take-off in the biggest engines.

Squeeze. The air passes through a compressor, rows of small spinning blades that squash it tighter and tighter. Squeezed air gets hot, the way a bicycle pump warms up.

Bang. In the combustion chamber, fuel is sprayed into the squeezed air and burns steadily. It isn't really a bang but a continuous roaring flame.

Blow. The hot gas expands and rushes out of the back. On the way, it spins the turbine, which is joined by a shaft to the compressor and fan.

Big fans and quiet engines

The first jet engines pushed all their air through the flame and out of a narrow nozzle. They were loud and thirsty. Most airliners now use turbofans. A giant fan at the front pushes most of the air around the outside of the hot core instead.

Moving a large amount of air fairly slowly turns out to be more efficient than moving a little air very fast. On the biggest engines, the fan is about 3.4 metres across. Most of the push comes from this cool stream of air, which is also quieter.

Hotter than the metal can stand

The gas hitting the first turbine blades can be hotter than the melting point of the metal they are made of. Engineers keep them working with three tricks. The blades are grown as single metal crystals, which makes them very strong. They are coated with a thin layer of heat-resistant ceramic. And cooler air is piped through hollow channels inside each blade and out through tiny holes, wrapping it in a thin protective film.

Each blade is about the size of your hand, but at full power it can feel a pull equal to the weight of a heavy truck as it spins.

How does a rocket work?A rocket doesn't push against the air. It throws hot gas out of the back so hard that the gas pushes it forward.

Two inventors, one idea

In 1930, Frank Whittle, a young officer in Britain's Royal Air Force, patented a design for a jet engine. Few people were interested, and he struggled for money for years. Meanwhile, in Germany, Hans von Ohain was working on the same idea independently. His engine powered the first jet aeroplane flight on 27 August 1939, just days before the Second World War began in Europe. Whittle's engine flew in 1941.

After the war, jets changed travel. They could fly higher, above most bad weather, and far faster than propeller planes. A trip from Mumbai to London, which once took days with many stops, now takes about ten hours.

Jets from India

India's air force flies jets including the Tejas, a light fighter designed in India. Its engine is American-made, but Indian scientists have spent many years developing their own jet engine, called Kaveri. Building jet engines is one of the hardest jobs in engineering, and only a handful of countries can make the most powerful ones.

Today, the jets that carry passengers cruise at about 900 km/h, around 10 to 12 km up, often riding fast winds called jet streams.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A jet engine moves the plane by pushing against the air behind it.

Truth: The engine throws a huge amount of air backwards very fast, and the air pushes back on the engine, driving it forward. It doesn't need anything behind it to push against. Rockets work the same way, which is why they can fly in empty space.

Try this

Make a balloon jet. Thread a long piece of string through a drinking straw and tie the string tightly between two chairs. Blow up a balloon, hold its neck shut, and tape it to the straw. Let go. Air rushing out of the back pushes the balloon forward along the string, the same push a jet engine makes.
