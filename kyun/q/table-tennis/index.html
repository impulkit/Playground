How fast is table tennis?

A hard table tennis smash can cross the whole table in about a tenth of a second, less time than it takes you to blink.

The short answer

Table tennis is one of the fastest ball games there is, not because the ball flies furthest, but because the distances are so short. A top player's smash can travel at more than 100 km/h. At that speed it crosses the 2.74 m table in about a tenth of a second.

That's quicker than most people can even react. So elite players don't just react. They read their opponent's body, bat angle and swing, and guess where the ball is going before it's struck. Add heavy spin that makes the ball curve, dip and kick sideways, and you get a game where points can be won and lost in under a second.

Surprising but true

In 1971, an American player hopped onto the Chinese team's bus at a tournament in Japan. The friendship that followed helped lead to the US president's visit to China a year later, a moment known as ping-pong diplomacy.

Faster than your brain

Seeing something and moving in response usually takes a person about a fifth of a second. Your eyes must send a signal to your brain, your brain must decide, and your muscles must move.

Top players get around this with practice. They've watched thousands of strokes, so they recognise the start of a swing and its likely result. Studies of expert players show their eyes often jump ahead to where the ball will land rather than following it all the way. Their quick reflexes matter, but their ability to predict matters more.

What is a reflex?When you touch something hot, your hand jerks away before your brain has even worked out that it hurts.

The secret of spin

Table tennis bats are covered with sticky rubber over a thin layer of sponge. When a player brushes the ball instead of hitting it flat, the rubber grips and sets the ball spinning, often dozens of times a second.

Spin changes everything. Topspin, where the top of the ball rolls forward, makes it dive down onto the table and leap forward off it. Backspin makes it float, then check and drop. Sidespin curves it through the air. The ball's spin also makes it jump off the opponent's bat in an unexpected direction. That's why players learn to read the brush of the bat, not just watch the ball. Grip and friction between ball and rubber are what make all of this possible.

From dinner tables to the Olympics

The game began in England in the 1880s as an after-dinner game. Families used books as a net, a cork or rubber ball, and sometimes the lids of cigar boxes as bats. A company named it "Ping-Pong", after the sound, and the name stuck in many countries.

Celluloid balls arrived around 1900, and rubber-covered bats soon after. Since 2014, balls have been made of a different plastic, which is slightly slower. Table tennis became an Olympic sport in 1988, and China has dominated it, winning all five gold medals at the 2024 Paris Games.

A bus ride that changed history

In 1971, during the Cold War, the USA and China had not had friendly contact for over 20 years. At the World Championships in Nagoya, Japan, American player Glenn Cowan missed his team bus and climbed onto the Chinese team's bus instead. China's star, Zhuang Zedong, chatted with him and gave him a silk picture as a gift.

Within days, China invited the US team to visit. It was a small, careful signal. In 1972, President Richard Nixon flew to Beijing, opening a new chapter between the two countries. People still call it ping-pong diplomacy.

India at the table

India has a long history in the sport, especially in Bengal and Tamil Nadu. Achanta Sharath Kamal won many Commonwealth Games medals over two decades, and Manika Batra won women's singles gold at the 2018 Commonwealth Games. In 2024, India's women's team reached the Olympic quarter-finals for the first time. As with badminton, a cheap table and two bats in a school or club hall are enough to start.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Table tennis is a gentle game that doesn't take much fitness.

Truth: At the top level, players make hundreds of quick sideways moves, lunges and explosive strokes in a match. They train strength, speed and stamina like other athletes, and their reactions are among the fastest in sport.

Try this

Hold a ruler at its top end and let a friend hold their finger and thumb open at the bottom, without touching it. Drop it without warning and see how far it falls before they catch it. The shorter the distance, the faster their reaction. Most people need about 15–20 cm, which is longer than a smash gives a player.
