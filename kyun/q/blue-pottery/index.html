What is Jaipur blue pottery?

Jaipur's famous blue pottery is made without any clay at all, which makes it one of the odder kinds of pottery in the world.

The short answer

Jaipur blue pottery is a kind of glazed ceramic made in and around Jaipur, in Rajasthan. You can spot it by its white background painted with patterns in deep cobalt blue, lighter turquoise and sometimes green or yellow. Flowers, vines, birds and geometric patterns are common.

Its strangest feature is hidden inside. Most pottery is made from clay. Blue pottery is shaped from a dough of powdered quartz stone, powdered glass, fuller's earth (Multani mitti), borax and plant gum. That recipe gives it a glassy white body, a bit like porcelain, though it is far more fragile.

Surprising but true

Blue pottery dough is made from powdered quartz stone and glass, held together with gum and fuller's earth, rather than ordinary potter's clay.

A craft that travelled

The look of blue pottery came from far away. In Persia and Central Asia, potters had made blue-and-white glazed tiles and dishes for centuries, influenced in turn by Chinese porcelain. Traders and craftsmen carried these ideas along routes like the Silk Road.

In India, blue glazed tiles decorated tombs, mosques and palaces under the Delhi Sultans and the Mughals. How the craft settled in Jaipur is less certain. One popular account says Jaipur's 19th-century ruler, Sawai Ram Singh II, encouraged it and supported local craftsmen. Historians note that the evidence is patchy, so it is safest to say the craft grew in Jaipur during the 1800s.

How a pot is made

Making blue pottery is slow and careful. The quartz and glass powders are mixed with gum and water into a dough. Because it has no clay, it cannot easily be thrown on a wheel like an ordinary pot. Instead, the dough is pressed into moulds, then the pieces are joined and smoothed by hand.

Once dry, each piece is coated with a white layer and painted. Blue comes from cobalt oxide and green from copper oxide. Finally, a clear glaze is added and the pieces are fired in a kiln at a fairly low temperature.

Firing is the riskiest moment. Traditional kilns are heated with wood or charcoal, and the craftsmen watch carefully, because too much heat can crack the pieces or make the colours run. The kiln is then left to cool slowly before it is opened. A few broken pieces in each batch are normal, which is one reason good blue pottery is not cheap.

Rescued from fading away

By the middle of the 20th century, blue pottery had almost died out in Jaipur. Only a few craftsmen still knew how to make it.

In the 1950s and 1960s, people worked to bring it back. The painter Kripal Singh Shekhawat trained craftsmen and added new colours and designs. Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay, a freedom fighter famous for reviving Indian crafts, also gave her support. Slowly the workshops grew again.

Blue pottery today

Today, workshops in Jaipur and nearby villages make plates, vases, bowls, tiles, doorknobs and beads. Tourists buy them in markets around the city's old walls. Some workshops let visitors try painting a tile themselves, using the same cobalt blue that potters in Persia used centuries ago. Blue pottery of Jaipur has a GI tag, a label that ties a product to the place where it is traditionally made.

The pieces are beautiful but not very tough. They can chip if knocked, and many are made for display more than daily use. Potters face a trade-off. The same recipe that makes those blues glow like gemstones also makes the bowl easy to break.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Blue pottery is made of clay like any other pot, just painted blue.

Truth: Its body is a dough of quartz, glass and other materials, with almost no clay. It is fired at a low temperature, which keeps the colours bright but leaves the pieces delicate.

Try this

Mix two spoons of flour with a little salt and enough water to make a stiff dough. Shape a small bowl, then let it dry for a day or two. Notice how it cracks if it dries unevenly, one of the problems blue-pottery makers have to solve with their clay-free dough.
