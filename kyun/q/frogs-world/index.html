Why are frogs in trouble?

About two in every five kinds of amphibians on Earth are now at risk of dying out, more than for mammals or birds.

The short answer

Frogs are in trouble around the world. In 2023, a huge study by hundreds of scientists found that about 41% of all known kinds of amphibians are at risk of extinction. Amphibians are the group that includes frogs, toads, salamanders and newts.

Several things are hurting them at once. Ponds and forests are being drained and cleared. A deadly fungus has spread across continents. The climate is getting hotter and drier in many places. And pesticides and other chemicals wash into the water where frogs lay their eggs.

Frogs are easy to harm because they live two lives, first as tadpoles in water and then as adults on land. Damage either home, and they suffer.

Surprising but true

A purple frog from the Western Ghats spends almost its whole life underground and comes up only to breed in the monsoon. Scientists described it only in 2003.

Two lives, twice the risk

Most frogs lay jelly-covered eggs in water. These hatch into tadpoles, which breathe with gills and nibble on algae. Over weeks or months they grow legs, lose their tails and change into frogs. This change is called metamorphosis.

So a frog needs clean water to breed and damp land or trees to live in. If a pond is filled in to build houses, or a stream dries up, the frogs that depend on it can vanish.

Frogs also breathe partly through their thin, moist skin. That lets oxygen in, but it lets pollution in too. Scientists sometimes treat frogs as an early warning: when frogs disappear, something is often wrong with the water or the land.

A killer fungus

In the 1990s, scientists in Australia and Central America found frogs dying in clean, protected rainforests. The cause turned out to be a fungus called chytrid. It attacks the skin, stopping frogs from taking in water and salts properly.

Chytrid seems to have spread from East Asia, probably carried around the world by the trade in frogs as pets and food. A 2019 study linked it to the decline of about 500 amphibian species, with around 90 thought to have died out completely.

One victim is the Panamanian golden frog, a bright yellow frog that was a national symbol of Panama. It has not been seen in the wild for years. Some survive in zoos and breeding centres, where scientists hope one day to return them to the forest.

India's hidden frogs

India is a frog hotspot. The Western Ghats alone are home to well over 200 kinds of amphibians, most found nowhere else on Earth. New species are still being discovered almost every year.

Some are very unusual. The purple frog is fat and round with a pointy nose. It lives underground, eats termites and comes up only for a few days in the monsoon to breed, joining the rainy-season chorus of other frogs. Indian dancing frogs live by fast forest streams. Since the noise of rushing water drowns out their calls, the males wave their legs to show off to females.

Many of India's frogs live in small patches of forest. When those patches are cleared for farms, roads or plantations, a species can be wiped out before anyone has even named it.

How people are helping

Around the world, people are fighting back. Scientists keep backup populations of rare frogs in zoos, and test treatments for chytrid. Volunteers in the UK and USA help toads and salamanders cross busy roads on rainy spring nights.

In India, researchers and nature groups run frog-watching walks during the monsoon and encourage people to record frog calls. Schools build small ponds without fish, which give tadpoles a safe home. Protecting wetlands, streams and patches of forest is the best help of all, because frogs need both water and land to survive.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Touching a toad gives you warts.

Truth: Warts on people are caused by a virus that spreads between humans, not by toads. The bumps on a toad's skin are glands. Some toads make a poison that can irritate your eyes or mouth, so wash your hands after handling one, but it won't give you warts.

Try this

On a rainy evening, sit quietly with an adult near a garden, park or field and listen for frogs. Try to count how many different calls you hear, such as croaks, chirps, whistles or clicks. Each kind of frog has its own call. If you can, record a few seconds on a phone to compare on another night.
