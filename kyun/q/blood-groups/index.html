What are blood groups?

In 1952, doctors in Mumbai found a blood group so rare that people who have it can receive blood only from others who share it.

The short answer

Everyone's blood is red, but not everyone's blood is the same. The surface of each red blood cell carries tiny markers made of sugars and proteins. Scientists call them antigens. Which markers you have decides your blood group.

The main system has four groups. Group A cells carry the A marker, group B the B marker, group AB carries both, and group O carries neither. A second marker, called Rh, is either there (positive) or not (negative). So someone might be A positive or O negative.

These markers matter when blood is given from one person to another. If the wrong group is used, the body's defences can attack the new blood.

Surprising but true

The rare "Bombay" blood group was first found in Mumbai. It's found in roughly 1 in 10,000 people in India, and far fewer in Europe.

Why the wrong blood is dangerous

Your immune system learns early which markers belong to you. Your plasma already carries antibodies against the ABO markers you don't have. Someone with group A, for example, has antibodies against B.

If that person is given group B blood, the antibodies grab the new cells and make them clump together, which can make a patient very ill. So hospitals always test both the patient and the donor blood before a transfusion. Group O negative red cells have no A, B or Rh markers, so in an emergency they suit almost anyone.

The doctor who sorted blood

For centuries, doctors who tried to move blood from one person to another found that it sometimes helped and sometimes killed. Nobody knew why. In 1901, the Austrian scientist Karl Landsteiner mixed blood from his colleagues in his lab in Vienna. Some samples clumped, others didn't. He worked out the pattern, which became the ABO groups.

His discovery made safe blood transfusions possible and won him the Nobel Prize in 1930. In 1940, he and a colleague in the USA found the Rh factor.

The Bombay blood group

In 1952, Dr Y.M. Bhende and his colleagues in Mumbai, then called Bombay, came across blood that didn't fit. It looked like group O in normal tests, yet it clumped with ordinary O blood. These people lacked a basic marker, called H, that nearly everyone else has.

People with the Bombay group can receive blood only from other Bombay donors. It's found in roughly 1 in 10,000 people in India and around 1 in a million in Europe. Indian blood banks keep lists of Bombay donors who can be called in an emergency.

Inherited from your parents

You get your blood group from your parents, through your genes. Each parent passes on one of the A, B or O versions. Two parents with group A can even have a child with group O, if each passes on a hidden O.

Groups are spread differently around the world. In the USA, O positive is the most common. In India, B and O are both very common, and group B is far more common than in Europe. Rh negative blood is rarer in India than in the USA.

Animals have blood groups too

Dogs have at least a dozen blood groups of their own. Cats have three main ones, A, B and AB. Vets match blood for pets just as hospitals do for people. Some cities even have dog blood banks.

Why giving blood matters

Blood can't be made in a factory, so every transfusion depends on a donor. Patients after accidents, mothers during difficult births and children with blood illnesses such as thalassaemia may all need regular transfusions. Knowing your blood group is useful, and when you're old enough, giving blood is one of the simplest ways to help a stranger.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Your blood group decides your personality.

Truth: This idea is popular in Japan and South Korea, where it's often printed in magazines. Large scientific studies have found no real link between blood group and personality.

Try this

Make a blood-match chart. Draw four boxes labelled A, B, AB and O. Use red stickers or dots for the A marker and blue ones for the B marker, putting both on AB and none on O. Then work out which groups could safely give to which, remembering that a body attacks markers it doesn't already have.
