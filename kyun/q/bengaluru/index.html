How did Bengaluru become India's tech city?

Long before software, Bengaluru was building aeroplanes, rockets and scientists. That head start helped turn a garden town into India's Silicon Valley.

The short answer

Bengaluru, the capital of Karnataka, did not become a tech city by accident. In the 1900s it gathered science institutes, aircraft factories and India's space agency. These trained thousands of engineers. When computer companies began looking for skilled people in the 1980s, Bengaluru was ready.

Its cool climate, colleges and English-speaking workers helped too. Today the city is home to thousands of software firms and start-ups, and to well over a crore people.

Surprising but true

Lalbagh in the middle of Bengaluru sits on rock about 3,000 million years old, among the oldest on Earth.

A mud fort and a garden

In 1537, a chieftain named Kempe Gowda I built a mud fort and laid out markets around it. Later rulers Hyder Ali and his son Tipu Sultan started Lalbagh, a huge garden, in the 1760s. The British built a large army base here in 1809, which brought roads, churches and schools.

Lalbagh's glasshouse, built in 1889, still hosts flower shows. Under the garden lies a low hill of ancient rock about 3,000 million years old. The Geological Survey of India has declared it a National Geological Monument. With its parks and tree-lined roads, Bengaluru became known as the Garden City.

Scientists first

In 1909 the Indian Institute of Science opened, set up with money from the industrialist Jamsetji Tata. C. V. Raman worked here, and it trained generations of scientists. In 1940 an aircraft factory was founded, which became Hindustan Aeronautics Limited. After independence, defence and electronics companies settled in the city.

The Indian Space Research Organisation, ISRO, has its headquarters in Bengaluru. Satellites are built here, and the teams that guide ISRO's missions to the Moon and Mars work from the city.

The software boom

In 1985 the American chip company Texas Instruments opened a centre in Bengaluru, linked to the USA by satellite. It showed that work could be sent across the world over data links. Indian companies such as Infosys and Wipro grew fast. A planned industrial area called Electronic City filled with offices.

Engineers here write code for banks, airlines and phone companies across the world, and design computer chips. In the 2010s, the city also became a hub for start-ups, building apps for food delivery, travel and phone payments.

Why companies came

Software needs people more than factories. A company setting up in the 1990s needed thousands of engineers, cheap office space and reliable links to customers abroad. Bengaluru had engineering colleges all around it, a mild climate that made long days easier, and a reputation as a science city. When one big company arrived, others followed, and so did the engineers. Economists call this clustering: firms gather where the skilled workers are, and workers move to where the jobs are.

Growing pains

So much growth so quickly has brought problems. Traffic jams are famous, and a short drive can take an hour at rush time. Bengaluru once had hundreds of lakes, many dug centuries ago as tanks to store rainwater. Many have been built over or polluted. Bellandur Lake was in the news for clouds of toxic foam. Citizens' groups and the city are now working to clean up the lakes and wetlands that remain.

Many languages, one city

Kannada is the state's language, but people in Bengaluru speak Tamil, Telugu, Hindi, Malayalam, English and many more. Engineers from across India and abroad have made it one of India's most mixed cities, much like its neighbour Hyderabad. In 2014 its name officially changed from Bangalore back to Bengaluru, the name Kannada speakers had always used.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Bangalore got its name from a story about boiled beans.

Truth: A popular tale says a hungry king was given boiled beans (benda kaalu) by an old woman. But an inscription from around 890 CE, found near the city, already mentions a place called Bengaluru, so the name is older than the story.
