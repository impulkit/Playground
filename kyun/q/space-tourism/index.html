Can ordinary people go to space?

In 2001 an American businessman paid about $20 million for a week on the space station, and since then more than a hundred private passengers have followed him into space.

The short answer

Yes, but so far only if they are very rich, very lucky or both. More than a hundred private passengers have now been to space, most of them on short hops that go up past 100 km, float for a few minutes and come back down.

A smaller group have gone into orbit, circling Earth for days, and a few have stayed on the International Space Station. Tickets have cost anywhere from a few hundred thousand dollars to tens of millions. In 2026, the two main companies selling short trips were both paused or rebuilding, so the queue to space is growing.

Surprising but true

The first space tourist, Dennis Tito, had once worked as an engineer at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, then made his fortune in finance.

The first tourists

In April 2001, Dennis Tito rode a Russian Soyuz rocket to the space station and spent about a week there. NASA wasn't happy at first, but Russia needed the money. A handful of others followed over the next decade, each paying millions. They included Anousheh Ansari, an Iranian-American engineer who in 2006 became the first woman to fly as a paying space tourist.

The real change came when private companies started building their own spacecraft. Making rockets that land and fly again, instead of falling into the sea, cut costs hugely. That is the story of reusable rockets.

How do rockets land themselves?A rocket booster taller than a 12-storey building can fall back from the edge of space and land upright on a ship's deck about the size of a football field.

Hops to the edge

In July 2021, two companies flew their founders within nine days of each other. Virgin Galactic's rocket plane was dropped from a carrier aircraft and fired its engine. Blue Origin's New Shepard rocket launched straight up from West Texas, and its capsule floated down under parachutes.

New Shepard's passengers included the actor William Shatner, aged 90, and Ed Dwight, who had trained as an astronaut candidate in the 1960s and finally flew in 2024 at 90 too. In May 2024, Gopi Thotakura, a pilot from Andhra Pradesh, became the first Indian to go to space as a tourist. In January 2026, Blue Origin paused these flights for at least two years after carrying 98 people.

Going round and round

Orbit is much harder than a hop. To stay up you must travel sideways at about 28,000 km/h, fast enough to cross India from top to bottom in under 10 minutes. In 2021, SpaceX flew four people with no professional astronauts aboard on a three-day orbit called Inspiration4. In 2024, a private mission called Polaris Dawn included the first spacewalk by people who were not government astronauts.

India has a strong link too. In 2025, Indian Air Force pilot Shubhanshu Shukla flew to the space station on a private mission called Axiom-4, training for India's own Gaganyaan flights.

What it feels like

Passengers describe huge pressure pushing them into their seats during launch, about three times normal weight for orbital rockets. Then the engines cut out and everything floats, including pens, crumbs and stomach contents, so sick bags are standard kit. Many tourists say the strongest moment is seeing Earth's thin blue skin of air against black space.

Your body changes fast up there. Fluid moves into your head and your sense of balance gets confused, as in space and the body.

Will it get cheaper?

Probably, slowly. Air travel was once only for the rich too. Virgin Galactic planned to restart flights with new ships in early 2027, after a delay from late 2026, while early customers had paid $200,000 to $250,000 and newer tickets cost about $750,000. Even so, that is many lifetimes of savings for most families. A single ticket would buy a good house in most Indian cities, with change to spare.

The cheaper route may be national programmes. And if you think floating is the strangest part, wait until you see how astronauts sleep, eat and use a toilet aboard the space station.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Space is very far away, beyond the clouds and the air, thousands of kilometres up.

Truth: The usual edge of space is just 100 km above the ground. That is a little less than the distance from Mumbai to Pune, or from New York City to Philadelphia, except straight up.

Try this

Throw a soft ball straight up and watch it closely. At the very top it seems to hang still for a moment. A suborbital rocket does the same thing, only 100 km up, and that hanging moment is when passengers float. Count how long your ball's "float" lasts, then picture it stretched to four minutes.
