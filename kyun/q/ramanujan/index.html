Who was Srinivasa Ramanujan?

A clerk in Madras with almost no formal training in advanced maths mailed a letter full of formulas to Cambridge, and it changed his life.

The short answer

Srinivasa Ramanujan was an Indian mathematician who lived from 1887 to 1920. With little formal training, he discovered thousands of results about numbers, fractions and infinite sums. Some of his formulas were so unusual that experts at first could not tell how he found them.

In 1913 he wrote to the English mathematician G. H. Hardy at Cambridge University. Hardy invited him to England, and together they did important work. Ramanujan became one of the youngest Fellows of the Royal Society, Britain's top scientific academy. He died at just 32, but mathematicians are still exploring his notebooks today.

Surprising but true

Mathematicians are still proving formulas from Ramanujan's notebooks more than a hundred years after he wrote them down.

A boy and a book

Ramanujan was born in Erode in 1887 and grew up in Kumbakonam, a temple town in Tamil Nadu. His family was poor. His father worked as a clerk in a cloth shop.

At about 15 he got hold of a book called A Synopsis of Elementary Results in Pure and Applied Mathematics by G. S. Carr. It listed thousands of results with almost no explanations. Ramanujan worked through them all, then went far beyond. He filled notebooks with his own discoveries.

He won a scholarship to college but cared only about maths. He failed his other subjects, lost the scholarship and later failed again at another college. For years he had no degree and no job, and he kept working on slate and paper.

The letter

In 1912 he got a job as a clerk at the Madras Port Trust, in today's Chennai. Friends and officers there encouraged him to send his work abroad. In January 1913, he wrote to G. H. Hardy and enclosed pages packed with formulas.

Hardy at first suspected it might be a joke. Then he and his colleague J. E. Littlewood studied it. Some results were already known, some were wrong, and some were entirely new and astonishing. Hardy later wrote that a few of them must be true, because no one would have the imagination to invent them. He arranged for Ramanujan to come to Cambridge.

Cambridge and the war

Ramanujan sailed for England in March 1914. A few months later the First World War began. Food was rationed and Cambridge was cold and grey. Ramanujan was a strict vegetarian and cooked for himself, often struggling to get the foods he was used to.

Still, the next few years were his most productive. With Hardy he found a way to count partitions, the number of ways a number can be written as a sum. For example, 4 can be made five ways: 4, 3+1, 2+2, 2+1+1 and 1+1+1+1. The numbers grow astonishingly fast, and their formula is one of the great results of the 20th century. In 1918 he became a Fellow of the Royal Society and the first Indian Fellow of Trinity College.

The taxi and the lost notebook

Ramanujan fell seriously ill in England. Doctors at the time suspected tuberculosis. Some modern experts think it may have been a liver infection caught in India. Hardy once visited him in hospital in a taxi numbered 1729 and said it seemed a dull number. Ramanujan replied that it was very interesting: the smallest number that is the sum of two cubes in two different ways.

He returned to India in 1919 and died in Kumbakonam in April 1920. In his last year he wrote about new functions that are still being studied. In 1976 an American mathematician, George Andrews, found a bundle of his last pages in a library at Trinity College, now called the "lost notebook". Ramanujan's formulas for pi are used in some of the fastest computer calculations of its digits. Ramanujan said his ideas came to him as gifts from Namagiri, his family goddess. Since 2012, India celebrates his birthday as National Mathematics Day.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ramanujan never went to school or college.

Truth: He did well at school in Kumbakonam and won a scholarship to college, but he spent so much time on maths that he failed his other subjects and lost it. He taught himself advanced mathematics mostly alone, from a few books.

Try this

Check Ramanujan's taxi number for yourself. Work out 1 × 1 × 1 and 12 × 12 × 12 and add them. Then work out 9 × 9 × 9 and 10 × 10 × 10 and add those. Both should give 1729. Can you find any smaller number that is the sum of two cubes in even one way?
