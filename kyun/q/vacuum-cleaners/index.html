How does a vacuum cleaner suck up dust?

A vacuum cleaner doesn't really suck. It lowers the air pressure inside, and the air of the room pushes the dust in.

The short answer

Inside every vacuum cleaner is an electric motor that spins a fan very fast. The fan throws air out through the back of the machine. That leaves the air inside a little thinner than the air in the room. Scientists say the pressure inside is lower.

Air always flows from where the pressure is higher to where it is lower. So air from the room rushes in through the nozzle to fill the gap, and it carries dust, crumbs and hair with it. Before the air leaves through the back, it passes through a bag or a filter that catches the dirt.

So it isn't really sucking. The room's air is pushing the dirt in.

Surprising but true

One of the first powered vacuum cleaners, built in London in 1901, was so big it was pulled by horses and parked in the street while long hoses snaked in through the windows.

Pressure does the pushing

All around you, air is pressing on everything. You don't feel it because it presses equally from all sides. When one side has less pressure, things move. Wind is air moving from high pressure to low pressure across the countryside. A vacuum cleaner makes a tiny, fast wind into its nozzle.

Your lungs work the same way. When the muscle under your lungs pulls down, the space inside your chest gets bigger and the pressure drops. Air from outside rushes in. Nobody pulls it.

That's also why a narrow nozzle cleans better than a wide one. The same amount of air has to squeeze through a smaller opening, so it moves faster and grabs more dirt.

The horse-drawn vacuum

In 1901, an English engineer named Hubert Cecil Booth watched a demonstration of a machine that blew dust off train seats. Most of the dust just landed somewhere else. Booth thought sucking would be better. The story goes that he tested the idea by laying a handkerchief on a chair and sucking through it with his mouth. The cloth came away dirty.

Booth built a machine powered by an engine. It was so large it sat on a horse-drawn cart in the street, and long hoses went in through the windows of London homes. Some wealthy households reportedly invited friends round to watch. He was even hired to clean the carpets at Westminster Abbey before the coronation of King Edward VII in 1902.

From janitor to household name

In 1907, James Murray Spangler, a cleaner at a department store in Ohio, USA, got coughing fits from the dust stirred up by his carpet sweeper. He built a small electric vacuum from a fan motor, a pillowcase and a broom handle, and patented it in 1908. He sold the rights to a relative's husband, William Hoover. The company did so well that in Britain people still say "hoovering" for vacuuming.

Later, in the 1980s and 1990s, the British inventor James Dyson built a bagless vacuum that spins air in a cone, a cyclone, so dust is flung out to the sides. He said he built more than 5,000 prototypes before it worked well.

Why fine dust matters

Household dust is made of skin flakes, fibres, soil, pollen and tiny creatures called dust mites. For people with allergies or asthma, dust can cause sneezing, itchy eyes and wheezing.

Cheap filters let the finest dust blow straight out of the back and into the air. Better vacuums use HEPA filters, which trap tiny particles that a normal filter misses. Today many homes in India and the USA also have small round robot vacuums, which steer around furniture with sensors. They use exactly the same pressure trick as Booth's horse-drawn giant.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A vacuum cleaner makes a vacuum inside it, a space with no air at all.

Truth: The air inside is only a little thinner than the air in the room. That small difference is enough to make a strong rush of air into the nozzle. A true vacuum, like outer space, is something no household machine could make.

Try this

Drink through a straw and notice what you're doing. Your mouth makes a little space with lower pressure, and the air pressing on the drink's surface pushes the liquid up the straw. Now pinch a tiny hole in the straw and try again. It gets much harder, just like a vacuum cleaner with a leaky hose.
