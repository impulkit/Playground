Which birds can you spot in an Indian city?

The loud "ku-oo" that wakes you in summer is a koel, a bird that tricks crows into raising its chicks.

The short answer

Even in the busiest Indian city, you can spot a dozen kinds of birds without leaving home. The most common are the house crow, the common myna, the rock pigeon and the rose-ringed parakeet. Look up and you'll see black kites circling. Listen and you'll hear red-vented bulbuls, Asian koels and, in many places, house sparrows.

With a little patience, you may also find purple sunbirds sipping nectar, spotted doves on rooftops, white-throated kingfishers on wires and oriental magpie robins singing from walls. Cities have water, trees, food scraps and plenty of ledges for nesting, so many birds do very well in them.

Surprising but true

Rose-ringed parakeets, the green parrots common in Indian cities, have escaped from cages and set up wild flocks in London, Brussels and other European cities.

The big four

The house crow is grey around the neck and very bold. Crows are clever. They learn which houses throw out scraps and at what time, and they remember faces of people who have chased them. Common mynas are brown with yellow beaks and yellow patches around the eyes. They walk rather than hop, and they chatter constantly.

Rock pigeons gather wherever people drop grain. Rose-ringed parakeets are bright green, with long tails and red beaks. They screech as they fly past in flocks at dawn and dusk. Some escaped pet parakeets now live wild in London and other European cities, where people are often surprised to see a tropical parrot in winter.

The trickster koel

In spring and summer, the koel's loud, rising "ku-oo, ku-oo" can go on for hours. It is the male calling. He is glossy black with red eyes. The female is brown with white spots.

Koels don't build nests. The female lays her eggs in a crow's nest, often while the male distracts the crows. The crows hatch the koel chicks and feed them as their own. This trick, called brood parasitism, is common among cuckoos, and the koel is a member of the cuckoo family. Indian poets have written about its song for centuries.

Kites over Delhi

Look up over Delhi and you'll often see dozens of birds soaring in slow circles. These are black kites, brown birds of prey with forked tails. They feed on scraps from markets, meat shops and rubbish dumps, and they sometimes snatch food from people's hands.

Kites are often hurt by sharp kite-flying strings, especially around festivals like Independence Day and Makar Sankranti. In Delhi, a pair of brothers have spent years rescuing injured kites in their small basement. Their story became the documentary film All That Breathes, which was nominated for an Oscar in 2023.

Where are the sparrows?

Older Indians remember house sparrows hopping into kitchens. In some big cities, they became much harder to find. Modern buildings have fewer gaps for nests, gardens have fewer insects for chicks to eat, and shops sell grain in sealed packets.

The State of India's Birds report in 2023 found that sparrows were doing fine across India as a whole, but had dropped in several large cities. In 2012, Delhi made the house sparrow its state bird to draw attention to it. World Sparrow Day is on 20 March, and many families now put up small nest boxes and water bowls.

Becoming a city birder

You don't need special gear to start. A notebook, a window and some quiet time are enough. Binoculars help, and a field guide or a free app can tell you what you are seeing. Birdwatching is one of the easiest hobbies to begin, because birds are almost everywhere.

Scientists also welcome help. Every count you add to a citizen science website like eBird shows researchers which birds are doing well and which are disappearing. A child's balcony list in Pune or Patna can end up in a national report.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pigeons in Indian cities are native wild birds that have always lived there.

Truth: City pigeons come from the rock dove, a wild bird of cliffs. They were tamed thousands of years ago, and today's city flocks are mostly the descendants of tame pigeons gone wild. Building ledges are their new cliffs.

Try this

Sit by a window or on a balcony for ten minutes in the early morning and write down every bird you see or hear. Draw a quick sketch or note its colour and size. Do it again in the evening and see whether the list changes. Over a week, you'll have your own city bird count.
