Why are the Western Ghats so important?

A wall of old hills along India's west coast catches the monsoon, feeds rivers used by crores of people, and hides animals found nowhere else.

The short answer

The Western Ghats are a long chain of hills and mountains that runs for about 1,600 km along India's west coast, from Gujarat down to the southern tip at Kanyakumari. They are not very tall by world standards, but they matter enormously.

They catch the southwest monsoon, so their slopes are among the wettest places in India. That rain feeds great rivers like the Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri, which carry water across southern India to farms and cities. The forests are also packed with plants and animals that live nowhere else.

In 2012, UNESCO named the Western Ghats a World Heritage Site. Scientists list them as one of the world's top hotspots of biodiversity, meaning a place unusually rich in different kinds of life.

Surprising but true

The purple frog of the Western Ghats spends almost its whole life underground and was described by scientists only in 2003.

A wall that catches the rain

Every June, moist winds blow in from the Arabian Sea. When they reach the Ghats, they are forced upwards. The air cools, its water vapour turns to clouds, and rain pours down on the western slopes.

Towns like Agumbe in Karnataka can get several metres of rain a year. Then the air spills over the top and sinks down the eastern side, now much drier. That is why Pune is drier than Mumbai, and Bengaluru gets far less rain than coastal Mangaluru.

The rain doesn't stay in the hills. The Godavari begins near Nashik and the Kaveri at Talakaveri in Kodagu. Both rise close to the west coast, yet flow east across the whole peninsula into the Bay of Bengal.

Life found nowhere else

Long isolation and steady rain have given the Ghats a huge number of endemic species, ones that live in this region and nowhere else. The lion-tailed macaque, a black monkey with a silver mane, swings through the canopy. The Nilgiri tahr, a wild goat, clings to grassy cliffs near Munnar in Kerala. The king cobra, the world's longest venomous snake, hunts other snakes in the leaf litter.

New species are still being found. In 2003, scientists described the purple frog, a bloated, pig-nosed creature that lives underground and comes up only to breed when the monsoon arrives. Its closest relatives live in the Seychelles, a clue that India and those islands were once joined.

On the hills of the Nilgiris, a shrub called neelakurinji flowers only once every 12 years, turning whole slopes blue-purple.

Older than the Himalayas

The Western Ghats are not young, folded mountains. Their steep western edge is the broken rim of a continent. When India split from Madagascar, roughly 90 million years ago, the edge of the land was left as a step, and erosion has been carving it ever since. India's long drift north left this cliff facing the sea.

In the north, around Mahabaleshwar, the hills are built from layers of dark lava called basalt. These came from the giant eruptions that made the Deccan Traps about 66 million years ago, around the time the dinosaurs died out.

Protecting the hills

Around 2011, a panel of experts led by the ecologist Madhav Gadgil recommended strong protection for large parts of the Ghats. Many villagers, farmers and state governments argued that the rules would harm jobs and development. A later committee proposed protecting a smaller area. People still disagree about how to balance forests, farms, mines and roads, and the debate continues in all six states the hills cross.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Western Ghats are young mountains, like the Himalayas.

Truth: They are far older. The Himalayas began rising about 50 million years ago, while the edge of the Western Ghats formed much earlier, as India broke away from Madagascar and the land was later shaped by erosion.
