What is symmetry?

The Taj Mahal is a perfect mirror image of itself, except for one thing inside that was never meant to be there.

The short answer

Something has symmetry when you can do something to it and it still looks exactly the same. You might reflect it in a mirror, turn it, or slide it along.

The most familiar kind is mirror symmetry. Draw a line down the middle of a butterfly and one side is a reflection of the other. That line is called a line of symmetry. A capital A has one. A capital H has two. A circle has an endless number.

Mathematicians use symmetry to describe shapes, crystals, patterns and even the laws of physics. Artists and architects use it because our eyes find it calm and balanced. Many letters, logos and flags are designed this way on purpose.

Surprising but true

Inside the Taj Mahal, Shah Jahan's tomb sits off to one side of his wife's. It is the only thing in the whole design that breaks the symmetry.

Turning and sliding

Some shapes have no mirror line but still have symmetry. Spin a pinwheel a quarter turn and it looks the same as before. That is rotational symmetry. A shape that looks the same after a quarter turn has four-fold symmetry, because it matches itself four times in one full turn.

Most starfish have five arms and five-fold symmetry. Snowflakes have six-fold symmetry, because of the way water molecules link up as they freeze into ice.

The third kind is sliding, or translational, symmetry. A row of identical fence posts, or a sari border with the same flower repeated over and over, looks the same if you slide it along by one step.

The Ashoka Chakra on India's flag has 24 spokes. Turn it by one spoke and it looks exactly the same, so it matches itself 24 times in a single turn.

A building made like a mirror

The Taj Mahal in Agra was built in the 1600s by the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan as a tomb for his wife, Mumtaz Mahal. Almost everything about it is planned around a central line. The tomb has a matching minaret at each corner. The garden is split into four equal parts by water channels. Even the long pool in front was laid out to hold a reflection of the building.

There is one famous exception. Mumtaz Mahal's tomb lies right at the centre. When Shah Jahan died, he was buried beside her, slightly off to one side. His tomb is the one thing in the whole complex that breaks the symmetry.

Symmetry in living things

Most animals, including people, have rough mirror symmetry from left to right. Scientists call it bilateral symmetry. It suits creatures that move forward, with eyes, ears and legs in matched pairs.

Flowers often have rotational symmetry instead. A lily has three or six petals arranged around its centre. A butterfly's wings are near copies of each other because both grow from the same instructions in the same body.

Living things are never perfect, though. Your two hands are mirror images, but you probably write with only one of them. Your heart sits a little to the left. A male fiddler crab breaks the rule in a showier way: one of his claws is small and the other is huge, sometimes almost as big as the rest of his body.

Symmetry by hand

Across India, people draw rangoli and kolam patterns on floors and doorsteps, especially during festivals. Many are built on a grid of dots, and the artist turns and reflects the same loops again and again until a shape with four-fold or eight-fold symmetry appears.

The patterns in rangoli and in the painted tiles of mosques and palaces follow the same rules a mathematician would use. In the 1890s, mathematicians proved that there are exactly 17 different ways to repeat a pattern across a flat surface. Artists in many places had already used nearly all of them centuries before.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The two halves of a human face are exact mirror images.

Truth: Nobody's face is perfectly symmetrical. Eyes, ears, eyebrows and the corners of the mouth all differ a little from side to side. Our faces are only roughly symmetrical, which is normal.

Try this

Fold a sheet of paper in half and open it again. Put a few blobs of paint or ink on one side only, close it, press gently and open it. You have made a shape with mirror symmetry. Now try folding a paper square twice, cutting small shapes from the edges and opening it out to see a pattern with four matching parts.
