How do airships float?

The Hindenburg was more than three times as long as a jumbo jet, and it floated over the Atlantic on a gas lighter than air.

The short answer

An airship floats for the same reason a boat floats. A boat pushes water aside, and the water pushes back up. An airship pushes air aside. Its huge bag is filled with helium, a gas much lighter than air. If the air pushed aside weighs more than the whole airship, including its gas, frame, engines and passengers, the airship rises.

This push is called buoyancy. One cubic metre of helium lifts only about 1 kg, so airships have to be gigantic. Engines and propellers then push them forward, and fins steer them, much like a fish in water.

Surprising but true

The spire on top of New York's Empire State Building was first designed as a mooring mast for airships, though the idea never really worked.

Blimps and zeppelins

A blimp has no rigid frame inside. It is a giant balloon kept in shape by the pressure of the gas, like a long party balloon. Let the gas out and it collapses into a heap of fabric. Goodyear has flown blimps over the USA since 1925, and for decades they have carried TV cameras above big American football and baseball games.

A rigid airship, often called a zeppelin after the German Count Ferdinand von Zeppelin, has a skeleton of light metal girders covered in fabric. Inside are separate gas bags. Rigid airships could be built far bigger. Early gas bags were made from cow intestines, called goldbeater's skin, and one big airship needed the guts of hundreds of thousands of cattle.

Going up and down

Inside many airships are extra bags called ballonets, filled with ordinary air. To go down, the crew pumps air in. The airship becomes heavier and sinks. To go up, they let air out. It works like a fish's swim bladder or a submarine's tanks.

Pilots also tilt the nose up or down and use engine power, a bit like an aeroplane. Landing is the hard part. A gust can push a huge, light airship sideways, so ground crews used to grab ropes and pull it down by hand.

The age of giants

In the 1920s and 1930s, giant airships crossed oceans. In 1929, the Graf Zeppelin flew around the world. The Hindenburg was about 245 m long, more than three times the length of a jumbo jet. It had a dining room, cabins and a lounge with a piano.

The Empire State Building in New York was even given a mooring mast so airships could tie up at the top and passengers could step off. Strong winds round the tower made that far too dangerous.

Why hydrogen lost

The Hindenburg was filled with hydrogen, the lightest gas of all. It gives a little more lift than helium, but it burns. The USA controlled most of the world's helium at the time and would not sell it to Germany.

On 6 May 1937, as the Hindenburg came in to land at Lakehurst, New Jersey, it caught fire. In about half a minute it was gone. Thirty-six people died, though most passengers survived. Newsreel film shocked the world, and the age of the giant passenger airship ended almost overnight.

Airships return

Helium cannot burn, and modern airships use it. But helium is rare and comes from natural gas wells. Once it leaks out, it floats away into space.

Airships are making a quiet comeback. In 2024, a new rigid airship called Pathfinder 1, about 122 m long, began test flights in California. In 2025, India's defence research agency DRDO tested a small airship that climbed to about 17 km, higher than airliners fly. Airships use far less fuel than planes and can hover for days, so people hope to use them for carrying cargo to remote places. A weather balloon goes even higher, about 30 km, until it swells so much in the thin air that it bursts.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Airships float because helium has no weight.

Truth: Helium does have weight, just much less than air. An airship floats because the air it pushes aside weighs more than the airship and its gas, the same idea that lets a ship float on water.

Try this

Tie a helium party balloon to a paper cup with a short string. Add paper clips to the cup one by one until the balloon stops rising and just hovers. Count the clips. A typical party balloon lifts only around 10 grams, a few clips' worth.
