What is traditional Indian jewellery?

A bronze statue from Mohenjo-daro, about 4,500 years old, shows a young woman wearing about two dozen bangles on one arm.

The short answer

Indian jewellery is one of the oldest jewellery traditions in the world. People here have worn bangles, necklaces, earrings and nose rings for thousands of years, made of gold, silver, shell, glass, beads, flowers and even lac, a resin made by insects.

Jewellery in India is much more than decoration. It can show which region a person comes from, whether a woman is married, or a family's faith. It marks weddings, births and festivals. For many families it is also a way to save money, since gold keeps its value. Each region has its own styles, from the gem-studded kundan of Rajasthan to the gold temple jewellery of Tamil Nadu.

Surprising but true

For many centuries, India was the world's main source of diamonds. That only changed after big diamond mines were found in Brazil in the 1720s.

Bangles from the Bronze Age

In the ancient Indus cities, people made bangles from shell, copper and baked clay, and beads from carnelian, a red-orange stone. Some carnelian beads were drilled with tiny holes through their whole length, a slow and skilled job. Harappan beads have been found as far away as Mesopotamia, in today's Iraq.

The famous bronze Dancing Girl from Mohenjo-daro, now in the National Museum in Delhi, wears a stack of bangles that covers almost her whole left arm. Many women in India still wear bangles today, often glass ones that jingle when they move.

Gold, gems and royal treasure

India has long loved gold. Most Indian gold jewellery is 22 carat, which means it is about 91.6% gold mixed with a little copper or silver. Pure gold is too soft for everyday wear. Since 2021, jewellers in a growing number of districts have had to hallmark gold, stamping it to show its purity.

For many centuries India was the world's main source of diamonds, many of them found near Golconda, close to today's Hyderabad. Hyderabad also became famous for pearls. Indian kings collected huge gems, and Mughal emperors wore jewelled turban ornaments and necklaces of emeralds and rubies.

Styles from different regions

Kundan comes from Rajasthan and the Mughal courts. Craftsmen press uncut or flat-cut gems into a bed of very pure gold foil, which holds them without claws. The back of a kundan piece is often decorated with meenakari, bright enamel colours of red, green and blue, fused onto the metal with heat. Jaipur is famous for both.

Temple jewellery from Tamil Nadu, made in towns such as Nagercoil, was first designed to adorn statues of gods, and later worn by Bharatanatyam dancers and brides. It shows gods, goddesses, peacocks and lotuses in gold. In Odisha, Cuttack craftsmen make tarakasi, delicate silver filigree from thin twisted wires shaped into flowers and lace-like patterns.

Jewellery and life's big moments

At many Hindu weddings, the groom ties a mangalsutra, a necklace of black beads and gold, around the bride's neck. In parts of India, married women also wear toe rings, red and white bangles, or a nose ring. Muslim, Sikh and Christian families have their own traditions, from the Sikh steel kara bracelet to wedding necklaces in Kerala's Syrian Christian families.

Gold given to a bride at her wedding has traditionally been seen as her own property and a safety net for hard times. That is one reason Indian families buy so much gold.

Men wear it too

In the past, Indian kings and nobles often wore more jewellery than queens, with strings of pearls, armbands and jewelled turbans. Men in many communities still wear rings, chains and earrings. In the Deccan, craftsmen at Bidar developed bidri, silver inlaid into dark metal, for boxes and hookahs as well as ornaments. A single piece of jewellery can carry a family's history, passed from grandmother to granddaughter for generations.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pure 24-carat gold makes the best jewellery.

Truth: Pure gold is beautiful but very soft, so it bends, scratches and loses its shape. Most Indian jewellers use 22-carat gold, which is mixed with a little copper or silver to make it stronger.

Try this

Make a paper kundan necklace. Cut shapes from a cereal box and cover them with aluminium foil to look like gold. Glue on circles of coloured paper or sweet wrappers as gems, and join the pieces with string. Try copying a peacock or lotus pattern.
