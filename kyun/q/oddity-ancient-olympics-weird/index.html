What strange events were in the ancient Olympics?

At the ancient Olympics, men raced in heavy bronze helmets, trumpeters competed to be loudest, and an emperor won a chariot race he never finished.

The short answer

The ancient Olympics, held at Olympia in Greece for more than a thousand years, had some events that would look very odd today. There was a race in armour, a fighting sport with almost no rules, a mule-cart race, and contests for trumpeters and heralds. Athletes competed without clothes, and the long jump was done holding stone or metal weights.

The Games were part of a religious festival for Zeus, king of the gods in Greek myths. Winners got only a wreath of olive leaves. It was cut with a golden knife from a sacred wild olive tree growing near the temple of Zeus. Their home cities, though, often gave them free meals for life, statues and fame.

Surprising but true

For the first 13 Games, according to tradition, there was only one event, a single sprint of about 192 m.

One race, then lots more

According to tradition, the first Games in 776 BC had just one event, the stadion. It was a sprint of one length of the stadium, about 192 m, roughly twice the length of a modern 100 m track race. Runners started from stone lines set into the ground, with grooves for their toes. Some of these starting lines can still be seen at Olympia today, and visitors like to race each other along the old track. A cook named Koroibos is listed as the first winner.

Over the next 200 years, more events were added: longer runs, wrestling, boxing, chariot races and the pentathlon. At its peak, the festival lasted five days, and tens of thousands of visitors camped around Olympia.

Racing in armour

From 520 BC, one of the races was the hoplitodromos. Runners wore a bronze helmet and carried a heavy round shield, and early on they wore leg guards too. All that metal could weigh well over 10 kg, as heavy as a big bag of rice.

It was meant to train men for war, when soldiers had to run at the enemy in full armour. Pictures on Greek vases show runners dropping their shields, and other racers tripping over them.

Fighting with almost no rules

The pankration mixed boxing and wrestling. Kicking, punching, joint locks and choke holds were all allowed. The only rules were no biting and no gouging eyes. A fight carried on until one man gave up by raising a finger.

Greek boxers had no rounds and no weight classes. They wrapped their hands in leather straps instead of gloves. Indian wrestlers in old akharas trained hard too, but most of their bouts used far stricter rules than the pankration.

Strange events that came and went

From 500 BC, there was a race for carts pulled by mules. It was dropped after about 56 years, perhaps because people found it boring or undignified. Another event, for mares, came and went at about the same time.

In 396 BC, contests for trumpeters and heralds were added. They were judged on how loud and clear they were. The winners then got the job of announcing the other events. One trumpeter, Herodoros of Megara, is said to have won ten times in a row.

An emperor gets his way

In AD 67, the Roman emperor Nero came to compete. The Games were even moved by two years to fit his travel plans. He entered a chariot race with ten horses, fell out of his chariot, and failed to finish. The judges still crowned him champion.

After Nero's death, his win was struck from the official list. The Games carried on until around AD 393. When they were revived in Athens in 1896, the organisers kept the running, wrestling and discus, and left out the mule carts and trumpeters. Some unusual sports did survive into modern times, and a few are listed among today's weirdest sports.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The ancient Olympics stopped all wars in Greece while they were on.

Truth: A sacred truce protected athletes and visitors travelling to and from Olympia. It did not end wars between Greek cities, which often kept fighting elsewhere.
