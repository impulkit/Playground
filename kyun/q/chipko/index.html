What was the Chipko movement?

In 1974, a group of village women in the Himalaya stopped a team of loggers simply by refusing to leave their trees.

The short answer

The Chipko movement was a protest by villagers in the Himalayan hills of today's Uttarakhand in the 1970s. When logging companies came to cut their forests, people stood in the way, sometimes hugging the trees so they could not be felled. Chipko means "to stick to" in Hindi.

Most of the protesters were village women, who gathered firewood, fodder and water from the forest every day. They knew better than anyone what losing it would mean.

The movement became famous across India and the world. In 1980, the government banned cutting green trees in the region's hill forests for 15 years.

Surprising but true

Chipko inspired a similar protest in Karnataka in 1983, called Appiko, which means "hug" in Kannada.

Why the hills were angry

In the 1960s and early 1970s, the government sold the right to cut trees in the hills to companies from the plains. The timber went to cities and factories. Villagers were often not even allowed to cut a few trees for farm tools.

In 1970, a huge flood on the Alaknanda river swept away bridges, roads and villages. Many people blamed the bare, logged slopes, where nothing held the soil in place. Without roots, hillsides crumble in heavy rain, causing floods and landslides.

The women of Reni

In March 1974, loggers arrived at the forest near Reni, a village in Chamoli district. Most of the men were away, called to collect government payments for land.

A woman named Gaura Devi, head of the village women's group, gathered about 27 women and girls. They walked into the forest and told the loggers that cutting the trees would mean cutting them first. The women stayed all night. The loggers gave up and left.

The people who spread the word

Chipko had no single leader. A year before Reni, in 1973, Chandi Prasad Bhatt and his village cooperative in Mandal had stopped a sports-goods company from felling ash trees. Bhatt later won the Ramon Magsaysay Award.

Sunderlal Bahuguna carried the message to Delhi. He met Prime Minister Indira Gandhi, and in 1980 her government brought in the logging ban. Then, between 1981 and 1983, he walked about 4,800 km across the Himalaya, village by village.

What the forest gave

For a family in the hills, the forest was a kind of shop, pharmacy and water tank rolled into one. Oak and other broadleaf trees gave leaves to feed cows and goats, and fallen branches to cook with. Their roots soaked up rain and let it out slowly into springs.

Women did most of this work. They often walked for hours each day carrying loads of firewood and fodder on their heads. When forests shrank, the walks got longer. That is why women were at the front of Chipko. They were the first to feel the loss.

Chipko protesters also had a slogan. Roughly translated, it asked what the forest gives, and answered: soil, water and pure air. Many people across India still know it.

An older story

Chipko protesters often recalled a story from the Thar Desert. In 1730, at Khejarli near Jodhpur, the Bishnoi community, whose faith forbids cutting green trees, refused to let a ruler's men fell their khejri trees. According to the tradition, Amrita Devi and 363 Bishnois gave their lives.

Seeds that travelled

Chipko inspired the Appiko movement in Karnataka and protests in many other countries. In Kenya, Wangari Maathai was building a tree-planting movement led by women at around the same time. Historians say Chipko showed that poor villagers could be some of the world's most determined environmentalists, because the forest was their home.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Chipko was about saving trees because they were beautiful.

Truth: For the villagers, the forest meant firewood, fodder for animals, clean springs and soil that stayed on the slopes. They protested because cutting the forest threatened their food, water and homes.

Try this

Adopt a tree near your home or school. Wrap a piece of string around its trunk at about the height of your chest and measure the string to find its girth. Each month, measure again and note any birds, insects or flowers you spot. After a year you will know that tree better than almost anyone.
