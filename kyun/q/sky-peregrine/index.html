How fast can a peregrine falcon dive?

A peregrine falcon diving after a pigeon can go faster than a Japanese bullet train, making it the fastest animal on Earth.

The short answer

A peregrine falcon cruising through the air flies at about 65 to 90 km/h, fast for a bird but nothing record-breaking. Its secret is the dive, which falconers call a stoop. It climbs high above its prey, tucks in its wings and falls, steering as it goes. In a stoop it can pass 320 km/h, as fast as the quickest Shinkansen bullet trains.

In a famous experiment in 1999, a trained falcon named Frightful was released from a small plane and filmed diving at about 390 km/h. No other animal is known to move faster. That's why the peregrine is usually called the fastest animal on Earth.

Surprising but true

In the 1960s peregrines nearly disappeared from much of the USA because a pesticide called DDT made their eggshells so thin they cracked under the parents.

Built like a dart

At the top of a stoop, a peregrine folds its long, pointed wings tight against its body. Its shape becomes a smooth teardrop, which slips through the air with very little drag. Gravity does the rest, pulling it faster and faster.

Small adjustments of its wings and tail let it steer, even at full speed. It often strikes its prey, usually another bird, with its feet in mid-air, then swoops round to catch it as it falls.

Breathing and seeing at top speed

Air rushing in at over 300 km/h could make breathing hard. Peregrines have small bony cones in their nostrils that break up the blast of air. Some jet engines use a similar cone shape at the front to slow the air before it rushes in. People often point to the falcon as a natural example of the same idea.

To protect their eyes, peregrines use a third eyelid, a clear film that sweeps across each eye like a windscreen wiper. Their eyesight is several times sharper than ours, good enough to pick out a single pigeon in a distant flock.

Nearly lost, then saved

In the middle of the 1900s, peregrine numbers crashed across North America and Europe. The cause was DDT, a pesticide sprayed on farms. It built up in small birds, and then in the falcons that ate them. DDT made their eggshells so thin that they broke when the parents sat on them.

The USA banned most uses of DDT in 1972. Scientists bred peregrines in captivity and released young birds into the wild, sometimes from the roofs of tall buildings. The plan worked. In 1999 the peregrine was taken off the US endangered species list, one of conservation's great comebacks.

Falcons and people

People have trained falcons to hunt for thousands of years, a sport called falconry. In the Arab world, India and Central Asia, peregrines and their relatives were prized by kings. Paintings from the Mughal court show emperors and nobles with hunting falcons perched on their gloved wrists.

Falcons now have modern jobs too. At some airports, trained falcons patrol the runways and scare away flocks of smaller birds that could be sucked into jet engines.

City falcons

Peregrines naturally nest on high cliffs. To a peregrine, a skyscraper is just a cliff with windows, and the streets below are full of pigeons. Today they nest on buildings, bridges and church towers in cities like New York, London and Chicago, and some have live webcams that thousands of people watch.

The word peregrine means "wanderer", and northern peregrines fly thousands of kilometres to escape winter, some of them reaching India. India also has its own resident peregrine, the shaheen, which nests on cliffs and old forts. And the strangest part? A falcon in a stoop falls faster than a human skydiver, who tops out at around 200 km/h, as you'll see in the story of skydiving.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The cheetah is the fastest animal in the world.

Truth: The cheetah is the fastest animal on land, at about 100–110 km/h in short bursts. A diving peregrine falcon is more than three times faster, which makes it the fastest animal of all.

Try this

Drop two sheets of the same paper from the same height, one flat and one crumpled into a tight ball. The ball wins easily, because it pushes aside less air. A peregrine does the same thing in the sky by folding its wings close and turning itself into a teardrop.
