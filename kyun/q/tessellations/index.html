Which shapes fit together without gaps?

In 2023, a retired print technician from England found a shape that mathematicians had hunted for decades. People nicknamed it "the hat".

The short answer

A tessellation is a pattern of shapes that covers a flat surface with no gaps and no overlaps, like tiles on a bathroom floor.

Only three regular shapes can do it alone: equal-sided triangles, squares and hexagons. The reason is in their corners. Where tiles meet at a point, their angles must add up to a full turn of 360 degrees. Six triangle corners of 60 degrees do that. So do four square corners of 90 degrees, and three hexagon corners of 120 degrees.

A regular pentagon has corners of 108 degrees. Three of them make 324, leaving a gap. Four make 432, which is too many. So regular pentagons never fit.

Mix shapes and more patterns appear. Squares and octagons together make a common floor pattern, with a small square filling each gap between four octagons. Two octagon corners of 135 degrees and one square corner of 90 add up to exactly 360.

Surprising but true

Every triangle, and every four-sided shape, can tile a floor with no gaps, however lopsided it is.

The great pentagon hunt

Lopsided pentagons are a different story. Some of them do tile, and for about a hundred years mathematicians tried to find every kind.

In 1975, the American writer Martin Gardner described the hunt in a magazine column. A reader named Marjorie Rice, a homemaker in San Diego with no maths training beyond high school, began drawing in her kitchen. Over the next few years she discovered four new kinds that the experts had missed.

In 2015, three researchers at the University of Washington Bothell used a computer search to find a fifteenth kind. Two years later, a French mathematician, Michaël Rao, used a computer to show that the list was finally complete. There are exactly 15.

Patterns that never repeat

Most tilings repeat. Slide a chessboard pattern along by two squares and it lands on itself. But in the 1970s the British scientist Roger Penrose found a pair of shapes that tile a floor forever without the pattern ever repeating.

That raised a puzzle. Could a single shape do the same thing? In 2023, David Smith, a retired print technician from Yorkshire, England, who loved playing with shapes, found one. It looks a little like a T-shirt or a hat, so people called it "the hat". With three mathematicians, he proved it covers a flat surface but never settles into a repeating pattern.

Tiles in art and buildings

People have been making tessellations for thousands of years. Islamic artists built some of the most complicated ones, using stars and polygons in tiles and carved stone. You can see them at the Alhambra palace in Spain, and in the stone screens and inlay of Mughal buildings such as the Taj Mahal.

In the 1930s, a Dutch artist named M.C. Escher visited the Alhambra and came home inspired. He began drawing tessellations of birds, fish and lizards that lock together perfectly.

Mathematicians later showed there are exactly 17 basic ways to repeat a pattern across a flat surface, each with its own symmetry. Artists in many places had already found most of them by eye.

Nature's tiles

Bees build their combs from hexagons. Of the three regular shapes that tile, the hexagon uses the least wall to hold the most space, which saves wax.

The same shape turns up where cooling lava cracks into tall columns, or where mud dries out and splits into a pattern of plates. At the Giant's Causeway in Northern Ireland, about 40,000 stone columns pack tightly together, many of them six-sided. In each case, the cracks or walls meet at corners that close up with no gaps, the same rule that decides which tiles fit on your floor.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Only triangles, squares and hexagons can tile a floor.

Truth: Those are the only regular shapes that can, where all sides and angles are equal. Many irregular shapes tile too, including every triangle, every four-sided shape and 15 known kinds of pentagon.

Try this

Cut a square of card. Cut any wiggly shape out of one side and tape it onto the opposite side. Trace around your new tile again and again, sliding it along each time. It fits with no gaps, just like the drawings of the Dutch artist M.C. Escher.
