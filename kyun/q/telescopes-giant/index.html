What are the giant telescopes being built now?

On a mountain in Chile, Europe is building a telescope with a mirror 39 m wide, longer than three school buses parked end to end.

The short answer

Three huge telescopes, much bigger than any working today, are being planned or built. Each has a main mirror between 25 and 39 m wide. For comparison, the biggest telescopes working now have mirrors of about 10 m.

The Extremely Large Telescope, or ELT, is the furthest along. The European Southern Observatory is building it on a mountain in Chile's Atacama Desert. The Giant Magellan Telescope is also planned in Chile. The Thirty Meter Telescope was meant for Hawaii, but its future is uncertain. Astronomers want them to photograph planets around other stars and see the first galaxies.

Surprising but true

The mirrors for the Giant Magellan Telescope are being made in a lab built underneath the football stadium at the University of Arizona.

Why bigger is better

A telescope's mirror works like a bucket catching rain, except it catches light. Double the width of the bucket and it catches four times as much. A 39 m mirror gathers far more light than the 6.5 m mirror on the Webb telescope.

More light means fainter things become visible. A bigger mirror also shows finer detail. ESO expects the ELT's pictures to be about 15 times sharper than Hubble's. That could be enough to pick out a rocky planet next to a bright star and study the gases in its air.

Mirrors made of many pieces

Nobody can make a single, perfect slab of glass 39 m wide, and no road could carry it. So the ELT's main mirror is made of 798 hexagonal segments that fit together like tiles. Motors behind each one adjust it many times a second, keeping the whole surface in shape as the wind blows and the temperature changes.

The Giant Magellan Telescope takes a different path. It will use seven round mirrors, each 8.4 m across, arranged like the petals of a flower. Together they act like one mirror about 25 m wide. Each mirror is cast in a spinning oven at the University of Arizona, in a lab under the stands of the university's football stadium. A mirror takes years to cast, cool and polish.

Are there planets around other stars?Thirty-five years ago, nobody had found a single planet around another star. Now we know of more than 6,000.

Fixing the twinkle

Stars twinkle because Earth's moving air bends their light. That is pretty, but it blurs telescope pictures. The giant telescopes will use a trick called adaptive optics.

They shine powerful lasers into the sky, which make a glowing dot high in the atmosphere, a kind of artificial star. Sensors watch how the air wobbles that dot. A thin, flexible mirror then bends its shape hundreds of times a second to cancel the wobble. The result can be nearly as sharp as a telescope in space.

Mountains and arguments

Telescopes need high, dry, dark places with steady air. Chile's Atacama Desert is one of the best, which is why it hosts so many observatories.

The Thirty Meter Telescope, or TMT, chose Maunakea, a tall volcano on Hawaii's Big Island. Many Native Hawaiians consider the mountain sacred, and there are already a dozen telescopes on it. In 2019 protesters blocked the road to stop construction. Supporters say the telescope would bring jobs and science. Opponents say the mountain's top has been used enough. A site in Spain's Canary Islands has been studied as a backup. India is one of TMT's partners and is building parts for it, including hardware for its mirror segments.

When will they open?

Giant telescopes take decades and cost billions. As of 2026, the ELT is planned to start observing around 2029. In 2025, the US National Science Foundation moved the Giant Magellan Telescope into its final design stage, but full construction still needs more money. TMT's path is less clear.

All three use the same basic idea as Isaac Newton's first reflecting telescope, built in 1668: a curved mirror that gathers light to a point. Newton's mirror was a few centimetres wide. The new ones are nearly the width of a building.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Now that we have space telescopes like Webb, big telescopes on the ground are no longer needed.

Truth: Ground telescopes can be far bigger than anything we can launch, so they collect much more light. Space and ground telescopes work as a team, each doing jobs the other can't.

Try this

In a dim room, look closely at a friend's eyes, then switch on a bright light and watch their pupils shrink. Your pupil is the opening that lets light in, just like a telescope's mirror. In the dark it opens wide to catch more light, which is exactly why astronomers want bigger mirrors.
