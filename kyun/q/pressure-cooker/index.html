How does a pressure cooker cook faster?

A pressure cooker's whistle is a tiny safety valve, letting out just enough steam to keep water boiling at about 120°C instead of 100°C.

The short answer

In an open pot, water can't get hotter than its boiling point, which is about 100°C at sea level. Any extra heat just turns more water into steam, and the steam drifts away.

A pressure cooker has a lid that locks on and a rubber ring, called a gasket, that seals the gap. When the water boils, the steam can't escape. It builds up and squeezes everything inside, raising the pressure. Under higher pressure, water must get hotter before it boils, about 120°C in a typical cooker. Food cooked at 120°C instead of 100°C softens much faster. Rajma that might take more than an hour in an open pot can be ready in well under half that time.

Surprising but true

The first pressure cooker was shown in London in 1679 by the French scientist Denis Papin, who called it a "steam digester" and used it to soften bones.

The whistle and the weight

On top of the lid sits a small metal weight over a narrow pipe. While the pressure is low, the weight sits snugly and blocks the pipe. As steam builds up, it eventually pushes hard enough to lift the weight. A jet of steam rushes out with a loud whistle, the pressure drops a little, and the weight drops back down.

So the whistle is really a pressure limiter. It keeps the inside of the cooker at roughly the same high pressure the whole time. In millions of Indian kitchens, cooks count whistles as a kind of timer: two whistles for rice, perhaps five for chana. They aren't measuring pressure, just how long the food has been cooking hot.

Papin's bone digester

In 1679, the French scientist Denis Papin, working in London, showed members of the Royal Society a heavy iron pot with a tightly clamped lid. He called it a "steam digester". It could soften bones until they crumbled, and he later cooked a supper in it for the society's members.

Papin also added a safety valve, a weighted lever that let steam escape before the pot burst. Many inventors after him experimented with the push of steam, and similar ideas later helped shape the first steam engines.

How did the steam engine change the world?The first useful steam engines were built to pump water out of flooded coal mines. Within a century they were pulling trains across India.

Why India loves the cooker

Indian cooking relies heavily on dal, beans, chickpeas and rice, all of which need a long time in hot water. A pressure cooker saves time and fuel, which matters when gas cylinders cost money. Companies such as Hawkins and Prestige have sold cookers in India for decades, and in many families a cooker is one of the first things bought for a new home.

Cookers are popular in the USA too, often as electric "multi-cookers" with buttons instead of whistles. These use the same idea, just with a sensor and a chip deciding when the pressure is right. When cooking is done, the pressure has to come down before the lid can open. Many cooks simply wait for the cooker to cool, so the steam slowly turns back into water and the pressure falls on its own.

High in the mountains

Cookers matter most where the air is thin. In Leh, in Ladakh, water in an open pot boils at about 88°C. Potatoes and rice stay hard for a long time, and some foods never cook properly. A pressure cooker raises the pressure back up, so the water can get hot enough again.

Mountaineers on Himalayan expeditions carry small cookers for the same reason. Soldiers posted at high altitude along India's northern borders use them every day. Without the trapped steam, a simple bowl of hot dal would be very hard to make.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pressure cookers can easily explode.

Truth: Modern cookers have several layers of safety, including the whistle, a backup safety valve or plug, and a lid that won't open while pressure is high. Accidents are rare and usually happen when a vent is blocked with food or someone forces the lid open too early.

Try this

Get a plastic medicine syringe with no needle from a chemist or first-aid kit. Pull the plunger halfway out, press your fingertip firmly over the open end, and push the plunger in. Feel the trapped air pushing back. That push is pressure, the same thing that builds up inside a sealed cooker.
