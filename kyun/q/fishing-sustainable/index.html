What is sustainable fishing?

In 1992, Canada banned cod fishing off Newfoundland because the fish had all but vanished. More than thirty years later, the cod still haven't fully come back.

The short answer

Sustainable fishing means catching fish in a way that leaves enough behind to breed, so there will be just as many fish next year and the year after. It's a bit like a bank account. Spend only the interest and the money lasts for ever. Spend the savings and one day it's gone.

To do this, fishers and governments set limits on how many fish can be caught, how big they must be and when and where fishing is allowed. They also try to protect other sea animals, and the seabed itself, from being harmed by nets and lines.

Billions of people eat fish, and millions earn their living from it, from small boats in Kerala to giant trawlers in the North Atlantic. Keeping the oceans full matters to all of them.

Surprising but true

Since 2022, the world has produced more fish and seafood from farms than from wild catch.

When the fish ran out

The Grand Banks, off Newfoundland in Canada, were once among the richest fishing grounds on Earth. Early European sailors reported cod so thick that they could almost scoop them up in baskets.

In the 20th century, huge factory trawlers began catching cod around the clock. Catches soared, then collapsed. By 1992, the cod had almost disappeared. Canada banned cod fishing there, and tens of thousands of people lost their jobs overnight.

Scientists expected the cod to bounce back within a few years. More than three decades later, they still haven't fully recovered. Once a fish population falls too low, the whole ecosystem can change around it.

The world's catch

The Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations checks the health of fish stocks every two years. Its 2026 report found that about 62% of the stocks it tracks are fished at sustainable levels. The rest are being caught faster than they can breed.

The amount of fish caught from the wild has stayed at around 90 million tonnes a year since the late 1980s. All the growth since then has come from fish farms, known as aquaculture. In 2022, for the first time, farms produced more fish and seafood than wild fishing.

Catching the wrong animals

Nets don't choose. Fishing gear often traps animals nobody wanted, such as young fish, turtles, dolphins, seabirds and sharks. This is called bycatch. Shrimp and prawn trawling is especially wasteful, because the nets have small holes and scoop up almost everything.

Some fixes are simple. Nets with bigger holes let young fish escape. Trawl nets can be fitted with a turtle excluder device, a metal grid with a flap that lets sea turtles push their way out. Long fishing lines can be set at night, when seabirds aren't diving for bait.

Bottom trawling, dragging heavy nets across the seabed, can also wreck coral and sponge gardens that took centuries to grow.

Rules that help

Many countries close fishing during breeding seasons. In India, mechanised boats stop fishing for 61 days each year, during the monsoon months. On the east coast the ban runs from mid-April to mid-June, and on the west coast from June to July.

Rules can bring fish back. Atlantic bluefin tuna, a favourite for sushi, was heavily overfished. After years of strict catch limits, its numbers rose, and in 2021 the IUCN, which tracks endangered species, moved it out of the "endangered" group.

What shoppers can do

Some fish carry labels, like the blue tick of the Marine Stewardship Council, which certify they come from well-managed fisheries. Eating a wider range of local fish, not just a few famous kinds, also spreads the pressure. Asking "where was this caught?" is a small question that fishers and shopkeepers increasingly expect to hear.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The ocean is so huge that we could never run out of fish.

Truth: Many fish populations have crashed from overfishing, like the cod off Canada. The FAO estimates that over a third of the world's assessed fish stocks are now fished faster than they can recover.
