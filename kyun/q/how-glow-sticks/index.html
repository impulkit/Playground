How do glow sticks glow?

Snap a glow stick and you break a tiny glass tube inside, starting a chemical reaction that makes light without any heat or battery.

The short answer

A glow stick is a plastic tube holding two separate liquids. One liquid sits inside a thin glass tube, and the other fills the space around it. When you bend the stick, the glass cracks and the two liquids mix.

That mix starts a chemical reaction that gives off energy. A colourful dye in the liquid catches that energy and lets it out again as light. Because almost none of the energy becomes heat, a glow stick stays cool in your hand. Scientists call this chemiluminescence, which simply means light from chemistry.

Surprising but true

A glow stick put in the freezer can still glow faintly the next day, because cold slows the chemical reaction down instead of stopping it.

What is inside

The glass tube usually holds hydrogen peroxide, the same chemical used to bleach hair, but weaker. Outside the tube is a liquid called a phenyl oxalate ester, mixed with a dye. Neither liquid glows on its own. You could keep a glow stick in a drawer for years and it would stay dark.

Snap it, and the peroxide meets the ester. They react to form a short-lived, high-energy molecule. Within a split second, that molecule breaks apart and hands its energy to the dye. The dye glows with its own colour. Swap the dye, and you change the light from green to pink to blue. Shaking the stick only mixes the liquids better so the whole tube lights up evenly. The dye decides the colour. Green and yellow-green look especially bright to human eyes, because our eyes are most sensitive to those colours. That is one reason so many safety glow sticks are green.

Why do some sea creatures glow?On some nights, waves on Indian beaches break in electric blue, lit by billions of living things smaller than a grain of sand.

Light without heat

A traditional light bulb works by heating a wire until it glows white hot. Most of the electricity becomes heat, which is why old bulbs could burn your fingers. A candle wastes even more energy as heat.

A glow stick skips the heat. The energy from the reaction goes almost straight into light. That makes it safe to use where a spark could be dangerous, such as near leaking gas or fuel. Soldiers, divers, miners and rescue teams carry them because they work in rain and under water and cannot set anything on fire.

Speed it up, slow it down

Like most chemical reactions, the glow stick reaction runs faster when it is warm. Drop a glowing stick into warm water and it becomes much brighter, but it may fade within an hour or two. Put it in a freezer and it dims to a faint shine, yet it can keep going for a day or more.

This is why people sometimes save half-used glow sticks in the freezer. It works, but it is not a recharge. The chemicals are simply being used more slowly. Once they run out, the stick is finished.

Who made the first ones

In the early 1960s, chemists in the USA, including Edwin Chandross at Bell Laboratories, studied reactions that gave off light. Researchers at a company called American Cyanamid then found brighter and longer-lasting mixtures. The US Navy was interested, because sailors needed a light that would not spark.

By the 1970s, glow sticks were sold in shops. Since then they have been used as safety lights, fishing lures, Halloween costumes and concert decorations. Inside, they should never be cut open, because the liquid can sting skin and eyes.

People did not invent cold light, though. Living things got there first, hundreds of millions of years earlier. Fireflies flash to find mates, using a reaction that turns almost all its energy into light. Glowing mushrooms, squid and jellyfish do the same. And in the black depths of the sea, the anglerfish dangles a glowing lure in front of its mouth, a living glow stick with teeth behind it. It is one of thousands of animals that glow in the dark ocean, a trick called bioluminescence.

Quick facts

Kind of light
chemiluminescence, light made by a chemical reaction
Inside
a thin glass tube, two liquids and a dye
Developed
1960s and 1970s, by chemists in the USA
Typical glow time
from a few hours to about 12 hours
Heat produced
almost none, so it is called cold light

Myth or fact?

Myth: Putting a used glow stick in the freezer recharges it.

Truth: Nothing gets recharged. The cold only slows the reaction, so the chemicals last longer. Once they are used up, the light is gone for good.

Try this

Ask an adult for two glow sticks of the same colour. Snap both, then put one in a cup of warm tap water and the other in a cup of ice water. Turn off the lights and compare them after five minutes, then again after an hour.
