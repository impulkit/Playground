What is a shooting star really?

It's not a star at all. It's usually smaller than a grain of rice.

The short answer

A shooting star, or meteor, is a speck of space dust burning up as it rushes into our air. It has nothing to do with real stars.

Most of these specks are smaller than a grain of rice. They hit Earth's atmosphere at tens of kilometres per second, far faster than any bullet. At that speed, the air in front of them is squeezed and heated so violently that the speck and the air around it glow white-hot. The streak of light lasts a second or so, about 100 km above the ground.

Surprising but true

Where the dust comes from

Space is sprinkled with dust and grit. Much of it was shed by comets as they passed the Sun, and some comes from asteroids bumping together.

On any dark, clear night, you might see a few meteors an hour. But at certain times of year, Earth ploughs straight through a comet's trail of dust. Then dozens of meteors an hour can streak across the sky. This is a meteor shower.

The great showers

The Perseids peak around 12 August each year. They come from the dust of Comet Swift-Tuttle. The Geminids peak around 13 to 14 December and are often the richest shower of the year. Unusually, they come from an asteroid called Phaethon rather than a comet.

All the meteors in a shower seem to fly out from one point in the sky, like the view through a car windscreen as snow rushes towards you. Each shower is named after the constellation where that point lies.

Sometimes a shower becomes a storm. In November 1833, people across North America saw the Leonids fall so thickly that some thought the stars were dropping out of the sky. It became one of the most famous sky events in American history.

Fireballs and meteorites

A bigger piece, perhaps the size of a marble or a football, makes a much brighter streak called a fireball. Very rarely, part of a space rock survives the fall and lands on the ground. That rock is called a meteorite.

Scientists treasure meteorites. Many are about 4.6 billion years old, left over from when the planets were forming. A few have even come from Mars and the Moon, knocked off by impacts long ago.

In 2013, a rock about 20 m wide exploded high above Chelyabinsk in Russia, brighter than the Sun for a moment. Rocks that big are rare. Astronomers keep watch for them, as you can read in could an asteroid hit Earth today?

A crater in Maharashtra

Lonar Lake in Maharashtra sits inside a bowl almost 2 km wide, blasted out when a large meteorite hit the Deccan's volcanic rock long ago. Scientists still debate exactly when that happened. The lake's water is salty and alkaline, and it sometimes turns pink, thanks to tiny living things in the water.

How to watch

You don't need a telescope. Telescopes see only a small patch of sky, but meteors can appear anywhere. The best plan is to get far from city lights, lie back and look up. Meteor counts are often higher after midnight, when your side of Earth faces forward into the dust, like a car's windscreen in the rain.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A shooting star is a star falling from the sky.

Truth: Stars are giant balls of gas, far bigger than Earth and light-years away. A shooting star is a tiny speck of space dust burning up about 100 km above the ground.

Try this

On a clear night during a meteor shower, go with an adult to the darkest open place you can, away from street lights. Lie back on a mat and look at a wide patch of sky. Give your eyes 20 minutes to adjust, and keep a tally of how many meteors you see in half an hour.
