Why does Japanese use three scripts?

A single Japanese sentence can mix three different scripts, plus a few English letters, and Japanese readers don't even blink.

The short answer

Japanese uses three scripts because it borrowed one and then invented two more to fit its own language.

About 1,500 years ago, Japan had no writing of its own. It borrowed Chinese characters, which the Japanese call kanji. But Japanese grammar is very different from Chinese. Japanese words change their endings to show things like tense, and the language uses small grammar words that Chinese doesn't have. Kanji were clumsy for that.

So Japanese writers simplified some kanji into quick sound symbols, called kana. Over time these became two sets: rounded hiragana and angular katakana. Today kanji carry the main meaning of words, hiragana handle grammar endings and little words, and katakana are used mostly for foreign words and sound effects.

Surprising but true

The word "emoji" comes from Japanese, not English. It joins e (picture) and moji (character), and has nothing to do with the English word "emotion".

Three jobs, one sentence

Take the sentence 私はアイスクリームを食べます, "I eat ice cream". 私 (I) and 食 (eat) are kanji. は, を and べます are hiragana, doing the grammar work. アイスクリーム, aisukurīmu, is katakana for the borrowed English word "ice cream".

Mixing scripts helps readers. Japanese is usually written without spaces between words, so the switch from kanji to hiragana shows where one word ends and the grammar begins. It works like a built-in highlighter.

Many kanji can be read in more than one way. 生 can be sei, shō, nama or i(kiru), depending on the word. Books for young readers print tiny hiragana beside hard kanji to show how to say them. These helpers are called furigana.

There is even a fourth set of symbols in daily use: rōmaji, the Roman letters of English. You see them in company names, on station signs for visitors, and when people type. Most Japanese people type by spelling words out in Roman letters on a keyboard, and the computer turns them into kana and kanji.

The women who wrote in kana

In the 800s and 900s, men at the Japanese court were expected to write formal Chinese. Hiragana was seen as less serious and was used widely by women. Women writers used it to produce some of the greatest books in Japanese.

Around the early 1000s, a court lady known as Murasaki Shikibu wrote The Tale of Genji, a long story about life at the emperor's court in what is now Kyoto. It is often called one of the world's first novels. It was written mostly in hiragana, the script men looked down on.

Learning thousands of characters

The Japanese government keeps an official list of 2,136 kanji for everyday use, last updated in 2010. Children learn about a thousand of them in primary school and the rest in secondary school.

They also learn all 46 basic hiragana and 46 katakana, usually in their first year. Hiragana come first, so a six-year-old can write any word in hiragana even before learning its kanji. Japanese students also practise brush writing, called shodō, a form of calligraphy.

Up, down and sideways

Traditionally, Japanese was written in vertical columns, from top to bottom, starting on the right. Many novels and newspapers still are, and so are many manga, which are read from what an English reader would call the back of the book. Textbooks on maths and science, and most websites, use horizontal lines from left to right instead.

Japanese writing has also given the world new words. "Emoji" is Japanese for "picture character". The first popular set of emoji was designed for Japanese mobile phones in 1999, before they spread worldwide as emojis on every phone.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Japanese is a dialect of Chinese, because it uses Chinese characters.

Truth: Japanese and Chinese are unrelated languages with very different grammar and sounds. Japan borrowed Chinese characters long ago, the way English borrowed the Latin alphabet, and then built its own scripts too.

Try this

Find a manga or a Japanese product label at home or in a library. Look for three kinds of writing. Kanji look busy and squarish, hiragana are soft and curvy, and katakana are sharp and angular. Count how many of each you can spot on one page.
