Why is glue sticky?

Glue works by doing two jobs at once. It has to cling to the things you're joining, and it has to cling to itself.

The short answer

When glue is runny, it can flow into every tiny bump and crack on a surface. Even paper or wood that feels smooth is full of microscopic hills and valleys. The glue seeps into them and gets very close to the surface itself. Up close, its molecules are attracted to the molecules of the surface, a pull called adhesion.

Then the glue changes from liquid to solid. Some glues dry as water or another liquid escapes. Others harden through a chemical reaction. As they set, the glue's own molecules grip each other tightly, a pull called cohesion. Now you have a solid bridge, gripping one surface on one side and the other surface on the other.

A good glue needs both kinds of grip. If either one fails, the join comes apart.

Surprising but true

Superglue was discovered by accident during World War Two, when scientists were trying to make clear plastic gun sights and kept finding that everything stuck together.

The first glue makers

People have been making glue since long before history was written. In Italy, archaeologists have found stone tools with traces of birch tar on them, left by Neanderthals more than 100,000 years ago. Birch tar is made by heating birch bark without much air, until a sticky black goo oozes out. It was used to fix stone blades onto wooden handles.

Later peoples boiled animal skins and bones to make glue, and the ancient Egyptians used it on furniture. In India, craftspeople have long used natural resins from trees, and in many homes a paste of cooked rice or flour still sticks paper together. Stone Age toolmakers were, in a sense, the first chemists.

How modern glues set

White school glue is sold as PVA, and under brand names like Fevicol in India and Elmer's in the USA. It is tiny bits of sticky plastic floating in water. As the water soaks away and evaporates, the plastic bits crowd together and fuse into a clear, bendy film.

Superglue works differently. It reacts with the tiny amount of water that is almost always present in the air and on surfaces. That reaction links its molecules into long chains within seconds. Epoxy glue comes in two tubes. Mixed together, the two liquids react and harden into a tough plastic.

What is plastic made of?Almost every plastic is built the same way: small molecules linked into chains thousands of links long.

Happy accidents

In 1942, the American chemist Harry Coover was trying to make clear plastic for gun sights. One of his chemicals stuck to everything and was set aside as useless. Years later, in 1951, Coover and a colleague came across it again and saw what it could be. It went on sale in 1958 as superglue.

Sticky notes started with a failure too. In 1968, Spencer Silver at the American company 3M made a glue that was too weak. It stuck, but peeled off easily. Years later, his colleague Art Fry used it on bookmarks for his church songbook, so they wouldn't fall out. The little yellow notes went on sale in 1980.

Nature's glues

Living things make glue too. Mussels in tide pools spin threads tipped with a protein glue that holds firm underwater, something most human glues can't do. Scientists are copying it to make glues for surgery.

Spiders coat parts of their webs with sticky droplets to trap insects. Some caddisfly larvae glue tiny stones into tube-shaped homes. And geckos climb walls without any glue at all, using millions of tiny hairs on their toes that are attracted to the surface, the same pull that helps glue hold on.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Glue doesn't stick to the inside of its bottle because the bottle is made of something special.

Truth: The main reason is that most glue needs to dry or react before it sticks, and the closed bottle stops that happening. Many glue bottles are also made of plastics like polythene that glue grips poorly, which helps a little.

Try this

Make your own paste. Mix two spoons of flour with about two spoons of water in a small bowl until it is smooth and thick, like curd. Use it to stick strips of newspaper or coloured paper together. Leave it overnight and see how firmly it holds once the water has dried.
