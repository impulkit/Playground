Who is Ruby Bridges?

On 14 November 1960, a six-year-old girl walked into a school in New Orleans guarded by four US marshals, while an angry crowd shouted outside.

The short answer

Ruby Bridges is an American woman who, as a six-year-old in 1960, became the first Black child to attend William Frantz Elementary School in New Orleans, Louisiana. Until then, it had been a school for white children only.

Her first day, on 14 November 1960, became one of the most famous moments of the American civil rights movement. Crowds of adults shouted at her. Four US marshals, sent by the federal government, walked her up the steps. She went back every day, for the whole year.

Today Ruby Bridges travels and speaks to schoolchildren about fairness and getting along.

Surprising but true

For almost her whole first year at William Frantz Elementary, Ruby Bridges was the only child in her class. Her teacher taught her alone.

Why she needed guards

In the American South, Black and white children went to separate schools, by law. Schools for Black children usually got less money, older books and fewer teachers. In 1954, the US Supreme Court ruled in a case called Brown v. Board of Education that this was against the Constitution.

But many places dragged their feet. New Orleans did not begin mixing its schools until 1960, and only after a judge ordered it. Black children who wanted to transfer had to pass a hard test. Ruby passed. She was one of only six chosen, and two of the six later decided not to go.

The other three girls started at a different school that same morning. Ruby went to William Frantz alone.

What is courage?Brave people feel fear just like everyone else. Courage is what they do while their heart is still pounding.

A class of one

When Ruby arrived, almost all the white parents pulled their children out. Many teachers refused to teach her. One agreed: Barbara Henry, a young teacher who had just moved from Boston.

For most of the year, Mrs Henry and Ruby sat in a classroom together, just the two of them. They read, did sums and played games. Ruby has said she did not understand at first why the school was so empty. She was not even allowed to go out to play at break, for her safety.

Every morning, protesters waited outside. Ruby later said she thought the noise was like Mardi Gras, the big carnival that fills New Orleans with parades.

A family that paid a price

Standing up cost Ruby's family dearly. Her father lost his job at a petrol station. Her grandparents, who farmed in Mississippi, were forced off the land they had worked on. A local shop told her family not to come back.

But many people helped too. Neighbours, both Black and white, babysat, watched the house and walked behind the marshals' car. A child doctor named Robert Coles met Ruby every week to talk about how she was feeling. He was struck by one thing she told him: she prayed every day for the people shouting at her.

The painting in the White House

In 1964, the American artist Norman Rockwell painted a picture of Ruby's walk to school. It shows a small girl in a white dress and shoes, with a ruler and pencils, between four tall marshals whose faces are cut off by the frame. Behind her is a wall with a thrown tomato and an ugly slur scrawled on it.

The painting, called The Problem We All Live With, is now one of the most famous paintings in American history. In 2011, it hung outside the Oval Office in the White House, and Ruby, then in her fifties, stood beside it.

What came next

By the second year, more children came back to the school, both Black and white. Ruby finished elementary school there, later worked as a travel agent, and raised four sons. In 1999 she started the Ruby Bridges Foundation to promote respect between children of all backgrounds.

Her story sits alongside other ordinary people who refused to back down. Five years earlier, Rosa Parks had refused to give up her seat on a bus in Montgomery, Alabama.

Myth or fact?

Myth: After the US Supreme Court banned school segregation in 1954, schools were mixed straight away.

Truth: Many states and school boards resisted for years. Six years later, Ruby Bridges still needed federal marshals to get through the door, and some schools stayed segregated well into the 1970s.
