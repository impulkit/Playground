Why are some people good at directions?

London taxi drivers memorise about 25,000 streets, and brain scans show a part of their brain used for maps actually grows.

The short answer

Being good at directions is partly a skill that grows with practice. Your brain builds an inner map using special cells in an area called the hippocampus, deep inside each side of the brain. The more you explore and pay attention to where things are, the stronger that inner map gets.

Some people are naturally better at it than others, and genes may play a part. But where you grew up, how much you walked around as a child and how much you rely on phone maps all seem to matter a lot.

Surprising but true

In one Australian Aboriginal language, Guugu Yimithirr, people do not say left or right. They say north, south, east or west, even for something on their own body.

A GPS inside your head

In 1971, scientist John O'Keefe found cells in the brains of rats that fired only when the rat was in one particular spot. He called them place cells. Later, in 2005, May-Britt and Edvard Moser in Norway found grid cells. They fire in a pattern of triangles as an animal moves, like the squares on graph paper.

Together, these cells help the brain know where you are and which way you are heading. Other cells fire depending on which direction your head is facing, a bit like a compass. In 2014 the three scientists shared the Nobel Prize for their discoveries. Humans have the same kinds of cells.

The taxi drivers' brains

To drive a black cab in London, drivers must pass a test called "The Knowledge". They learn about 25,000 streets and thousands of landmarks, and it often takes three or four years. They may not use a map or GPS during the test.

In 2000, scientists in London scanned taxi drivers' brains. The back part of their hippocampus was larger than in other people, and the longer they had driven, the bigger it tended to be. Later studies followed trainees and found the change appeared in those who passed. It showed that learning a city can actually reshape the brain.

Where you grew up matters

In 2016, scientists launched a phone game called Sea Hero Quest, where players steer a boat to find sea creatures. About 4 million people played it. It became one of the biggest studies of navigation ever.

Researchers found that people who grew up in the countryside, or in cities with old, twisting streets, were better at finding their way on average. People who grew up in neat grid cities, like many in the USA, were a little worse. India's best-known grid city is Chandigarh, planned with straight roads and numbered sectors in the 1950s. Practising with complicated routes as a child seems to train the brain.

The game also showed differences between countries. Men did slightly better than women on average, but the gap was smaller in countries where men and women are more equal. That suggests culture and practice explain much of it.

Does GPS make us worse?

Some studies suggest people who follow turn-by-turn phone directions all the time build weaker inner maps. When you just obey "turn left in 200 metres", your brain does not need to work out where you are.

Some cultures train direction from early childhood. Speakers of Guugu Yimithirr in Australia always use compass directions, so they always know which way is north. Pacific Islanders once crossed thousands of kilometres of ocean by reading stars, waves and birds.

Animals have their own tricks. Homing pigeons can find their way back over hundreds of kilometres, perhaps using the Sun, smells and the Earth's magnetic field. Your phone, meanwhile, finds you by timing signals from satellites far above, as explained in how GPS works. Turn it off on a short, safe walk with an adult, and your hippocampus gets the workout instead.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Some people are just born with no sense of direction, and nothing can change it.

Truth: Practice makes a big difference. People who walk, explore and use their own memory instead of following phone directions all the time tend to get better at finding their way.

Try this

Without looking at a map, draw the route from your home to your school or a nearby shop, with landmarks. Then compare it with a real map or a family member's drawing. Notice what you remembered, what you missed and what you got in the wrong place.
