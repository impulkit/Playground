Why are there Celsius and Fahrenheit?

When Anders Celsius invented his temperature scale, water boiled at 0 degrees and froze at 100. Other scientists flipped it round after he died.

The short answer

Different scientists invented different temperature scales in the 1700s, and different countries got used to different ones.

The Fahrenheit scale, made in 1724, puts the freezing point of water at 32 degrees and the boiling point at 212. The Celsius scale, made in 1742, puts freezing at 0 and boiling at 100, which is neater. Most of the world, including India, switched to Celsius along with the metric system.

The United States kept Fahrenheit for weather, cooking and fevers. A few other places, such as the Bahamas and the Cayman Islands, also use it. Scientists everywhere use Celsius or the Kelvin scale.

Surprising but true

At minus 40 degrees, Celsius and Fahrenheit agree. It is the only temperature where both scales show the same number.

The thermometer maker

Daniel Gabriel Fahrenheit was born in 1686 in Danzig, now Gdańsk in Poland, and worked mostly in the Netherlands. He made some of the best thermometers of his time, and he was among the first to use mercury inside them.

For his zero, he chose the coldest temperature he could reliably make in his workshop: a mixture of ice, water and a salt called ammonium chloride. He then used other fixed points, including the temperature of the human body. After some adjustments, water ended up freezing at 32 degrees and boiling at 212, with 180 degrees in between.

His scale spread through the Netherlands and Britain, and from Britain to its colonies, including America and India.

The upside-down scale

Anders Celsius was a Swedish astronomer. In 1742 he suggested a simpler scale with 100 steps between the freezing and boiling points of water. But in his version, water boiled at 0 and froze at 100. Colder things had higher numbers.

Celsius died in 1744. Soon after, other scientists, including the famous Swedish naturalist Carl Linnaeus, turned the scale the right way up. For a long time it was called "centigrade", meaning "a hundred steps". In 1948, it was officially renamed Celsius in his honour.

The coldest possible cold

Cold has a limit. As things cool, their atoms move more slowly. At about −273.15 °C, atoms would have the least movement possible. This is called absolute zero.

In 1848, the British scientist William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, proposed a scale starting there. The Kelvin scale uses the same size of degree as Celsius, but its zero is absolute zero. Water freezes at about 273 kelvin. Scientists use it because it has no negative numbers. Nothing can be colder than zero.

Converting between them

To change Celsius to Fahrenheit, multiply by 9, divide by 5, then add 32. So 30 °C is 86 °F. To go the other way, subtract 32 and multiply by 5/9.

The two scales cross at one point: −40 degrees is the same in both. Very cold places, like parts of Siberia and Canada, sometimes reach that temperature.

Mixing scales in daily life

Even in Celsius countries, Fahrenheit hangs around. In India, many people still check a fever in Fahrenheit and know that about 98.6 °F is normal. Doctors and older fever thermometers often use it.

That 98.6 comes from a German doctor, Carl Wunderlich, who published his measurements in 1868. He found an average of 37 °C, which is 98.6 °F. A 2020 study in the United States suggested the average there has slowly fallen since then, to a little below 37 °C. Normal temperature also varies from person to person and through the day, so if you feel unwell, a trusted adult or doctor is the best judge.

Weather can be extreme in either scale. In May 2016, Phalodi in Rajasthan recorded 51 °C, India's highest temperature on record. That's about 124 °F, hot enough to make desert sand painful to touch.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Everyone's normal body temperature is exactly 98.6 °F, or 37 °C.

Truth: That number comes from a German doctor's measurements in the 1800s. Normal temperature varies from person to person and through the day, and recent studies suggest the average today is a little lower than 37 °C.

Try this

Fill three bowls with water, one cold, one lukewarm and one warm (not hot). Put one hand in the cold bowl and one in the warm bowl for a minute. Then put both hands in the lukewarm bowl. One hand feels warm and the other cool. This is why scientists need thermometers instead of trusting their skin.
