What is rainwater harvesting?

One millimetre of rain falling on one square metre of roof gives you exactly one litre of water, and over a monsoon that adds up to tankers' worth.

The short answer

Rainwater harvesting means catching rain where it falls instead of letting it run into drains. In most systems, the rain lands on a roof, flows through gutters and pipes, and is filtered to remove leaves and dirt. Then it goes one of two ways.

It can be stored in a tank and used for washing, gardening or flushing toilets. Or it can be sent down into the ground through a pit or an old well, where it soaks in and refills the groundwater that wells and borewells draw from. Many homes and schools in India do both. It is an old idea that has become important again as cities grow and wells run dry.

Surprising but true

A 100 square metre roof in Chennai catches more than a lakh litres of rain in an average year, roughly what a family of four might use in several months.

Doing the maths

The calculation is simple. One millimetre of rain on one square metre gives one litre of water. Chennai gets roughly 1,400 mm of rain in an average year. A roof of 100 square metres, a little bigger than a badminton court, could catch about 1.4 lakh litres.

Not all of it can be saved. Some splashes away or evaporates, and the first rain of the season is usually thrown away because it washes off a dirty roof. Even so, a well-built system can save most of what falls. During heavy monsoon storms, that also means less water flooding the streets.

How a system works

Gutters along the roof edge collect rain. A pipe carries it down to a filter, often a box or drum packed with layers of gravel, coarse sand and sometimes charcoal. A small device called a first-flush diverter can send the dirtiest water away before the rest is saved.

From the filter, water flows into a covered tank or into a recharge pit. A recharge pit is a hole a few metres deep, filled with stones and sand, that lets water seep slowly into the earth. Tanks must be kept tightly closed so mosquitoes can't breed in them.

An old Indian idea

People in India were harvesting rain long before pipes and pumps. In the Thar Desert, families built kunds, round underground tanks with a sloping catchment around them, to store rain for the dry months. Tamil Nadu has thousands of old irrigation tanks called eris. In Gujarat and Rajasthan, people built grand stepwells to reach water stored in the ground.

In the 1980s and 1990s, villagers in the Alwar district of Rajasthan, working with an organisation led by Rajendra Singh, repaired and built hundreds of small earthen dams called johads. These hold rainwater long enough for it to soak in. Local streams that had dried up began to flow again for more of the year.

Chennai learns a lesson

Chennai has no big river to rely on, and its groundwater was falling fast. In 2003, Tamil Nadu made rooftop rainwater harvesting compulsory for buildings. Many people saw water levels in their wells rise in the years that followed.

But rules only work if systems are looked after. In 2019, after weak rains, Chennai's main reservoirs almost dried up and the city depended on tankers and even a water train. That crisis pushed many families to repair old pits and clean blocked filters.

Around the world

In parts of the USA, rainwater laws can surprise people. In Colorado, where rivers are shared out by strict water rights, homes were only allowed to use rain barrels from 2016, and only two barrels each. Texas, on the other hand, encourages rain harvesting with tax breaks.

Cities from Bengaluru to Berlin now ask new buildings to catch rain. Each roof only adds a little. But when thousands of roofs send rain into the ground, the water that feeds a neighbourhood's borewells can slowly start to rise.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Rainwater from a roof is pure, so you can drink it straight away.

Truth: Rain picks up dust, leaves and bird droppings from roofs and pipes. Harvested water is great for gardens, toilets and refilling groundwater, but it needs filtering and treatment before anyone drinks it.

Try this

Put a straight-sided container, such as a clean tin or jar, in an open spot before it rains. After the rain, measure the depth of water in millimetres with a ruler. Multiply that by the area of your roof in square metres (ask an adult to help estimate it) to see how many litres fell on your home.
