How does an escalator work?

The steps of an escalator never stop. They fold flat at the top, slide underneath and come back up.

The short answer

An escalator is a moving loop of steps fixed to a chain. An electric motor at the top turns gear wheels that pull the chain round and round, like the chain on a bicycle. The steps ride up one side of the loop and return underneath.

Each step has four small wheels. The front pair runs on one track, the back pair on another. On the sloping part, the two tracks are set at different heights, so the step stays level and you can stand on it. Near the top and bottom, the tracks come together. The steps flatten into a moving walkway, then tip under the floor and head back round.

A rubber handrail runs on its own loop, driven by the same motor so it moves at nearly the same speed as the steps.

Surprising but true

The word escalator was once a company's brand name. Otis owned it until 1950, when a US ruling said it had become an ordinary word that anyone could use.

The comb at the top

Look closely at an escalator step and you'll see deep grooves running along it. At each end, the steps slide under a metal plate with teeth like a comb, which fit into those grooves. The comb lifts your shoes off as the step disappears, so nothing gets pulled in.

That is why escalator signs warn you to step off promptly and keep loose laces and long clothes away from the edges. Yellow lines along the sides mark the gap where the step meets the wall. There's an emergency stop button at each end, usually red, that anyone can press if someone falls.

Fairground beginnings

In 1896, the American inventor Jesse Reno built a moving ramp at Coney Island, an amusement park in New York. It was a sloping belt with ridges, not steps, and people rode it for fun. Around the same time, Charles Seeberger designed a moving staircase with flat steps. He worked with the Otis company, which also made lifts, and their machine was shown at the great Paris Exhibition of 1900.

Seeberger named it the "escalator", from scala, the Latin word for steps. The name became so common that in 1950 the US Patent Office ruled that Otis could no longer own it.

Escalators under the city

Escalators move far more people than lifts, because they never stop to wait or open doors. That makes them perfect for busy train stations. In London, the Underground has hundreds of them, some descending deep below the streets. Moscow's metro has some of the longest in the world, taking more than two minutes to ride.

In India, escalators are now common at metro stations in Delhi, Bengaluru and other cities, and at busy stations on Indian Railways. For many people, their first escalator ride was at a mall or a new metro station, and it can feel strange at first. Holding the handrail and stepping on with a confident stride helps.

Standing still or walking

In many cities, people stand on one side and leave the other free for walkers. In London it's stand on the right. In Delhi and Mumbai, the habit varies by station.

Engineers have studied what works best. In 2015 and 2016, London tried a test at Holborn, which has a very tall escalator. Staff asked everyone to stand on both sides at rush hour. Far more people got up per minute, because the walking lane had often been half empty. On short escalators, though, walking up may still help the crowd move.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Walking on an escalator always gets everyone there faster.

Truth: In a 2015–16 trial at Holborn station in London, asking people to stand on both sides moved more passengers up a very tall escalator at rush hour. On long escalators, few people choose to walk, so the walking lane is often half empty. On short ones, walking may make more sense.

Try this

On your next escalator ride with an adult, watch the steps at the top. Count how many lie flat before they slide under the comb plate, and look at the grooves on each step and the teeth of the comb. Hold the handrail and notice whether your hand stays level with your feet or creeps ahead.
