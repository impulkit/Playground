Who was Kalpana Chawla?

A girl from Karnal who loved aeroplanes became the first woman born in India to fly into space.

The short answer

Kalpana Chawla was an Indian-born American engineer and NASA astronaut. She grew up in Karnal, Haryana, studied aeronautical engineering in Chandigarh, and moved to the United States for further study. In 1997 she flew on the Space Shuttle Columbia and became the first woman born in India to travel to space.

Her second flight, in 2003, ended in tragedy. Columbia broke apart as it returned to Earth, and all seven astronauts on board died. Kalpana Chawla was 40. She is remembered in India and the United States as someone who followed a big dream from a small town, and as a gifted engineer and pilot.

Surprising but true

Before becoming an astronaut, Kalpana Chawla was a certified flight instructor, licensed to fly seaplanes and gliders as well as ordinary aeroplanes.

Growing up in Karnal

Kalpana was the youngest of four children. As a girl she was drawn to aeroplanes and liked drawing them. After school in Karnal, she joined Punjab Engineering College in Chandigarh in the late 1970s to study aeronautical engineering, the science of how aircraft fly. She was one of very few women in her course.

In 1982 she moved to the United States. She earned a master's degree from the University of Texas at Arlington and then a PhD in aerospace engineering from the University of Colorado in 1988. She later became an American citizen.

Engineer and pilot

After her PhD, Kalpana worked at NASA's Ames Research Center in California. She used computers to study how air flows around aircraft wings and bodies, especially when planes take off and land vertically.

She loved flying for its own sake. She held licences to fly aeroplanes, seaplanes and gliders and was a certified flight instructor. Her husband, Jean-Pierre Harrison, was a flying instructor, and they flew together for fun.

NASA picked her as an astronaut in December 1994, and she began training the next year.

Two flights on Columbia

Her first spaceflight, STS-87, launched in November 1997. Over about 16 days, the crew studied how weightlessness affects plants, materials and other experiments. Kalpana operated the shuttle's robotic arm. While in orbit, she spoke by phone with India's prime minister, I. K. Gujral.

Her second flight, STS-107, launched on 16 January 2003. The seven-person crew worked around the clock in two shifts, running about 80 experiments. Kalpana Chawla spent about 30 days in space across her two missions.

The Columbia disaster

On 1 February 2003, Columbia was coming home. As it plunged through the upper air over Texas at many times the speed of sound, it broke apart. All seven crew members died.

Investigators found the cause. During launch, a piece of foam insulation had broken off the shuttle's huge fuel tank and hit the edge of the left wing, making a hole. On re-entry, scorching hot gas got inside the wing and destroyed it. NASA made big safety changes, and the Space Shuttle programme ended in 2011.

How she is remembered

In 2003, India renamed its first dedicated weather satellite Kalpana-1 in her honour. A medical college in Karnal, a hall at her old university in Texas, an asteroid and a cargo spacecraft that flew to the space station in 2020 all carry her name.

Many young people, especially girls in India, say her story made the idea of becoming an engineer or astronaut feel possible. Others have since followed her into space, including Sunita Williams, whose father came from Gujarat, and Shubhanshu Shukla. Shukla flew to the space station in 2025 and is training for India's own Gaganyaan programme.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Kalpana Chawla was the first Indian in space.

Truth: The first Indian citizen in space was Rakesh Sharma, in 1984. Kalpana Chawla, an American citizen by then, was the first woman born in India to go to space, in 1997.

Try this

Fold three paper planes with different wing shapes, such as wide wings, narrow wings and wings with the tips folded up. Throw each one gently from the same spot, five times, and measure how far it goes. That is what aerospace engineers like Kalpana Chawla did, with computers and wind tunnels.
