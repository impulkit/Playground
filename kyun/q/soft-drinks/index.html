Why are fizzy drinks fizzy?

Every bottle of soda holds a gas squeezed in under pressure, waiting for the moment you open the cap to escape.

The short answer

Fizzy drinks are fizzy because of carbon dioxide, the same gas you breathe out. At the factory, the gas is forced into cold, sweetened water under high pressure, and the bottle is sealed fast. Under that pressure, far more gas dissolves in the liquid than it normally could.

When you open the bottle, you hear a hiss: that is the pressure escaping. Now the liquid holds more gas than it can keep, so the extra gas slowly comes out as bubbles. Some of the carbon dioxide also forms a weak acid in the water, called carbonic acid. It gives soda water its sharp, slightly tingly taste on the tongue.

Surprising but true

The goli soda bottle sold in Indian towns is sealed by a glass marble, held tightly against a rubber ring by the pressure of the gas inside.

Squeezing gas into water

Gas dissolves better in liquid when it is pushed in hard, and when the liquid is cold. Scientists describe this with a rule called Henry's law. A sealed bottle of cola is under several times the normal air pressure, a bit like a car tyre.

Bubbles need a place to start. They form on tiny scratches, specks of dust or rough surfaces, which scientists call nucleation sites. That is why bubbles rise in streams from the same spots on the side of a glass, and why dropping certain sweets into a bottle of soda can make it foam out.

A scientist next to a brewery

In 1767, the English scientist Joseph Priestley lived next door to a brewery in Leeds. Beer gives off carbon dioxide as it ferments, and a layer of the gas lay over the vats. Priestley hung a bowl of water above it and found the water picked up a pleasant, fizzy taste.

He later worked out how to make carbonated water at home and published his method in 1772. A few years later, in 1783, a watchmaker named Johann Jacob Schweppe started making soda water to sell in Geneva. The company named after him still exists.

Why are bubbles round?Blow a bubble through a square wand and it still comes out round. The bubble's skin is quietly solving a geometry problem.

From pharmacy to the world

In the 1800s, soda water was thought to be good for health and was sold in American pharmacies, with flavoured syrups mixed in at a counter called a soda fountain. In 1886, a pharmacist named John Pemberton created Coca-Cola in Atlanta, Georgia. Pepsi followed in 1893 in North Carolina.

Bottling made these drinks travel. By the middle of the 20th century, American colas were sold in almost every country, helped by enormous advertising budgets.

India's own fizz

In 1977, Coca-Cola stopped selling in India after a dispute over new government rules for foreign companies. Indian firms filled the gap. Parle launched Thums Up, a strong, spicy cola, to sit beside its orange Gold Spot and lemony Limca. When Coca-Cola returned in 1993, it bought these brands, and Thums Up is still one of the best-selling soft drinks in India.

Long before big brands, there was goli soda. Its thick glass bottle, called a Codd-neck bottle after the Englishman Hiram Codd who patented it in 1872, has a marble trapped in its neck. The gas pushes the marble up against a rubber seal. To open it, the seller presses the marble down with a pop, a sound that many Indian adults still connect with summer holidays.

How much sugar

A regular 330 ml can of cola contains about 35 g of sugar, roughly seven teaspoons. The cold temperature and the acid make that sweetness taste less strong than it really is. Sugary, acidic sips through the day are hard on teeth, so health experts suggest keeping these drinks as an occasional treat, and the bubbles are just as fizzy in plain soda water.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A tooth left in cola will dissolve overnight.

Truth: Cola is acidic and can soften tooth enamel over time, especially if you sip it slowly all day, but a tooth will not vanish overnight. The real harm comes from frequent sugary, acidic drinks with no break for saliva to repair the teeth.

Try this

Pour a glass of soda water and drop in a raisin or a grain of uncooked rice. Watch bubbles gather on its rough surface, lift it to the top, pop, and let it sink again. Count how many times it rises in five minutes.
