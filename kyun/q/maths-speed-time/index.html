How do you work out how long a journey takes?

Chandrayaan-3 took about 40 days to reach the Moon, while Apollo 11 took about 4. Both answers come from the same simple rule.

The short answer

To work out how long a journey takes, divide the distance by the speed. If a train goes 300 km at an average of 100 km/h, it takes 300 ÷ 100 = 3 hours.

The same rule can be turned around. Speed is distance divided by time, and distance is speed multiplied by time. Some people remember it with a triangle: D at the top, S and T at the bottom. Cover the one you want, and the other two show you what to do.

The tricky parts are not the division. They are using the right units, turning decimals into minutes, and remembering that real journeys speed up and slow down.

Surprising but true

If you drive 30 km/h one way and 60 km/h back, your average speed for the round trip is 40 km/h, not 45.

Turning hours into minutes

Say a family drives 90 km to visit their grandparents at an average of 60 km/h. 90 ÷ 60 = 1.5. So the trip takes 1.5 hours.

Here is where many people slip. 1.5 hours is not 1 hour 50 minutes. The .5 means half an hour, and half of 60 minutes is 30. So the answer is 1 hour 30 minutes. In the same way, 0.25 hours is 15 minutes and 0.1 hours is 6 minutes. It is a useful trick to practise with fractions.

Units must match too. If the distance is in metres and the speed is in km/h, convert one first. Otherwise the answer is a thousand times wrong.

Average speed, not top speed

A car might zoom along at 100 km/h on a highway and then crawl at 10 km/h through a town. What matters for journey time is the average speed for the whole trip. That is total distance divided by total time.

Averages can trick you. Drive 60 km to a town at 30 km/h, which takes 2 hours. Drive back at 60 km/h, which takes 1 hour. You've gone 120 km in 3 hours, so your average is 40 km/h. Most people guess 45. The slow half counts more because you spend longer doing it.

Map apps on phones make these calculations all the time, using live traffic speeds from other phones on the road, as explained in how GPS works.

Fast humans and faster light

In 2009, Usain Bolt ran 100 metres in 9.58 seconds, still the world record in 2025. That works out at about 10.4 metres every second, or roughly 37.6 km/h on average. At his fastest moment he reached about 44 km/h, quicker than many cars drive in city traffic.

Light is far faster. It travels about 300,000 km every second. The Sun is about 150 million km away. So sunlight takes about 150,000,000 ÷ 300,000 = 500 seconds to reach us. That is 8 minutes and 20 seconds. The sunlight you see right now left the Sun before you started reading this page.

The slow road to the Moon

The Moon is about 384,000 km away. In 1969, Apollo 11 got there in about four days, using a huge rocket that blasted it straight across.

India's Chandrayaan-3 launched on 14 July 2023 and landed on 23 August, about 40 days later. That was not because it was slow. ISRO used a smaller rocket, so the spacecraft looped around Earth in bigger and bigger orbits, picking up speed each time, before swinging out to the Moon. A longer path used far less fuel.

So the shortest trip is not always the best one. And the strangest part? Spacecraft heading to the outer planets often fly past other planets first, stealing a little speed from each. That turns journey-time maths into a puzzle of speed and distance across the whole Solar System.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If you go 30 km/h on the way there and 60 km/h on the way back, your average speed is 45 km/h.

Truth: You spend twice as long on the slow half, so it counts for more. For a 60 km trip each way, you take 2 hours there and 1 hour back, covering 120 km in 3 hours. That is 40 km/h.

Try this

Measure a 20-metre stretch in a park or playground with a tape or by counting steps. Time yourself walking it, then running it. Divide 20 by your time in seconds to get your speed in metres per second, then multiply by 3.6 to turn it into km/h.
