Who was Raja Ram Mohan Roy?

A Bengali scholar who knew at least five languages spent years campaigning against the custom of sati, and in 1829 the colonial government banned it.

The short answer

Raja Ram Mohan Roy was a scholar and reformer from Bengal who lived from about 1772 to 1833. He is often called one of the makers of modern India. He campaigned against sati, the custom in some communities of a widow dying on her husband's funeral pyre. He argued for women's rights, modern education and a free press, and started a new religious movement, the Brahmo Samaj.

He did all this at a time when the East India Company ruled Bengal, and many of the debates he started went on long after his death.

Surprising but true

Ram Mohan Roy died in Bristol, England, in 1833, and his tomb there is built like a small Indian temple pavilion, or chhatri.

A boy with many languages

Ram Mohan Roy was born in the village of Radhanagar in Bengal, into a Brahmin family. As a young man he studied Persian and Arabic, the languages of government and learning under Mughal rule, and Sanskrit, the language of Hindu scripture. Later he learned English, and he is said to have studied Hebrew and Greek too.

Reading so widely made him ask questions. He found that the oldest Hindu texts, the Upanishads, spoke of one formless God. He began to argue that many later customs, including idol worship and some harsh rules for women, were not found in these texts at all.

Against sati

Sati was not practised everywhere in India, but in parts of Bengal in the early 1800s, hundreds of cases were recorded each year. Many widows were pressured into it by relatives. Ram Mohan Roy wrote pamphlets showing that the scriptures did not demand it, and he visited cremation grounds to persuade families to stop.

His work helped convince the Governor-General, Lord William Bentinck, who banned sati in Bengal on 4 December 1829. Many orthodox Hindus were angry and formed a group to fight the ban. They sent an appeal to London, and Roy sailed to England to argue against it. The ban stayed.

Newspapers and schools

Roy believed people needed information to think for themselves. In the early 1820s he started newspapers in Bengali and Persian. When the government brought in strict rules on printing in 1823, he protested publicly, in an early campaign for free speech in India.

He also wanted Indian students to learn science, mathematics and English alongside Indian languages. He supported new schools in Calcutta, now Kolkata, and argued that modern subjects would help India more than learning by rote.

The Brahmo Samaj

In 1828, Roy founded the Brahmo Sabha in Calcutta, later called the Brahmo Samaj. Its members met to worship one God without idols, using readings from the Upanishads and hymns. The movement attracted many thinkers, including members of the Tagore family. It played a big part in what is called the Bengal Renaissance, a burst of new ideas in literature, science and society.

Last years in England

In 1830, the Mughal emperor Akbar II, by then a ruler in name only, gave Roy the title "Raja" and sent him to Britain to plead for a bigger pension. In England, Roy met politicians and writers and was widely admired.

In 1833 he fell ill while visiting Bristol and died there. He is buried at Arnos Vale Cemetery, under a tomb shaped like a chhatri. A statue of him stands in the centre of Bristol, and the city still holds events in his memory.

How he is remembered

Many Indians remember Roy as a brave reformer. Some historians point out that he worked closely with British officials and that the reforms he pushed mainly reached educated, upper-caste families at first. Still, later reformers such as Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar, who campaigned for widows to remarry, built on the path he opened.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ram Mohan Roy wanted Indians to give up Indian traditions and copy the British.

Truth: He admired some Western ideas, like science and a free press, but he based many of his arguments on old Hindu texts, especially the Upanishads. He wanted reform from within, not to replace Indian culture.
