How did one man plant a whole forest?

In 1979 a teenager in Assam found dozens of snakes dead from the heat on a bare sandbank, and decided to plant trees there.

The short answer

Jadav Payeng did it the slow way: one seedling at a time, every day, for more than 40 years. In 1979, when he was about 16, he began planting bamboo on a bare, sandy island in the Brahmaputra river in Assam. Then he planted trees. He watered them, protected them and kept going, mostly by himself.

That sandbank is now a forest of about 550 hectares, bigger than Central Park in New York. People call it Molai forest, after his nickname. Rhinos, deer, wild boar and even tigers live there, and wild elephants pass through every year. Jadav Payeng is known across the world as the Forest Man of India.

Surprising but true

Forest officials only learned about Jadav Payeng's forest in 2008, when a herd of wild elephants they were tracking disappeared into it.

The snakes on the sandbank

The Brahmaputra is wide and powerful, and its floods shift sand and soil every year. In 1979, after a big flood, Payeng found many snakes dead on a sandbank near his home. There were no trees to give shade, and the sun had baked them.

He asked village elders what could be done. They told him that only trees could bring the island back to life. So he started, with bamboo first, because it grows quickly and its roots hold loose sand together.

A forest grows itself

For the first years, life on the sandbank was hard. Payeng carried water to the plants by hand. He also brought red ants from his village, because ants help mix and enrich the soil.

As the trees grew, the forest started to help itself. Birds came and dropped seeds. Fallen leaves made new soil. Shade kept the ground moist. More plants sprouted without anyone planting them. This is how forests spread in nature, too, only Payeng gave it a head start.

Elephants, tigers and rhinos

In 2008 forest officials were chasing a herd of about 100 wild elephants that had damaged village crops. The herd vanished into a thick forest that wasn't on any map. That's how officials met Jadav Payeng and saw what he had built.

Since then, Molai forest has become home to animals that need space and cover. Bengal tigers have been seen there, as have one-horned rhinos, the kind famous in nearby Kaziranga. Vultures and many other birds nest in its trees.

Why sandbanks need forests

The Brahmaputra carries a huge load of sand and silt down from the Himalayas. Its channels shift almost every year, swallowing fields in one place and building new sandbanks, called chapori, in another. Families in Assam often lose homes and farmland to the river.

Roots hold soft ground together, slow down floodwater and trap fresh silt. A sandbank covered in trees and grass is much harder for the river to wash away than a bare one, and Payeng's forest has come through many monsoon floods.

Living next to a forest isn't always easy, though. Elephants that shelter there sometimes raid crops and damage houses, and some villagers have asked for trees to be cut. Payeng argues that the animals need the forest, and that without it they would wander even further into farms.

Fame, but the same life

A short film called Forest Man, made in 2013, carried his story around the world. In 2015 India gave him the Padma Shri, one of its highest honours. He has been invited to speak to students in many countries.

Payeng still lives simply, keeping cows and selling milk, and he keeps planting. Erosion by the river is a big worry in Assam: nearby Majuli, the great river island, has lost much of its land over the past century.

He isn't alone in this kind of quiet, stubborn work. In Karnataka, a woman who couldn't have children of her own raised hundreds of banyan trees along a highway, and her story is told in the life of Saalumarada Thimmakka.

Myth or fact?

Myth: One person can't make any real difference to the environment.

Truth: Jadav Payeng's forest now shelters rhinos, deer and tigers, and elephants pass through it every year. Small actions, repeated every day for decades, can grow into something huge.

Try this

Plant a seed and keep a growth diary. Put a few soaked moong beans or a mango seed in a pot of soil on a sunny windowsill. Water it a little each day and measure it every few days with a ruler. Draw what you see. In a month you'll have a tiny piece of Jadav Payeng's work.
