How did fast food begin?

Two thousand years ago, people in Pompeii grabbed hot snacks from street counters, and archaeologists have found the painted counters still standing.

The short answer

Ready-to-eat food for busy people is older than most cities. Romans bought hot stews at street counters, Chinese traders slurped noodles at market stalls, and Indian towns have had snack sellers for as long as anyone can remember.

What we now call fast food, though, took shape in the USA in the 20th century. Burger chains like White Castle, starting in 1921, and McDonald's, which reorganised its kitchen in 1948, worked out how to make the same food quickly, cheaply and identically, again and again. Cars, highways and advertising carried the idea across America, and later across the planet.

Surprising but true

The first McDonald's restaurants in India, opened in 1996, served no beef at all. Instead of the Big Mac, the chain invented the "Maharaja Mac", first made with lamb.

Snacks in Pompeii

In Roman towns, many poor people lived in small rooms without kitchens. So they ate out. Pompeii had dozens of thermopolia, counters open to the street with big clay pots sunk into them. The pots held hot food such as stews, beans and wine.

In 2020, archaeologists in Pompeii uncovered a thermopolium painted with ducks, a rooster and a dog. Traces of pork, fish, snails and duck bones were found in its jars. The shop had been serving lunch when Vesuvius erupted in 79 CE.

What happened at Pompeii?In 79 CE a volcano buried a Roman town so quickly that bread was left in its ovens, and it stayed hidden for about 1,700 years.

India's own fast food

Long before burger chains, Indian streets were full of food made in seconds. A pani puri seller can fill and hand over a puri faster than you can eat it. Samosas, chaat and kathi rolls were all designed for eating standing up.

Mumbai's vada pav, a spicy potato fritter in a soft bun, is often called the city's burger. It is said to have been sold first by a snack seller named Ashok Vaidya near Dadar station in the 1960s, for mill workers who needed a cheap, filling lunch. Street food like this still feeds millions of Indians every day, often faster than any chain.

The burger line

In 1921, White Castle opened in Wichita, Kansas, selling small square burgers for five cents. Its owners made the kitchens spotless and open to view, to convince Americans that cheap meat could be safe.

Then, in 1948, Richard and Maurice McDonald shut their drive-in in San Bernardino, California, for a few months. They reopened with only nine items and a kitchen run like a factory. Each worker had one task. Burgers were made before anyone ordered. They called it the Speedee Service System. Henry Ford had done something similar decades earlier, when he sped up car making by moving each car past a line of workers who each fitted one part.

Going global

A milkshake-machine seller named Ray Kroc visited the brothers in 1954 and saw the future. He began selling franchises, letting other owners open copies of the restaurant using the same rules. He later bought the company. By the 1970s, chains such as McDonald's, KFC and Burger King were spreading around the world.

In India, the chains had to change. McDonald's opened its first Indian restaurant in Delhi in 1996 with no beef or pork. It added the McAloo Tikki burger and plenty of vegetarian choices. Domino's, Pizza Hut and KFC adjusted too, with paneer toppings and spicier flavours.

The trouble with speed

Fast food is cheap and tasty, but much of it is high in fat, salt and sugar and low in fibre. Eating it often is linked to health problems, which is why doctors suggest keeping junk food as an occasional treat. Big chains have also been criticised for the waste they create and for advertising aimed at children.

Some new food businesses try to keep the speed and lose the downsides. Yet the cheapest, quickest healthy meal in Mumbai may still be the home-cooked lunch that arrives by dabbawala.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Fast food is an American invention.

Truth: Cheap, ready-to-eat food sold to people in a hurry is ancient and worldwide, from Roman thermopolia to Chinese noodle stalls and Indian chaat carts. What the USA developed was the fast-food chain, with the same food, made the same way, in thousands of places.
