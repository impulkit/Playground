What are hot springs?

In a Himalayan valley, cooks at a gurdwara boil rice for pilgrims in water that comes out of the ground already steaming.

The short answer

A hot spring is a place where warm or hot water flows out of the ground. It starts as ordinary rain or snow that soaks down through cracks in the rock. Deep underground, the rock is hot, and the water heats up. Hot water is lighter than cold water, so it rises back to the surface through other cracks.

In some places the heat comes from magma, molten rock not far below the surface, as at Yellowstone in the USA or in Iceland. In others the water simply sinks very deep, because the Earth gets hotter the further down you go, roughly 25–30 °C warmer for every kilometre.

A geyser is a special kind of hot spring that erupts, shooting water and steam into the air every so often.

Surprising but true

A microbe discovered in a Yellowstone hot spring in the 1960s gave scientists a key ingredient for the DNA tests used around the world today.

How a geyser blows

A geyser needs three things: plenty of water, a strong source of heat and a narrow, twisting pipe in the rock. Water collects in the pipe and is heated from below. The weight of the water above squeezes it, so it can get hotter than 100 °C without boiling, much like water in a pressure cooker.

Eventually some of it does turn to steam. The steam pushes water out of the top, which lowers the pressure below, so a lot of very hot water suddenly flashes into steam at once. Whoosh. The geyser erupts, then slowly refills and starts again. Yellowstone's Old Faithful does this every hour or two.

The name comes from Geysir, a spring in Iceland that once shot water high into the air.

Rainbows made by microbes

Grand Prismatic Spring in Yellowstone is more than 100 m across, about as wide as a football field is long. Its centre is deep blue, ringed by yellow, orange and brown.

The colours come from microbes, living things too small to see. Different kinds survive at different temperatures, so they grow in rings as the water cools on its way out.

In the 1960s, the scientist Thomas Brock found a microbe called Thermus aquaticus living in a Yellowstone spring. It makes chemicals that keep working even when very hot. One of them later became the key ingredient in PCR, a method for copying DNA. PCR is used in crime labs, medical tests and the COVID-19 tests millions of people took. Some of the most useful microbes live in the strangest places.

India's hot springs

India has hundreds of hot springs. At Manikaran, in the Parvati valley of Himachal Pradesh, the spring water is very hot. A gurdwara there uses it to cook food for its free kitchen, and Hindu temples stand close by. Further up the valley is Kheerganga, where trekkers soak in a hot pool high in the mountains.

Rajgir in Bihar has hot springs mentioned in old Buddhist and Jain stories. In Sikkim, the springs of Yumthang are popular with visitors after a cold drive into the mountains. Many Indian springs smell of rotten eggs, from a gas called hydrogen sulphide that the water carries up from below.

Heat you can use

Hot underground water is a source of geothermal energy. In Iceland, it heats most homes. Steam from hot rocks also spins turbines to make electricity in places like California, Kenya and New Zealand. India has tested geothermal power at sites such as Puga in Ladakh.

Animals enjoy it too. In the mountains of Japan, wild snow monkeys climb into hot springs in winter to warm up.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Hot springs are always safe to swim in because the water is natural.

Truth: Some hot springs are hot enough to scald badly, and some contain harmful microbes or acids. That is why parks like Yellowstone have boardwalks and strict rules, and why people only bathe in springs that are known to be safe.

Try this

Ask an adult to fill one bottle with warm tap water and another with cold, then add a few drops of food colouring to each. Gently lay each bottle on its side in a clear bowl of room-temperature water. Watch the warm coloured water rise towards the top while the cold sinks, just as hot water rises through cracks in the ground.
