Why do we have belly buttons?

Your belly button is a scar, the very first one you ever got, and a study once found over 2,000 kinds of bacteria living in just 60 of them.

The short answer

Before you were born, you grew inside your mother for about nine months. You could not eat or breathe there, so everything you needed came through the umbilical cord, a soft tube connecting your tummy to the placenta. The placenta passed oxygen and food from your mother's blood into the cord, and carried waste away.

After birth, a baby can breathe and feed, so the cord is no longer needed. It is clamped and cut. That does not hurt, because the cord has no nerves. The little stump left behind dries up and falls off within a week or few. What remains is a scar: your belly button, or navel.

Surprising but true

Scientists who swabbed 60 belly buttons in the USA found about 2,400 kinds of bacteria, and many had never been described before.

Innies and outies

Most people have an innie, a navel that dips inwards. Some have an outie, which sticks out. The shape depends on how the skin and the tissue under it healed after the stump came off. It has nothing to do with how the cord was cut.

Some babies have a small bulge at the navel called an umbilical hernia, where a bit of tissue pushes through a gap in the tummy muscles. It looks odd but usually closes by itself within the first few years.

A rainforest in your navel

In 2012, scientists in North Carolina in the USA started the Belly Button Biodiversity project. They swabbed the navels of 60 volunteers and grew the bacteria they found. In total they counted about 2,400 kinds. Many had never been named before. One person's navel held bacteria that had only been found in soil in Japan, though he had never been there.

Most of these microbes are harmless or helpful. A belly button is warm, slightly damp and rarely scrubbed, so it makes a cosy home. The scientists compared it to a rainforest, with a few common kinds and a long tail of rare ones.

Are all germs bad?Right now, about as many bacteria live in and on you as there are cells in your body, and most of them are on your side.

Belly button fluff

Ever pulled a tiny ball of fluff from your navel? A chemist in Austria studied hundreds of samples and found it is mostly fibres from your clothes, mixed with dust, dead skin and sweat. Little hairs on your tummy act like a ratchet, scraping fibres off your shirt and pushing them towards the navel as you move.

People with hairier tummies tend to collect more. An Australian man spent years saving his own navel fluff and filled several jars, earning a world record for it.

Who else has one

Almost all mammals have belly buttons, because almost all of them grow inside their mothers attached to a placenta. Dogs, cats, cows and dolphins all have one, though fur often hides it and the scar is usually flat and thin.

Animals that hatch from eggs, like birds, crocodiles and turtles, are fed by a yolk inside the egg. They have a small yolk-sac scar for a while, but it usually fades. The odd one out is the platypus, a mammal that lays eggs, and it has no belly button at all.

Looking after it

Keeping your navel clean is easy: wash it in the shower or bath and dry it well. Deep innies can trap sweat and lint, which may smell if left for a long time. If a belly button becomes red, sore or leaks fluid, tell a parent or doctor.

For new babies, doctors and nurses usually advise keeping the cord stump clean and dry until it drops off by itself. The cord was only one part of a bigger story. In nine months, you grew from a single cell smaller than a full stop into a baby of about 3 kg, with every finger, eyelash and heartbeat in place.

Myth or fact?

Myth: An outie belly button happens because the doctor cut the cord badly.

Truth: How or where the cord is cut does not decide the shape. It depends on how the skin heals as the stump falls off, and sometimes on a small bulge of tissue called an umbilical hernia, which usually closes on its own.

Try this

Make a family survey. Ask everyone at home, kindly, whether they have an innie or an outie, and keep a tally. Then look gently at a pet dog's or cat's tummy with an adult. Most have a tiny, flat scar hidden in the fur.
