Which moon has hundreds of volcanoes?

Jupiter's moon Io has about 400 active volcanoes, and some blast plumes hundreds of kilometres into space.

The short answer

The moon with hundreds of volcanoes is Io, one of the four big moons of Jupiter. It has about 400 active volcanoes, more than any other world we know, including Earth. Some of them spray plumes of gas and dust hundreds of kilometres above the surface.

Io is about the size of our own Moon. But instead of grey dust and craters, it is covered in yellow, orange, red and black patches of sulphur and fresh lava. Photos of it are often compared to a pizza.

Io stays hot because Jupiter's gravity keeps squeezing it, heating its rock from inside.

Surprising but true

Io's volcanoes were spotted in 1979 by Linda Morabito, a navigation engineer who noticed a strange shape at the edge of a Voyager 1 photo.

Squeezed by a giant

Jupiter is more than 300 times as heavy as Earth. Io orbits close to it, going round once every 42 hours. Its path is not a perfect circle, so sometimes Io is a little closer to Jupiter and sometimes a little further away.

That means Jupiter's pull on Io keeps changing. The moon's solid surface bulges up and down, by as much as 100 metres, as it goes around. That is taller than a 30-storey building, rising and falling every day and a half.

On Earth, the Moon's pull raises the ocean tides. On Io there are no oceans, so the rock itself is pulled. All that bending creates heat, the same way a paper clip warms up when you bend it back and forth. The other big moons, Europa and Ganymede, keep tugging on Io's orbit, so the squeezing never stops.

How the volcanoes were found

Galileo Galilei discovered Io in January 1610 with one of the first telescopes. For more than 350 years it was just a dot of light. Astronomers named it after Io, a character from Greek myths, but knew almost nothing about its surface.

In March 1979, NASA's Voyager 1 spacecraft flew past. A few days later, a navigation engineer named Linda Morabito was studying an image to help work out the craft's position. She noticed a strange curved cloud rising from the edge of Io. It was a volcanic plume, the first active volcano ever seen beyond Earth.

Just days before, three scientists had published a paper predicting that tidal heating might make Io volcanic. They were right.

Lakes of lava

Io's biggest volcano is Loki Patera, a lava lake about 200 km across. That is wider than the distance from Mumbai to Pune. Its surface cools and sinks, then fresh lava wells up, in waves that sweep across the lake.

NASA's Juno spacecraft flew close to Io in December 2023 and February 2024. Its pictures showed islands in Loki Patera, and a surface so smooth that Juno saw sunlight glinting off it like glass.

Some of Io's lava seems hotter than almost any that erupts on Earth today. The famous volcano Pele has thrown up plumes about 300 km high, with material raining back down in a red ring more than 1,000 km wide.

A moon that leaks

Io loses about a tonne of material to space every second. Sulphur and oxygen from its volcanoes are swept away by Jupiter's huge magnetic field, forming a glowing doughnut of charged gas around the planet.

That doughnut, and the radiation around Jupiter, make Io a deadly place for spacecraft. Probes have to zoom in and out quickly to protect their electronics.

Io is the most extreme of Jupiter's moons, but not the only strange one. Its neighbour is icy where Io is hot, and the same squeezing may keep a hidden ocean liquid under its shell. And the strangest part? That neighbour, Europa, might be one of the best places to search for life beyond Earth.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Moons are dead, cold rocks where nothing ever happens.

Truth: Io is the most volcanically active place known in the Solar System. Other moons have geysers, ice volcanoes and hidden oceans, so moons can be some of the busiest worlds around.

Try this

Take a soft rubber ball or a lump of modelling clay and squeeze it hard in your hand again and again for a minute. Notice that it gets slightly warm. Io is heated in a similar way, by being squashed and stretched by Jupiter's gravity.
