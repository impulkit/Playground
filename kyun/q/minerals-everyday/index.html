Which minerals are in everyday things?

Your pencil, your toothpaste, your phone and the window you look through all began as rocks dug out of the ground.

The short answer

A mineral is a natural, solid substance with a fixed recipe of chemical elements, whose atoms are lined up in a repeating pattern called a crystal. Rocks are made of minerals, the way a fruit salad is made of different fruits.

Almost everything around you that isn't alive, or made from living things, came from minerals. The salt on your food is a mineral called halite. Glass is made mostly from quartz sand. The core of a pencil is graphite. Metals like iron, copper and aluminium come from minerals called ores, which are dug up and processed.

Even a smartphone holds dozens of different elements that started out inside rocks.

Surprising but true

There is no lead in a "lead" pencil. The grey core is graphite, a soft form of carbon, mixed with clay.

In the kitchen and the bathroom

Start with the dining table. Salt is crystals of halite, which is mined from old seabeds or dried from seawater in salt pans along the coasts of Gujarat and Tamil Nadu. Steel spoons are mostly iron, smelted from ores like haematite. Odisha and Jharkhand hold some of India's biggest iron ore mines.

In the bathroom, toothpaste often contains fine powders of chalk or silica, which gently scrub teeth. The fluoride in many toothpastes comes from minerals too. Talcum powder is ground-up talc, the softest mineral there is. White porcelain sinks and cups are made from a clay mineral called kaolinite, fired in a hot kiln.

How is glass made from sand?Glass is sand that has been melted and then cooled so quickly that its atoms never get the chance to line up neatly again.

The pencil that isn't lead

In the 1500s, people in Borrowdale, in northern England, found a deposit of a soft black mineral that left dark marks. They thought it was a kind of lead and called it "black lead". It was actually graphite, a form of pure carbon.

Shepherds used it to mark their sheep. Soon it was being cut into sticks and wrapped in wood. Today, pencil makers grind graphite and mix it with clay. More clay makes a harder, lighter pencil. More graphite makes a softer, darker one.

Graphite and diamond are both pure carbon. The only difference is how the atoms are arranged, yet one is soft enough to write with and the other is the hardest natural mineral on Earth.

Inside your phone

A smartphone is a small mineral museum. The screen is a tough glass made from quartz sand. The tiny chips are built on silicon, which also comes from quartz. Copper carries electricity through the wiring, and a little gold coats some contacts because it never rusts.

The battery needs lithium, and often cobalt and nickel. Magnets in the speakers and the buzzing motor use rare earth elements, which are not actually rare but are hard to separate.

Getting these minerals out of the ground has costs. Some mines damage forests and rivers. In some places, including parts of India's mica belt in Jharkhand, children have been found working in dangerous mines, a problem linked to child labour around the world. Mica is the glittery mineral that adds shimmer to some paints and cosmetics.

Hard, soft and in between

In 1812, a German scientist named Friedrich Mohs ranked ten minerals by hardness, from talc at 1 to diamond at 10. A mineral can scratch anything below it on the list. Your fingernail is about 2.5, a copper coin about 3 and steel about 5.5. Quartz, at 7, scratches glass, which is why sand blowing in the wind slowly frosts car windscreens.

Geologists still use the Mohs scale in the field. It is a quick way to tell one shiny grey rock from another with nothing but a coin.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pencil lead is made of the metal lead, so chewing a pencil can poison you.

Truth: Pencils have never contained lead. The core is graphite and clay. The name stuck because people in the 1500s thought graphite was a kind of lead.

Try this

Gather a piece of blackboard chalk, a lump of rock salt or a big salt crystal, a pebble and a smooth piece of soapstone if you have one. Try to scratch each with your fingernail, then with a copper coin. Put them in order from softest to hardest, like a mini Mohs scale.
