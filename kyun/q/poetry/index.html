What makes something a poem?

The oldest writer in history whose name we know was a poet, a priestess in Mesopotamia about 4,300 years ago.

The short answer

A poem is writing that pays special attention to sound, rhythm and shape, as well as meaning. Prose, like this paragraph, runs on until the edge of the page. A poem is usually broken into lines, and the poet decides exactly where each one ends.

Most poems use some of these tools: a beat, rhyme, repeated sounds, vivid pictures in words, and a lot of meaning packed into few words. No one tool is required. Experts have argued for centuries about where poetry stops and prose begins. In the end, a poem is writing that asks you to read slowly and listen.

Surprising but true

Before writing was common, poems helped people remember. The Vedas were passed on by heart for many centuries before anyone wrote them down, and their rhythms helped make that possible.

The oldest kind of literature

Poetry is older than writing. Before people could write things down, they needed a way to remember laws, prayers, family histories and stories. Rhythm and repetition make words stick in the memory. That is why you can recall song lyrics far more easily than a shopping list.

The oldest named author we know of, Enheduanna, was a poet. She was a priestess in the city of Ur in Mesopotamia around 2300 BCE, and hymns carrying her name were copied by scribes for centuries. In India, the hymns of the Rigveda were composed in the Vedic age and passed on by heart, in exact rhythms, long before they were written down.

In the south, the Tamil poems of the Sangam period, roughly 2,000 years old, sorted the land into five types: hills, forest, farmland, seashore and dry wasteland. Each landscape went with a mood, such as lovers meeting in the hills or waiting by the sea. A single flower named in a poem could tell readers where they were and how to feel.

Tools of the poet

Rhythm is the pattern of strong and weak beats. Sanskrit poets built strict metres, like the shloka, which has four quarters of eight syllables each. Tradition says the sage Valmiki spoke the first shloka in grief after seeing a hunter kill a bird, and went on to compose the Ramayana in that metre.

Rhyme is the matching of end sounds, like "moon" and "June". It is common in English, Hindi and Urdu poems and songs.

Imagery means pictures made of words. "The fog comes on little cat feet" is the American poet Carl Sandburg's way of showing fog creeping silently in.

Compression means saying a lot in a little. A two-line doha by Kabir or a three-line haiku can hold a whole idea.

Poems people sing

For most of history, poems were performed aloud, often with music. The bhakti poets Mirabai and Kabir composed verses that are still sung in homes and temples across India. Ghazals in Urdu and Persian are sung at concerts. Many film songs are poems set to tunes.

Rap is a modern example. Hip-hop artists use rhythm, rhyme and wordplay as carefully as any poet, often with rhymes inside lines as well as at the end. Some universities now study rap lyrics alongside older poetry.

Breaking the rules

In 1855, the American poet Walt Whitman published Leaves of Grass. It used long, loose lines without regular rhyme or metre, a style now called free verse. Many readers were shocked. Today, free verse is probably the most common kind of poem written in English.

Poets keep finding new shapes. Some make "concrete poems", where the words are arranged into a picture. Some write prose poems that look like ordinary paragraphs. Whether something counts as a poem is often settled by how it makes readers listen.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A poem has to rhyme.

Truth: Many poems don't rhyme at all. Most old Sanskrit, Greek and Latin poetry doesn't, and neither do haiku or modern free verse. Rhythm and careful word choice matter more than rhyme.

Try this

Take a sentence from your science or history book and rewrite it as a poem. Break it into short lines wherever you would pause, swap one dull word for a vivid one and read it aloud. You may find a poem was hiding in it all along.
