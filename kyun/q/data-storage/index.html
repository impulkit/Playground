How much can a memory card hold?

In 1956, a computer disk that weighed about a tonne held about as much as one phone photo. Now a card smaller than your fingernail holds hundreds of thousands.

The short answer

A memory card stores information as billions of tiny electrical charges, each one standing for a 0 or a 1. One 0 or 1 is called a bit. Eight bits make a byte, enough to store a single letter.

The cards in today's phones and cameras hold anywhere from a few gigabytes to about 1 or 2 terabytes. A terabyte is roughly a million megabytes. That is enough for hundreds of thousands of photos, or a few hundred hours of video. The biggest cards can hold more than all the books in a large school library, turned into text, many times over.

Surprising but true

The first computer hard disk, built by IBM in 1956, was about the size of two refrigerators and stored only about 3.75 megabytes.

Counting in bytes

Storage sizes climb in steps of about a thousand.

  • - A kilobyte (KB) is about a thousand bytes: a short email.
  • - A megabyte (MB) is about a million bytes: one song, or a phone photo.
  • - A gigabyte (GB) is about a billion bytes: a long film.
  • - A terabyte (TB) is about a trillion bytes.

To get a feel for those big numbers, think of each byte as a grain of rice. A gigabyte of rice would fill a small room. A terabyte would fill a large building.

A disk the size of two fridges

In 1956, IBM sold a machine called the RAMAC 350. Inside was a stack of 50 spinning metal disks, each about 60 cm across. It weighed about a tonne and was roughly as big as two refrigerators. It could store about 3.75 megabytes, about the size of a single photo from your phone.

Companies rented it rather than buying it, because it was so expensive. Earlier computers stored data on punched paper cards, where each hole stood for a bit.

How a memory card works

A memory card uses flash memory, invented by Fujio Masuoka at Toshiba in Japan in the 1980s. Inside are billions of microscopic cells built on a silicon chip. Each cell can trap a tiny bundle of electric charge, and keep it there even when the power is off.

A cell with charge might mean 0 and one without might mean 1. Newer cards squeeze in more by storing several levels of charge in each cell, so one cell can hold two, three or four bits. Nothing moves inside, which is why a memory card survives being dropped far better than the old spinning disks. Each cell does wear out slowly, though. After many thousands of rounds of being written and erased, it stops holding charge reliably, so the card quietly stops using it.

Nature's memory card

Your body has its own storage system, and it is far denser than any card. DNA stores the instructions for building you using four chemical letters instead of two. Scientists have managed to store books, pictures and even a short film in man-made DNA. One gram of DNA could in theory hold many millions of gigabytes. DNA also lasts. Scientists have read DNA from mammoth bones more than a million years old, while a memory card may lose its data after some years sitting unused in a drawer.

Reading and writing DNA is still slow and costly, so your photos are safe on silicon for now. But the idea that information can be packed into molecules is changing how engineers think about storage.

Myth or fact?

Myth: When you delete a file, it disappears from the card straight away.

Truth: Usually the device only marks that space as free, so it can be used again. The old data can stay there until something new is written on top. That is why special tools can sometimes rescue deleted photos.
