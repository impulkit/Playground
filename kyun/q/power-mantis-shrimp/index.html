Why does the mantis shrimp punch so hard?

A sea creature the length of a pencil can punch so fast that the water around its fist boils for an instant and flashes with light.

The short answer

The mantis shrimp punches hard because its arm works like a loaded catapult. Its muscles slowly squeeze a spring inside the arm while a latch holds it back. When the latch lets go, the club-shaped arm shoots forward at about 80 km/h in water, as fast as a car on a highway.

That speed, packed into a body about 10 to 15 cm long, smashes the hard shells of snails, crabs and clams. The punch is so fast it creates collapsing bubbles that strike the prey again a split second later. Few animals of any size can match it.

Surprising but true

Each punch hits twice. First the club strikes, then a collapsing bubble of vapour slams into the prey a split second later.

Not really a shrimp

Mantis shrimps are crustaceans, related to crabs, lobsters and real shrimps, but they belong to their own group. There are about 450 species. They live mostly in warm, shallow seas, including around coral reefs and along India's coasts.

Scientists sort them into two kinds. "Spearers" have sharp, spiny arms to stab soft fish. "Smashers" have heavy, hammer-like clubs to break shells. The peacock mantis shrimp, with its bright green, red and blue colours, is the most famous smasher.

The catapult inside

No muscle can contract fast enough to throw a punch like this. So the mantis shrimp cheats. A part of its arm shaped like a horse's saddle works as a spring. Muscles bend it slowly, storing energy. A tiny latch holds the arm locked.

When the latch releases, the spring snaps back and flings the club forward in less than three thousandths of a second. The club accelerates more than 10,000 times faster than an object falling under gravity. A bullet leaving a gun barrel accelerates even faster, but the shrimp's club does it underwater, where moving fast is much harder.

Bubbles that boil and glow

The club moves so fast that water can't keep up behind it. The pressure drops so low that the water briefly turns into vapour bubbles, a process called cavitation.

These bubbles collapse almost at once. As they do, they release a burst of energy, a tiny flash of light and heat of thousands of degrees for a fraction of a second. So the prey gets hit twice: first by the club, then by the shock of the collapsing bubbles. Even if the club misses, the bubbles can stun.

Unbreakable hammers

A punch like that should shatter the club itself. Yet a mantis shrimp can strike thousands of times between moults. Its club is built in layers of fibres twisted in a spiral pattern, like stacks of plywood turned slightly each time. When a crack starts, it has to twist round and round the layers instead of running straight through.

Engineers are copying this design to make lighter, tougher helmets, armour and aeroplane parts.

Eyes like nothing else

The mantis shrimp also has some of the strangest eyes on Earth. Humans have three types of colour receptors. Mantis shrimps have 12 or more. They can see ultraviolet light and polarised light, which is light vibrating in a particular direction.

Each eye moves on its own stalk and can judge distance without the other. Scientists think their eyes help them spot prey, rivals and mates in a flash.

Handle with care

Aquarium keepers tell stories of mantis shrimps cracking glass tanks, which is why some are kept behind thick acrylic. Divers call them "thumb splitters" because a careless hand can be badly cut.

Another small sea creature uses bubbles as a weapon too. The pistol shrimp snaps its claw shut so fast that it fires a jet of water and makes one of the loudest sounds in the sea.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The mantis shrimp sees colours far better than humans, because it has so many colour receptors.

Truth: It has 12 or more receptor types, but tests show it is actually worse than us at telling similar colours apart. It seems to recognise colours quickly instead of comparing them, and it can also see ultraviolet and polarised light.

Try this

Feel stored energy at work. Press your middle finger hard against your thumb, then flick it, like flicking a marble. Now try to move the same finger as fast without pressing first. The press-and-release is a tiny version of the mantis shrimp's spring-and-latch punch.
