What is street theatre?

No stage, no tickets, no curtains. A drumbeat on a street corner, a circle of curious people, and the play begins.

The short answer

Street theatre is drama performed in public places, such as street corners, markets, parks, bus stands and factory gates. The audience doesn't buy tickets. People simply stop, gather round and watch, and they are free to walk away.

In India it is often called nukkad natak, a street-corner play. The plays are short, usually 15 to 30 minutes, with few props and no costumes beyond a scarf or a cap. Actors use loud voices, songs, drums and big movements so people at the back can follow. Many plays are about everyday problems, like prices, health, schooling or elections, and they often end by asking the crowd a question.

How a nukkad natak works

A performance usually starts with noise. Actors beat a dholak, clap, sing or shout a slogan until a crowd forms. Then they make a circle and step inside it.

One actor might play three characters in five minutes, swapping a cap for a dupatta to show the change. The group can become a bus, a wall or a crowd with their bodies. There are no lights or speakers, so voices must carry over horns and street sellers. Jokes keep people from drifting off. When it ends, actors often pass around a cloth bag for donations.

Older than you'd think

Plays outdoors are very old. In medieval Europe, actors performed religious stories on wagons that rolled from one town square to the next. In Italy from the 1500s, travelling troupes of commedia dell'arte played on simple platforms in public squares, with masked characters and made-up dialogue.

India has its own long traditions of open-air performance, from the bahurupiya, who wanders in disguise, to folk forms like Tamasha and Nautanki. Ramlila plays, staged in open grounds before Dussehra, gather whole neighbourhoods.

Janam and Safdar Hashmi

Modern street theatre took off in India in the 1970s and 1980s. In 1973 a group of young people in Delhi formed Jana Natya Manch, often called Janam. Its best-known member, the writer and director Safdar Hashmi, wrote plays that were performed at hundreds of street corners and factory gates.

On 1 January 1989, Janam was performing a play called Halla Bol near Delhi when the group was attacked. Safdar Hashmi was badly hurt and died the next day. Two days later, his wife Moloyshree Hashmi and the group returned to the same place and finished the play. Thousands came to watch. Every year since, Janam has gone back to perform there on 1 January.

Plays with a purpose

Because street plays are cheap and go where people already are, they are used to spread ideas. Government departments and charities have used nukkad nataks to explain vaccines, voting, clean water and children's rights. A play in a village square can reach people who never read posters or watch adverts.

That power is also why street theatre can be controversial. Plays sometimes criticise powerful people. In a democracy, the right to perform such plays is linked to free speech, though in many places groups still need police permission to perform in public.

On American streets

In the United States, the Bread and Puppet Theater began in New York in 1963 and later moved to a farm in Vermont. It is known for giant puppets, some taller than a house, carried through streets in parades. Its founders also handed out fresh bread they had baked to the audience.

The San Francisco Mime Troupe, founded in 1959, has given free shows in the city's parks since 1962. Like a nukkad natak, its plays are funny, loud and about things in the news.

Why people stop to watch

A good street play pulls you in because it is happening right in front of you, in your own lane. You can see the actors sweat. The drums are real, the jokes are about your town, and the person laughing next to you might be your neighbour. For a few minutes, an ordinary corner becomes a theatre.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Street theatre is just amateur acting by people who couldn't get on a real stage.

Truth: Many street theatre performers are highly trained, and some famous actors began in street groups. Playing outdoors, with traffic noise and a moving crowd, takes special skills of voice, movement and timing.

Try this

With a few friends, make a five-minute street play about something in your school, such as saving water or keeping the playground clean. Start by clapping a rhythm or beating a steel plate to gather an audience. Use no props except things in your school bag, and end with a question for the crowd.
