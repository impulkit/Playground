Why do animals say different things in different languages?

An Indian dog says "bhau bhau", a Japanese dog says "wan wan" and an American dog says "woof", yet all three bark exactly alike.

The short answer

Animals don't speak in words. A bark or a crow is a noisy mix of sounds that doesn't match any human language. When people try to write it down, they have to squeeze it into the sounds their own language uses.

English speakers hear "woof". Hindi speakers hear "bhau bhau". Japanese speakers hear "wan wan". Spanish speakers hear "guau guau". None of them is wrong. Each is a best guess, shaped by the sounds a child grows up hearing.

Words that copy sounds are called onomatopoeia, from Greek words meaning "making a name". Every language has them, but each one makes them differently. The word itself is famously hard to spell, even for adults.

Surprising but true

Japanese has a word, "shiin", for the sound of complete silence.

Squeezing noises into words

Each language uses a limited set of sounds. English has the sound "th", which Japanese does not. Hindi has sounds like "bh", which English rarely uses. When a sound copies a bark, it gets built from the pieces each language already has.

Once a word is chosen, it sticks. Children learn it from parents, books and cartoons, and then hear the animal that way. That's why a rooster crows "cock-a-doodle-doo" in English, "kukdoo-koo" in Hindi, "kikeriki" in German, "cocorico" in French and "kokekokko" in Japanese.

Ducks quack in English, go "coin-coin" in French and "gaa gaa" in Japanese. Even cats vary. English cats say "meow", Japanese cats say "nyaa" and Korean cats say "yaong". Pigs oink in English and go "buu buu" in Japanese.

The frog that went Hollywood

In English, frogs say "ribbit". But most frogs don't sound like that at all. Many people think the word spread through Hollywood films. These often used recordings of one small frog from the US West Coast, the Pacific tree frog, whose call does sound a bit like "ribbit". India alone has more than 400 kinds of frogs and toads, and they croak, whistle, click and peep.

Japanese frogs say "kero kero" or "gero gero". In Hindi, frogs croak "tarr tarr", or "tarr-tarr" in the monsoon, when they call from every puddle.

Words for things that don't make sounds

Some languages go much further. Japanese has thousands of these sound-picture words. "Doki doki" is a pounding heartbeat. "Kira kira" is the twinkle of something sparkly. "Shiin" is the sound of complete silence. Many are written in a special script, part of Japanese writing.

Hindi and other Indian languages love doubled sound words too. Rain falls "tip tip", a phone rings "tring tring" and something sizzles "chhan". Many Bollywood songs use them in their lyrics. Children's rhymes in Tamil, Bengali and Marathi are full of them too, because they are fun to say.

Comics depend on these words. "POW", "BAM" and "WHOOSH" turn silent pictures into noisy action. In Japanese manga, sound words are drawn as part of the picture itself.

Animals that really have accents

Here is the twist. Dogs and roosters sound the same everywhere, but some animals really do have local dialects.

Many songbirds learn their songs from adults nearby. Over generations, birds in different areas develop slightly different tunes, a little like accents. Scientists have mapped dialects in sparrows across North America, part of the long story of why birds sing.

Humpback whales in one ocean share a song that slowly changes over time, and whales in different oceans sing differently. Even naked mole rat colonies have their own chirping styles.

So while human words for animals are just guesses, some animals pass down their own sounds as a kind of culture. And the strangest part? Scientists are only beginning to decode what these calls mean, a puzzle at the heart of animal communication.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Dogs and roosters in different countries make different sounds.

Truth: A dog in Delhi and a dog in Tokyo bark the same way. What changes is how people turn the sound into words, using the sounds their own language already has. Some birds and whales, though, really do have local dialects.

Try this

Record a real animal sound, like a crow, a dog or a pigeon, on a phone with an adult's help. Play it to a few people and ask each to write down the sound as a word. Compare the spellings. Nobody will write exactly the same thing.
