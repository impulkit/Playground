Where did playing cards come from?

Playing cards began in China over 1,000 years ago, travelled through the Islamic world to Europe, and in India became round, hand-painted works of art.

The short answer

Playing cards were most likely invented in China by about the 9th century CE, during the Tang dynasty. China already had paper and woodblock printing, so making sets of small printed cards was possible.

Cards spread west along trade routes. Players in the Islamic world, especially Mamluk Egypt, made packs with suits of cups, coins, swords and polo sticks. In the 1370s cards reached Europe, where suits changed again into today's hearts, diamonds, clubs and spades. In India they took a different path, becoming the round, hand-painted ganjifa cards of the Mughal courts.

Surprising but true

Some Indian ganjifa packs had 10 suits and 120 cards, one suit for each of the ten avatars of the god Vishnu.

Paper and printing

Cards need paper, and paper was invented in China. A short record from the 9th century mentions members of a princess's family playing a "leaf game", which many historians think was a card game. Early Chinese cards may have been linked to paper money and dice.

By the time cards reached Europe, people there were learning to print with woodblocks too. Some of the earliest European prints were playing cards. The printing press, invented a little later, made them cheaper still.

The journey west

Historians believe cards reached Europe from the Mamluk Sultanate, which ruled Egypt and Syria. A beautiful Mamluk pack from about the 15th century survives in a museum in Istanbul.

European records suddenly mention cards in the late 1370s, often because cities were banning them as a form of gambling. Italian and Spanish packs kept suits close to the Mamluk ones: cups, coins, swords and batons. German makers chose hearts, bells, acorns and leaves. In France, around 1480, card makers settled on the simple symbols most of the world now uses.

How did the printing press change the world?Before the printing press, a single book could take a scribe months to copy by hand. Within fifty years of Gutenberg, Europe had printed millions.

Ganjifa, cards of the courts

In India, the Mughal emperors and their courts played ganjifa, a game that came from Persia. Ganjifa cards are round, and were often painted by hand on cloth, ivory, tortoiseshell or lacquered paper. The Mughal pack had eight suits with 12 cards each.

Indian artists created their own versions. Dashavatara ganjifa had ten suits, one for each avatar of Vishnu, from the fish to Kalki. A rich pack could take an artist weeks to paint. Craftspeople in Sawantwadi, in Maharashtra, and in a few other towns still make them by hand.

Kings, queens and jokers

The court cards in a French-style pack show a king, queen and jack. Old European packs often had only male figures, such as a king and two knights. The queen became standard in France.

The joker arrived much later. It was added in the United States in the 1860s for a game called euchre. Different countries also use different numbers of cards. Some Italian, Spanish and German games use 32, 36 or 40 cards.

Games, maths and magic

Card games mix luck and skill. You cannot choose your cards, but you can choose how to play them. In India, families play games such as rummy and teen patti at weddings and festivals, and the Bengali game of twenty-nine is popular in the east.

A 52-card pack can be shuffled into more possible orders than there are atoms on Earth, a fact that makes probability experts smile. That is why a well-shuffled pack is almost certainly in an order nobody has ever seen before. Magicians use the same pack, along with sleight of hand, to pull off card tricks that seem impossible.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The 52 cards stand for the 52 weeks of the year, and the four suits for the four seasons.

Truth: It is a popular idea, but there is no evidence card makers planned it. Packs had different numbers of cards in different places, and 52 became standard slowly, over centuries.

Try this

Shuffle a pack of cards well and turn them over one at a time, counting how many cards you turn before the first ace appears. Do it five times and work out the average. Then think about why the answer is usually around ten.
