What happened at Jallianwala Bagh?

On a festival afternoon in 1919, soldiers blocked the main way out of a walled garden in Amritsar and opened fire on an unarmed crowd.

The short answer

On 13 April 1919, thousands of people gathered in Jallianwala Bagh, a walled open ground in Amritsar, near the Golden Temple. Some had come for a peaceful protest meeting. Many were families visiting the city for Baisakhi, a spring harvest festival and a special day for Sikhs.

Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer arrived with soldiers, blocked the main entrance and, without warning, ordered them to fire. They fired for about ten minutes, until their ammunition was nearly used up. The official count was 379 dead, but Indian inquiries believed about 1,000 died, with many more wounded. The massacre turned millions of Indians against British rule.

Surprising but true

In protest at the killings, Rabindranath Tagore gave up the knighthood the British had given him four years earlier.

Why people were angry

During the First World War, more than a million Indians served in the British army. Many Indians hoped for more self-rule afterwards. Instead, in March 1919, the government passed the Rowlatt Act, which let officials jail people suspected of political activity without trial.

Mahatma Gandhi called for peaceful protests across India. In Amritsar, two popular local leaders, Dr Saifuddin Kitchlew and Dr Satyapal, were arrested on 10 April. Crowds protested, some turned violent, and several people, both Indian and British, were killed. The British imposed strict control and banned public meetings, but the ban was poorly announced, and many people did not know about it.

Ten minutes in the garden

Jallianwala Bagh was surrounded by walls and the backs of houses, with only a few narrow ways in and out. Dyer placed his soldiers at the main entrance, which also blocked the main exit.

He gave no order to disperse. The soldiers fired into the crowd, aiming where people were most crowded. There was nowhere to hide. Some people tried to climb the walls, and some jumped into a well in the garden. A curfew that night meant the wounded could not be helped. Bullet marks can still be seen in the walls today.

The world reacts

At first, news spread slowly, because Punjab was under martial law. When it came out, there was outrage. The poet Rabindranath Tagore wrote to the Viceroy, giving up the knighthood he had received in 1915. He said he wanted to stand beside his countrymen.

The British government set up the Hunter Committee to investigate. Dyer told it that he had wanted to make a strong impression on the whole of Punjab. The committee criticised him, and he was removed from his command. Yet many in Britain treated him as a hero, and a newspaper collected money for him.

A turning point

The massacre changed the freedom struggle. Many Indians who had trusted British promises now wanted full independence. In 1920 Gandhi launched the Non-Cooperation Movement, asking Indians to boycott British courts, schools and goods.

A well-known story says that a 12-year-old boy named Bhagat Singh visited the garden soon after and took home a little of its soil. Years later, in 1940, a man named Udham Singh shot dead Michael O'Dwyer, who had governed Punjab in 1919, in London.

The memorial today

Jallianwala Bagh is now a national memorial with a tall flame-shaped monument, opened in 1961. Visitors can see the narrow entrance, the walls with bullet marks and the Martyrs' Well.

British leaders have since called the massacre shameful. In 2019, its 100th anniversary, Prime Minister Theresa May called it a "shameful scar" on British Indian history, though the British government has not made a formal apology. Every 13 April, people gather in the garden to remember.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The people in Jallianwala Bagh had gathered for a riot.

Truth: Most of the crowd were unarmed. Many had come for a peaceful protest meeting, and many others were visitors in Amritsar for the Baisakhi festival, unaware of a ban on gatherings.
