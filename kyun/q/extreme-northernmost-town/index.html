Where is the northernmost town in the world?

In Longyearbyen, the Sun doesn't rise for months, cats are banned, and you need a rifle to walk out of town because of polar bears.

The short answer

The northernmost proper town on Earth is Longyearbyen, on the island of Spitsbergen in the Svalbard group, which belongs to Norway. It sits at about 78 degrees north, roughly 1,300 km from the North Pole. That is closer to the Pole than Delhi is to Bengaluru.

About 2,500 people lived in Longyearbyen and nearby Ny-Ålesund in 2026. Longyearbyen has a school, a university centre, a supermarket, a church and a cinema. A few research bases and military camps sit further north, but nobody would call them towns.

Surprising but true

Longyearbyen's tiny graveyard stopped taking burials around 1950, because the frozen ground keeps bodies from rotting.

Four months of night

Because of the tilt of the Earth, the far north has the strangest seasons on the planet. From about late October to mid-February, the Sun never climbs above the horizon. For weeks in the middle of winter, it is dark even at noon.

Then it flips. From late April to late August the Sun never sets. Children play football at midnight in bright light, and people hang thick curtains or tape foil over windows so they can sleep. Clocks still say it's night, but the sky never agrees.

In the long dark, the sky often lights up with the green curtains of the aurora.

Polar bears and a rifle rule

Polar bears roam the sea ice and coasts all around Svalbard. A hungry bear can walk into town looking for food, so anyone leaving the settled area must carry a way to scare one off, and usually a rifle too. Signs at the edge of town show a polar bear in a red triangle.

Shops and banks ask customers to leave their rifles at the door. Schoolchildren learn what to do if they ever meet a bear, the way children in Kerala might learn about snakes. Cats are not allowed at all. The ban protects the seabirds that nest on the cliffs every summer.

The frozen ground

Below a thin top layer, the ground in Longyearbyen stays frozen all year. This is called permafrost. Houses stand on wooden or metal poles so their warmth doesn't melt the soil beneath them.

The cold has an odd side effect. In the old graveyard, bodies from the 1918 flu outbreak barely decayed. Scientists even took samples to study that virus. Burials stopped there around 1950.

Even food arrives the hard way. Almost nothing grows on Svalbard, so fruit, vegetables and milk come by ship or plane from mainland Norway. Coal mining built the town in 1906, started by an American businessman named John Munro Longyear. That is where the name comes from: Longyear's town.

The permafrost is now warming, and in 2015 an avalanche off the mountain destroyed several homes. Svalbard is warming faster than almost anywhere on Earth.

A vault for the world's seeds

Dug into a mountain just outside town is the Svalbard Global Seed Vault, opened in 2008. It holds more than a million packets of seeds from almost every country, including India, kept frozen at minus 18°C. It is a backup copy of the world's crops in case disaster strikes a seed bank somewhere else.

Why India has a home here

In 1920, many countries signed a treaty about Svalbard. It lets citizens of the signing countries live and work there, and India is one of them. In 2008, India opened Himadri, a research station at Ny-Ålesund, about 100 km north of Longyearbyen. Indian scientists there study glaciers, the air and how the Arctic affects the monsoon.

So the northernmost town in the world has a small Indian connection. For the same science in the far south, India runs bases on the ice of Antarctica, and those are even colder.

Myth or fact?

Myth: It is against the law to die in Longyearbyen.

Truth: There is no such law. The graveyard stopped taking burials around 1950 because the frozen ground stops bodies decaying. People who are very ill or very old are usually moved to mainland Norway, where there are proper hospitals.
