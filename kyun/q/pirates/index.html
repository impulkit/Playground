Were pirates real?

Pirates were very real, but most of what you know about them, from walking the plank to treasure maps, comes from one Scottish novel.

The short answer

Yes, pirates were real, and they still exist. A pirate is someone who attacks and robs ships at sea. Piracy is as old as sailing itself. Ancient Egyptians, Greeks and Romans all complained about sea raiders, and in 75 BCE pirates even kidnapped a young Julius Caesar and held him for ransom.

The pirates most people picture lived during the "Golden Age of Piracy", from about 1650 to 1730. They attacked treasure ships and merchant ships in the Caribbean, along the coast of North America and in the Indian Ocean. Some, like Blackbeard, became famous in their own lifetimes.

Surprising but true

Many pirate crews voted for their captain and could vote him out, at a time when most countries let very few people vote at all.

Life on a pirate ship

Many pirates were former sailors who had escaped harsh treatment on navy or merchant ships. A pirate ship could be crowded, dirty and dangerous, but it offered a share of the loot and some say in how things were run.

Many crews signed written rules, called articles. They set out how prizes would be shared, how much money an injured pirate would get, and how quarrels would be settled. Crews often elected their captain by vote and could remove him. Pirates were still violent criminals who stole, threatened and sometimes killed. Most were caught or died young.

Blackbeard and his ship

Edward Teach, better known as Blackbeard, was one of the most feared pirates of all. He is said to have tied slow-burning fuses into his long black beard to look terrifying in battle. In 1718 he blockaded the harbour of Charleston, South Carolina, for about a week.

Later that year, he was killed in a fight with British navy sailors off the coast of North Carolina. In 1996, divers found a wreck near Beaufort, North Carolina, now identified as his flagship, Queen Anne's Revenge. Archaeologists have since raised cannons, anchors and thousands of smaller objects, turning the shipwreck into a window on real pirate life.

Pirates in the Indian Ocean

One of the richest pirate raids of the age happened near India. In 1695, the English pirate Henry Every attacked the Ganj-i-Sawai, a huge ship belonging to the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb, as it sailed home from the Red Sea with pilgrims and treasure.

The emperor was furious and blamed the English. For a time, the East India Company feared it might lose its right to trade in India, and it had to promise to help guard Mughal ships. Every himself disappeared, and nobody knows for sure what became of him.

Who counts as a pirate can depend on who is telling the story. Kanhoji Angre, admiral of the Maratha navy in the early 1700s, attacked British and Portuguese ships along the west coast. The British called him a pirate. Many in India remember him as a naval commander defending his coast.

How did a trading company end up ruling India?A group of London merchants set out to buy pepper. Within 200 years, their company ran its own army and governed most of India.

Pirates in stories and today

In 1883, Robert Louis Stevenson published Treasure Island. Its buried treasure, maps marked with an X, wooden legs and parrots shaped how pirates have been shown ever since. The black flag with a skull and crossbones was real, though. Different pirates flew different versions to frighten ships into giving up without a fight.

Piracy has not ended. In the 2000s, pirates from Somalia attacked ships in the Gulf of Aden and the Arabian Sea. Navies from many countries, including India and the United States, now patrol these waters to protect sailors.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pirates often made their prisoners walk the plank.

Truth: There are very few recorded cases, and almost all from late in pirate history. The image of walking the plank became famous mainly through stories, plays and films.
