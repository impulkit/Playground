How big was Megalodon?

Megalodon's teeth could be as long as your hand, and they are almost all we have left of it.

The short answer

Megalodon was the biggest shark that ever lived, and one of the largest predators ever. Most scientists estimate it grew to about 15–20 m long. That is longer than a city bus, and roughly the length of a Mumbai local train coach. It may have weighed several times as much as a large elephant.

But nobody knows exactly. A shark's skeleton is made of cartilage, the bendy material in your ears and nose, which rarely becomes a fossil. Almost everything we know about Megalodon comes from its teeth, plus a few fossil backbones. Scientists compare these with living sharks to estimate its size, and different methods give different answers.

Surprising but true

Megalodon was probably warm-bodied. A 2023 study suggested it kept its body several degrees warmer than the water around it.

Teeth as long as your hand

Megalodon's name means "big tooth", and its teeth are some of the most common large fossils in the world. The biggest are triangular, with jagged edges like a steak knife, and about 18 cm along the slanting edge.

Like other sharks, Megalodon grew new teeth all its life. They sat in rows, and when a front tooth broke or fell out, one from behind moved forward, a bit like a conveyor belt. A single shark might shed thousands of teeth. That is very different from people, who get just two sets of teeth in a lifetime.

Megalodon teeth have been found on every continent except Antarctica, from the coasts of North Carolina and Florida to Japan, Australia and Europe. Fossil hunters often find them on beaches and in rivers.

Working out the size

Scientists estimate Megalodon's size by comparing its teeth with those of living sharks, especially the great white. Bigger sharks have bigger teeth, so the tooth size gives a rough body length.

A fossil from Belgium helped. It is a set of backbones from one Megalodon, and from it, researchers built a 3D model of a shark about 16 m long. Some recent studies suggest Megalodon may have had a longer, slimmer body than a great white, which would change the estimates. That is why most books give a range rather than a single number.

What it ate

Megalodon lived from about 23 to 3.6 million years ago in warm seas around the world. It hunted big prey: dolphins, seals, sea turtles and whales. Fossil whale bones have been found with deep bite marks that match Megalodon's teeth.

Young Megalodons probably grew up in shallow, warm coastal waters, where there were fewer big predators. Fossil sites in Panama and elsewhere hold many small Megalodon teeth together, which suggests these were nursery areas, much like the ones some sharks use today.

In 2023, scientists studied chemicals in Megalodon teeth and suggested it was warmer than the water around it, by around 7 °C. Being warm-bodied may have helped it swim fast and hunt in cooler water, but it also meant it needed huge amounts of food.

Why it disappeared

Megalodon became extinct about 3.6 million years ago. Scientists think several things combined. The oceans cooled, and some of its favourite prey, smaller whales, declined. Great white sharks, which were spreading through the oceans at around that time, may have competed with it for food.

A modern blue whale is longer and far heavier than even the biggest estimates for Megalodon. Sharks themselves are still here, with over 500 living species, and you can see Megalodon's teeth in many natural history museums today.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Megalodon may still be alive in the deep ocean.

Truth: Megalodon became extinct about 3.6 million years ago. A huge warm-bodied shark would need lots of food near the surface and would leave fresh teeth everywhere. None have been found. A 2013 TV programme claiming it was still alive was fiction made to look real.
