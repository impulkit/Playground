When did people start building houses?

About 15,000 years ago, people in what is now Ukraine built round huts out of mammoth bones, with giant jawbones stacked to make the walls.

The short answer

People have built shelters for hundreds of thousands of years, but simple huts of sticks and leaves rot away and leave almost no trace. The oldest huts we can be fairly sure about are around 20,000 to 25,000 years old. Houses that stayed in one place, with solid walls of mud, stone or brick, came later, after people began farming about 10,000 years ago.

Before that, most people moved with the seasons, following animals and ripening plants. They sheltered in cave mouths and rock overhangs, and built light huts and tents they could leave behind or carry. Once people stayed put to tend crops and animals, it made sense to build something stronger.

Surprising but true

At Çatalhöyük in Turkey, about 9,000 years ago, houses were packed so tightly that there were no streets. People walked across the rooftops and climbed in through holes in the roof.

Not really cave people

The idea of "cavemen" is a bit misleading. Deep caves are dark, damp and chilly. People did use the entrances and shallow rock shelters, which kept off rain and wind. At Bhimbetka in Madhya Pradesh, people used hundreds of rock shelters over thousands of years and covered the walls with paintings of animals, hunters and dancers.

But most early camps were out in the open. They don't survive as well. Caves protect bones, tools and charcoal from weather, so archaeologists find more there. That gave the false impression that everyone lived in caves.

The first huts

At a site called Ohalo II, on the shore of the Sea of Galilee in Israel, archaeologists found the remains of brush huts about 23,000 years old. The floors held seeds, fish bones and stone tools. The site survived because it was flooded and buried in mud.

In Ukraine, at Mezhyrich, people built round huts from mammoth bones about 15,000 years ago. Big jawbones and leg bones formed the walls, probably covered with hides. A single hut could use bones from many mammoths, some probably collected from animals that had died naturally. In a land with few trees, bones were the best building material around.

Farming builds villages

When people began farming, they needed to stay near their fields and store grain. Permanent villages appeared. In the Middle East, early farmers built round houses of stone and mud, then rectangular ones that were easier to join together.

At Çatalhöyük, in what is now Turkey, thousands of people lived in a town of mud-brick houses about 9,000 years ago. The houses were joined wall to wall with no streets. People climbed ladders to the roofs and entered through a hole that also let out smoke. Inside, they plastered and painted the walls and buried family members under the floors.

Mud-brick homes in South Asia

At Mehrgarh, at the foot of the Bolan Pass in Pakistan, farmers built houses of sun-dried mud brick as early as about 7000 BCE. They grew barley and wheat and kept goats and cattle. Some houses had several small rooms, possibly for storing grain.

Thousands of years later, the people of the Indus Valley took building to a new level. In cities like Harappa, Mohenjo-daro and Dholavira, they made standard-sized fired bricks and laid out streets and covered drains. Many Harappan homes had their own bathing areas.

Homes shaped by place

Around the world, people built with whatever they had. Inuit families built snow houses for winter hunting trips. Ancestral Puebloans in the American Southwest built stone and adobe homes, including the cliff dwellings at Mesa Verde, about 800 years ago. In Kerala, homes grew sloping roofs to shed monsoon rain.

The basic needs haven't changed: keep dry, stay warm or cool, and feel safe. A modern flat in Bengaluru and a mammoth-bone hut in Ukraine do the same job.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Cave people lived inside caves.

Truth: Some did shelter in caves and rock overhangs, but most early people lived in the open in huts, tents and windbreaks. The word "caveman" stuck because caves preserved their tools, bones and paintings better than open camps did.

Try this

Build a model shelter in a garden, park or on a table using only things you find, like sticks, leaves, cardboard and string. Pour a cup of water over it, or blow on it with a fan or hair dryer on cool. Which materials kept the inside dry and still? Early builders learned the same lessons by trial and error.
