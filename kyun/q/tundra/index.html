What is the tundra?

In the Arctic tundra the ground a short way down stays frozen all year, so trees can't put down deep roots, and the whole summer lasts about two months.

The short answer

The tundra is a cold, treeless kind of land. It is found in the far north, around the Arctic Ocean, and high on mountains all over the world, above the line where trees stop growing.

Winters are long, dark and bitterly cold. Summers are short and cool. Underneath the surface lies permafrost, ground that stays frozen all year, sometimes for thousands of years.

Only low plants can live there: mosses, lichens, grasses, small shrubs and flowers that hug the ground out of the wind. The word tundra comes from a Finnish and Sami word for a high land with no trees.

Surprising but true

Frozen tundra soil holds the remains of plants that died thousands of years ago. As it thaws, microbes start eating them and release carbon dioxide and methane into the air.

The frozen layer

Permafrost is the key to the tundra. In summer only the top layer of soil thaws, often less than a metre deep. Below that, it stays frozen solid.

Melted snow can't drain down through the ice. So in summer the tundra turns into a soggy patchwork of ponds, bogs and streams. From an aeroplane it can look like broken glass scattered over a brown carpet.

Tree roots can't push down into frozen ground, and the growing season is short, about 50 to 60 days a year. That is why most tundra plants are tiny. Some Arctic willows are full-grown trees that rise only a few centimetres above the ground.

Life in a short summer

When summer comes to the Arctic, the Sun barely sets. Plants race to grow, flower and make seeds in a few weeks.

Animals arrive to feast. Huge herds of caribou, known in Europe and Asia as reindeer, walk hundreds of kilometres to feed on summer plants. Millions of geese, sandpipers and other birds migrate north to nest, because there is lots of food and fewer predators than in the south. Mosquitoes hatch from the ponds in clouds.

Some animals stay all year. The Arctic fox turns white in winter. Musk oxen grow coats so thick that cold barely reaches their skin. Lemmings live in tunnels under the snow, where it is warmer than the open air.

Tundra on mountains

You don't have to go to the Arctic to find tundra. Climb high enough up any mountain and the forest thins out, then stops. Above this treeline lies alpine tundra.

In India, alpine tundra covers high parts of the Himalayas, in places like Ladakh, Spiti and northern Sikkim. Here you find hardy cushion plants, yaks, blue sheep and, very rarely, a snow leopard. In the USA, Rocky Mountain National Park has tundra you can drive to on Trail Ridge Road.

Alpine tundra usually has no permafrost, but it has strong winds, fierce sunlight and cold nights even in summer.

People of the tundra

People have lived on the Arctic tundra for thousands of years. The Inuit of Canada, Greenland and Alaska hunted seals, whales and caribou. The Sami of northern Europe and the Nenets of Siberia have herded reindeer for centuries, following the animals across the land.

Life is changing for them now. The Arctic is warming faster than almost anywhere else on Earth.

A thawing freezer

Permafrost works like a giant freezer. It holds the remains of plants and animals that died long ago, never fully rotted. Scientists estimate that the world's permafrost holds about twice as much carbon as there is in the air today.

As the tundra warms, the ice in the ground melts. Microbes wake up and start breaking down the old remains. They give off carbon dioxide and methane, gases that trap more heat and add to climate change.

Melting permafrost causes trouble on the surface too. In parts of Alaska and Siberia, roads buckle, houses tilt and the ground slumps into holes as the ice inside it disappears.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The tundra is frozen and lifeless all year round.

Truth: In summer the top layer thaws and the tundra bursts into life. Mosses, grasses and tiny flowers bloom, insects swarm, and millions of birds fly in from the south to nest.

Try this

Put a small tray of soil in the freezer overnight. Next day, pour a little water on top and watch what happens. The water can't soak in and sits in puddles, just as melted snow does on tundra in summer, which is why the tundra becomes boggy and full of pools.
