Why are Roald Dahl's stories so popular?

When Roald Dahl was a schoolboy, a chocolate company sent new bars to his school for the boys to taste and rate. He never forgot it.

The short answer

Roald Dahl was a British writer who lived from 1916 to 1990. His children's books, including Charlie and the Chocolate Factory, Matilda, The BFG, The Witches and Fantastic Mr Fox, have sold many millions of copies and been translated into dozens of languages.

His stories are popular because they take children's side. Grown-ups in Dahl's books are often greedy, cruel or ridiculous, and clever children beat them. The stories are funny and a little bit gross, full of made-up words and nasty surprises. Readers feel the writer is a naughty friend who knows exactly what bores them.

Surprising but true

Before writing Charlie and the Chocolate Factory, Roald Dahl was a fighter pilot who crash-landed in the desert in North Africa during the Second World War.

A boyhood full of stories

Dahl was born in Llandaff, near Cardiff in Wales. His parents came from Norway, and he spent summer holidays there. His father died when he was three.

At boarding school in England, he was often unhappy. He wrote later about harsh punishments from teachers, and many of his fictional grown-ups seem to come from that time. At Repton School, the chocolate company Cadbury sometimes sent boxes of new chocolate bars for the boys to test. Dahl dreamed of working in a chocolate lab and inventing a famous bar. Decades later, the dream became Willy Wonka's factory.

Pilot, spy and storyteller

When the Second World War began, Dahl joined the Royal Air Force as a fighter pilot. In 1940 his plane crash-landed in the Libyan desert, and he was badly hurt. He recovered and flew again in Greece, before headaches forced him to stop.

He was then sent to Washington, D.C., to work at the British Embassy. There he also passed information to British intelligence, and he began writing about his flying adventures. His first book for children, The Gremlins (1943), was about tiny creatures said to sabotage aircraft, an idea RAF pilots joked about.

Dahl's magic tricks

Dahl's books share a few tricks. Characters get the punishments they deserve, often in spectacular ways: Augustus Gloop is sucked up a chocolate pipe. Children are brave and clever, like Matilda, who teaches herself to read at three. And the language is a feast.

The BFG speaks gobblefunk, full of words like "whizzpopper" and "snozzcumber". Dahl also loved rhymes and nonsense poems. In 2016, the hundredth anniversary of his birth, the Oxford English Dictionary added several words connected with his books. That shows how dictionaries follow the words people actually use.

From 1978, most of his children's books were illustrated by Quentin Blake. His scratchy, lively drawings fit Dahl's stories so well that many readers can't picture one without the other.

A complicated man

Dahl's life had sadness in it. His daughter Olivia died of measles in 1962, aged seven, and he later urged parents to vaccinate their children. He also helped invent a medical valve after his baby son was injured in an accident.

He also said hurtful things. In interviews he made antisemitic remarks, attacking Jewish people. In 2020 his family publicly apologised for them. In 2023 his publisher changed some words in new editions, which caused a big argument about whether old books should be edited. The publisher then decided to keep the original versions in print too.

His birthday, 13 September, is celebrated as Roald Dahl Day in many schools. A museum about his life opened in 2005 in Great Missenden, the English village where he lived and wrote in a hut at the bottom of his garden.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Roald Dahl only wrote stories for children.

Truth: He first became famous for spooky, twisty short stories for adults. He also wrote film scripts, including the James Bond film You Only Live Twice and Chitty Chitty Bang Bang.
