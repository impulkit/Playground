Why can't anyone read the Indus script yet?

Over 4,000 years ago, people in the Indus cities carved tiny messages on stone seals. Nobody alive today can read a single one.

The short answer

The people of the Indus cities, such as Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, used a set of signs between about 2600 and 1900 BCE. They carved them on small square seals, pots, copper tablets and bangles. More than 4,000 inscribed objects have been found.

The trouble is that the texts are tiny. Most have about five signs, and none is long. Nobody knows what language the Indus people spoke, and no inscription has turned up that gives the same words in a known script. Without a long text, a known language or a translation, there is very little to grab hold of.

Surprising but true

Most Indus inscriptions are only about five signs long, shorter than a typical text message.

Small seals, big puzzle

A typical Indus seal is about the size of a postage stamp, carved from a soft stone called steatite. Many show an animal, often a one-horned creature that looks like a unicorn, with a row of signs above it.

Traders probably pressed the seals into wet clay to mark bundles of goods, a bit like a stamp on a parcel today. That would explain why the messages are so short. A name, a title or the owner's town needs only a few signs. In 1875, the British archaeologist Alexander Cunningham published one of these seals from Harappa. He had no idea that it belonged to a civilisation thousands of years older than he guessed.

Counting the signs

Scholars have listed roughly 400 different signs. That number is a clue. An alphabet like English needs only 26 letters, while Chinese uses thousands of characters. A script with a few hundred signs probably mixes signs for whole words with signs for sounds, as Egyptian hieroglyphs did.

Some signs crowd together at the left edge of a seal, as if the carver ran out of room. This suggests the writing usually ran from right to left. In 1977 the Indian scholar Iravatham Mahadevan published a huge list of every known inscription and sign, which researchers still use today.

Writing or symbols

In 2004 three researchers argued that the signs were not writing at all. They said the signs might be symbols, like road signs or family badges, that never recorded spoken words.

Other scientists answered by studying how often each sign follows another. They found patterns that look much more like language than like random symbols. The argument has not been settled. There is also no agreement on the language. Some scholars, including Mahadevan and the Finnish expert Asko Parpola, think it was an early form of a Dravidian language, related to Tamil. Others prefer links to Sanskrit or to a language that has since vanished.

A signboard in the desert

At Dholavira, in the Rann of Kutch in Gujarat, archaeologists in the early 1990s found ten large signs lying on the ground near a gateway. Each sign is roughly as tall as a school ruler is long. They may once have been fixed to a wooden board above the gate, like a city's name over its entrance.

Even this, the biggest Indus inscription, is only ten signs. It shows that the script was used in public as well as on seals, and that ordinary passers-by may once have read it easily.

What would crack it

Code-breakers need more text. A long inscription, or a bilingual one, could change everything. Indus seals have been found as far away as Mesopotamia, so a seal with a matching cuneiform label is one hope. Until then, the script remains one of the great unsolved puzzles of the ancient world, sitting on museum shelves in Delhi, Karachi and London.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Indus script was deciphered years ago.

Truth: Many people have announced a decipherment, some in newspapers. None has convinced other experts, because none can read new inscriptions in a way that others can test and repeat.
