What did ancient Egyptians believe?

Every night, the Egyptians said, the Sun sailed through a dark underworld and fought a giant snake. Every morning, it won.

The short answer

For about 3,000 years, the people of ancient Egypt believed that gods ran every part of life: the rising Sun, the Nile flood, birth, death and even beer-making. More than a thousand gods' names are known. Many had human bodies and animal heads, like Anubis with the head of a jackal, or Horus with the head of a falcon.

The Egyptians also believed that death was a journey to another life. A person who lived well, and whose body was carefully prepared, could reach a happy land of reeds and fields. That belief explains mummies, tombs packed with goods, and the pyramids themselves.

Surprising but true

Egyptians believed that after death your heart was weighed against a single feather. If it was heavier, a monster with a crocodile's head ate it.

The Sun's dangerous night

To the Egyptians, the Sun god Ra sailed across the sky each day in a boat. At sunset he entered the Duat, the underworld, and travelled through it all night.

There, a giant serpent called Apep waited to swallow him. Every night, Ra and his helpers fought Apep off. Every dawn, the Sun rose again because Ra had won. Priests chanted spells to help him. A sunrise wasn't something to take for granted.

A beetle pushing the Sun

Egyptians watched dung beetles roll balls of dung across the sand and bury them. Later, new beetles crawled out, as if born from nothing. They pictured a giant scarab god, Khepri, rolling the Sun across the sky the same way.

Scarab charms became some of the most common lucky objects in Egypt, worn by the living and placed with the dead.

How do dung beetles use the stars?In 2013, scientists put tiny cardboard caps on dung beetles in a planetarium and found they steer by the Milky Way.

Osiris, Isis and Set

The most famous Egyptian story is about a family. Osiris was a good king. His jealous brother Set killed him and scattered his body across Egypt.

Osiris's wife, Isis, searched the land and gathered the pieces. With magic, she brought him back long enough to have a son, Horus. Osiris became ruler of the dead. Horus grew up, fought Set for the throne and lost an eye in battle, which was magically healed. The "Eye of Horus" became a symbol of protection, and you can still see it on jewellery today.

Every pharaoh was said to be Horus on Earth, and became Osiris after death.

Weighing the heart

When someone died, Egyptians believed they faced a test in the Hall of Truth. Anubis placed the dead person's heart on one side of a scale and a feather on the other. The feather stood for Maat, the goddess of truth and order.

If the heart balanced, the person went on to the afterlife. If it was heavier, Ammit, a monster with a crocodile's head, a lion's front and a hippo's back, gobbled it up. To help, families buried the dead with spells written on papyrus in hieroglyphs. We call these collections the Book of the Dead.

Cats, crocodiles and millions of mummies

Animals carried a god's power. Cats were linked with Bastet, a goddess of home and protection. Crocodiles belonged to Sobek, and ibis birds to Thoth, the god of writing.

People bought mummified animals as offerings, and some temples held them in staggering numbers. Millions of mummified ibises have been found in underground galleries at Saqqara. In 1888, a farmer found a huge cat cemetery, and tens of thousands of cat mummies were shipped to England, where many were ground up as fertiliser.

Why the Nile mattered

Most Egyptian beliefs come back to the river. The Nile flooded each summer and left rich black mud for farming. Egyptians linked this to the god Hapi, and to tears of Isis mourning Osiris. Their whole year was built around the flood, which you can follow in the story of the Nile.

When the Greeks later ruled Egypt, they met these gods and saw their own in them. They matched Thoth with Hermes and Ra with Helios. The idea of judging a person's deeds after death also turns up in many other traditions, from the court of Yama in Hindu stories to the scales in medieval European paintings. Whether those Greek myths borrowed more than names from Egypt is a question historians still enjoy arguing about.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Egyptians worshipped animals as gods.

Truth: Most Egyptians saw animals as signs or homes of a god's power, not as gods themselves. A cat could stand for the goddess Bastet, much as a statue or image represents a god in other religions.
