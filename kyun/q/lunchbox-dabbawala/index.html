How do Mumbai's dabbawalas deliver lunches?

Every working day, thousands of men in white caps carry home-cooked lunches across a city of 2 crore people, and almost nothing goes missing.

The short answer

In Mumbai, many people like to eat lunch cooked at home, even when they work across the city. So every morning, a dabbawala collects a metal lunchbox, called a dabba or tiffin, from a family's door. By lunchtime it is on an office desk, still warm. In the afternoon the empty box travels all the way back.

Before 2020, about 5,000 dabbawalas moved up to 2 lakh lunchboxes a day. In 2026 there were about 3,000, carrying more than 50,000 boxes a day. They do it with bicycles, wooden handcarts and the city's crowded local trains, and with a simple painted code instead of computers.

Surprising but true

Most dabbawalas never went far in school. Business schools around the world now teach their delivery system as a lesson in organisation.

A relay race across the city

No single person carries a box from start to finish. One dabbawala collects boxes from homes in his area and cycles them to the nearest railway station. There, boxes from many homes are sorted into crates by where they are going. The crates ride the train into the city. At the other end, a new team sorts them again and carries them to offices.

A box might change hands three or four times. Each person knows only his own short stretch, and knows it extremely well. It works a bit like a relay race where nobody is allowed to drop the baton.

What is an algorithm?You follow algorithms every day, from brushing your teeth to finding a word in a dictionary. Computers just follow them faster.

The secret code

Each lunchbox lid carries a few painted marks: colours, letters and numbers. Together they say which station the box starts from, which station it goes to, the building, and sometimes the floor. Many dabbawalas never studied much in school, so the code was designed to be read at a glance, not written out in long addresses.

The system has hardly changed in over a hundred years, because it doesn't need to. It's fast, cheap and works even when phones and computers fail.

Why not just use an app?

Plenty of apps now deliver food in Mumbai. But an app needs riders on motorbikes stuck in traffic, while a dabbawala crate rides a train that runs every few minutes no matter what the roads look like. The boxes cost very little to send, the food is from home, and the empty box comes back the same day. For many families, that still beats ordering in.

How it began

The story usually begins in 1890, when Mahadeo Havaji Bachche started delivering lunches to workers in what was then Bombay. Many people travelled long distances to work, there were few canteens, and families wanted their own food. The business grew with the city.

Most dabbawalas come from farming families in villages near Pune. Many belong to the Warkari tradition and wear a white cap. They run their work as a cooperative: each member is a partner, not an employee, and they share what the group earns.

Famous visitors

In 2003, Prince Charles of the UK visited the dabbawalas and later invited two of them to his wedding. Researchers from business schools, including Harvard, have studied them. Companies that deliver parcels with scanners and barcodes look at the dabbawalas and ask how people with painted lids manage nearly as well.

Hard times

When offices closed during the COVID-19 lockdowns in 2020, deliveries stopped almost overnight and many dabbawalas went back to their villages. Some have returned, and some now carry food from restaurants as well as homes. The city has changed, but a home-cooked thali packed into a steel tiffin is still worth crossing Mumbai for.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The dabbawalas make one mistake in every 16 million deliveries.

Truth: Nobody has carefully counted. The number came from a magazine in 1998 and stuck. What we do know is that mistakes are rare enough for businesses around the world to study the system.

Try this

Make your own dabba code. Pick a colour for your home street, a letter for your school and a number for your classroom. Paint or draw the code on a lunchbox lid, then ask a friend to work out where it is going without telling them.
