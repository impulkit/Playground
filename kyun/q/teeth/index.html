Why do we lose our baby teeth?

Your adult teeth are already hiding inside your jaw, waiting under your baby teeth like passengers queuing for the next train.

The short answer

A baby's mouth and jaw are small, so they can only fit small teeth. Children grow a first set of 20 baby teeth, also called milk teeth. As your face and jaw grow, there's room for bigger, stronger teeth, and more of them.

Your adult teeth have been forming inside your jaw since you were very young. When one is ready, it pushes up against the baby tooth above it. Your body slowly dissolves the baby tooth's root. With no root to hold it, the baby tooth gets wobbly and falls out, and the adult tooth moves into the gap.

Most children start losing teeth around age 6 or 7, usually the bottom front ones first, and finish by about 12.

Surprising but true

A baby tooth that falls out naturally has almost no root. The root is slowly dissolved by your body as the adult tooth pushes up underneath.

Two sets, then no more

Humans get only two sets of teeth. Baby teeth start growing before birth, hidden in the gums, and the first one usually appears at about six months. All 20 are in by about age three.

A full adult set has 32 teeth. The last four, called wisdom teeth, often don't appear until the late teens or twenties. Some people never get them, and some have them removed because there isn't room.

Once your adult teeth are in, that's it. If one is lost, it won't grow back. Dentists can fill the gap with an artificial tooth, but your body can't make a third set.

Harder than bone

The outside of a tooth is coated in enamel, the hardest substance in your body. It's even harder than bone. Under it is a slightly softer, yellowish layer called dentine. In the middle is the pulp, with nerves and blood vessels. That's the part that hurts if a tooth is damaged.

Enamel has no living cells, so it can't repair itself. Bacteria in your mouth feed on sugar and make acid, which can dissolve tiny holes in it. Brushing twice a day with a fluoride toothpaste and a good toothbrush protects it.

Animals that never run out

Some animals have far more chances than we do. Sharks grow row after row of teeth. When one falls out, the one behind moves forward, and a shark may get through thousands in its life.

Elephants take another route. They get six sets of big grinding molars, one after another, moving forward along the jaw like a slow conveyor belt. When the last set wears down, usually in old age, an elephant finds it hard to chew. An elephant's tusks are teeth too: they are giant front teeth that keep growing.

Throw it on the roof

People around the world have traditions for lost baby teeth. In the USA, Britain and Canada, many children put a tooth under their pillow for the Tooth Fairy, who may leave a coin. In many parts of India, children throw the tooth onto the roof, sometimes asking a sparrow or crow to bring a strong new one. In Spain and Latin America, a little mouse called Ratoncito Pérez collects it.

All these customs mark the same moment: a small step in growing up.

Teeth tell your age

Because teeth appear in a fairly fixed order, dentists can estimate a child's age from an X-ray of their jaw. Archaeologists do the same with ancient skulls. Teeth are so tough that they are often the best-preserved part of a skeleton, sometimes lasting hundreds of thousands of years.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Baby teeth don't matter, because they fall out anyway.

Truth: Baby teeth help children chew, speak clearly and keep space in the jaw for the adult teeth. Badly decayed baby teeth can cause pain and infection, so they need brushing just as much as adult ones.

Try this

Look in a mirror and count your teeth, top and bottom. Compare the number with the 20 baby teeth and 32 adult teeth a full set can have. Ask an adult in your family how many teeth they have, and whether they remember losing their first one.
