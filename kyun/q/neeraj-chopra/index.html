How did Neeraj Chopra win Olympic gold?

His family sent a chubby village boy to the gym to lose weight. He wandered over to watch the javelin throwers instead.

The short answer

Neeraj Chopra won the javelin gold medal at the Tokyo Olympics on 7 August 2021 with a throw of 87.58 metres, made on his second attempt. Nobody beat it. It was independent India's first ever Olympic medal in athletics, and only the second individual Olympic gold for India, after Abhinav Bindra's in shooting in 2008.

He won it with years of training, a lot of technique and a smart start. His big early throw put pressure on everyone else, and none of them could answer it.

Surprising but true

The men's javelin was redesigned in 1986 because an East German athlete had thrown it 104.80 metres, dangerously close to the edge of the stadium. That athlete, Uwe Hohn, later coached Neeraj Chopra.

A boy from Khandra

Neeraj grew up in Khandra, a village near Panipat in Haryana, in a large farming family. As a boy he was overweight, and relatives teased him. Around the age of 12 or 13 his family sent him to the Shivaji Stadium in Panipat to get fit.

There he saw older athletes throwing the javelin and asked to try. One senior athlete, Jaiveer Choudhary, noticed that the boy could already throw a long way without any training. Neeraj began practising every day, travelling to better training centres as he improved.

Breaking through

In 2016 Neeraj threw 86.48 metres at the World Under-20 Championships in Poland. It was a world record for juniors. Soon after, he joined the Indian Army as a junior commissioned officer, which gave him a salary and time to train.

Elbow surgery in 2019 kept him out for months. He worked with foreign coaches, including the German Uwe Hohn, the only man to throw the old javelin more than 100 metres, and the biomechanics expert Klaus Bartonietz.

The night in Tokyo

In the Tokyo final, Neeraj threw 87.03 metres on his first attempt and 87.58 metres on his second. The favourite, Germany's Johannes Vetter, struggled and did not reach the final rounds. Neeraj raised his arms before his javelin even landed, sure it was a winner.

India celebrated for days. The Athletics Federation of India declared 7 August National Javelin Day. Neeraj dedicated the medal to Milkha Singh, who had died a few weeks earlier, still hoping to see an Indian win athletics gold.

Science of a throw

A men's javelin weighs 800 grams and is about 2.6 metres long. Throwers sprint up to the line, plant the front leg hard and whip the body round. The javelin leaves the hand at about 100 km/h. After that, gravity and air decide where it lands.

Before 1986, men's javelins sometimes flew so far they threatened to land on the running track. In 1984 Uwe Hohn threw 104.80 metres. Officials moved the javelin's centre of balance forward, so it tips nose-down sooner and sticks in the ground instead of sliding flat.

The angle matters. Throw too flat and it lands early. Throw too high and it stalls in the air. Experts say the best release angle for a javelin is lower than 45 degrees, because air pushes on the long spear as it flies.

More medals

In 2023 Neeraj became world champion in Budapest. At the Paris Olympics in 2024 he threw 89.45 metres but won silver, behind Pakistan's Arshad Nadeem, who set an Olympic record of 92.97 metres. The two are friends, and their mothers spoke kindly of each other's sons, which moved fans in both countries.

In May 2025, in Doha, Neeraj finally threw past 90 metres, reaching 90.23. For a boy who first went to the stadium to lose weight, it was a long way to come.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Javelin throwers win mainly because they have very strong arms.

Truth: Speed from the run-up and a sudden "block" with the front leg matter as much as the arm. The whole body works like a whip, passing energy from the legs to the hips, shoulders and finally the hand.

Try this

Throw a paper aeroplane or a soft foam ball at different angles, very low, medium and very high, and mark where each lands. Try each angle three times. You will usually find the best angle is somewhere in the middle, a lesson every javelin thrower learns.
