How are computer chips made?

A computer chip starts as sand. Months later it holds billions of switches, each far too small to see even with an ordinary microscope.

The short answer

A computer chip is a thin slice of silicon covered with billions of tiny electric switches called transistors, all connected by metal wires thinner than many viruses. Each transistor can be on or off, which lets the chip work with 1s and 0s.

Making one is a long chain of steps. Sand is turned into ultra-pure silicon. The silicon is grown into a large crystal, sliced into thin discs called wafers, then patterned with light and chemicals hundreds of times, layer by layer. Finally the wafer is cut into individual chips, tested and sealed in a plastic or ceramic case. The whole process often takes around three months.

Surprising but true

Chip factories keep their air thousands of times cleaner than a hospital operating theatre, because one speck of dust can ruin a chip.

From sand to shining discs

Silicon is the second most common element in Earth's crust, after oxygen. Most beach and desert sand is quartz, which is silicon joined to oxygen. Chipmakers start with very pure quartz, then heat and treat it to remove almost everything else.

The silicon must be about 99.9999999 percent pure. That is roughly one stray atom in a billion. It is melted, and a small seed crystal is dipped in and slowly pulled up while turning. Silicon freezes onto it in one perfect crystal, a shining grey cylinder that can be taller than a ten-year-old.

Diamond saws slice the crystal into wafers less than a millimetre thick, usually 300 mm across. They are polished until they shine like mirrors.

How is glass made from sand?Glass is sand that has been melted and then cooled so quickly that its atoms never get the chance to line up neatly again.

Drawing with light

The patterns on a chip are far too small to draw by hand. Instead, the wafer is coated with a light-sensitive chemical. Light shines through a mask, a glass plate carrying the pattern, and the chemical changes where the light lands. The changed areas are washed away, and the uncovered silicon can then be etched or treated.

This is repeated dozens or hundreds of times to build up layers of transistors and wiring. The newest machines, made by the Dutch company ASML, use extreme ultraviolet light and cost well over a hundred million dollars each. Some of the lines they print are only a few dozen atoms wide.

Cleaner than an operating theatre

A single speck of dust is like a boulder on a chip's tiny streets. So chips are made in cleanrooms, where air is filtered and pushed down from the ceiling all the time. Workers wear full-body suits, nicknamed bunny suits, with hoods, masks and boots.

Even so, not every chip on a wafer works. Each one is tested. The good ones are cut out and packaged. Some chips that work slightly less well are sold as cheaper, slower models.

How it all began

The transistor was invented in 1947 at Bell Labs in New Jersey by John Bardeen, Walter Brattain and William Shockley. It replaced bulky glass vacuum tubes and won them the 1956 Nobel Prize in Physics.

In 1958 Jack Kilby at Texas Instruments built the first integrated circuit, putting several parts onto one piece of material. Robert Noyce soon made a version on silicon that was easier to mass-produce. The area south of San Francisco filled with chip companies and became known as Silicon Valley.

Today Taiwan makes most of the world's most advanced chips. India is building its first big chip factories, including one in Dholera, Gujarat. A modern phone chip, built on the same ideas as those early computers, holds more than 10 billion transistors.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Computer chips are made of silicone, the rubbery stuff in kitchen spatulas.

Truth: Chips are made of silicon, a hard, shiny grey element found in sand. Silicone is a different, rubbery material that contains silicon mixed with other elements. The names are easy to mix up.

Try this

Make a sun print, the same idea chipmakers use. Put a leaf, a key or a coin on a sheet of dark coloured paper and leave it in a sunny window for a few days without moving it. Lift the object and you will see its outline where the paper did not fade. Chipmakers do the same with light and patterns, only far smaller.
