What were the ancient kingdoms of Korea?

For about 700 years, three rival kingdoms shared the Korean peninsula. One of them built a stone star-watching tower in the 600s that still stands today.

The short answer

For hundreds of years, the Korean peninsula was home to three rival kingdoms: Goguryeo in the north, Baekje in the south-west and Silla in the south-east. Historians call this the Three Kingdoms period.

Old Korean histories say the three were founded in the first century BCE. They fought, made alliances and traded with each other and with China and Japan. Each built palaces, tombs and Buddhist temples full of beautiful objects.

In 668 CE, Silla defeated the other two with help from China and united most of the peninsula. A few centuries later, a kingdom called Goryeo took over. From its name we get the English word Korea.

Surprising but true

A Korean legend says a princess from a far-off land called Ayuta sailed to Korea in 48 CE and married a king. Some people link Ayuta with Ayodhya, and South Korea helped build a memorial to her there.

Goguryeo: horse riders of the north

Goguryeo was the largest kingdom. At its height it stretched far beyond today's Korea into what is now north-east China. Its people were famous horse riders and archers.

Goguryeo tombs are decorated with colourful wall paintings. They show hunters on horseback, wrestlers, dancers and the four guardian animals: a blue dragon, a white tiger, a red bird and a black tortoise twined with a snake. These paintings tell us a lot about what people wore and how they lived.

Goguryeo was strong enough to beat back huge Chinese armies more than once in the 600s.

Baekje: artists and teachers

Baekje sat in the warm, fertile south-west. Its craftspeople made delicate gold crowns, glazed tiles and a famous gilt-bronze incense burner topped with a phoenix.

Baekje had close ties with Japan. Its scholars and monks took Chinese writing, Buddhism and craft skills across the sea. Many early Japanese temples were built with help from Baekje builders.

Silla: kingdom of gold

Silla began as the smallest and most cut-off kingdom. Its capital was Gyeongju, in the south-east. Royal tombs there are huge grassy mounds, and inside archaeologists have found gold crowns hung with jade pieces shaped like commas, which shimmered as the king moved.

Silla also had three queens who ruled in their own right, which was rare in that part of the world. One of them, Queen Seondeok, ruled from 632 to 647. The stone tower called Cheomseongdae is said to have been built in her time.

Cheomseongdae is about 9 m tall and shaped like a bottle, built from 362 stones. It is one of the oldest surviving observatories in East Asia. Watchers may have climbed in through a window halfway up to study the sky, long before telescopes.

A princess from far away

A Korean history called the Samguk Yusa, written in the 1200s, tells of Heo Hwang-ok. She was a princess from a land called Ayuta who sailed to Korea in 48 CE and married King Suro of Gaya, a small kingdom between Baekje and Silla.

Some people believe Ayuta was Ayodhya in India. Historians can't prove it, and others suggest places in Thailand or China. Still, millions of Koreans with the family names Kim and Heo trace their families back to her. In 2001 a memorial to her was opened in Ayodhya, and South Korea later helped make it larger.

What came after

Unified Silla flourished. Its builders made the Seokguram grotto, a cave temple with a calm granite Buddha, and the Bulguksa temple nearby. Both are UNESCO World Heritage Sites.

The kingdom of Goryeo followed in 918. In 1377 Goryeo monks printed a Buddhist book called the Jikji with movable metal type, the oldest surviving book printed this way, decades before Gutenberg in Europe. Korea's own alphabet, Hangul, came later still, in the 1440s. Today you can walk among the old tombs of Gyeongju or visit Seoul, where these kingdoms are remembered in museums and K-dramas.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ancient Korea was simply a part of China.

Truth: The Korean kingdoms had their own rulers, languages and art. They traded with China, borrowed ideas such as Buddhism and Chinese writing, and sometimes fought China's armies, but they were separate states.
