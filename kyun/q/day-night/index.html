Why do we have day and night?

Right now, the ground under your feet is moving faster than a jet plane, and you can't feel a thing.

The short answer

Earth spins like a top, turning once on its axis about every 24 hours. At any moment, half of Earth faces the Sun and has day, while the other half faces away into space and has night.

As Earth turns, your part of the planet moves into the sunlight, which is sunrise, and later out of it, which is sunset. The Sun looks as if it travels across the sky, but it is really Earth that is moving.

Surprising but true

Days are slowly getting longer. When the dinosaurs lived, about 70 million years ago, a day lasted about 23 and a half hours.

A planet that spins

Earth spins from west to east. That is why the Sun rises in the east and sets in the west. If you could look down on Earth from above the North Pole, you would see it turning anticlockwise.

Earth is about 40,000 km around at the equator. To spin once a day, the ground there moves at about 1,670 km/h, faster than most passenger jets. In Bengaluru, near the equator, the speed is about 1,630 km/h. In New York, which is farther north, it is about 1,260 km/h, because the circle there is smaller.

We don't feel it because the spin is smooth and steady, and everything around us, including the air and the oceans, moves along with us. It is like sitting in a smooth train: you only feel the motion when it speeds up or slows down.

An ancient idea

For most of history, it seemed obvious that the Sun moved around Earth. But in 499 CE, the Indian mathematician Aryabhata wrote that Earth spins on its axis, and that the stars only appear to move. He compared it to a person on a moving boat who sees the trees on the bank moving backwards.

In Europe, the idea that Earth spins and goes around the Sun became widely accepted in the 1600s. In 1851, the French scientist Léon Foucault hung a heavy ball on a long wire in Paris. As the hours passed, the direction of its swing slowly turned, showing that Earth was turning beneath it.

Who was Aryabhata?Around 1,500 years ago, a 23-year-old astronomer in India wrote that the stars only seem to move because the Earth itself is spinning.

One country, one clock

Because Earth spins, sunrise moves steadily westward across the planet. In India, the Sun rises in Arunachal Pradesh nearly two hours before it rises in western Gujarat. Yet the whole country uses one clock, Indian Standard Time.

The United States is much wider from east to west, so its 50 states use six main time zones. When it is 9 in the morning in New York, it is only 6 in Los Angeles, and still dark in Hawaii.

Days are getting longer

Earth's spin is very slowly slowing down. The Moon's pull raises tides in the oceans, and the friction of all that moving water acts like a gentle brake. A day now is a few thousandths of a second longer than a day about a century and a half ago.

Over millions of years, that adds up. In 2020, scientists studied the daily growth rings in a fossil shell from about 70 million years ago. They found that a year then had about 372 days, so each day was only about 23 and a half hours long.

Bodies that follow the Sun

Almost all living things follow the rhythm of day and night. Many flowers open in the morning and close at night. Owls hunt in the dark, while crows are busy by day.

Inside your body, a natural clock, about 24 hours long, tells you when to feel awake and when to sleep. Light in the morning helps set it. That is why travelling across many time zones makes people feel tired and muddled, a feeling called jet lag, until their body clock catches up with the Sun.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Day and night happen because the Sun moves around the Earth.

Truth: The Sun only looks as if it moves across the sky. Earth spins on its axis once a day, carrying us into the sunlight and then out of it again.

Try this

Make day and night with a torch and a ball. In a dark room, ask a friend to hold a torch steady as the Sun. Stick a small paper dot on a ball to mark where you live, and slowly turn the ball from west to east (anticlockwise when seen from above the North Pole). Watch your dot move from night, into sunrise, through day and into sunset.
