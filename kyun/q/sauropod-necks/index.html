Why did sauropods have such long necks?

Some necks were 15 metres long. That's longer than a bus. Just the neck.

The short answer

Moving a 50-tonne body costs a lot of energy. A long neck let a sauropod reach a huge area of leaves while standing still, sweeping its head up, down and sideways.

The necks were surprisingly light, with air pockets inside the bones, like a bird's. Their heads were tiny. They didn't chew; they stripped leaves and swallowed them whole, and their giant gut did the slow work of breaking them down.

Surprising but true

Mamenchisaurus's neck was about 15 m long, roughly half its whole body.

A neck like a crane

A crane at a building site doesn't drive around to lift each load. It stays put and swings its arm. Many scientists think a sauropod's neck worked the same way.

Walking, for an animal the weight of ten elephants, burned a great deal of energy. Standing in one spot and sweeping a long neck across trees and ferns was far cheaper. Every mouthful reached without a step was energy saved for growing and staying alive. A sauropod also needed a vast amount of food, so the more ground it could cover from one spot, the better.

Built light

A neck 15 metres long, made of solid bone, would have been impossibly heavy. Sauropod neck bones were full of hollow spaces. Air sacs, linked to the lungs, pushed into the bones and left them more like honeycomb than solid stone.

Birds have the same system today. It's one of the many clues linking dinosaurs to birds. Air sacs may also have helped sauropods breathe through such long necks, moving air in one direction through their lungs, as birds do, rather than in and out like your lungs.

Counting neck bones

A giraffe has seven neck bones, the same number as you or a mouse. Mammals almost always have seven, however long the neck. Sauropods had no such limit. Many had 15 or more neck bones, and Mamenchisaurus had 19.

Each bone could also be long, in some giants more than a metre. More bones and longer bones together made those record necks.

Why the head stayed small

A big head on a long neck would be like holding a watermelon on the end of a fishing rod. Sauropods kept their heads small and light. Their teeth were pegs or spoons, made for raking leaves, not chewing.

Without chewing, they could eat fast. The food went to an enormous gut, where it sat for a long time while microbes broke it down. This let them feed almost constantly, and get very big.

High or low?

Here the scientists disagree. Some think necks were held high, like a giraffe's, to reach treetops other animals couldn't. Others point out that pumping blood up to a head 9 metres above the heart would need a giant, powerful heart.

Many now think it varied. Brachiosaurus had long front legs and probably reached high. Diplodocus may have swept low over ferns. Either way, the neck let them feed without moving.

A long-neck from India

India had sauropods too. Barapasaurus, from the Godavari valley in Telangana, was one of the early ones, about 18 metres long. Its name means "big-leg lizard".

Later, giant titanosaurs roamed India and laid eggs in Gujarat and Madhya Pradesh. In South America, titanosaurs like Argentinosaurus may have weighed more than 60 tonnes. Long necks were one part of how sauropods became the biggest animals ever to walk on land.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Sauropods were too heavy to walk on land, so they lived in lakes and swamps.

Truth: Scientists once thought this, more than a hundred years ago. Footprints, leg bones and their deep chests show they walked on dry land, holding up their weight on pillar-like legs, as elephants do.

Try this

Stand in the middle of a room with a broom held out like a long neck. Without moving your feet, see how much of the floor you can touch with the broom's end. Then try with just your hand. A long neck works like that broom, letting a heavy animal reach food without walking.
