How do submarines stay underwater for months?

A nuclear submarine can make its own air and drinking water from seawater, so the thing that usually forces it home after months at sea is running out of food.

The short answer

Most submarines have to come up regularly, because their diesel engines need air and their batteries need charging. Nuclear submarines are different. Their reactor makes heat without burning anything, so it needs no air. A single load of fuel can last many years.

With power to spare, a nuclear submarine makes everything else it needs. Machines turn seawater into fresh water. Other machines split water to make oxygen. Chemical filters soak up the carbon dioxide the crew breathes out.

So in theory it could stay down for years. In practice, patrols usually last two or three months. The limit is people: their food, their health and how long they can live in a steel tube without sunlight.

Surprising but true

Nuclear submarines make oxygen by passing electricity through seawater, splitting water into oxygen and hydrogen.

How it dives and rises

A submarine has ballast tanks between its inner and outer hulls. To dive, valves open and seawater floods in. The sub gets heavier and sinks. Small fins called hydroplanes tilt it up or down, like the wings of an aircraft.

To rise, the crew blows high-pressure air into the tanks, pushing the water out. The sub gets lighter and floats up. This is all about buoyancy: an object floats when it weighs less than the water it pushes aside.

The inner hull, called the pressure hull, is made of thick, strong steel. At 300 metres down, the water presses on every square centimetre about 30 times harder than air does at the surface.

Making air and water

Air is the first problem. A crew of about 100 to 150 people uses a lot of oxygen. Nuclear submarines run electricity through water, splitting it into oxygen, which goes into the air, and hydrogen, which is pumped overboard. Scrubbers soak up carbon dioxide. Backup "oxygen candles" can release oxygen in an emergency.

Water comes from the sea. Distilling machines boil seawater and collect the steam, leaving salt behind, much as people purify water on land. Some of the water cools the reactor, and the rest goes to drinking, cooking and showers.

Seeing in the dark

Under the sea there are no windows to look out of and light fades within a few hundred metres. Submarines "see" with sound. Sonar works much like the echolocation bats and dolphins use: send out a ping, listen for the echo, and work out how far away something is.

Most of the time, though, military subs listen silently, so as not to give away where they are. Near the surface, a periscope or a mast with cameras pokes up to look around.

Nautilus breaks the ice

The world's first nuclear-powered submarine, the US Navy's USS Nautilus, was launched in 1954. It shared its name with Captain Nemo's submarine in a novel by Jules Verne. In August 1958, it travelled under the Arctic ice and became the first vessel to pass beneath the North Pole.

Today, only a few countries operate nuclear submarines. India joined them with INS Arihant, its first home-built nuclear submarine, which was commissioned in 2016. India also runs diesel-electric submarines like INS Kalvari.

Life on board

Crews live by the clock, not the sun. Many submarines run on shifts, so there's always someone awake. Bunks are narrow, and in some subs sailors share beds in turns, called "hot racking". Food is planned carefully: fresh fruit and milk run out first, then frozen and tinned food takes over.

Sailors keep fit on exercise machines, watch films and study. Many say the hardest part isn't the danger but missing the sky. When a submarine surfaces after a long patrol, the first breath of real air is something crews talk about for years.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Military submarines can dive to the very bottom of the ocean.

Truth: Most military submarines work at depths of a few hundred metres. The deepest parts of the ocean are about 11 km down, and only special craft built for that one job, like the bathyscaphe Trieste in 1960, have reached them.

Try this

Make a mini submarine with an empty plastic bottle and a flexible straw. Fill the bottle with water in a sink, push the straw in, and seal around it with modelling clay. Blow gently through the straw to push water out and watch the bottle float up, then let water back in to make it sink. That's how ballast tanks work.
