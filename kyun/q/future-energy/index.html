Where will our energy come from in 2050?

In 2025, for the first time, renewable sources made more of the world's electricity than coal did, and sunlight is now the fastest-growing source of all.

The short answer

Most experts expect that by 2050, much more of our energy will come from the Sun and the wind, stored in giant batteries and carried along much bigger power grids. Hydropower, nuclear power and perhaps hydrogen will fill the gaps. Coal and oil will probably still be used, but far less than today.

That shift has already begun. In 2025, renewable sources made 33.8% of the world's electricity, just ahead of coal at 33.0%, according to the energy research group Ember. Solar power grew so fast that it passed wind for the first time. How far it goes by 2050 depends on money, politics and technology, and forecasts disagree. That is why this article is marked mixed.

Surprising but true

In 2025, solar power alone covered about three quarters of the growth in the world's electricity demand.

The Sun takes over

A solar panel turns light straight into electricity, with no moving parts. Twenty years ago, solar power was among the most expensive ways to make electricity. Since then, the price of solar panels has fallen by around 90%. Now, in sunny places, it is often the cheapest electricity there is.

India is racing ahead. In July 2026, the country had about 165 gigawatts of solar capacity, enough on a bright noon to run about 200 crore ceiling fans at once. Huge solar parks now cover patches of desert in Rajasthan and Gujarat. The government aims to reach 500 gigawatts of capacity from non-fossil sources by 2030.

How is India using the power of the Sun?In 2014 India had less than 3 gigawatts of solar power. By August 2026 it had more than 168, enough to make it one of the top solar countries.

The problem of night

The Sun sets and the wind drops, but people still want lights on at 9 pm. So the big puzzle of 2050 is storage. Batteries are getting cheaper every year, and power companies now build battery farms the size of football grounds.

Another trick is pumped hydro. When electricity is cheap, pumps push water uphill into a lake. When it is needed, the water rushes down through turbines. It is like a giant rechargeable battery made of water and mountains.

Wind, water and atoms

Wind turbines are getting taller and their blades longer. Some offshore blades are longer than a football field, catching steadier winds out at sea. Dams already give a large share of power in countries like Norway and Brazil, as well as parts of the Himalaya.

Nuclear power, which splits atoms, makes no smoke and runs day and night. Some countries, including India, plan to build more reactors. Others are closing them because of cost and worries about waste. Scientists honestly disagree on how big nuclear's part should be.

The wild cards

Some technologies could change everything, or turn out to be too hard. Nuclear fusion copies the way the Sun shines, by squeezing atoms together. Experiments have released bursts of energy, but power plants are not expected before the 2040s, and maybe later.

Green hydrogen, made by splitting water with renewable electricity, could fuel steel plants, ships and lorries. Right now it is expensive. India launched a National Green Hydrogen Mission in 2023 to make it cheaper, aiming to produce about 5 million tonnes a year by 2030. Scientists are also working on tidal power, geothermal heat from deep underground and space-based solar panels, though no one knows which will work at large scale.

What it means for you

By 2050, today's 10-year-olds will be in their mid-thirties. They may drive electric cars, cook on induction stoves and live in homes with panels on the roof. The air in cities like Delhi and Los Angeles could be much cleaner. The biggest change might be invisible: fewer greenhouse gases warming the planet. That is the real reason behind the whole shift, and why climate change and energy are two halves of the same story.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Solar and wind can never run a country because the Sun sets and the wind stops.

Truth: That is a real challenge, but it is being solved with batteries, pumped water storage, power lines that move electricity between regions, and backup sources like hydro and nuclear. Some countries already get most of their electricity from renewables on many days.

Try this

Do an energy audit at home. With an adult, list every device that plugs in or uses fuel, and note which ones run all day, like the fridge. Then look at an electricity bill to see how many units your home used last month. Pick one change, like switching off fans in empty rooms, and compare next month's bill.
