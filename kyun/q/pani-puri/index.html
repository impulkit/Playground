Why does pani puri have so many names?

Golgappa, puchka, gupchup, pani ke batashe: travel across India and the same crispy, water-filled snack changes its name every few hundred kilometres.

The short answer

Pani puri is a small, hollow, crisp ball of fried dough. You crack a hole in the top, fill it with potato, chickpeas or sprouts, dip it in spicy, tangy water, and pop the whole thing into your mouth at once. It's one of India's favourite street foods.

It has so many names because it spread across a country with dozens of languages and hundreds of dialects. Each region adopted the snack and named it in its own way. In Delhi it's golgappa, in Kolkata puchka, in Odisha gupchup, in Lucknow pani ke batashe, and in Mumbai pani puri.

Surprising but true

In July 2023, Google celebrated pani puri with an interactive Doodle game in which players served puris with the right flavours of water.

One snack, many names

Each name tells a little story. "Pani puri" simply means "water puri". "Golgappa" combines "gol" (round) with "gappa", from gobbling it in one mouthful. "Gupchup" may come from the way the puri fills your mouth so you can't talk, staying "gup-chup", or quiet.

"Puchka" in Bengal may come from the crack the puri makes when it bursts. "Pani ke batashe" compares it to batasha, a light, airy sugar sweet. In parts of Gujarat, it's called pakodi, and in some places "phulki", meaning puffed.

Why do people from the same country speak differently?An old Hindi saying goes "kos kos par badle paani, chaar kos par baani". Every few kilometres the water changes, and so does the way people speak.

These names show how food moves. A snack travels with people, but each place gives it a local name, a local filling and local water.

How the puri puffs

The puri is made from a dough of semolina, wheat flour or both. It's rolled very thin and cut into small circles. When the circles hit hot oil, the little bit of water inside the dough turns to steam in a flash.

The steam pushes the top and bottom of the circle apart, like blowing up a tiny balloon. The outside fries crisp around a hollow centre. Making perfect, evenly puffed puris takes skill, and many stalls buy them from specialist makers. It's a neat bit of cooking science: water becoming gas and taking up more than a thousand times as much space.

The water makes it

The spiced water, or pani, is what people argue about most. A classic version blends mint, coriander, green chillies, tamarind, black salt and roasted cumin. It's sour, spicy and sometimes very hot.

Many stalls also offer sweet water, made with tamarind and jaggery or dates. In Kolkata, puchka water is usually sour with tamarind, and the potato filling is mashed with chillies and spices. Mumbai stalls may offer several waters, from garlic to lemon to hing (asafoetida).

The art of eating it

Pani puri is usually eaten standing at a cart. The seller fills one puri at a time and hands it to you on a small leaf or paper bowl. You must eat each one immediately, before it goes soggy, and the seller keeps going until you say stop.

Many sellers give a free last puri, filled only with dry stuffing and spices, called a "sukha puri". Regulars know to ask for it.

Who invented it?

Nobody knows. A popular story says that in the Mahabharata, Draupadi was given leftover potatoes and a little flour, and told to feed her five husbands. She invented pani puri. It's a fun tale, but there's no historical evidence for it.

Food historians think the snack probably developed in North India in fairly recent centuries. Potatoes and chillies, now key ingredients, only reached India in the last 500 years. What we do know is that pani puri is now eaten everywhere, from village fairs to Indian restaurants in New Jersey.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Draupadi from the Mahabharata invented pani puri.

Truth: This is a popular folk tale, told as a test her mother-in-law gave her to feed five sons with very little flour. There's no historical record of where pani puri began, and the snack as we know it is probably much more recent.
