Where have spacecraft landed on the Moon?

The Moon is dotted with old landers, rovers, flags and footprints, and an Indian landing site now has its own name, Shiv Shakti point.

The short answer

Spacecraft have landed in many parts of the Moon's near side, the half that always faces Earth, and more recently on the far side and near the south pole.

The first soft landing, meaning a landing gentle enough to survive, was the Soviet Union's Luna 9 in February 1966. Between 1969 and 1972, six American Apollo missions put 12 astronauts on the Moon. China landed the first spacecraft on the far side in 2019. In August 2023, India's Chandrayaan-3 landed closer to the south pole than any mission before it.

By the end of 2025, five countries had made soft landings: the Soviet Union, the USA, China, India and Japan. Private companies have started landing too.

Surprising but true

The footprints left by Apollo astronauts more than 50 years ago are probably still there, because the Moon has no wind or rain to wipe them away.

The race of the 1960s

Landing on the Moon is hard. There is no air, so parachutes don't work, and a spacecraft has to slow itself down using rockets alone. Many early attempts crashed.

The space race between the USA and the Soviet Union pushed both countries to try again and again. After Luna 9, the US Surveyor 1 landed a few months later. Both sent back pictures showing that the surface was firm enough to stand on.

Then, on 20 July 1969, Apollo 11 touched down on a flat plain called the Sea of Tranquillity. Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin became the first people to walk on the Moon. Five more crewed landings followed. The last, Apollo 17 in December 1972, landed in a valley called Taurus-Littrow, and nobody has walked on the Moon since.

What was the space race?In just twelve years, two rival countries went from launching a beeping metal ball to putting people on the Moon.

Robots that rolled and dug

The Soviet Union chose robots. In 1970, Luna 16 scooped up Moon soil and flew it back to Earth without any astronauts. The same year, Luna 17 delivered Lunokhod 1, a remote-controlled rover that looked like a bathtub on eight wheels. Drivers on Earth steered it for about ten months and covered around 10 km, sending back thousands of pictures. They had to drive with a delay of a few seconds, since radio signals take time to cross the gap to the Moon and back. In 1973, Lunokhod 2 drove about 39 km, a distance record on another world that lasted until a Mars rover beat it in 2014.

A new wave of landers

For almost 40 years after 1976, no spacecraft made a soft landing on the Moon. Then China's Chang'e 3 landed in 2013. Chang'e 4 made history in 2019 by landing in Von Kármán crater on the far side, which is impossible to see from Earth. China later brought back soil from the near side in 2020, and from the far side in 2024, a first.

India's first attempt, Chandrayaan-2's Vikram lander, crashed in 2019. Four years later, Chandrayaan-3 landed safely on 23 August 2023, at about 69 degrees south. India named the spot Shiv Shakti point, and 23 August is now celebrated as National Space Day. The rover Pragyan drove around and measured the soil.

In January 2024, Japan's SLIM lander touched down within about 100 m of its target, though it tipped onto its nose.

Companies join in

In February 2024, the American company Intuitive Machines landed Odysseus near the south pole, the first privately built spacecraft to land on the Moon. It landed tilted on its side but still sent back data. In March 2025, another company, Firefly Aerospace, set its Blue Ghost lander down upright and working.

Several other landers, from Israel, Russia and a Japanese company, crashed in recent years. Each failure taught engineers something about one of the hardest parts of any space mission: the last few kilometres.

What is still there

Every landing site is a kind of museum. The bottom halves of the six Apollo landers, three moon buggies, scientific instruments and dozens of objects remain where they were left. NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter has photographed them from orbit, and its pictures even show the dark trails where astronauts walked. With no wind or rain, those spacesuit bootprints could last for millions of years.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The flag at the first Moon landing is still standing, waving in the breeze.

Truth: The Moon has no air, so flags can't wave. The Apollo flags had a rod along the top to hold them out. Buzz Aldrin said the Apollo 11 flag was knocked over by exhaust when the spacecraft left.

Try this

Fill a tray with flour or fine sand and smooth it flat. Press your thumb or a shoe into it to make a print, then blow gently across it, and finally sprinkle a little water on it. See how wind and rain erase prints on Earth, while on the Moon, with neither, they stay.
