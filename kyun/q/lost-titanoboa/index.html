How big was Titanoboa?

About 60 million years ago, a snake longer than a school bus and heavier than a small car slid through the swamps of South America.

The short answer

Titanoboa was a giant snake that lived about 60 million years ago in what is now Colombia, in northern South America. Scientists estimate it was about 13 m long and weighed around 1,100 kg.

That makes it longer than a typical school bus and heavier than a small car. Its body was so thick that at its widest, it would have reached up past a grown-up's waist. It is the biggest snake ever found, though a newer discovery from India may rival it.

Surprising but true

In 2024, scientists announced another giant snake found in a mine in Gujarat. Named Vasuki indicus, it may have been as long as Titanoboa, or even longer.

Found in a coal mine

Titanoboa's bones were found in the Cerrejón coal mine, one of the world's largest open-pit mines. Huge machines dig out coal there from rocks that formed in an ancient swampy rainforest.

In the early 2000s, scientists exploring the mine found fossils of giant turtles, crocodile relatives and fish. Among them were snake backbones far larger than those of any living snake. In 2009, a team announced the new giant in the journal Nature. They named it Titanoboa cerrejonensis, "titanic boa from Cerrejón".

Measuring a monster

A snake's body is mostly a long chain of backbones, called vertebrae, with ribs attached. Titanoboa's vertebrae were each about as wide as a man's hand stretched out.

By comparing them with the vertebrae of living boas and anacondas, scientists worked out how long and heavy the whole snake must have been. Because they had bones from many individuals, they could estimate the biggest ones well, though the numbers are still estimates.

The longest snake ever measured in captivity is a reticulated python called Medusa, at about 7.7 m. Titanoboa was almost twice as long.

A life in the swamp

Titanoboa lived after the dinosaurs had gone. The world was warm, and much of northern South America was covered in steamy rainforest with wide rivers and swamps, a bit like the Amazon today.

Like modern anacondas, it probably spent much of its time in water, which helped support its enormous weight. It was likely an ambush hunter, lying still and then grabbing prey. Its main food was probably fish, including lungfish more than 2 m long. It may also have caught crocodile relatives.

Titanoboa wasn't venomous. Like boas and pythons, it would have killed by squeezing.

Snakes and hot weather

Snakes are cold-blooded. Their bodies take the temperature of their surroundings, so very big snakes need warm places to stay active. The 2009 team suggested that, to grow so large, Titanoboa needed average temperatures of about 30 to 34°C, hotter than tropical South America today.

Other scientists argued that body size alone can't tell us the temperature so precisely. This debate is still going on.

India's giant snake

In 2024, scientists from IIT Roorkee described another huge snake, found in a lignite mine at Panandhro in Kutch, Gujarat. It lived about 47 million years ago. They named it Vasuki indicus, after Vasuki, the king of serpents in Hindu stories, who was wrapped around a mountain as a rope when gods and demons churned the ocean.

Based on its vertebrae, it may have been between about 11 and 15 m long. That could make it as long as Titanoboa, or even longer. It belonged to a different family of snakes, now extinct, and may have spread from India to Africa and Europe.

After the giants

No snake today comes close to these sizes. Snakes are still among the most surprising animals around, from pythons to India's own king cobra, which can grow over 5 m. Long before Titanoboa, the swamps of the world were ruled by another kind of giant, as the story of the giant insects shows.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Titanoboa lived alongside the dinosaurs and hunted them.

Truth: Titanoboa appeared a few million years after the big dinosaurs died out, about 66 million years ago. It never met a T. rex. It mostly ate fish, and perhaps crocodile relatives.

Try this

Measure Titanoboa. Take a ball of string and measure out 13 m along a corridor, playground or garden, with an adult's help. Lie down next to it and see how many of you would fit end to end. Then mark 7.7 m, the length of the longest snake ever measured in captivity.
