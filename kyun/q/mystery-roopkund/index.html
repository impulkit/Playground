Why are there skeletons in Roopkund Lake?

High in the Himalaya, at about 5,000 m, a small glacial lake is ringed with the bones of hundreds of people. DNA tests in 2019 made the mystery even stranger.

The short answer

Nobody knows the full answer yet. What scientists do know is that the bones at Roopkund came from several groups of people who died in different centuries.

A study in 2019 tested the DNA of 38 skeletons. Most belonged to people with South Asian ancestry who died between about 800 and 1000 CE. A second group, who died roughly 200 years ago, had ancestry like people from Greece and Crete today. One person had East Asian or Southeast Asian ancestry. How each group died, and why the Mediterranean group was there at all, is still a puzzle.

Surprising but true

DNA showed that some of the people who died at Roopkund around 1,000 years ago were from South Asia, but another group, who died only about 200 years ago, had ancestry like people living around the eastern Mediterranean today.

A lake of bones

Roopkund sits at about 5,000 m in the Himalaya of Uttarakhand, below the peak of Trishul. It is small, about 40 m across, and frozen for much of the year. When the snow melts each summer, bones show through the ice and the rocky slopes around it.

In 1942, a forest ranger named H. K. Madhwal came across the lake and was shocked. Hundreds of skeletons lay in and around the water. Some still had hair, bits of skin and even shoes, preserved by the cold. Because it was the Second World War, some people first guessed they were Japanese soldiers who had sneaked into India. They weren't.

The hailstorm idea

For years, the most popular explanation was a deadly storm. In 2004, scientists studying some skulls found short, deep cracks on the top of the heads. Something round and hard had hit them from above.

Local songs tell of the goddess Nanda Devi sending iron-hard hail to punish travellers who angered her. Some researchers suggested a real hailstorm with stones as big as cricket balls struck a group in the open. With nowhere to shelter at that height, many could have died within minutes. It remains a guess, and it may explain only some deaths.

What the DNA showed

Then came the 2019 study, by scientists from India, the USA and Europe. They pulled ancient DNA from the bones and used carbon dating to work out when each person died.

The results surprised everyone. The deaths were not all at once. The South Asian group seemed to have died in several events over a long period. The Mediterranean group died around 1800 CE. Chemicals locked in the bones showed that the groups had also eaten quite different diets during their lives. They were not one party of travellers.

Pilgrims on a holy path

Roopkund lies on the route of the Nanda Devi Raj Jat, a pilgrimage held roughly every 12 years. Thousands of people walk for about three weeks into the high mountains to honour the goddess Nanda Devi.

Many scientists think at least some of the South Asian group may have been pilgrims on an early form of this journey. But nobody has found records of the route that far back. And it is still unclear why people with Mediterranean ancestry were walking in the high Himalaya around 1800 CE.

Protecting the lake

Over the years, trekkers took bones and other remains as souvenirs, and some remains have been disturbed. Today the area is better protected, and visitors are asked to leave everything where it is.

Roopkund is a hard trek, and the thin air at that height can make people ill. Walkers usually climb slowly over several days to let their bodies adjust. Whoever died there had climbed into one of the harshest places in India. Each breath at that height holds only about half the oxygen of a breath on a Mumbai street, as the science of altitude explains.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Roopkund skeletons are from one army or group of travellers who all died together.

Truth: That was the old idea. Ancient DNA and carbon dating showed the bones belong to several groups who died at very different times, separated by roughly a thousand years.
