Who are the Bauls of Bengal?

The tune of Bangladesh's national anthem comes from a song by a Baul, a wandering singer who worked as a village postman.

The short answer

The Bauls are wandering singers and mystics of Bengal, in eastern India and Bangladesh. They travel from village to village, singing songs about love, God and the meaning of life, often for food or small gifts. Many wear loose robes in saffron or patched colours and keep their hair long.

A Baul usually plays the ektara, a one-string instrument made from a gourd and a split bamboo, and may tie bells to the ankles and dance while singing. Their songs say that God is not found in temples, mosques or holy books, but inside each person. They call this inner divine presence the maner manush, "the person of the heart". In 2008 UNESCO added Baul songs to its list of the world's intangible cultural heritage.

Surprising but true

Two Baul singers appear on the cover of a 1967 album by the American singer Bob Dylan.

Who becomes a Baul

Bauls come from Hindu and Muslim families, and some have no family background in the tradition at all. Many leave ordinary village life to follow a teacher, called a guru, and learn the songs and spiritual practices by living with them.

They mix ideas from Vaishnava Hinduism, Buddhist traditions and Sufi Islam, and they reject caste. The word baul may come from a Sanskrit word meaning "mad" or "restless", because Bauls are said to be mad with love for the divine. Nobody is quite sure.

Lalon Fakir

The most famous Baul was Lalon Fakir, also called Lalon Shah, who lived in the 1700s and 1800s near Kushtia, in today's Bangladesh. Little is known for certain about his early life, and he refused to say which religion he was born into. One of his songs asks why people care so much about a person's caste or religion when everyone is born and dies the same way.

Lalon composed hundreds of songs, which his followers learned by heart. His shrine at Kushtia hosts a large festival every year, where Bauls sing through the night.

Tagore and the anthem

The poet Rabindranath Tagore grew up hearing Baul songs on his family's estates in rural Bengal. He collected them, wrote about them and borrowed their simple, powerful style in his own work. Some of his best-loved songs follow Baul tunes.

The clearest example is "Amar Sonar Bangla", which Tagore wrote in 1905. Its melody is based on a Baul song by Gagan Harkara, a village postman. In 1971 the song became the national anthem of Bangladesh. So every time the anthem is played, a Baul's tune is heard.

The instruments

Besides the ektara, Bauls play the dotara, a long-necked instrument with several strings, and a small drum called the dubki or duggi. The khamak, or anandalahari, is a little drum with a string attached. The player tightens and loosens the string to make a twanging, talking sound.

The ektara itself is simple but clever. Squeezing its two bamboo arms changes the string's tension, and with it the pitch of the note.

Bauls and the world

In the 1960s, the Baul singer Purna Das Baul travelled to the United States and met musicians there. Two Bauls appear with Bob Dylan on the cover of his 1967 album John Wesley Harding. Purna Das Baul went on to perform around the world for decades.

Today the biggest gathering is the fair at Joydev Kenduli in Birbhum, West Bengal, held every January around Makar Sankranti. Thousands of Bauls arrive, set up camps, and sing day and night by the Ajay river. Some people worry that Baul music is becoming a show for tourists, while others say new audiences help keep it alive.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Baul songs belong to just one religion.

Truth: Bauls draw on Hindu, Buddhist and Muslim Sufi ideas, and many reject divisions of religion and caste altogether. They sing about finding the divine inside the human body and heart.

Try this

Make a simple one-string instrument. Stretch a rubber band lengthwise around an empty tissue box or a small plastic tub, and slide a pencil under it as a bridge. Pluck it, then press the band in different places, or pull the box sides, and listen for higher and lower notes.
