Why do people shake hands?

Nearly 3,000 years ago, an Assyrian sculptor carved two kings shaking hands on a throne base, one of the oldest pictures of a handshake ever found.

The short answer

People shake hands to greet, to agree and to show trust. A handshake says "we are equals" and "I mean you no harm". It is used to meet new people, finish a deal, congratulate a winner or make peace after an argument.

Nobody knows exactly where it started. Pictures of handshakes go back nearly 3,000 years, to ancient Assyria in today's Iraq. Ancient Greeks carved them on gravestones. A popular story says people offered an open right hand to show they had no weapon, and shaking it up and down would shake loose any hidden dagger. That is a nice tale, but there is no strong evidence for it.

Surprising but true

A study in Wales in 2014 found that a handshake passed on many more bacteria than a fist bump, about ten times as many in their tests.

Kings and gravestones

On a carved throne base from about 850 BCE, the Assyrian king Shalmaneser III is shown clasping hands with a Babylonian king to seal an alliance. It is one of the oldest known images of a handshake. In Mesopotamia, the gesture meant a deal was agreed.

In ancient Greece, from the 5th century BCE, gravestones often show the dead person shaking hands with a family member. It may have meant a farewell, or the bond between them continuing after death. Greeks called this gesture dexiosis, the joining of right hands. The Romans used handshakes for promises and marriages.

Quakers and equals

For centuries in Europe, people greeted others by bowing, curtsying or tipping a hat, depending on who was more important. In the 1600s, the Quakers, a Christian group in England, believed that everyone was equal before God. They refused to bow to anyone and shook hands instead.

The Quakers helped spread handshakes in Britain and in American colonies like Pennsylvania. By the 1800s, books on manners described the handshake as a normal greeting between gentlemen. Over time, it became common for everyone, in business, sport and politics.

Greetings around the world

The handshake is not used everywhere. In India, many people greet with namaste, pressing palms together, sometimes with a small bow. It needs no touch, and it is a sign of respect. In Japan, people bow, and the depth of the bow shows how much respect is meant.

In France and parts of South America, friends often kiss on the cheek. Maori people in New Zealand perform the hongi, pressing noses and foreheads together. In some cultures, men and women who are not related don't touch at all when greeting. Knowing what to do takes practice, and getting it wrong can be awkward.

Germs and the pandemic

Hands carry germs. That's why washing hands is one of the best ways to stop illness spreading. In 2014, researchers in Wales tested greetings with gloves dipped in bacteria. A handshake passed on many more bacteria than a high five, and about ten times as many as a fist bump.

When COVID-19 spread in 2020, handshakes almost vanished. People tried elbow bumps, foot taps, waves and namaste. World leaders were seen greeting each other with folded hands. Many people predicted handshakes would never return. In most places, though, they came back.

What a handshake says

Many people believe a handshake reveals something about a person. A firm grip is often seen as confident. A limp one can seem shy or unfriendly. Some studies suggest people do judge each other on handshakes, though first impressions can be wrong.

In sport, handshakes mark respect. Cricketers shake hands after a match. Footballers shake hands before kick-off. In business, a handshake has long sealed a deal, even before the contract is signed. It is a tiny gesture with a long history, and it stays alive because humans everywhere want a way to show trust. Myth or fact?

Myth: Handshakes definitely started as a way of showing you weren't holding a sword.

Truth: It's a popular explanation, and it might be partly true, but there's no clear evidence for it. Ancient pictures show handshakes as signs of agreement, friendship and trust, not weapon checks.

Try this

Try a glitter germ test. Put a little glitter or flour on one person's hand to stand for germs. Then try different greetings with others, such as a handshake, a high five, a fist bump and a namaste. Count how much glitter ends up on each person's hand. Wash all hands well afterwards.
