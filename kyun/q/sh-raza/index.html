Who was S.H. Raza?

As a restless schoolboy in a forest village, Sayed Haider Raza was told to stare at a dot on the wall. Decades later, that dot made him famous.

The short answer

Sayed Haider Raza was one of the most important Indian painters of the 20th century. He was born in 1922 in a village in the forests of Madhya Pradesh, trained in Nagpur and Bombay, and spent most of his life in France.

He is best known for the bindu, a dark circle placed at the centre of a painting with bright shapes and colours spreading out around it. These abstract works don't show people or places in the usual way. Instead they try to capture energy, nature and ideas from Indian philosophy. His paintings now hang in major museums and sell for crores of rupees.

Surprising but true

Raza lived in France for about 60 years, yet the paintings that made his name are full of Indian ideas, colours and memories of the forests along the Narmada.

A dot on the classroom wall

Raza grew up in Mandla district, near the Narmada river, where his father was a forest officer. He later remembered dense jungles, dark nights and the fear and wonder they gave him.

He also remembered a teacher. Young Raza found it hard to concentrate, so the teacher drew a dot on the school wall and told him to stare at it. Raza said that exercise stayed with him all his life. Decades later, the same simple mark became the heart of his art.

He studied at the Nagpur School of Art and then at the Sir J.J. School of Art in Bombay. In his early years he painted watercolours of Bombay's streets and buildings.

The Progressive Artists

In 1947, the year India became independent, Raza joined a few young painters in Bombay to form the Progressive Artists' Group. Its members included F.N. Souza and M.F. Husain. They wanted Indian art to be bold and modern, and to break away from both old-fashioned British academic painting and the gentle style of the Bengal School.

Their timing mattered. A new nation was asking what it wanted to look like, and these artists offered fresh answers. Several of them went on to become famous around the world.

Why are wheels round?A square wheel would bump you up and down at every turn. A circle's secret is that its middle never moves up or down.

Sixty years in France

In 1950, Raza won a scholarship to study in Paris. He stayed for about 60 years, married the French painter Janine Mongillat and lived partly in a village in southern France.

At first he painted French landscapes, with thick paint and stormy colours. Then, from the 1970s, something shifted. Raza began to look back toward India. He drew on the colours of Rajasthani miniatures and the black, red and yellow of Indian earth. He also read old Hindu and Buddhist ideas about how the world began.

Around 1980 the bindu arrived. He painted it again and again, surrounded by triangles, squares, rings and wavy lines. Titles were short and often Indian: Bindu, Prakriti (nature), Germination.

He usually worked in acrylic paint on canvas, building up flat, glowing areas of colour with clean edges. Some critics found the later works too repetitive. Admirers answer that no two are alike. Like a singer returning to a favourite raga, he kept changing the colours, the rhythm of the shapes and the mood around the same small circle.

Coming home

Raza returned to live in India in 2010, at the age of 88, and kept painting in Delhi almost until his death in 2016. As he wished, he was buried in Mandla, next to his father.

In 2010, his painting Saurashtra sold at auction in London for about ₹16 crore. Raza used much of his wealth to set up the Raza Foundation, which gives grants to young artists, poets and dancers.

He received the Padma Vibhushan, one of India's highest civilian honours, in 2013, and France made him a member of its Legion of Honour in 2015. A painter from a forest village had found a language of shapes that people on both continents could feel.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Abstract paintings with no people or objects in them don't mean anything.

Truth: Abstract artists like Raza often filled their pictures with ideas, feelings and memories. Raza said his circles and colours came from the landscapes of his childhood and from Indian thinking about the beginning of life.

Try this

Draw a dot in the middle of a sheet of paper. Then, using only crayons or paints, build outward from it with circles, triangles or stripes, picking colours that remind you of a place you love. Give your painting a one-word title, the way Raza often did.
