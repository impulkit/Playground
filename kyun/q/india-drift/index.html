How did India travel across the planet?

India hasn't always been where it is. It sailed thousands of kilometres.

The short answer

Long ago, India was part of a giant southern supercontinent called Gondwana.

About 120 million years ago, India broke away. It drifted north on a tectonic plate, faster than almost any other land on Earth.

Around 50 million years ago it crashed into Asia. It's still pushing north today.

Surprising but true

At top speed, India moved about 15 cm a year, as fast as your hair grows.

Continents on the move

Earth's outer shell is broken into plates that move a few centimetres a year, carried by the slowly churning mantle below. Over millions of years, that adds up to thousands of kilometres.

In 1912, a German scientist named Alfred Wegener suggested that the continents had once been joined and had drifted apart. He pointed to coastlines that fit together like a jigsaw, and to the same fossil plants and animals on continents now separated by oceans. A fern-like plant called Glossopteris, for example, is found in India, Africa, Australia, South America and Antarctica.

Most scientists rejected his idea for decades, because nobody could explain what force moved the continents. Wegener died on an expedition in Greenland in 1930. Only in the 1960s did new evidence from the ocean floor show he had been right.

Why India was so fast

Scientists think India's plate was thin and was pulled strongly as the ocean floor in front of it sank into the mantle. It raced north faster than most continents ever have.

At its fastest, it moved about 15 to 20 cm a year, about as fast as hair grows. That sounds slow, but kept up for millions of years, it carried India thousands of kilometres. Scientists still debate exactly why. One idea is that India lost the deep, cool "roots" that slow most continents down, perhaps when it was heated by plumes of hot rock.

Lava on the way

About 66 million years ago, India passed over a hot spot in Earth's mantle, the same one that now sits under the island of Réunion. Huge cracks opened, and lava poured out again and again, layer upon layer. The flows covered much of western and central India and built the Deccan plateau. The eruptions happened around the same time as the asteroid strike that ended the age of dinosaurs. Some scientists think the gases from the lava made things worse for life everywhere, while others think the asteroid did almost all the damage.

You can still see those layers as giant steps in the hills of the Western Ghats, around places like Mahabaleshwar. The black soil of the Deccan, so good for growing cotton, formed as that lava rock weathered.

Clues it really happened

Matching rocks and fossils in India, Africa, Antarctica and Australia, magnetic patterns frozen in old lava, and modern GPS measurements all tell the same story.

Living things carry the story too. The purple frog of the Western Ghats spends most of its life underground. Its closest living relatives are tiny frogs in the Seychelles, islands that broke off from India long ago. The two lines of frogs have been apart ever since.

Still moving

India still pushes into Asia. That's why the Himalayas keep rising and why the region has earthquakes.

Today scientists measure the movement with satellite navigation, the same technology behind GPS in phones. Fixed stations across India show the land creeping north-east by about 4 to 5 cm every year. In about a million years, that adds up to around 50 km.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The continents moved long ago, but they have stopped now.

Truth: They are still moving, a few centimetres every year. GPS stations can measure it. India is still pushing north into Asia today, which is why the Himalayas keep rising and the region keeps having earthquakes.
