Why is Istanbul in two continents?

In Istanbul you can eat breakfast in Europe, take a 20-minute ferry, and eat lunch in Asia without leaving the city.

The short answer

Istanbul sits on both sides of the Bosphorus, a narrow strip of sea that joins the Black Sea to the Sea of Marmara. Geographers draw the line between Europe and Asia along this strait, so the city spills across two continents. The old centre, with its palaces and great domed mosques, is on the European side. Millions of people live on the Asian side and cross every day by ferry, bridge or tunnel.

The city grew there for a reason. Whoever held this spot could control ships going between the Black Sea and the Mediterranean, and roads going between Europe and Asia. For over 1,500 years, that made it the capital of mighty empires.

Surprising but true

Three names, three empires

Greek settlers founded a town called Byzantion here around 660 BCE. In 330 CE, the Roman emperor Constantine made it his new capital and called it Constantinople. After the western half of the Roman Empire fell, the eastern half carried on from here for another thousand years. Historians call it the Byzantine Empire.

In 1453, the young Ottoman sultan Mehmed II besieged the city. A huge chain blocked the harbour, called the Golden Horn. So Mehmed's men built a road of greased logs and hauled dozens of ships over a hill behind the city and into the water beyond the chain. Within weeks the city fell. It became the capital of the Ottoman Empire, and the name Istanbul slowly took over.

The church that became a mosque

The most famous building in the city is Hagia Sophia, finished in 537 CE under the emperor Justinian. For nearly a thousand years it was one of the largest churches in the world, with a dome that seems to float above a ring of windows.

When the Ottomans took the city, they turned it into a mosque and added tall minarets. In 1934 the Turkish government made it a museum. In 2020 it became a mosque again, while staying open to visitors. Across the square stands the Blue Mosque, named for the blue tiles inside.

Markets, tulips and cats

The Grand Bazaar, opened in the 1460s, is one of the oldest covered markets in the world. Around 4,000 shops line its maze of roofed streets, selling carpets, lamps, spices and gold. Goods from the Silk Road and the spice routes once passed through traders here.

Tulips grew wild in Central Asia, and Ottoman sultans loved them. They planted tulips in palace gardens and painted them on tiles. In the 1500s tulip bulbs travelled from Istanbul to Europe and on to the Netherlands, where they later became a national symbol.

Istanbul is also famous for its street cats. Thousands roam freely, and shopkeepers and neighbours leave out food and water. Some cats have become local celebrities.

Crossing between continents

For most of history, the only way across the Bosphorus was by boat. The first bridge opened in 1973. In 2013, a railway tunnel called Marmaray opened deep under the strait, built in sections on land, then sunk into a trench on the seabed. Digging it up turned out to be a bonus: workers found an old harbour with the remains of dozens of Byzantine shipwrecks.

At its narrowest, the Bosphorus is only about 700 metres wide, about the length of seven football fields. Huge oil tankers squeeze through it past the waterfront houses.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Istanbul is the capital of Turkey.

Truth: Ankara has been the capital since 1923. Istanbul is the largest city and the centre of business and culture, a bit like Mumbai in India or New York in the USA.
