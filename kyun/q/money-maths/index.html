How does interest make money grow?

In 1790, Benjamin Franklin left a small sum to two American cities, with orders not to spend most of it for 200 years. It grew into millions.

The short answer

When you put money in a bank, the bank pays you a little extra for letting it use your money. That extra is called interest. It is usually given as a percentage each year.

There are two kinds. With simple interest, you earn interest only on the money you first put in. With compound interest, the interest is added to your savings, and then next year you earn interest on that too.

At first, the difference is small. Over many years, compound interest pulls far ahead, because the pile it grows on keeps getting bigger. It is the same doubling that makes big numbers explode.

Surprising but true

A shortcut called the Rule of 72 tells you how long money takes to double. It was written down by an Italian mathematician in 1494.

Watching money grow

Suppose you put ₹1,000 in an account paying 10% a year.

With simple interest, you earn ₹100 every year. After 10 years you have ₹2,000. After 30 years, ₹4,000.

With compound interest, the first year also gives ₹100, so you have ₹1,100. But the next year you earn 10% of ₹1,100, which is ₹110. The year after, ₹121. After 10 years you have about ₹2,594. After 30 years you have more than ₹17,000, over four times as much as simple interest gives.

Real bank rates are usually lower than 10%, but the pattern is the same. Time matters more than anything.

The Rule of 72

There is a quick way to estimate how long money takes to double. Divide 72 by the interest rate. At 6% a year, money doubles in about 12 years. At 9%, about 8 years.

The Italian mathematician Luca Pacioli described this rule in a famous book on arithmetic in 1494. He didn't explain where it came from, which suggests merchants were already using it. It works because of the way growth by percentages adds up, and it gives a good answer for rates between about 4% and 12%.

Franklin's 200-year experiment

When the American inventor and statesman Benjamin Franklin died in 1790, he left £1,000 each to the cities of Boston and Philadelphia. The money was to be lent to young tradespeople, and most of it was to be left to grow for 200 years.

Part of each fund was spent after 100 years, as Franklin had planned. The rest kept growing. By 1990, when the 200 years were up, the two funds together were worth several million dollars. The money went towards training and education in both cities.

Interest works both ways

When you borrow money, you are the one paying interest. Loans for houses, cars and education all charge it. Credit cards can charge very high rates, sometimes more than 30% a year in India. If a credit card bill isn't paid off, the debt can compound just as fast as savings do, but against you.

Banks make their money from the gap. They pay savers a lower rate than they charge borrowers. Governments also borrow and pay interest, which is one reason taxes are needed.

The king and the chessboard

An old Indian story tells of the inventor of chess, who asked a king for a reward of rice. He wanted one grain on the first square of the board, two on the second, four on the third, doubling each time for all 64 squares.

The king laughed at such a small request. Then his officials did the sums. The last square alone needed more rice than the whole world now grows in a hundred years. The legend of chess is told in many versions, but it shows what steady doubling can do over time.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Albert Einstein called compound interest "the eighth wonder of the world".

Truth: This quote is all over the internet, but nobody has found any evidence that Einstein ever said it. Like many famous-sounding sayings, it was pinned on a famous name.

Try this

Put one grain of rice on the first square of a chessboard drawn on paper, two on the second and four on the third, doubling each time. Stop when you run out of rice or patience. Then work out how many grains square 20 would need. That doubling is how compound interest grows over very long times.
