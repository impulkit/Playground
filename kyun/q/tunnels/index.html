How do people dig tunnels under rivers and mountains?

A modern tunnelling machine is longer than a train, crawls a few metres an hour and builds the walls behind it.

The short answer

There are a few main ways to build a tunnel. For shallow tunnels, workers can dig a trench, build the tunnel inside it and then cover it over. This is called cut-and-cover, and many older underground railways were built like this.

Through hard rock, crews drill holes, fill them with explosives and blast the rock away, a few metres at a time. Then they clear the rubble and strengthen the walls with steel and concrete.

For long tunnels through softer rock or under cities and rivers, engineers use a tunnel boring machine. Its front is a giant spinning wheel covered in cutters that grinds through the ground. Behind it, the machine fits curved concrete pieces into rings that become the tunnel's walls. It is a moving factory, often longer than a train.

Surprising but true

The first tunnel under a big, busy river, below the Thames in London, used a digging shield inspired by the shipworm, a creature that bores through wooden ships.

Brunel's tunnel under the Thames

In 1825, the engineer Marc Brunel began digging a tunnel under the River Thames in London. Nobody had ever tunnelled under a big, busy river before. The soft, wet mud kept threatening to cave in.

Brunel had studied the shipworm, which bores through wood while protecting itself with its shell. He designed an iron shield for his workers to stand inside as they dug, inching forward bit by bit. The river burst in more than once, and his young son, Isambard Kingdom Brunel, nearly drowned. The tunnel finally opened in 1843. It still carries trains today, and nearly every soft-ground tunnel since has used a shield.

Under the sea

The Channel Tunnel joins England and France. It is about 50 kilometres long, and almost 38 kilometres of it lie under the sea. Teams dug from both sides, through a layer of chalky rock that was soft enough to cut but tight enough to keep water out. In 1990, the two sides broke through to each other less than a metre apart. Trains began running in 1994.

Kolkata now has India's first underwater metro. Its twin tunnels pass beneath the Hooghly river, joining Howrah to the city centre. Trains began carrying passengers in 2024, and they cross under the river in under a minute.

Through the mountains

Mountains bring other problems: hard rock, heat deep inside, water gushing from cracks and, sometimes, rocks that shift. The Gotthard Base Tunnel in Switzerland, opened in 2016, runs about 57 kilometres beneath the Alps. It is the longest railway tunnel in the world, and in places there are more than 2 kilometres of rock overhead.

In the Himalaya, the Atal Tunnel near Manali opened in 2020. It is about 9 kilometres long and lies above 3,000 metres. It lets traffic avoid the Rohtang Pass, which is often closed by snow for months. The young Himalaya are still rising and full of cracked rock, which makes tunnelling there especially hard.

Keeping a tunnel safe

Long tunnels need fresh air, so giant fans push stale air out and pull clean air in. They need lights, drainage pumps and escape routes. The Channel Tunnel actually has three tunnels: two for trains and a smaller service tunnel between them for workers and emergencies.

Tunnel crews have old traditions too. In many countries, a tunnel boring machine is given a woman's name before it starts digging. In Europe, a small statue of Saint Barbara, the protector of miners, is often placed at the tunnel entrance. Indian crews often hold a puja before the cutters first bite into the rock.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Tunnels under rivers and seas are always pipes laid on the bottom.

Truth: Some are, built from huge concrete sections sunk into a trench. But many, like the Channel Tunnel, are bored through solid rock well below the seabed, where the rock itself keeps the water out.

Try this

In a sandpit or a big tub, make two mounds, one of dry sand and one of damp sand. Try to push a tunnel through each with your fingers. The dry one collapses. Now line a tunnel in the dry sand with a cardboard tube, like the concrete rings in a real tunnel, and see how it holds.
