What are the Special Olympics?

The Special Olympics began in a backyard in Maryland in 1962, where Eunice Kennedy Shriver ran a summer sports camp for children many people had written off.

The short answer

The Special Olympics is a worldwide sports movement for people with intellectual disabilities. An intellectual disability means a person learns and understands things more slowly than most people, and may need help with some everyday tasks.

Special Olympics athletes train all year in sports like athletics, swimming, football, basketball and bocce, an Italian bowling game. They compete in local, national and world games.

Its spirit is in the athlete oath, spoken at every event: "Let me win. But if I cannot win, let me be brave in the attempt."

Surprising but true

At the Special Olympics, athletes are first grouped with others of similar ability, so a race is close and everyone has a real chance of winning.

A backyard camp

In the early 1960s, people with intellectual disabilities were often kept out of sight. Many lived in large institutions and had little chance to play sports or even go to school.

Eunice Kennedy Shriver thought that was wrong. Her own sister, Rosemary, had an intellectual disability. In 1962 Shriver opened a summer day camp in the backyard of her home in Maryland, in the USA. She called it Camp Shriver. Children came to swim, ride horses, kick balls and climb.

The children did far more than many experts expected. Shriver became convinced that sport could change how the world saw people with intellectual disabilities.

The first games

On 20 July 1968, the first International Special Olympics Summer Games were held at Soldier Field, a big football stadium in Chicago. About 1,000 athletes from the USA and Canada took part.

From there the idea spread fast. Winter games began in 1977. Today Special Olympics says it reaches nearly 4 million athletes and Unified partners in 177 countries and territories, as of 2024. World Games, summer and winter, are held every few years in different countries.

Making races fair

Special Olympics does something unusual before a competition. Athletes are first timed or tested, and then placed in small groups called divisions with others of a similar ability. A runner who takes 20 seconds for a race won't be up against one who takes 40 seconds.

This makes every race close and exciting. An athlete with less experience has as much chance of winning gold in their division as a fast one in theirs.

Everyone who finishes is recognised. The top three get medals, and the others in each final get ribbons.

Playing together

Special Olympics also runs Unified Sports. Here, people with and without intellectual disabilities play on the same team. A Unified team mixes players with and without intellectual disabilities, often in roughly equal numbers, all of a similar skill level.

Teammates train and compete as equals. Schools in many countries now run Unified clubs, and friendships often carry on off the field. This is part of a bigger idea, inclusion, which means building a world where everyone can take part.

India and the Special Olympics

India's programme, Special Olympics Bharat, trains hundreds of thousands of athletes across the country. Indian athletes have brought home large hauls of medals from World Games, in sports such as athletics, cycling, roller skating and powerlifting.

Some Indian athletes have travelled abroad for the first time to compete. For their families, seeing them on a podium with the national flag can be as big a moment as any cricket victory.

How it differs from the Paralympics

People often mix up the Special Olympics and the Paralympics. The Paralympics are held right after the Olympics, in the same city. They are for top athletes, mostly with physical or visual impairments, and only the very best qualify, just as in the Olympics.

The Special Olympics are for people with intellectual disabilities of every ability level. A beginner and a world-class athlete are both welcome. The goal is to give everyone the chance to train, compete and be cheered on, however fast or slow they are.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Special Olympics and the Paralympics are the same thing.

Truth: They are separate movements. The Paralympics are for elite athletes with mainly physical or visual impairments, and only the fastest qualify. The Special Olympics are for people with intellectual disabilities at every level of ability, with training all year round.
