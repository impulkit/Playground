How did one man make cheap sanitary pads for India?

A school dropout from Coimbatore spent years building a machine that big companies had kept to themselves, and ended up in a Bollywood film.

The short answer

When girls and women reach a certain age, their bodies have a monthly period, a few days of light bleeding that is a normal part of growing up. For that, most people use a sanitary pad. In the 1990s, pads in India were expensive, and many women used old cloth instead, which can cause infections if it isn't clean.

Arunachalam Muruganantham, a workshop welder from Coimbatore in Tamil Nadu, decided to fix this. After years of failures, he built a simple machine that lets a small group of people make good pads cheaply. He sold his machines mostly to groups of village women, who make and sell the pads themselves.

Surprising but true

Arunachalam Muruganantham tested his early pads on himself, wearing a rubber pouch of animal blood under his clothes, because few women would talk to him about it.

It started at home

Around 1998, Muruganantham saw his wife Shanthi hiding a dirty rag. She told him that if she bought pads, the family couldn't afford milk. He bought a packet to give her and was shocked: a few grams of cotton were being sold at a high price.

He tried making pads from cotton, and they failed. The trouble was that almost no woman would test them or talk about them. So he wore one himself, with a rubber pouch of animal blood under his clothes to see how it worked. People in his village thought he had gone mad. For a while, his wife and mother moved out.

The secret material

The big companies didn't use ordinary cotton. It took Muruganantham about two years to discover their real ingredient: fluffy fibres made from pine wood pulp, a form of cellulose. Factory machines that turned this pulp into pads cost crores of rupees, far beyond any village.

So, like many Indian inventors, he built his own. His machine has four simple steps: it fluffs the pulp, presses it into shape, wraps it in a soft cover, and cleans it with ultraviolet light. It is simple enough for people with no factory training to learn quickly.

Jobs as well as pads

Muruganantham could have sold his design to a large company and become rich. Instead he sold machines mostly to women's self-help groups and charities. Each machine gives work to a handful of women, who buy the materials, make the pads and sell them to neighbours. That is a different way of thinking about business: the aim is to spread the work, not keep it.

His machines spread across India and to other countries. In 2014 TIME magazine named him one of the 100 most influential people in the world. In 2016 India gave him the Padma Shri.

Talking about it

Part of Muruganantham's work has simply been getting people to talk. For years, many girls in India missed school during their periods, partly because schools lacked clean toilets and pads. Many state governments now hand out free or cheap pads to schoolgirls, and lessons about growing up are part of health education in many schools.

From a village to the movies

In 2018 Bollywood released PadMan, a film based on his story. The same year, a short documentary called Period. End of Sentence. followed women running one of his machines in a village near Hapur in Uttar Pradesh. It won an Oscar in 2019.

Things have changed a lot. India's national health survey found that about 58 percent of women aged 15 to 24 used hygienic methods in 2015–16, rising to about 77 percent in 2019–21. Since 2019, government medicine shops have sold pads for one rupee each. Good public health often looks like this: small, cheap and close to home.

And Muruganantham isn't the only Indian who changed lives with a stubborn idea. A man in Assam once planted a whole forest, tree by tree, on a bare sandbank, and you can meet him in the story of the forest man.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Periods are something to be ashamed of, so nobody should talk about them.

Truth: Periods are a normal, healthy part of growing up for about half of all people. Not talking about them made it harder for girls to get pads, stay in school and stay healthy. Asking a parent, teacher or doctor is always fine.
