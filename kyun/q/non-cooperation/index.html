What was the Non-Cooperation Movement?

In 1920, Gandhi asked Indians to beat the British Empire by simply refusing to help run it, and millions of people stopped.

The short answer

The Non-Cooperation Movement was a huge, peaceful protest against British rule in India, led by Mahatma Gandhi from 1920 to 1922. Its idea was simple. The British ruled India with the help of Indians: as clerks, soldiers, lawyers, students and buyers of British goods. If Indians stopped cooperating, the government would struggle to work.

So people left government schools and colleges. Lawyers walked out of British courts. Some gave back medals and titles. Crowds lit bonfires of foreign cloth and began wearing homespun cotton instead. It was the first time the freedom movement reached so deeply into villages and small towns.

The Congress approved the plan at a special session in Calcutta in September 1920 and confirmed it at Nagpur that December. At Nagpur it also changed itself. Anyone could now join by paying four annas a year, and committees were set up in districts and villages, working in Indian languages as well as English.

Surprising but true

Gandhi stopped the movement at its height, after one violent clash in a small town, even though many of his own colleagues begged him to carry on.

Why 1920?

The years after the First World War left many Indians angry. More than a million Indians had served in the British war effort, and they expected more self-rule in return. Instead, in 1919, the Rowlatt Act let the police jail people without trial.

That April, British troops fired on a peaceful crowd at Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar, killing hundreds. At the same time, many Indian Muslims were upset that Britain was breaking up the Ottoman Empire, whose sultan many saw as the Khalifa, a religious leader. Their Khilafat movement joined with Gandhi's campaign, bringing Hindus and Muslims together in a way rarely seen before.

What people gave up

Gandhi promised swaraj, self-rule, within a year if people stuck together. Thousands of students left British-run colleges. New national schools opened, including Jamia Millia Islamia and Gujarat Vidyapith, both founded in 1920 and still running.

Famous lawyers, including Motilal Nehru and C.R. Das, gave up rich careers. Women joined in large numbers, picketing shops that sold foreign cloth and liquor. When Britain's Prince of Wales toured India in late 1921, many cities greeted him with closed shops and empty streets. Spinning wheels hummed in homes, and wearing khadi, handspun cloth, became a badge of protest.

Gandhi himself handed back the Kaisar-i-Hind medal the British had given him for running an ambulance corps in South Africa. Rabindranath Tagore had already given up his knighthood in 1919, after Jallianwala Bagh. Even so, Tagore had doubts about parts of the movement, such as students leaving school, and he and Gandhi argued about it in public, politely but firmly.

Chauri Chaura

On 4 February 1922, in the small town of Chauri Chaura in today's Uttar Pradesh, police fired on protesters. The angry crowd set fire to the police station, and 22 policemen died.

Gandhi was horrified. He believed that freedom won by violence would lead to more violence. A week later he called off the whole movement. Many leaders were shocked, including Motilal Nehru and the young Subhas Chandra Bose, who felt the movement was close to success. Soon after, Gandhi was arrested and sentenced to six years in prison, though he was released after two.

What it changed

The British did not leave in 1922, and the promise of swaraj in a year was not met. The Hindu-Muslim unity of those years also faded after the Khilafat cause ended.

Still, the movement turned the Congress from a party of educated leaders into a mass movement of farmers, workers, students and women. It made boycott and homespun cloth familiar tools, building on the earlier Swadeshi movement. Eight years later, ordinary Indians would use the same methods when they followed Gandhi on the Salt March.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Non-violent protest is a weak, easy way to protest.

Truth: Non-cooperation asked people to give up jobs, schools, titles and income, and thousands went to jail. Staying calm in the face of arrests and beatings took organisation and great courage.

Try this

With a classmate, list five everyday things your school or town needs people to do to keep running, such as driving buses or selling vegetables. Talk about what would happen if everyone stopped doing one of them for a day. That is the idea behind non-cooperation.
