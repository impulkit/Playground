Why do rainforests have so many kinds of life?

A scientist once found as many kinds of ants on a single rainforest tree as live in the whole of Britain.

The short answer

Rainforests are warm and wet all year, with plenty of sunlight. Plants can grow every month, and there is food every month for the animals that eat them. Many rainforests have also stayed warm and wet for millions of years, giving life a very long time to branch out into new kinds.

Rainforests cover only about 6% of Earth's land, but they may hold half or more of all land species. Many of those species haven't been named yet. Every expedition still turns up new insects, frogs and plants.

Surprising but true

In Peru, the biologist E. O. Wilson counted 43 kinds of ants living on one tree, about the same number as in all of the British Isles.

Warm, wet and steady

Tropical rainforests lie near the equator, where the Sun is high all year. There is no winter. It rains often, usually more than 2,000 mm a year. That is more than twice what falls on Delhi or Chicago in an average year.

With warmth, water and light, plants can grow without pause. Through photosynthesis, they turn sunlight into food, and that food feeds everything else. More plant growth means more leaves, fruits and flowers to eat, and more kinds of animals that can make a living.

A forest of floors

A rainforest isn't one habitat. It is several stacked on top of each other, like the floors of a building.

The tallest trees, called emergents, poke out above the rest, 50 metres or more up. Below them, the canopy forms a thick roof of leaves, where most animals live. Monkeys, birds, frogs and insects may spend their whole lives there and never touch the ground. Under the canopy is the dim understorey, and at the bottom, the dark, damp forest floor.

Each layer has its own light, temperature and food. And each tree is covered in smaller homes: mosses, ferns and orchids grow on branches, and tiny pools of water form in the leaves of some plants, where frogs lay eggs. More kinds of homes mean more kinds of life.

Partners and specialists

When food is available all year, animals can specialise. Some eat only one kind of fruit or leaf. Many plants depend on one particular animal to carry their pollen or seeds. The fig wasp is a famous example: each kind of fig has its own kind of tiny wasp, and neither can survive without the other.

The cacao tree, which gives us chocolate, comes from the Amazon region and is pollinated mostly by tiny flies called midges. Partnerships like these make ecosystems rich, but also fragile. Lose one partner and the other may vanish too.

Poor soil, rich forest

Strangely, most rainforest soil is poor. Heavy rain washes nutrients away. When leaves and branches fall, fungi, termites and bacteria break them down within weeks, and roots quickly take the nutrients back up. Almost all the goodness is held in the living forest, not in the ground.

That is why rainforest cleared for farming often stops producing good crops after only a few years.

Rainforests of India and the USA

India has rainforests in the Western Ghats, along the west coast, and in the Northeast. The Western Ghats are older than the Himalaya and are home to thousands of plant species found nowhere else, along with lion-tailed macaques and purple frogs. UNESCO made them a World Heritage Site in 2012.

In the 1970s and 1980s, scientists, writers and local people in Kerala campaigned to stop a dam that would have flooded Silent Valley, a patch of untouched forest. The dam was dropped, and Silent Valley became a national park in 1984.

The United States has rainforests too. El Yunque in Puerto Rico is tropical. Olympic National Park in Washington State has a temperate rainforest, cool rather than hot, where moss hangs from giant trees.

Forests under pressure

Rainforests are being cut down for farms, cattle ranches, mining and timber. When they go, species are lost, and carbon stored in trees escapes into the air, adding to climate change. Protecting them is one of the biggest conservation tasks in the world.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Rainforest soil must be extremely rich, since so much grows there.

Truth: Most tropical rainforest soil is thin and poor. Nutrients are held in the living plants, and when leaves fall, fungi and insects recycle them so fast that little builds up in the ground.
