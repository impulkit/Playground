Why is Venus the hottest planet?

Venus is almost twice as far from the Sun as Mercury, yet it is hot enough to melt lead, day and night.

The short answer

Venus is the hottest planet because of its air. It is wrapped in an extremely thick atmosphere made mostly of carbon dioxide. Sunlight gets in and warms the ground. The warm ground gives off heat, but the thick air won't let it escape back to space.

This is called the greenhouse effect, and on Venus it has run out of control. The surface stays at about 465 °C everywhere, at the poles, on the night side and in the middle of the day. That's hotter than a pizza oven, and hot enough to melt lead.

Surprising but true

Venus spins so slowly, and backwards, that one turn takes longer than its whole year. The Sun rises in the west there.

Earth's strange twin

Venus is almost exactly the same size as Earth and made of similar rock. From far away, they could pass for twins. Up close, they couldn't be more different.

The air at Venus's surface presses down about 90 times harder than on Earth. Standing there would feel like being nearly a kilometre deep in the ocean. Clouds of sulphuric acid cover the whole planet, so thick that you could never see the Sun clearly from the ground. Those bright clouds reflect so much sunlight that Venus is the brightest object in our night sky after the Moon.

A greenhouse gone wild

Scientists think Venus may once have been cooler, perhaps even with shallow oceans, though that is still debated. Being closer to the Sun, it got warmer. Water evaporated, and water vapour traps heat too, so it warmed even more. Eventually the oceans boiled away completely.

Carbon dioxide that on Earth is locked up in rocks and seawater ended up in Venus's air. The result is a planet that can't cool down. Earth has a greenhouse effect too, and a gentle one keeps us warm enough to live. Burning fuels adds carbon dioxide to our air, which is why scientists study Venus to understand climate change.

Why is Earth getting warmer?In 1856, an American scientist showed that one gas in the air traps heat. That gas is now at its highest level for hundreds of thousands of years.

The landers that melted

Landing on Venus is one of the hardest jobs in space exploration. The Soviet Union kept trying. In 1970, Venera 7 became the first spacecraft to land on another planet and send back data, though only faintly, for about 20 minutes.

In 1982, Venera 13 landed and sent back the first colour pictures of the surface: flat, orange-brown rock under a yellowish sky. It survived for about two hours before the heat and pressure destroyed it. No lander has lasted longer.

Backwards and slow

Venus spins the opposite way to most planets, and very slowly. One full turn takes 243 Earth days, while its trip around the Sun takes 225. So one spin takes longer than one trip around the Sun. On Venus, the Sun rises in the west and sets in the east.

Nobody knows for sure why it spins backwards. One idea is that a giant collision long ago knocked it over. Another is that the Sun's pull on its thick air slowly changed its spin.

Going back to Venus

After years of attention on Mars, Venus is getting visitors again. In 2024, India's government approved the Venus Orbiter Mission, which ISRO plans to launch towards the end of the 2020s to study its surface and air. NASA and the European Space Agency are also planning Venus missions.

In Hindi, Venus is Shukra, which gives Friday its name, Shukravaar. Because it shines so brightly near sunrise and sunset, Venus is often called the morning star or the evening star, even though it is a planet. You'd need a very tough thermometer to measure it up close, though.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Mercury is the hottest planet because it's closest to the Sun.

Truth: Venus is hotter. Distance matters less than air. Venus's thick carbon dioxide atmosphere traps heat everywhere, so even its night side is hotter than Mercury's sunniest spot.

Try this

Put a thermometer in each of two identical glass jars and stand them side by side in the sun. Cover one jar tightly with cling film. Check both every ten minutes for half an hour. The covered jar traps heat and warms faster, a small version of what Venus's thick air does.
