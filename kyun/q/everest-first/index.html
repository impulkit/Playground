Who first climbed Mount Everest?

On 29 May 1953, a beekeeper from New Zealand and a Sherpa climber from Darjeeling stood on top of the world for about 15 minutes.

The short answer

Edmund Hillary from New Zealand and Tenzing Norgay, a Sherpa climber who lived in Darjeeling, India, were the first people known to stand on the summit of Mount Everest. They reached it at about 11:30 in the morning on 29 May 1953.

They were part of a British expedition led by John Hunt, with hundreds of porters and many climbers who carried supplies higher and higher up the mountain. Hillary and Tenzing spent about 15 minutes on top, took photographs and left small offerings in the snow. Then they climbed down. The news reached London on the morning of Queen Elizabeth II's coronation.

Surprising but true

Hillary and Tenzing agreed to say they reached the summit together. Only in 1955 did Tenzing reveal in his autobiography that Hillary had stepped onto the top first.

Finding the highest peak

In the 1800s, the Great Trigonometrical Survey of India measured the land with chains, telescopes and lots of maths. From the plains, surveyors measured angles to distant peaks of the Himalaya, mountains still being pushed up as India presses into Asia.

In 1852, calculations showed that a mountain called Peak XV was the highest on Earth. The mathematician Radhanath Sikdar is often credited with the discovery. In 1865 the peak was named after George Everest, a former head of the survey, though he had objected. Local people already had their own names: Chomolungma in Tibetan and, later, Sagarmatha in Nepali.

Earlier attempts

British expeditions tried from Tibet in the 1920s and 1930s. In 1924, George Mallory and Andrew Irvine were last seen climbing high on the summit ridge before cloud hid them. They never came back. Mallory's body was found in 1999, but his camera was not. Nobody knows if they reached the top.

The 1953 climb

The 1953 team came from Nepal, which had recently opened to outsiders. They set up a chain of camps up the mountain. Climbers used bottled oxygen, heavy metal tanks they carried on their backs.

Above 8,000 m is the "death zone", where the air holds so little oxygen that the body slowly weakens. Your lungs work harder and harder but cannot take in enough. A first summit team, Tom Bourdillon and Charles Evans, got within about 100 m of the top on 26 May before turning back.

Three days later, Hillary and Tenzing set off from a tiny tent at about 8,500 m. Near the top, they faced a steep rock step about 12 m high, now called the Hillary Step. Hillary wriggled up a crack beside it, and Tenzing followed. Soon after, there was nowhere higher to go.

Two heroes

Tenzing had been on six earlier Everest expeditions. He left sweets and biscuits in the snow as an offering, in the Buddhist tradition. Hillary left a small cross that Hunt had given him. Tenzing unfurled flags of the United Nations, Britain, Nepal and India.

Both men became world-famous. Tenzing later led field training at the new Himalayan Mountaineering Institute in Darjeeling, founded in 1954. Hillary spent decades raising money to build schools and hospitals for Sherpa communities in Nepal.

After the first

In 1975 Junko Tabei of Japan became the first woman on top. In 1984 Bachendri Pal became the first Indian woman to reach the summit. Thousands of people have now climbed Everest, and in busy seasons there are queues near the top.

The mountain is still growing by a few millimetres each year as India's plate pushes into Asia. In 2020, China and Nepal announced a new joint height of 8,848.86 m, measured using satellites and a team that carried equipment to the summit.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The top of Everest is the farthest point on Earth from the planet's centre.

Truth: Because Earth bulges at the equator, the summit of Mount Chimborazo in Ecuador is farther from Earth's centre, even though Everest is highest above sea level.

Try this

Climb a flight of stairs quickly and count your breaths in the next minute. Then rest and count again. At the top of Everest, each breath holds about a third of the oxygen it does at sea level, so climbers breathe hard even when standing still.
