How do headphones work?

The first headphones the US Navy bought were made around 1910 by a man in Utah working at his kitchen table.

The short answer

Inside each earpiece of your headphones is a tiny loudspeaker, called a driver. It has a magnet, a coil of thin wire and a light, springy sheet called a diaphragm. Your phone sends an electrical signal that changes very fast, matching the shape of the music.

That signal flows through the coil, turning it into a little electromagnet. As the current flips back and forth, the coil is pushed and pulled by the fixed magnet, and it shakes the diaphragm. The diaphragm squeezes and stretches the air in front of it, making sound waves. Because your ear is only a centimetre or two away, a tiny movement is enough.

Surprising but true

Headphones and microphones are nearly the same machine running in opposite directions. Speak into some earbuds and they can pick up your voice.

Shaking the air

Sound is just air being pushed in waves. Low notes push slowly, perhaps 50 times a second for a deep drum. High notes push thousands of times a second. Humans can hear from about 20 to 20,000 vibrations a second, though older people lose the highest ones.

The diaphragm in an earbud has to wiggle to match every one of those vibrations at once. It moves less than the width of a hair for quiet music. Bigger over-ear headphones have larger drivers, which helps with the deep bass notes, a bit like a big drum compared with a small one.

Born on a kitchen table

Early telephone operators used heavy single earpieces. Around 1910, Nathaniel Baldwin, an engineer in Utah, built a lighter, more sensitive pair at home and sent it to the US Navy. Navy radio operators needed to hear faint signals clearly, and the Navy ordered more.

For decades, headphones were tools for pilots, soldiers and radio workers. In 1958 an American company, Koss, sold stereo headphones made for listening to music. Then in 1979, Sony's Walkman cassette player let people carry music in their pockets. Suddenly headphones were everywhere: on buses in Tokyo, on Mumbai locals and on school buses in Ohio.

A microphone in reverse

A microphone works in almost exactly the opposite way. Sound waves shake a diaphragm, which moves a coil near a magnet, and that creates a small electrical signal. Engineers sometimes joke that a headphone is just a microphone that has learned to talk.

The same idea grows bigger in loudspeakers at concerts, which shake the air hard enough to feel in your chest.

Wireless and noise-cancelling

Most headphones sold today are wireless. A tiny radio, using Bluetooth, receives music from your phone as digital code. A chip turns the code back into an electrical wave, and a small battery powers it all.

Noise-cancelling headphones go one step further. Little microphones listen to the noise around you, such as the rumble of a plane. A chip then creates the exact opposite wave and adds it to your music. When the two waves meet, they cancel out, a clever trick explained in noise cancelling.

Sound through your bones

Some headphones don't sit in your ear at all. Bone-conduction headphones rest on your cheekbones and shake your skull very slightly. The vibrations travel through bone straight to your inner ear. Runners like them because they can still hear traffic.

People have known about this for a long time. Stories say that Beethoven, as he went deaf, held a rod between his teeth and pressed it against his piano to feel the notes.

Listening safely

The hair cells in your inner ear turn vibrations into nerve signals. Very loud sound can bend and damage them, and they do not grow back. In 2019 the World Health Organization warned that over a billion young people risked hearing loss from unsafe listening. Turning the volume down a notch, and taking breaks, protects ears you will need for the rest of your life.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If the music doesn't hurt, it can't damage your hearing.

Truth: Hearing damage often happens without any pain. Loud sound for long periods slowly harms tiny hair cells in the inner ear, and they don't grow back. Many doctors suggest keeping volume at or below about 60 percent, for no more than about 60 minutes at a time.

Try this

Put a pair of earbuds playing music (at low volume) into a paper cup or a glass and listen from a little way off. The cup collects and directs the sound, making it louder. Try different containers and see which one works best as a speaker.
