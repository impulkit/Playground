Why don't penguins' feet freeze?

Emperor penguins stand on Antarctic ice at −40°C for months.

The short answer

A penguin's feet are kept just above freezing, at around 1 or 2°C. Not warm, just warm enough not to freeze. The trick is in the plumbing. The blood vessels carrying warm blood down into the feet run right alongside the ones bringing cold blood back up.

As the two streams pass each other, the warm blood heats the cold blood, so the heat goes back into the body instead of leaking out through the feet. Scientists call this a counter-current system. Your own blood does a weaker version of the same trick in your hands and feet.

Penguins can also squeeze shut the vessels to their feet when it gets very cold, letting through only enough blood to keep the toes alive.

Surprising but true

Emperor penguin dads balance the egg on their feet for about two months in the Antarctic winter, without eating anything.

A coat with two layers

Emperor penguins have a thick layer of fat, a few centimetres deep, under their skin. On top of that is a coat of stiff, tightly packed feathers, with fluffy down underneath that traps still air.

The coat works so well that the outer surface of the feathers can actually be colder than the air around it. Very little body heat escapes. Snow can settle on a penguin's back without melting.

Penguins spend a lot of time preening, which means combing and oiling their feathers with their beaks. The oil comes from a gland near the tail and keeps the feathers waterproof, so icy water never reaches the skin.

The great huddle

In the Antarctic winter, it can be −40°C with fierce winds. The sun barely rises for weeks. Thousands of emperor penguins squeeze together in a huddle, packed tighter than commuters in a Mumbai local train at rush hour.

The huddle moves slowly, like a giant, shuffling blob. Birds on the windy edge work their way in, and birds in the middle drift out, so each one takes turns in the warm centre. Inside a tight huddle the temperature can climb above 20°C. Scientists have found that huddling saves each penguin a large share of its energy through the long winter.

Dad's long wait

In May or June, at the start of winter, the mother lays one egg and passes it carefully to the father. If it touches the ice for long, it freezes. Then she walks back to the sea to feed, sometimes many kilometres away.

The father balances the egg on his feet, under a warm fold of feathered skin called a brood pouch, for about two months. He eats nothing at all. By the time the chick hatches, he may have gone around four months without food and lost almost half his weight. If the mother is late, he can even feed the chick a milky liquid made in his throat.

Built for the sea

Penguins can't fly in air, but they "fly" underwater with stiff wing-flippers. Emperors can dive deeper than 500 m and stay under for over 20 minutes, chasing fish, squid and krill. Their bones are solid, not hollow like those of flying birds, which helps them sink.

They need sea ice too. Emperors raise their chicks on ice joined to the coast, and in 2022 the ice broke up early at several colonies, before the chicks had grown waterproof feathers. As the world warms, scientists worry about their future, and in 2022 the USA listed them as a threatened species. India's Antarctic research stations, Maitri and Bharati, are among many that study the changing ice.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Penguins live at the North Pole as well as the South Pole.

Truth: Almost all wild penguins live in the southern half of the world, and none live in the Arctic. Polar bears live in the Arctic, so a penguin and a polar bear would never meet outside a zoo.

Try this

Fill two cups with cold water from the fridge. Wrap one tightly in a thick woolly sock and leave the other bare. After half an hour, dip a finger in each. The wrapped cup stays colder, because the sock traps a layer of still air, just as a penguin's feathers keep the cold out and the warmth in.
