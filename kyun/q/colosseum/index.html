What happened in the Colosseum?

Under the Colosseum floor was a maze of tunnels and lifts that could raise a lion straight up into the arena, as if by magic.

The short answer

The Colosseum was a giant stadium in the centre of Rome where crowds came to watch shows put on by the emperor. The most famous were gladiator fights, in which trained fighters battled each other with swords, spears, nets and shields. There were also hunts of wild animals brought from across the empire, and public executions of criminals.

The shows were free. Emperors used them to win popularity, to show off the empire's reach and to keep the people of Rome entertained. A day at the games could run from morning hunts to afternoon gladiator bouts.

Surprising but true

The famous "thumbs down" signal for killing a gladiator probably comes from an 1872 painting, not from ancient Rome. Nobody knows for sure which way Roman thumbs pointed.

Building a giant

The Colosseum's real name was the Flavian Amphitheatre, after the family of emperors who built it. Emperor Vespasian began it around 70 CE, on land where the unpopular emperor Nero had built a private lake. His son Titus opened it in 80 CE with 100 days of games.

It was built with stone, brick and Roman concrete, on four storeys of arches. About 50,000 people could fit inside. That is fewer than at a big cricket ground today: the Narendra Modi Stadium in Ahmedabad holds more than twice as many. But no stadium of its time came close.

Getting 50,000 people in and out

The Colosseum had 80 entrances at ground level, and most were numbered. Spectators carried tokens telling them which entrance, section and row to use. Passages called vomitoria let the crowd pour out quickly, so a full stadium could empty fast.

Seats were arranged by rank. Senators sat closest to the arena, ordinary men higher up, and women and the poor at the very top. Over the seats, sailors handled a huge canvas awning on ropes to block the sun.

Gladiators and hunts

Most gladiators were enslaved people, prisoners of war or condemned criminals. A few were free men who signed up for money and fame. They trained in schools next to the Colosseum and fought in set styles: one with a net and trident, another with a sword and big shield. Popular gladiators were celebrities, painted on walls and scratched in graffiti.

Not every fight ended in death. Gladiators were expensive to train, and many bouts ended when one fighter gave up. The crowd and the emperor then decided whether the loser would be spared.

Animal hunts brought lions, leopards, bears, ostriches and elephants from Africa and Asia. Thousands of animals died in the games over the years. Today most people find these shows cruel, and a few Romans, such as the philosopher Seneca, wrote that the bloodshed disgusted them.

The world beneath the floor

Under the wooden arena floor, covered with sand, lay the hypogeum: two storeys of tunnels, cages and machinery, added by Emperor Domitian. Workers turned capstans, upright winding drums, to haul cages up on ropes and pulleys. Trapdoors opened, and animals or scenery appeared in the middle of the arena. The word "arena" comes from the Latin for sand, which soaked up blood and gave fighters grip.

After the games

Gladiator fights ended in the early 400s CE, and animal hunts stopped about a century later. Over the next thousand years, the Colosseum was used for housing, workshops and even as a fortress. Earthquakes, including a strong one in 1349, brought down part of the outer wall. People carried away the fallen stone for new buildings.

The holes you see all over its walls once held iron clamps, which were dug out and melted down. Today the Colosseum is one of the most visited monuments on Earth, a symbol of Rome, and a reminder of how a city entertained itself 2,000 years ago.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In the Colosseum, thumbs down meant "kill the gladiator" and thumbs up meant "let him live".

Truth: Roman writers mention a "turned thumb" but do not say which way it turned. The thumbs-down idea became famous from a painting by Jean-Léon Gérôme in 1872. Some historians think a hidden thumb, pressed into the fist, may have meant mercy.
