How were India's temple sculptures carved?

At Ellora, sculptors carved a whole temple out of a single hill, starting at the top and cutting downwards, with no way to stick back a piece they got wrong.

The short answer

India's great temple sculptures were carved by hand. Teams of sculptors used iron chisels and hammers to cut away stone, bit by bit, until gods, dancers, elephants and flowers appeared.

They did not work alone or make it up as they went. A master builder, the sthapati, planned the temple. Old texts called the Shilpa Shastras set out rules for how each god should stand, what it should hold and how its body should be measured.

Some sculptures were carved separately and set into the walls. Others were cut straight out of the living rock, still joined to a cliff or boulder. The result is thousands of temples covered with figures, from Konark in the east to the Hoysala temples of Karnataka.

Surprising but true

In Mamallapuram, near Chennai, families of stone carvers still work with chisels in the same town where Pallava sculptors carved giant rock reliefs about 1,300 years ago.

Measuring with the face

How do many carvers make figures that match? The texts gave them a measuring system. One common unit was the length of the figure's own face, from hairline to chin, called a tala.

A standing god might be nine or ten face-lengths tall. Arms, eyes and fingers each had a set size. This let a group of carvers work on different parts of a temple and still keep everything in proportion, the way a recipe lets cooks in different kitchens make the same dish. It also gave the figures their balance and symmetry.

Carving into the cliff

At Mamallapuram, south of Chennai, sculptors of the Pallava kings worked in the 7th century. Their most famous work, often called Arjuna's Penance or the Descent of the Ganga, covers two huge boulders about 29 m long. More than a hundred figures crowd onto it: gods, people, a cat standing on its hind legs and life-size elephants.

A crack runs down the middle. The carvers made it into a river, with snake beings swimming up it.

At Ellora in Maharashtra, in the 8th century, the Kailasa temple was cut from a single hill of basalt. Workers dug trenches around a block of rock, then carved the temple inside it from the top down. There was no room for big mistakes, since nothing could be glued back on. How they planned the Kailasa temple so precisely is something archaeologists still argue about.

Lace in stone

The Hoysala kings of Karnataka built temples at Belur and Halebidu in the 12th and 13th centuries. Their sculptors used a soapstone-like rock, a kind of schist. It is soft when freshly cut, so carvers could make bead necklaces, curling hair and bangles that stand away from the body.

Some Hoysala sculptors signed their work. Names such as Mallitamma appear on carvings, so for once we know who made them.

The tools and the people

Carvers started by splitting stone with wedges, then roughed out the shape with heavy chisels. Finer chisels came next, then files and polishing with sand or other stones. A single panel could take months, and a large temple could take several generations to finish.

Stone was not the only material. The bronze gods carried out in temple processions were made a different way, by casting. In Swamimalai, in Tamil Nadu, artists still model each figure in wax, cover it in clay and pour in molten metal, an ancient method you can learn about in bronze casting.

The craft passed down in families from parent to child. It hasn't died out. Mamallapuram still has a government college of sculpture, and its streets are lined with workshops where you can hear the tap of hammers. Temples being built today in India and abroad, including in the USA, often hire these carvers, who still check the old proportions before they start.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ancient temple sculptures were made by pouring stone into moulds.

Truth: The stone figures were cut by hand from solid rock with iron chisels and hammers. You can still see chisel marks on unfinished carvings at sites like Mamallapuram, where some panels were left half done.

Try this

Carve a small figure from a bar of soap using a blunt lolly stick or an old spoon handle. First draw your design on all sides, then scrape away a little at a time. You will quickly learn the sculptor's rule that you can always take more away, but never put it back.
