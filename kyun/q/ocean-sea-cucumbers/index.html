What are sea cucumbers?

Sea cucumbers breathe through their bottoms, and when attacked, some shoot out their own insides and simply grow new ones.

The short answer

Sea cucumbers are soft, sausage-shaped animals that live on the seabed all over the world. Despite the name, they aren't plants. They are echinoderms, the same animal family as starfish and sea urchins. Like their cousins, they have tiny tube feet and a body built in five sections, though you have to look closely to see it.

There are more than 1,200 known kinds. Some are the size of your finger, while others grow to about 3 metres long. They crawl slowly across the sea floor, swallowing sand and mud and digesting the tiny bits of food inside. They live everywhere from shallow rock pools to the bottom of the deepest ocean trenches.

Surprising but true

A small fish called the pearlfish lives inside sea cucumbers, slipping in and out through the opening they use to breathe and poo.

Vacuum cleaners of the seabed

A sea cucumber eats a bit like an earthworm. It shovels sand into its mouth with feathery tentacles, digests the bacteria, algae and dead scraps, and poos out cleaner sand. Over a year, that adds up. On one small reef in Australia's Great Barrier Reef, scientists estimated that sea cucumbers pass about 64,000 tonnes of sand through their bodies each year.

This churning keeps the seabed healthy. Their poo returns nutrients to the water, feeding algae and corals. It's also slightly alkaline, the opposite of acidic, which may help nearby coral reefs cope with the sea becoming more acidic.

Breathing through the bottom

Your lungs fill with air through your mouth and nose. Many sea cucumbers do something rather different. They pump seawater in through the anus into branching tubes called respiratory trees, take out the oxygen, then squirt the water back out.

That opening makes a handy front door for lodgers. Small, eel-like pearlfish live inside some sea cucumbers, sliding in tail first and coming out at night to feed. Tiny crabs and shrimps sometimes move in too.

Strange ways to stay safe

Sea cucumbers can't run, so they have unusual defences. When threatened, some species squirt out sticky white threads that tangle up crabs and fish. Others go further. They tear open their body wall and push out some of their guts. The attacker is distracted by the meal, and the sea cucumber crawls away. Over the next few weeks, it grows new organs.

Sea cucumbers can also change how stiff their skin is, in seconds. Special proteins let them go firm to resist an attack, or floppy enough to squeeze into a narrow crack in the rocks. Scientists are studying this trick for medical materials.

Dancers of the deep

In the deep ocean, sea cucumbers are everywhere. On some parts of the dark abyssal plains, kilometres down, they make up most of the weight of all the animals living on the sea floor. Cameras lowered into deep trenches, more than 8 kilometres down, often find sea cucumbers crawling across the mud.

Some can even swim. A pinkish, see-through species nicknamed the "headless chicken monster" flaps its webbed skirt and rises off the seabed to drift to new feeding spots. Many deep-sea kinds glow with blue light when touched.

Wanted for dinner

In parts of East and Southeast Asia, dried sea cucumber, called trepang or bêche-de-mer, is a prized and expensive delicacy. Centuries ago, sailors from Makassar in Indonesia sailed every year to northern Australia to collect sea cucumbers, trading with Aboriginal people there.

Heavy fishing has emptied many reefs. India banned catching all sea cucumbers in 2001, though smugglers still try to ship them abroad from the coasts of Tamil Nadu. Some even sell for more than fine meat, which is why they appear on lists of the world's most expensive foods.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Sea cucumbers are plants, like real cucumbers.

Truth: Sea cucumbers are animals. They eat, move, breathe and have babies. They're only named after cucumbers because of their long, lumpy shape.

Try this

Watch how a sea cucumber stiffens. Fill a small plastic bag with cornflour and water mixed thick, like toothpaste, and seal it. Squeeze it fast and it feels firm. Press slowly and it goes soft and runny. Sea cucumbers can change their skin from firm to floppy in a similar way, though they use special proteins, not cornflour.
