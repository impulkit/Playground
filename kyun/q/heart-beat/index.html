How fast do different hearts beat?

Your heart beats about 1 lakh times a day, without you ever asking it to.

The short answer

Your heart is a muscle about the size of your fist, pumping blood all around your body.

Small animals usually have faster hearts; big animals have slower ones. A mouse's heart can race at around 500 beats a minute, while an elephant's plods along at about 30. Yours, resting, is somewhere in between, and a child's heart usually beats faster than an adult's.

Put two fingers on your wrist and count for 15 seconds, then multiply by 4. That's your heart rate!

Surprising but true

In a lifetime, a human heart beats about 3 billion times.

Two pumps side by side

Your heart is really two pumps joined together. The right side sends tired blood, low in oxygen, to your lungs to pick up fresh oxygen. The left side takes that bright red blood and pushes it out to the rest of your body.

Each side has two rooms, or chambers: a smaller one on top that collects blood and a stronger one below that pumps it out. Flaps called valves snap shut after each push to stop blood flowing backwards. The "lub-dub" sound of a heartbeat is the noise of those valves closing.

At rest, an adult's heart moves about 5 litres of blood every minute, roughly the whole body's supply.

Its own spark

Your heart doesn't wait for orders from your brain. A tiny patch of special cells in its upper right chamber fires an electrical signal about once a second. The signal spreads across the heart and makes it squeeze. Doctors call this patch the heart's natural pacemaker.

Your brain can still speed it up or slow it down. When you run, feel scared or get excited, your heart beats faster to deliver more oxygen to your muscles.

The biggest and the fastest

The blue whale has the largest heart of any animal. One preserved by a museum in Canada weighed about 180 kg, about as much as two grown men. In 2019, scientists stuck a sensor on a wild blue whale and found its heart slowed to just 2 beats a minute on deep dives, saving oxygen. At the surface it sped up again to over 30.

At the other end, a flying hummingbird's heart can beat more than 1,000 times a minute, faster than you can tap a finger.

Small animals lose heat quickly and burn energy fast, so their hearts must work harder. Big animals are the opposite.

A doctor who was too shy

Before 1816, doctors listened to a patient's heart by pressing an ear to their chest. That year, a French doctor named René Laennec felt this was too awkward with a young woman patient. He rolled up a sheet of paper into a tube, put one end on her chest and listened at the other. The heartbeat came through clearly.

He went on to build a wooden tube and called it a stethoscope. Doctors in every hospital from Delhi to Denver still carry its descendant around their necks.

Three billion beats

Your heart beats about 1 lakh times a day. Over a long life, that adds up to about 3 billion beats. It's hard to picture a number that big, as you can see in big numbers. Through all of them, your heart never stops to rest. Between beats, it relaxes for a fraction of a second, and that tiny pause is all the rest it gets.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Your heart is on the left side of your chest.

Truth: It sits almost in the middle, behind your breastbone, with its bottom tip angled to the left. That tip gives the strongest thump, which is why we feel the beat more on the left.

Try this

Make a simple stethoscope. Roll a sheet of stiff paper into a tube about 3 cm wide and tape it. Place one end gently on a friend's chest, slightly left of centre, and press your ear to the other end in a quiet room. Listen for the "lub-dub" of their heart.
