What is the cloud?

Your photos in "the cloud" are not floating in the sky. They are sitting in a giant, humming, chilly building, probably hundreds of kilometres away.

The short answer

"The cloud" means using computers that belong to someone else, over the internet. When you save a photo to the cloud, it is copied across the internet to a powerful computer, called a server, in a building called a data centre. When you watch a film online, a server sends it to you in small pieces.

You never see these computers, so they feel like they are nowhere. That is where the name comes from. For decades, engineers drawing networks on whiteboards sketched the internet as a fluffy cloud, meaning "out there somewhere, we don't need to show the details".

Surprising but true

Most of the internet's traffic between continents does not go through satellites. It travels through cables lying on the ocean floor, many of them about as thick as a garden hose.

Inside a data centre

A big data centre can be the size of several football fields. Inside are long rows of metal racks, each packed with servers stacked like thin pizza boxes. Cables run overhead and under the floor. Lights blink, fans roar, and it is often quite cold.

All those computers get hot, so a lot of effort goes into cooling: giant air conditioners, chilled water, and sometimes cool outside air. Security is tight too. Visitors pass guards, locked doors and sometimes fingerprint scanners. Data centres also keep huge banks of batteries and diesel generators, so that a power cut in the town doesn't switch off the servers even for a second.

The world's largest cluster of data centres is in northern Virginia, near Washington, D.C. In India, the biggest hubs are around Mumbai and Chennai, close to where undersea cables come ashore.

Copies everywhere

The cloud's trick is keeping several copies. When you upload a photo, the company usually stores it on more than one machine, often in different buildings or even different cities. If a hard drive fails, or a flood shuts a building, your photo is still safe somewhere else.

Companies also spread their servers around the planet so data doesn't have to travel too far. Even at the speed of light, a message from Mumbai to New York and back takes a noticeable fraction of a second through the cables. For video calls and online games, that delay matters. A video you stream in Kolkata may come from a server in India, while your cousin in Chicago gets the same video from one in the United States.

Renting a computer

Big companies don't only use the cloud to store photos. Many rent computing power by the hour. A small startup in Bengaluru can rent a thousand servers for a day to test a new app, then hand them back, without buying a single machine.

Amazon Web Services began offering this kind of rental in 2006. Microsoft, Google and others followed. Today, many apps you use, from food delivery to online classes, run on rented cloud computers. When a cloud company has a big outage, thousands of websites can stop working at the same moment.

The cost in electricity

Data centres need a lot of electricity, for the servers and for cooling them. The International Energy Agency estimated that data centres used about 1.5 percent of the world's electricity in 2024, and that this is rising fast as artificial intelligence grows.

Some companies build data centres in cold places like Finland to save on cooling, or next to wind and solar farms. Others reuse the waste heat to warm nearby homes. Every photo you keep forever sits on a spinning or humming machine that someone has to power and cool.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The cloud is wireless and lives somewhere up in the sky.

Truth: The cloud is made of real computers in buildings on the ground. Your phone may use Wi-Fi or mobile signal for the first few metres or kilometres, but most of the journey is through cables, including cables under the sea.

Try this

Play "data centre". One person is the phone and stands at one end of a room. Two or three others are servers at the other end, each holding a copy of the same drawing. The phone asks for the drawing, and the nearest server walks it over. Now "switch off" one server. The drawing still arrives, because another server had a copy.
