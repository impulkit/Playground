What are India's mountain toy trains?

One little train in Darjeeling climbs a mountain by running in a loop over itself and zigzagging backwards up the slope.

The short answer

India's "toy trains" are small mountain railways built in British times to reach hill stations. The three most famous climb to Darjeeling in West Bengal, Ooty in Tamil Nadu and Shimla in Himachal Pradesh. Together they are a UNESCO World Heritage Site called the Mountain Railways of India.

Their tracks are narrow, their carriages are small and they crawl along, often at little more than jogging speed. That is the whole point. Big trains can't make tight turns or steep climbs. These little ones twist, loop and zigzag their way up thousands of metres, through forests, tea gardens and clouds.

Surprising but true

The Kalka–Shimla line passes through 102 tunnels in use on its 96 km climb, more than one tunnel for every kilometre.

Darjeeling: the loop and the zigzag

The Darjeeling Himalayan Railway opened in 1881. Its rails are just 610 mm apart, less than the width of a school desk. It climbs from the plains near Siliguri to about 2,258 m at Ghum, one of the highest stations in India, before dropping slightly to Darjeeling.

To gain height, engineers used two tricks. At loops such as the Batasia Loop, the train spirals around and passes over its own track. At zigzags, it drives forward up one slope, stops, then reverses up the next. Little blue steam engines first built in the 1880s and 1890s still puff along parts of the route.

In the 1969 film Aradhana, Rajesh Khanna sang Mere Sapno Ki Rani from a jeep racing beside this very train.

Nilgiri: the train with teeth

The Nilgiri Mountain Railway reached Ooty in 1908. On its steepest stretch, between Kallar and Coonoor, the slope is about 1 in 12. That means climbing 1 m for every 12 m forward, steeper than many roads.

Normal wheels would just spin on such a slope. So this section has a third rail with teeth, laid between the two running rails. A cogwheel under the engine locks into it, like gears meshing in a bicycle. The 46 km trip uphill takes around five hours, which makes it one of India's slowest trains.

Kalka–Shimla: a line of tunnels

The Kalka–Shimla Railway opened in 1903 to carry officials to Shimla, the summer capital. In 96 km it passes through 102 tunnels still in use and crosses hundreds of bridges, some built like stacked Roman arches. The longest tunnel, at Barog, is about 1.1 km long.

A local legend says the engineer who first dug the Barog tunnel, Colonel Barog, got his calculations wrong, and the two ends didn't meet. A villager named Bhalku is said to have helped guide the later builders. Historians can't confirm every detail, but the story is told in Himachal to this day.

Why they were built

The British wanted to reach their hill stations quickly, and to carry goods down from the hills. The Darjeeling train hauled tea, timber and supplies. Before it opened, the trip up could take days by cart and pony. These lines grew out of the larger Indian railway network spreading across the plains.

The USA has climbing trains too. The Mount Washington Cog Railway in New Hampshire, opened in 1868, was the first of its kind in the world. Its engines also grip a toothed rail.

Riding them today

The toy trains still run, though landslides and monsoon damage sometimes close parts of the lines for months. Tourists book months ahead, especially for steam rides. Some trips take longer than driving, but nobody rides a toy train to save time. On the Darjeeling line the track runs right through town markets, so shopkeepers step back from their stalls as the engine squeezes past.

They earned UNESCO status in stages: Darjeeling in 1999, Nilgiri in 2005 and Kalka–Shimla in 2008. What decides whether a place joins that list is explained in UNESCO World Heritage Sites.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Toy trains are called that because they are made for children or are not real railways.

Truth: They are full working railways that carry passengers, mail and goods. They are called toy trains because their narrow tracks, small carriages and little engines look like models next to normal trains.

Try this

Lay a long piece of string up a steep pile of books in a straight line, then lay another in zigzags or loops up the same pile. Measure both strings. The zigzag is longer but much less steep, which is exactly how mountain railways climb.
