What can a supercomputer do?

The fastest supercomputers do more sums in one second than every person on Earth could do in four years, working non-stop.

The short answer

A supercomputer is a machine built to do enormous amounts of maths very quickly. It is used for problems too big for any normal computer: forecasting the monsoon, designing aircraft, testing new medicines on screen and simulating how galaxies collide.

Speed is measured in FLOPS, or floating-point operations per second, which roughly means calculations with decimal numbers. The fastest machines now manage more than one exaflop: a billion billion calculations every second. In 2025, the fastest on the official world ranking, the TOP500 list, was El Capitan at the Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory in California.

Surprising but true

India built its own supercomputer, PARAM 8000, in 1991, after the United States had refused to sell India one.

Many hands make light work

A supercomputer isn't one magic chip. It is thousands of computers, called nodes, packed into rows of cabinets and connected by super-fast cables. Each node may have powerful graphics chips, the same kind used in gaming and AI.

A big problem is chopped into millions of small pieces. Each node works on its own piece and swaps results with its neighbours. It's like a whole school colouring a giant map, each child doing one square, rather than one person colouring all of it. The hard part is making the nodes talk quickly enough that nobody is left waiting.

How big is an exaflop?

An exaflop is 1 followed by 18 zeros. Suppose all 8 billion people on Earth each did one sum a second. Dividing a billion billion by 8 billion gives 125 million seconds. So it would take humanity about four years, without sleep, to match what the fastest machine does in one second.

All that work uses a lot of power. The biggest systems draw around 20 to 30 megawatts, as much as tens of thousands of homes. Much of that turns into heat, so many are cooled by water pumped through pipes beside the chips.

Forecasting the rain

Weather forecasting is one of the biggest jobs. The atmosphere is split into a 3D grid of boxes. For each box, the computer works out temperature, wind, pressure and moisture, then calculates how they will change a few minutes later, and repeats this again and again.

Smaller boxes mean better forecasts but far more sums. India's monsoon is famously hard to predict, so the government built powerful weather machines. One called Pratyush, installed at the Indian Institute of Tropical Meteorology in Pune in 2018, was among the fastest in India. Better forecasts help farmers decide when to sow and give warning before cyclones.

India's own machine

In the 1980s India wanted a supercomputer for weather work, but the United States, worried about military uses, refused to sell the most powerful kind. So in 1988 the government set up the Centre for Development of Advanced Computing, C-DAC, in Pune. Led by the computer scientist Vijay Bhatkar, its team built PARAM 8000 in 1991 by linking many smaller processors together, the same idea the world's fastest machines use today.

The PARAM family has grown ever since. Under the National Supercomputing Mission, dozens of PARAM systems now work in universities and labs across India.

Racing for the top

Every June and November the TOP500 list ranks the world's fastest machines. The United States, China, Japan and countries in Europe compete for the top spots. In 2022 Frontier, at Oak Ridge in Tennessee, became the first machine on the list to pass one exaflop. Japan's Fugaku held the top place before it.

Engineers are now building machines for artificial intelligence, which need huge numbers of simpler sums. Scientists also hope quantum computers might one day take on special problems that even an exaflop machine can't handle in a lifetime.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A supercomputer is one giant, super-powerful computer chip.

Truth: Modern supercomputers are thousands of ordinary-looking computers, called nodes, linked by very fast cables and working on pieces of the same problem at once. The speed comes from teamwork.

Try this

Time yourself doing 20 simple additions, such as 37 + 48, on paper. Work out how many you can do in a second. Then divide 1,000,000,000,000,000,000 (one exaflop) by your answer to find how many of you it would take to match a supercomputer for one second.
