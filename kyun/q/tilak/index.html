Who was Bal Gangadhar Tilak?

"Swaraj is my birthright, and I shall have it": Tilak's words, first spoken in Marathi, became one of the best-known slogans of India's freedom struggle.

The short answer

Bal Gangadhar Tilak was a teacher, journalist and political leader from Maharashtra. He was one of the first leaders to demand full self-rule for India, which he called swaraj, and to take that demand to ordinary people rather than only educated elites. People called him Lokmanya, "the one accepted by the people".

He used newspapers, festivals and fiery speeches to stir up feeling against British rule. The British jailed him more than once. He was also a scholar who wrote about ancient Indian texts. Tilak is admired as a fearless leader. He is also criticised by some historians for opposing certain social reforms of his time.

Surprising but true

While locked in a prison in Mandalay, Burma, for six years, Tilak wrote a whole book on the Bhagavad Gita, in pencil.

Teacher and editor

Tilak was born in 1856 in Ratnagiri, on the Konkan coast, and studied mathematics in Pune. With friends, he started schools and the Deccan Education Society, which founded Fergusson College in 1885. They believed Indians should run their own schools and teach patriotism alongside science.

In 1881 he helped launch two weekly newspapers: Kesari in Marathi and Mahratta in English. Kesari means lion. Its sharp articles attacked British policies, and it reached farmers and shopkeepers who couldn't read English. That reach made Tilak far more powerful than a leader writing only for the educated few.

Festivals as meeting places

British rules made large political meetings risky. Tilak found another way to gather people. From 1893, he encouraged public celebrations of Ganesh Chaturthi, which had mostly been a family festival at home. Neighbourhoods set up big Ganesh idols, held processions and organised talks and songs, some of them about the nation.

He also promoted a festival honouring the Maratha king Shivaji. These events built pride and drew huge crowds. Some historians note that festivals linked to one community could make others feel left out, and the question is still debated.

Swaraj is my birthright

Inside the Indian National Congress, Tilak led the "extremists", who wanted bold action. The "moderates", led by people like Gopal Krishna Gokhale, preferred petitions and slow reform. Tilak, Lala Lajpat Rai and Bipin Chandra Pal became known together as Lal-Bal-Pal.

When the British split Bengal in 1905, Tilak backed the protests that followed the partition of Bengal. He urged people to boycott British goods and buy Indian ones. His slogan, "Swaraj is my birthright, and I shall have it", spread across the country and became one of the movement's most repeated lines.

Six years in Mandalay

In 1908, Tilak was tried for sedition, meaning stirring up rebellion, because of articles in Kesari. He was sent to prison in Mandalay, in Burma, for six years. Far from home, often unwell, he read widely and wrote Gita Rahasya, a long study of the Bhagavad Gita. He argued that the Gita teaches people to act and do their duty, rather than to withdraw from the world.

He returned in 1914 and soon helped start a Home Rule League to push for self-government. Annie Besant set up a similar league at about the same time, and the two campaigns sent speakers into towns and villages across India. In 1916, Tilak also helped bring about the Lucknow Pact, an agreement between the Congress and the Muslim League to press the British together.

A complicated legacy

Tilak was a social conservative in some ways. In 1891, he opposed a British law raising the age at which girls could be married. He argued that Indians, not British rulers, should reform Indian customs. Critics then and now say this slowed down changes that would have protected girls.

Tilak died in Bombay on 1 August 1920. That same day, Gandhi launched the Non-Cooperation Movement, and the leadership of the struggle passed to him. Huge crowds followed Tilak's funeral procession. Gandhi later called him "the maker of modern India".

Myth or fact?

Myth: Tilak and Gandhi led the freedom movement together.

Truth: Tilak was the leading figure before Gandhi. He died on 1 August 1920, the very day Gandhi launched the Non-Cooperation Movement. Their methods also differed, and Tilak was more willing to accept forceful protest.
