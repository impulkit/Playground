What can shipwrecks teach us?

A shipwreck is a moment frozen in time, with dinner plates, tools and cargo left exactly where they were on the day it sank.

The short answer

A shipwreck is like a time capsule. When a ship sinks, everything on board goes down together: the cargo, the crew's tools, their clothes, their food, even the dice they played with. On land, old things get moved, reused or thrown away. On the sea bed, they often stay right where they fell.

By studying wrecks, archaeologists learn how ships were built, what people traded, what they ate and how they lived. Some wrecks have rewritten history books. Others answer smaller questions, like what sailors kept in their pockets 500 years ago.

Surprising but true

The Swedish warship Vasa sank on its very first voyage in 1628, less than 1.5 km from where it set sail. It was raised 333 years later, almost complete.

A floating museum from 1628

In August 1628, crowds in Stockholm watched the new Swedish warship Vasa set sail. It was huge, beautifully carved and loaded with cannons. A gust of wind tipped it over, water poured in through the gun ports, and it sank in minutes. It had travelled less than 1.5 km.

In 1961, after years of preparation, engineers lifted it from the harbour. The ship was so well preserved that visitors can now walk around it in a museum built just for it. Studying the Vasa showed exactly why it sank. It was too tall and narrow for its weight, with too little heavy ballast low in the hull to keep it upright.

Trade routes on the sea floor

Around 3,300 years ago, a ship sank off Uluburun, on the south coast of Turkey. When divers explored it in the 1980s, they found about 10 tonnes of copper, a tonne of tin, blue glass, ebony wood, ivory, gold jewellery and jars of food.

Those goods came from many lands, from Egypt to Cyprus to the Baltic, which supplied amber. The wreck proved how connected the ancient world was, long before the Romans.

Another wreck changed what we know about science. Off the Greek island of Antikythera, sponge divers in 1900 found a Roman-era wreck. Among its statues was a lump of corroded bronze gears, now called the Antikythera mechanism. It was an ancient calculator for tracking the Sun, Moon and planets.

Wrecks that tell personal stories

The English warship Mary Rose sank in 1545 during a battle with a French fleet, as King Henry VIII watched from the shore. When it was raised in 1982, archaeologists found thousands of objects, including longbows, medical tools, combs and shoes. They also found the skeleton of a small dog, nicknamed Hatch, that had probably been kept on board to catch rats. Scientists have even studied the crew's bones and teeth to learn where the sailors grew up.

The Titanic, found in 1985 by a team led by Robert Ballard, tells a more recent story. In 2022, searchers found Ernest Shackleton's ship Endurance, about 3,000 m below the ice-filled Weddell Sea near Antarctica. It had been crushed by ice in 1915 during an expedition to cross the continent. Its name is still clear on the stern.

Who do wrecks belong to?

Wrecks need protecting. Treasure hunters have broken up some sites to sell coins and objects, destroying the clues archaeologists need. Many wrecks are also graves, where sailors lost their lives.

In 2001, UNESCO agreed an international convention to protect underwater heritage, encouraging countries to study wrecks carefully and leave them in place where possible. India has also explored the sea for its history, with divers investigating underwater ruins off Dwarka in Gujarat and Poompuhar in Tamil Nadu.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Most shipwrecks are full of gold and treasure.

Truth: Most ships carried ordinary cargo such as grain, pottery, timber or metal, along with the crew's belongings. To archaeologists, those everyday things are the real treasure, because they show how people lived and traded.

Try this

Bury a few small, ordinary objects, such as a coin, a pencil, an eraser and a leaf, in a tray of damp sand. Leave it for a week, then dig them out carefully with a spoon, like an archaeologist. Notice which have changed and which haven't, and try to explain why.
