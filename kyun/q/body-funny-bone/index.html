Why does hitting your funny bone hurt so much?

Your funny bone isn't a bone at all. It's a nerve about as thick as a drinking straw, lying almost bare under the skin of your elbow.

The short answer

When you whack your "funny bone", you're actually hitting your ulnar nerve. This nerve runs from your neck down to your hand. At the elbow, it passes through a shallow groove on the inner side, with only skin and a thin layer of fat on top. There's no muscle to cushion it.

So a knock squashes the nerve against hard bone. That sends a sudden burst of signals racing to your brain. Your brain reads them as a strange mix of sharp pain, electric buzz and pins and needles, shooting down into your little finger. It isn't funny at all, but it usually wears off within a minute.

Surprising but true

The name may be a pun. The bone above your elbow is called the humerus, which sounds like "humorous".

A cable with no padding

Nerves are like electric cables. Most of them run deep inside your body, wrapped in muscle and fat for protection. The ulnar nerve is unlucky. For a few centimetres at the elbow, it lies just under the skin in a groove called the cubital tunnel.

You can feel the spot yourself. Find the bony bump on the inner side of your elbow, then the point of your elbow. The groove between them is where the nerve runs. Doctors sometimes tap it gently to test whether the nerve is working.

Why your fingers buzz

Your brain is good at many things, but it doesn't always know where a signal started. When the ulnar nerve is hit at the elbow, the brain gets a message from a cable that normally carries feeling from your little finger and part of your ring finger.

So the brain blames those fingers. You feel tingling there, even though nothing touched them. Doctors call this referred sensation. It is the same kind of confusion that makes brain freeze hurt in your forehead when the cold is really in your mouth.

Shocks, sparks and fizz

Why does it feel electric? Nerves really do send signals using electricity. Tiny charged particles move in and out of nerve cells, passing a pulse along the cable like a wave in a stadium crowd. A sharp knock makes many nerve fibres fire all at once.

That flood of jumbled signals feels like an electric shock. It's similar to the fizz you get when a foot has "fallen asleep". In that case, pressure on a nerve stops signals for a while, and when it lifts, the nerve wakes up crackling with pins and needles.

Why do your feet fall asleep?Sit on your foot through a long film and it goes numb, then fizzes like soda. A squashed nerve is sending your brain gibberish.

A joke in the name

Nobody knows exactly where the name "funny bone" came from. It has been used in English since at least the 1800s. One popular explanation is a pun. The long bone in your upper arm is called the humerus, which sounds like "humorous". Hit near the end of the humerus, and you get the "humorous bone".

Others say it's just because the feeling is so odd, or "funny peculiar" rather than "funny ha-ha". Either way, people find the name funnier than the feeling.

When it keeps tingling

A quick bump is harmless. But some people get tingling in their little finger even without bumping anything. That can happen if the nerve is squeezed for a long time, for example by leaning on your elbows or sleeping with arms bent tight. Long phone calls with a bent elbow can do it too.

If tingling or weakness in the hand keeps coming back, it's worth telling a parent or doctor. Usually small changes, like keeping your elbows straighter, sort it out.

Your body has other quick-fire wiring that works without you thinking. Tap just below the kneecap and your leg kicks before your brain even knows, thanks to the reflexes built into your spinal cord.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The funny bone is a special bone in your elbow.

Truth: It's a nerve, not a bone. The ulnar nerve runs through a groove in the elbow with almost nothing covering it, so a bump presses it straight against bone.

Try this

Gently feel the inside of your elbow, between the knobbly bone on the inner side and the point of your elbow. Roll your fingertip softly across the groove. You might feel a cord or a faint tingle in your little finger. That's the ulnar nerve. Be gentle, no tapping.
