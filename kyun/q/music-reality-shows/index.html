How do singing talent shows work?

Before singers meet the TV judges, most have already been picked from thousands of hopefuls by people you never see on screen.

The short answer

A singing talent show is a contest on television. Ordinary people audition, judges pick the best, and over many weeks singers are knocked out until one wins. In the later rounds, viewers usually vote by phone, text message or app to keep their favourites in.

Behind the scenes, there's far more going on. Producers hold early auditions away from the cameras, pick contestants with good voices and interesting stories, and edit hours of footage into a tight show. It's a real singing contest, and also a TV programme built to keep you watching.

Surprising but true

On The Voice, judges sit with their backs to the singer and only turn their chairs if they like the voice, without having seen the person.

From radio contests to Idol

Talent contests are old. American radio shows ran amateur contests in the 1930s and 1940s, and Indian radio and TV had singing competitions for children and adults.

In India, Zee TV's Sa Re Ga Ma began in 1995, hosted for several years by the singer Sonu Nigam. It later became Sa Re Ga Ma Pa and spawned a children's version, Li'l Champs.

In 2001, a British show called Pop Idol added judges with sharp tongues and votes from viewers at home. American Idol followed in 2002, and Kelly Clarkson won the first season. Indian Idol began on Sony TV in 2004, and its first winner was Abhijeet Sawant.

How a season works

First come mass auditions, often in several cities. Thousands of people queue, sometimes overnight, but most are turned away by producers before the famous judges see them. The few who appear on TV have already passed earlier rounds.

Next come theatre or studio rounds, where singers sing with a band, then in groups or duets. Finally the live shows begin. Each week, contestants sing, judges comment, and viewers vote. The singer with the fewest votes is usually sent home.

Votes and fan power

Public voting turns viewers into part of the show. It also means the best singer doesn't always win. A contestant with a strong home region or a moving story can collect huge numbers of votes.

In Indian Idol's third season in 2007, Prashant Tamang from Darjeeling won. People in the hills of North Bengal, Sikkim and Nepal voted for him in huge numbers. Counting votes fairly is a hard problem, much like counting them in a real election.

How does a country of 140 crore people vote?It's the biggest election on Earth. And the rule is: every voter counts.

The judges and the story

Judges are usually famous singers, composers or producers. Their job is partly to coach: they suggest songs, point out weak notes and tell a nervous singer to slow down. Some are kind, some are harsh, and their arguments are part of the fun. Producers know this, and choose judges who will clash.

Contestants' life stories are told in short films between songs. A singer who supports their family, or who travelled from a small village, gets a lot of attention. Critics say this can matter more than the singing itself.

Pressure on young singers

Children's versions are very popular, but some people worry about the pressure. Young contestants practise for hours, face public rejection, and may become famous overnight, then forgotten.

Some show winners went on to long careers. Kelly Clarkson became a major star. Many Indian playback singers, including Shreya Ghoshal, first came to national attention on TV singing contests. Others went back to ordinary life, which is not always a bad thing. Training your voice in front of a crowd teaches skills that last, whether or not you win.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Everything on singing reality shows happens live and unplanned.

Truth: Most episodes are recorded earlier and edited. Producers choose which singers and stories to show, and contestants usually pass several rounds off-camera before meeting the judges. Some finals do have live public voting.

Try this

Hold a "blind audition" at home. One person stands behind a door and sings or reads a line of a song. The listeners keep their eyes shut and write down what they liked about the voice, before they know who it was.
