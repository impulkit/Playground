How did Kathak spin its way through history?

A Kathak dancer can spin dozens of times without stopping, then freeze, perfectly still, on the last beat.

The short answer

Kathak began with storytellers. In northern India, travelling performers called kathakars acted out stories from the epics, especially tales of Krishna, with songs, gestures and steps. Over the centuries, the dance moved from temples and village squares into royal courts.

There it picked up speed, flair and new costumes. Today Kathak is known for dazzling footwork, called tatkar, and long sequences of spins, called chakkars. Unlike Bharatanatyam's deep bent-knee position, Kathak is danced mostly upright, with straight legs, and the dancer's feet beat out rhythms like a drum.

Surprising but true

Kathak dancers use the same trick as ballet dancers to avoid dizziness, fixing their eyes on one spot and whipping their head round last.

Stories in a spin

A Kathak performance mixes pure dance with storytelling. In a storytelling piece, the dancer might show Radha waiting for Krishna, or Krishna teasing the village girls by the river. The dancer uses gestures and the face, often gentler and more natural than in southern styles.

Then the rhythm takes over. The dancer recites strings of rhythm syllables, called bols, like "ta thei thei tat", and then dances exactly what was spoken. The last spin often lands on sam, the first beat of the rhythm cycle, with a sudden stop that makes audiences gasp.

In the royal courts

From the 1500s, Kathak was performed at Mughal courts, as well as Rajput courts in Rajasthan. Court life changed it. Dancers wore long, flared angarkha coats and caps instead of temple costumes. Persian poetry and court manners shaped new pieces.

Different centres grew their own styles, called gharanas. In Lucknow, the court of Nawab Wajid Ali Shah, in the 1800s, prized grace and expression. The Jaipur gharana became known for powerful footwork and spins. A third school grew in Banaras.

Feet that play the drum

A Kathak dancer wears ghungroo, strings of brass bells tied around each ankle. Experienced dancers often wear over a hundred bells on each foot. By changing how the heel and toes strike the floor, dancers can play soft, fast or thunderous patterns.

The dancer often "talks" with a tabla player. The drummer plays a pattern, and the dancer copies it with the feet, or the other way round. These playful exchanges can go faster and faster until the audience laughs and claps along.

Why the spins don't topple them

Spinning makes most people dizzy because fluid in the inner ear keeps sloshing after you stop, as you can read in how ears work. The brain gets mixed messages from the eyes and the ears.

Kathak dancers use spotting. They keep their eyes on a fixed point, then whip the head round quickly to find it again. Years of practice also seem to train the brain to pay less attention to the confusing signals. A strong dancer can finish dozens of spins and still hold a pose without wobbling.

From courts to cinema

In the 1900s, Kathak teachers moved from royal courts to schools and stages. One of the best known was Birju Maharaj of the Lucknow gharana, who taught generations of students and choreographed dances for films.

Hindi cinema loved Kathak. Many classic Bollywood films feature Kathak-style dances with swirling skirts and spins. Today Kathak is taught in cities from Delhi to New York, and some dancers mix it with flamenco, the Spanish dance, which also uses stamping feet and sharp turns.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Kathak was invented in the Mughal court.

Truth: Kathak grew from older traditions of storytellers, called kathakars, who acted out tales from the epics. Mughal and other royal courts later changed its costumes, music and style.

Try this

Stand in an open space with a grown-up nearby. Pick a spot on the wall at eye level. Turn slowly on the spot, keeping your eyes on that point as long as you can, then quickly turn your head to find it again. Try three slow turns, and compare how you feel with three turns without spotting.
