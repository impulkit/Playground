Did Australia lose a war to emus?

In 1932 Australian soldiers with machine guns went into battle against thousands of emus, and the birds mostly won.

The short answer

Yes, more or less, though it wasn't a real war. In late 1932, farmers in Western Australia asked for help because around 20,000 emus were eating and trampling their wheat. The government sent a small team of soldiers with two machine guns.

The emus were faster, sharper and better at scattering than anyone expected. After weeks of chasing, the soldiers had used thousands of bullets and the farms were still full of emus. The army pulled out. Newspapers had a field day, and the strange campaign has been remembered as the "Emu War" ever since.

Surprising but true

The army reportedly fired about 10 bullets for every emu it claimed to have killed.

Soldiers turned farmers

After the First World War, Australia gave many returning soldiers land to farm. Plenty of them ended up in the dry wheat country of Western Australia. Then the Great Depression arrived in 1929, wheat prices crashed, and these farmers were already struggling.

Emus normally move towards the coast after breeding. The newly cleared farmland, with water tanks for livestock and fields of grain, was perfect for them. In 1932 a huge wave of the birds moved onto the farms around Campion. They ate crops and broke fences, which let rabbits in too.

The birds

An emu is Australia's tallest bird and the world's second tallest after the ostrich. It stands up to about 1.9 m, taller than most grown men. It can't fly, but its long legs carry it at about 50 km/h, faster than Usain Bolt at full speed.

Emus are also tough. Their thick, shaggy feathers and strong bodies meant that a hit didn't always stop one. Like all birds, emus are descended from dinosaurs, a connection explained in birds and dinosaurs.

The battle of Campion

The farmers, many of them former soldiers, asked the defence minister for machine guns. In November 1932, Major G. P. W. Meredith of the Royal Australian Artillery arrived with two soldiers, two Lewis guns and about 10,000 rounds of ammunition.

It went badly from the first day. Whenever the soldiers got close, the emus split into small groups and ran. One plan was to wait in ambush by a dam where a thousand emus gathered. The gun jammed after only a few birds had been hit. Someone tried mounting a gun on a truck, but the truck couldn't keep up on the rough ground and the gunner couldn't aim.

Calling off the troops

The operation was stopped after about a week, restarted at the farmers' request, and finally ended in December. Meredith claimed 986 emus killed using about 9,860 rounds, roughly 10 bullets per bird. Thousands more remained. The defence minister was even asked about it in Parliament, and some politicians wondered whether medals should go to the emus. Meredith himself is often quoted as saying, in admiration, that an army with the bullet-carrying toughness of emus could face any force in the world.

What actually worked

In the years after, the government offered farmers a small payment, called a bounty, for every emu they killed. Farmers knew their own land and could deal with birds steadily over months. Bounties removed far more emus than the soldiers ever had. Better fences also helped keep the birds off crops. Long barrier fences, some stretching hundreds of kilometres, were built across Western Australia to keep emus, rabbits and wild dogs away from farmland.

Emus are now protected in most of Australia, and the species is common. It appears on the country's coat of arms beside a kangaroo. Conflicts between farmers and wildlife still happen worldwide, much like the struggles with invasive species, though here it was the farms that were the newcomers.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Australia formally declared war on emus.

Truth: No war was ever declared. It was a pest-control job carried out with army soldiers and weapons. Newspapers called it the Emu War, and the name stuck.
