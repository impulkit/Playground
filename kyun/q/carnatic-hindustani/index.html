What is the difference between Carnatic and Hindustani music?

India has two great classical music traditions. They share the same notes, yet a trained ear can tell them apart in seconds.

The short answer

Carnatic music comes mainly from South India and Hindustani music mainly from North India. Both are built on ragas, families of notes with their own moods and rules, and on talas, repeating cycles of beats. Both use the notes Sa, Re, Ga, Ma, Pa, Dha and Ni.

The difference is in how they are played. Carnatic music is built around fixed compositions and decorates almost every note with slides and shakes. Hindustani music gives more time to slow, open-ended exploring of a raga, often starting with no beat at all. The instruments differ too: you are likely to hear a mridangam drum in the south and a tabla in the north.

Surprising but true

The violin, a European instrument, arrived in South India around 200 years ago and became one of the most important instruments in Carnatic music.

One root, two branches

Both traditions grew from older Indian music, including temple songs and ancient texts. Over several centuries, North and South drifted apart. In the north, royal courts welcomed musicians from Persia and Central Asia, and new forms and instruments mixed with older Indian ones.

The emperor Akbar gathered great musicians at his court in the 1500s, including the singer Tansen, who became a legend. In the south, music was closely tied to temples and to devotional poetry in Telugu, Tamil, Kannada and Sanskrit. Historians still argue about exactly when the two traditions became fully separate.

Songs at the centre in the south

In the 1500s, the poet-composer Purandara Dasa created a set of graded lessons for Carnatic music. Students still start with them today, which is why he is called the father of Carnatic music.

Around 200 years ago, three composers known as the Trinity, Tyagaraja, Muthuswami Dikshitar and Syama Sastri, wrote thousands of songs called kritis. A Carnatic concert is mostly built around such songs. Singers add their own improvised passages, but the composition stays at the heart. Each January, musicians gather at Tyagaraja's resting place in Thiruvaiyaru to sing his songs together.

Why is Akbar called 'the Great'?The emperor who never learned to read built a huge library, and invited Hindus, Muslims, Jains, Zoroastrians and Christians to argue in front of him.

Slow unfolding in the north

A Hindustani performance often begins with an alap, a slow, beatless introduction. The musician explores the raga note by note, as if walking slowly around a building to see it from every side. Only later does the drum join in, and the speed builds towards a fast, thrilling end.

Hindustani music also links many ragas to times of day and seasons, as you can read in ragas and time. Musicians learn within families of style called gharanas, each with its own way of singing or playing.

Different instruments, different sounds

In the south, you will hear the veena, the violin, the flute, the barrel-shaped mridangam drum and the ghatam, a clay pot played as a drum. In the north, you will hear the sitar, the sarod, the bansuri flute, the shehnai and the tabla.

The harmonium, a small keyboard instrument with bellows, came from Europe in the 1800s. It became hugely popular in the north, even though All India Radio banned it from broadcasts for about 30 years, from 1940, saying it couldn't play the in-between notes of Indian music.

Meeting in the middle

Today, the two traditions often share a stage. In a jugalbandi, a Carnatic and a Hindustani musician take turns and answer each other, playing the same raga in their own styles.

Every December, Chennai hosts one of the biggest music festivals in the world, with hundreds of Carnatic concerts across the city. Hindustani festivals, such as the Sawai Gandharva festival in Pune, draw huge crowds too. In the USA, a yearly festival in Cleveland, Ohio, brings Carnatic musicians from India to American audiences.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Hindustani music belongs to Muslims and Carnatic music belongs to Hindus.

Truth: Both traditions have been shaped by musicians of many faiths. Hindustani music has many Hindu musicians and devotional songs as well as famous Muslim ustads, and Carnatic music has been played and loved by people of all backgrounds.

Try this

Sing "Sa Re Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni Sa" slowly, holding each note. Now sing it again, sliding gently from each note into the next, and add a small wobble on Ga. The second way is closer to how Carnatic singers decorate notes.
