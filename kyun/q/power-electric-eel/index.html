How does an electric eel make electricity?

One kind of electric eel can fire a shock of 860 volts, almost four times the voltage of a wall socket in India, and it is not even an eel.

The short answer

An electric eel's body is mostly a living battery. Most of its long tail is packed with thousands of flat cells called electrocytes, stacked in rows like coins. Each cell can make only a small voltage, a fraction of a volt. But when the eel's brain sends a signal, they all fire at the same instant, and their voltages add up.

Small, weak pulses help the eel sense its surroundings in muddy water. Big, strong pulses stun prey and frighten enemies. In 2019, scientists measured one species, Volta's electric eel, firing 860 volts, the strongest shock known from any animal.

Surprising but true

Electric eels must swim up to gulp air every few minutes. They get most of their oxygen by breathing air through their mouths.

Not really an eel

Electric eels live in the slow rivers and swamps of the Amazon and Orinoco in South America. They grow up to about 2.5 m long, as long as a small car, and look like snakes. But they are knifefish, cousins of catfish and carp, not true eels.

They have a strange habit. Their muddy rivers hold little oxygen, so electric eels swim up every few minutes to gulp air. Most of their oxygen comes from air breathed through their mouths, and an eel kept underwater for too long will drown.

Cells that work like batteries

Every animal uses electricity. Your own nerves send messages as tiny electrical pulses, and your heart beats because of them. Electric eels took this further. Their electrocytes are changed muscle cells that no longer move. Instead, each one pumps charged particles across its walls and then releases them all at once.

Each cell adds about 0.15 volts. Line up thousands, one after another, and the total climbs to hundreds of volts. The current flows out from the head end and back into the tail end through the water, through any fish in the way.

Shocking hunting tricks

Scientists have filmed electric eels doing clever things. When a fish hides, an eel can send out a quick double pulse. The jolt makes the hidden fish's muscles twitch, giving away its position. Then the eel blasts it with a full volley and gulps it down.

An eel can also curl around struggling prey, bringing its head and tail close together, which concentrates the shock. In 2021, scientists in Brazil reported Volta's electric eels hunting in a group of about 100, herding small fish into a ball before attacking together. Until then, people thought electric eels always hunted alone.

Leaping attacks

In 1800, the explorer Alexander von Humboldt described electric eels in Venezuela leaping from the water to shock horses driven into a pool. For two centuries, many scientists doubted the story. In 2016, a biologist in the USA showed that eels really do leap out and press their chins against a threat, sending the shock straight into it.

A shock from a big eel can knock a person down, and in rare cases people have drowned after being stunned. How the eel avoids shocking itself is not fully understood, though fatty tissue around its organs may work a little like the rubber coating on a wire. They are best admired from a distance.

Inspiring the first battery

In 1800, the Italian scientist Alessandro Volta built the first battery, a stack of metal discs and wet cardboard. He had been studying electric fish, and he called his invention an "artificial electric organ". Today, the strongest eel species is named after him, and his wobbly stack of discs was the ancestor of every battery in your home.

Electric eels are not the only animals that use electricity. Sharks and rays sense the faint electric fields of hidden prey, and the platypus hunts with its eyes closed, using its bill to feel the tiny electric signals of shrimp.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Electric eels are a type of eel.

Truth: They are knifefish, a group of South American fish more closely related to catfish and carp. Their long, snake-like shape fooled early naturalists, and the name stuck.
