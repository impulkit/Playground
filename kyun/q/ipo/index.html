What is an IPO?

One morning, a company that belonged to a small group of owners opens its doors to everyone. Anyone with a few thousand rupees can buy a tiny piece of it.

The short answer

IPO stands for Initial Public Offering. It is the first time a company sells its shares to the public.

A share is a small piece of a company. Before an IPO, the company is owned by its founders and a few investors. After it, anyone can buy and sell its shares on the stock market.

Surprising but true

The world's first company to sell shares to the public was the Dutch East India Company, in 1602. People queued in Amsterdam to buy a piece of its spice voyages.

Why companies do it

Growing a business costs money: for new factories, shops, research or networks. A company can borrow from a bank, but loans must be paid back with interest. Selling shares brings in money that does not have to be repaid.

In return, the new shareholders own part of the company. If it does well, their shares may become worth more, and they may get a share of the profits, called dividends. Early owners can also sell some shares to get back the money they put in.

How an IPO works

First, the company works with banks to decide how many shares to sell and at what price. In India, it must file a long document with SEBI, the market regulator, explaining its business, profits, debts and risks.

Then, for a few days, people apply to buy. If more people want shares than there are shares, applicants may get fewer than they asked for, sometimes chosen by lottery. Finally, the shares are listed on the stock exchanges, such as the BSE and NSE, and anyone can trade them. Prices then move up and down every second, written as percentages up or down.

An idea 400 years old

In 1602, the Dutch East India Company sold shares to ordinary people in Amsterdam to pay for spice voyages to Asia. It is often called the world's first IPO. Britain's East India Company also raised money from shareholders.

Today big IPOs can raise thousands of crores of rupees. In October 2026, Jio Platforms raised about ₹30,213 crore, India's largest IPO so far, and was valued at over ₹12 lakh crore on its first day. Over many years, owning good companies is one way people try to grow their savings, but it always carries risk. Nobody can know the probability of a share rising for sure.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Buying shares in an IPO is a sure way to make money.

Truth: Share prices can fall as well as rise. Some IPOs open lower than their issue price, and companies can struggle later. That is why SEBI makes companies publish detailed information, so buyers can judge the risk.

Try this

Imagine your class starts a lemonade stall and needs ₹1,000 for lemons and cups. Split the stall into 10 shares of ₹100 and "sell" them to friends. If the stall earns ₹200 profit, how much does each shareholder get? Now you have run a tiny IPO.
