Why is the universe speeding up?

In 1998, astronomers expected to find the universe slowing down. Instead they found it speeding up, pushed by something nobody can see.

The short answer

The universe has been growing since the Big Bang, about 13.8 billion years ago. For most of the 20th century, scientists assumed that gravity, pulling all the galaxies towards each other, would slowly brake that growth.

In 1998, two teams of astronomers checked. They were stunned. The growth wasn't slowing. It was speeding up, and had been for about the last 5 billion years. Something seems to push space apart, and it gets stronger relative to gravity as the universe grows. Scientists call this unknown something dark energy. It's one of the biggest unsolved puzzles in all of science.

Surprising but true

Dark energy seems to make up about 68% of everything in the universe, yet scientists still can't say what it is.

Measuring with exploding stars

How do you measure the speed of the whole universe? The teams used a special kind of exploding star, a type Ia supernova. These blasts all shine with nearly the same peak brightness, so they work like light bulbs of a known wattage. If one looks dim, it must be far away.

A single supernova can briefly outshine a whole galaxy of billions of stars. The teams found dozens of them, billions of light-years away. They were dimmer than expected, so they were farther away than they should have been if the expansion were slowing. In 2011, Saul Perlmutter, Brian Schmidt and Adam Riess shared the Nobel Prize in Physics for the discovery.

The universe's mystery ingredients

Add up everything we can see: stars, planets, gas, people, sandwiches. It comes to only about 5% of the universe. About 27% is dark matter, invisible stuff whose gravity holds galaxies together. And about 68% seems to be dark energy.

So nearly everything in the cosmos is made of things we've never directly detected. It's like discovering that the books you can read are only one shelf in a library the size of a city.

What is dark matter?Everything you have ever seen, every star, planet and person, makes up only about one-sixth of the matter in the universe. Nobody knows what the rest is.

Einstein's "fudge factor"

Back in 1917, Albert Einstein added an extra term to his equations of gravity, called the cosmological constant. He wanted a universe that stayed still. When Edwin Hubble showed in 1929 that galaxies were rushing apart, Einstein dropped the idea.

Now the cosmological constant is back. The simplest explanation for dark energy is that empty space itself has a tiny amount of energy built in. As space stretches, there's more of it, so there's more push. But when physicists try to calculate how much energy empty space should have, their answer is wildly too big, by a factor with more than 100 zeros. Nobody knows why.

Is dark energy changing?

A new survey called DESI, on a telescope in Arizona, has mapped millions of galaxies in 3D. In 2024 and again in 2025, its results hinted that dark energy might be getting weaker over time. If true, that would mean it isn't a fixed property of space after all.

Scientists are careful here. The hints are not yet strong enough to count as a discovery. Upcoming telescopes, including Europe's Euclid and NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, should help.

What happens next?

The fate of the universe depends on dark energy. If it stays steady, galaxies will keep drifting apart until, in many billions of years, astronomers here would see only our own small neighbourhood of galaxies, with dark sky beyond. If it grows stronger, some scientists have suggested it could one day pull apart even atoms. If it weakens, the universe might stop growing, or even shrink back. For now, it remains among the great unsolved problems, alongside puzzles that mathematicians have chased for centuries.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The universe is expanding into empty space outside it, like a bomb blast.

Truth: There is no "outside" that we know of. Space itself is stretching everywhere at once, carrying galaxies apart, rather than galaxies flying through empty space.

Try this

Draw dots on a balloon with a marker, then blow it up slowly. Watch how every dot moves away from every other dot, and the dots far apart separate fastest. That's how galaxies move apart as space stretches. Now picture someone blowing harder and harder as time goes on.
