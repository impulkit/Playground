Who were the Neanderthals?

Neanderthals died out about 40,000 years ago, yet if your family's roots lie outside Africa, a little of their DNA is probably inside you.

The short answer

Neanderthals were a kind of human who lived in Europe, the Middle East and Central Asia from about 400,000 to about 40,000 years ago. They were close relatives of our own species, Homo sapiens, a bit like cousins on the family tree of human evolution.

They were shorter and stockier than most people today, with big noses, wide chests and very strong arms. Their brains were on average as large as ours. They hunted, made tools, used fire and lived through some of the coldest parts of the last Ice Age. When modern humans spread out of Africa, the two groups met, and sometimes had children together.

Surprising but true

Neanderthals made glue by heating birch bark, and used it to fix stone points onto wooden handles about 200,000 years ago.

Bones in a German valley

In 1856, quarry workers digging in a cave in the Neander Valley, near Düsseldorf in Germany, found some heavy bones and a thick skull cap. At first, people thought they belonged to a sick modern man. Gradually scientists realised they were from a different kind of human, and named it after the valley.

Since then, remains of hundreds of Neanderthals have been found, from Spain and Gibraltar to Iraq and Siberia. No Neanderthal fossils have been found in India, though other early humans were living here at the same time.

Built for the cold

A Neanderthal's short, solid body held heat well, a little like how a round snowman melts slower than a thin icicle. They lived in caves and rock shelters, wore animal skins and kept fires burning.

They were skilled hunters of deer, wild horses, bison and sometimes mammoths, usually at close range with heavy spears. They also ate plants, nuts and mushrooms. Their front teeth are often heavily worn, which suggests they used their mouths like a third hand, gripping hides or tools while they worked. On the coast of Portugal, they gathered mussels and crabs. Their stone tools were carefully shaped, and some were glued to wooden handles with tar made by heating birch bark, a tricky process.

Did they think like us?

This is where scientists argue most. Neanderthals cared for injured family members. One man found in Shanidar Cave in Iraq had a withered arm and was partly blind, yet he lived for years, so others must have helped him. Some sites seem to show deliberate burials, though not everyone agrees.

There are also hints of decoration: shells coloured with red pigment, and eagle claws that may have been worn as jewellery. In 2018, researchers dated red markings in Spanish caves to more than 64,000 years ago, before modern humans arrived in Europe. If the dates are right, Neanderthals made cave paintings. Other experts doubt the dates.

Neanderthals inside us

In 2010, a team led by the Swedish scientist Svante Pääbo read most of the Neanderthal genome from ancient bones. Comparing it with modern DNA revealed a surprise. People whose ancestors lived outside Africa carry around 1 to 2 per cent Neanderthal DNA. Some of those genes affect skin, hair and the immune system. Pääbo won a Nobel Prize in 2022 for this work.

Why did they vanish?

By about 40,000 years ago, Neanderthals had disappeared. Nobody is sure why. They may have lost out to modern humans competing for the same food. Wild swings in climate may have made life harder. Their groups were small and scattered, so bad years hit hard. And some may simply have joined modern human groups, which is how their DNA reached us.

Most likely it was a mix of all these. In a sense, Neanderthals never completely vanished. Small pieces of them are still walking around in billions of people today.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Neanderthals were hairy, hunched, stupid brutes.

Truth: That picture comes from a mistaken study of one arthritic skeleton in the early 1900s. Neanderthals walked upright, made fine stone tools and glue, used fire, cared for injured relatives and survived for hundreds of thousands of years in harsh Ice Age climates.

Try this

Stand up straight, then hunch over like old drawings of "cavemen". Which way could you walk further, or throw better? Scientists now know Neanderthals stood just as upright as you. The hunched picture came from one skeleton of an old man with bad arthritis.
