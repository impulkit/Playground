What is a trade union?

In 1918, mill workers in Ahmedabad went on strike for better pay, and Gandhi fasted to support them. Within days, the owners agreed to talk.

The short answer

A trade union is a group of workers who join together to deal with their employer as one. One worker asking for better pay or safer machines can easily be ignored or fired. Hundreds asking together are much harder to ignore.

Unions bargain with employers over pay, working hours, holidays and safety. This is called collective bargaining. If talks fail, union members may go on strike, which means they stop working until a deal is reached. Unions also help members who are treated unfairly, and many campaign for laws that protect all workers, union members or not.

Surprising but true

May Day, celebrated as Workers' Day in India and many other countries, began with a campaign by American workers in 1886 for an eight-hour working day.

Born in the factories

Unions grew up alongside factories. During the Industrial Revolution, workers often laboured 12 to 16 hours a day, six days a week, in dangerous mills. Children worked beside adults. Employers held almost all the power.

In many countries, joining a union was at first treated as a crime. In 1834, six farm workers from the village of Tolpuddle in England were sentenced to be sent to Australia for forming a union. Huge protests followed, and they were later pardoned. Over the following decades, most industrial countries made unions legal.

May Day and the eight-hour day

In the USA, the American Federation of Labor was founded in 1886. That year, on 1 May, hundreds of thousands of American workers went on strike for an eight-hour working day. In Chicago, a protest in Haymarket Square days later ended with a bomb and deaths.

The campaign spread worldwide, and 1 May became Workers' Day in many countries. In America, workers are honoured instead on Labor Day, the first Monday in September.

A disaster in New York pushed the movement further. In 1911, a fire broke out at the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory, where many of the workers were young immigrant women. Some exit doors were locked, and 146 people died. The tragedy led to new fire safety laws, and the garment workers' union grew much stronger.

How did Chicago become a city of skyscrapers?In 1871, a fire burned the middle of Chicago to the ground. Rebuilding it, architects invented a new kind of building, and the skyscraper was born.

Unions in India

India's textile mills were the birthplace of its labour movement. In 1890, N. M. Lokhande formed the Bombay Mill Hands Association. In 1918, workers in Ahmedabad's mills struck for higher wages. Gandhi supported them and went on a fast, one of his first, and the mill owners agreed to arbitration.

Anasuya Sarabhai, the sister of one of the mill owners, sided with the workers. She helped form the Textile Labour Association in 1920. The same year, the All India Trade Union Congress was founded in Bombay, with Lala Lajpat Rai as its first president. The Trade Unions Act of 1926 allowed unions to register legally.

Why people disagree about unions

Supporters say unions give ordinary workers a fair voice and helped build the middle class in many countries. Workers in unions often earn more and have better benefits.

Critics argue that strikes can shut down hospitals, trains or schools and hurt the public. Some say high union wages can make companies less competitive or slow to change. In the USA, about one wage worker in ten belonged to a union in 2024, far fewer than in the 1950s. In India, most workers are in the informal sector, without a regular employer, which makes organising harder. New groups, like unions for delivery riders, are now trying to change that.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Unions only care about pay.

Truth: Unions have also fought for safer workplaces, shorter hours, paid holidays, sick leave and an end to child labour. Many protections workers now take for granted came from union campaigns.

Try this

Hold a pretend negotiation. One group plays a lemonade stall owner, and another plays the workers who squeeze lemons. The workers ask for more pay or shorter shifts; the owner explains the costs. Try to reach a deal both sides can accept. Notice how much stronger the workers feel when they speak together.
