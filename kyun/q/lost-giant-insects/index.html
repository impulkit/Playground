Why were insects once so big?

About 300 million years ago, dragonfly-like insects with wings nearly as wide as a cricket bat is long zoomed over swampy forests.

The short answer

Around 300 million years ago, Earth's air probably held a lot more oxygen than it does now, possibly up to about 30% or more instead of today's 21%. Many scientists think this let insects grow much bigger.

Insects don't have lungs. Air seeps into their bodies through a network of fine tubes. The bigger an insect gets, the longer those tubes must be, and the harder it is to get enough oxygen deep inside. With more oxygen in the air, each breath went further, so giant bodies could still get enough. Some winged insects of the time had wingspans of about 70 cm, about as wide as a pigeon's.

Surprising but true

A millipede relative called Arthropleura grew up to about 2.6 m long, longer than a small car, and was probably the biggest land arthropod that ever lived.

Forests of giant ferns

This time is called the Carboniferous period, meaning "coal-bearing". Huge swampy forests covered much of the land. Trees were often giant relatives of ferns and horsetails, some taller than a 10-storey building.

These plants pumped oxygen into the air. When they died, many fell into swamps and never fully rotted away, because few microbes could yet break down wood. Over millions of years, they were buried and turned into the coal that powers many factories and power stations today. That buried carbon may also explain why oxygen levels rose so high.

Griffinflies the size of hawks

The most famous giants were griffinflies, cousins of today's dragonflies. Meganeura, found in France, and Meganeuropsis, found in Kansas in the USA, had wingspans of about 65 to 70 cm. That is longer than a school ruler and a half, or close to a cricket bat.

They had huge eyes and spiny legs for grabbing prey in mid-air. Sharp jaws then chewed up other insects and maybe small amphibians.

The giant millipede

Crawling below them was Arthropleura, a millipede relative up to about 2.6 m long and about 50 cm wide. It was longer than a small car. Its fossil tracks, some as wide as a tyre, have been found in Scotland and North America. In 2024, scientists studying well-preserved fossils found that it had eyes on stalks, like a crab.

Some scorpions of the time grew to about 70 cm long, and a few mayflies had wings wider than your hand.

Oxygen or no enemies?

The oxygen idea has strong support. In laboratory tests, some insects raised in extra-oxygen air grew bigger over several generations. And when oxygen levels dropped later, giant insects seem to have disappeared.

The last griffinflies vanished around 252 million years ago, in the Great Dying. That disaster, probably caused by enormous volcanic eruptions in Siberia, wiped out most living species on land and in the sea. Insects bounced back, but the giants never returned.

But it may not be the whole story. When the giants lived, there were no birds, no bats and no flying reptiles. Nothing in the air could chase a big, slow insect. Later, as flying hunters appeared, smaller and nimbler insects survived better. Many scientists now think both oxygen and predators mattered.

The biggest insects today

Insects are still pretty big in some places. The Goliath beetle of Africa can weigh about as much as a tennis ball when it is a grub. The Atlas moth of South and Southeast Asia, found in India's northeast and Western Ghats, has wings up to about 25 cm across. Some stick insects in Borneo and China grow longer than 55 cm with their legs stretched.

They are still nowhere near the size of a griffinfly. The age of giant insects ended long ago, but millions of years later, another giant ruled the swamps, as the story of Titanoboa shows.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Giant insects lived alongside dinosaurs like T. rex.

Truth: The biggest insects lived about 300 million years ago, long before dinosaurs appeared. By the time of T. rex, about 66 million years ago, insects were about the size they are today.

Try this

Test how far air can soak in. Fill two straws with water, one short (5 cm) and one long (full length), then dip one end of each into a cup of water with food colour. Watch how much slower the colour spreads along the long straw. Insect breathing tubes have the same problem as they get longer.
