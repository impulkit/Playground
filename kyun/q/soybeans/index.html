Why are soybeans in so many foods?

Most of the world's soybeans are never eaten by people. They feed chickens, pigs and fish, and turn up in our food in disguise.

The short answer

Soybeans are packed with two things the food industry loves: protein and oil. A dry soybean is about 35 to 40% protein, more than most other beans, and about a fifth oil.

Crush a soybean and you get two products. The oil becomes cooking oil and goes into margarine, biscuits and snacks. The leftover meal, rich in protein, is fed to chickens, pigs, cattle and farmed fish. A small part of the crop becomes tofu, soy milk, soy sauce and the soya chunks many Indian families cook in curries.

Soy also hides in labels. Soy lecithin, which helps oil and water mix, is in many chocolates, breads and sauces.

From China to the world

Soybeans were first farmed in China, at least 3,000 years ago and perhaps much earlier. They spread to Korea, Japan and South-East Asia. Cooks there learned to turn the hard beans into soft foods by soaking, grinding and fermenting them. Soy sauce, miso from Japan and tempeh from Indonesia are all made with the help of microbes.

Tofu is made a bit like paneer. Soybeans are soaked and ground with water to make soy milk, then something is added to curdle it, and the curds are pressed into blocks.

Soybeans reached Europe and North America in the 1700s, but for a long time they were grown mainly as animal feed and to improve the soil.

A bean that feeds the soil

Soybeans are a kind of legume, like peas, lentils and chickpeas. Their roots have small bumps called nodules, where bacteria live. These bacteria take nitrogen gas from the air and turn it into a form the plant can use.

That means soybeans need less fertiliser than many crops, and they can leave the soil a little richer for the next crop. Farmers in the American Midwest often grow soybeans one year and maize the next.

The giant growers

Today, Brazil grows the most soybeans, almost 170 million tonnes in the 2024–25 season, according to the USDA. The USA is second. Argentina is another big grower and a leading seller of soybean meal. Together they grow more than half the world's soybeans. China buys huge amounts, mostly to feed its pigs.

Growing so much soy needs land. In Brazil, soy farms have spread into the Cerrado grasslands and, in the past, onto land cleared from the edges of the Amazon rainforest. Big buyers and the Brazilian government have tried to stop that, with mixed success.

Soy in India

Soybeans became a major crop in India only from the 1970s and 1980s. Madhya Pradesh and Maharashtra grow most of India's soybeans, more than four-fifths between them, and take turns at the top. Madhya Pradesh is often called the "Soya State". Rajasthan grows a lot too.

Farmers there sow soybeans with the monsoon rains in June and July and harvest them in autumn. Most of the crop is crushed for oil. The meal is sold as animal feed, in India and abroad, and some is turned into soya chunks. Soya chunks, made from soy flour after the oil is taken out, became popular as a cheap, protein-rich food that soaks up curry like meat.

A car made of beans

The American carmaker Henry Ford was keen on farm crops. He hoped they could replace metal and oil in factories. In 1941, he showed off a car with body panels made from a plastic said to contain soybeans and other plant fibres. The project stopped during the Second World War and never went into production.

Today, soy still turns up outside the kitchen, in some crayons, inks and foam for car seats. Not bad for a small, plain-looking bean.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Most soybeans are eaten as tofu and soy milk by vegetarians.

Truth: Only a small share of soybeans is eaten directly by people. Most are crushed into oil and meal, and the meal is fed to animals. So meat, eggs and farmed fish often depend on soy too.

Try this

Soak a handful of dried soybeans overnight, then put them on a damp cloth in a bowl and keep them moist for a few days. Watch them sprout. Pull one up gently and look at the roots for tiny bumps. Those nodules hold bacteria that help the plant take nitrogen from the air.
