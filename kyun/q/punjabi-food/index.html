What is Punjabi food like?

At the Golden Temple in Amritsar, volunteers cook free meals for tens of thousands of people every day, whoever they are.

The short answer

Punjabi food is hearty farm food from the plains of northwest India and Pakistan. Punjab means "land of five rivers", and its fertile fields grow wheat, mustard, maize and sugarcane. Meals centre on wheat rotis, rich dals, seasonal greens, and plenty of milk, curd, butter and ghee.

The tandoor, a big clay oven, gives Punjabi cooking its smoky breads and grilled meats. After 1947, Punjabi refugees carried this food to Delhi and beyond, opening roadside dhabas and restaurants. Dishes like butter chicken and dal makhani became famous worldwide, though they're more restaurant food than everyday home cooking.

Surprising but true

Butter chicken, now famous worldwide as a Punjabi dish, was created in Delhi restaurants after Partition in 1947, not in Punjabi villages.

Food from the fields

Punjab's land is flat and well watered by rivers and canals. In the 1960s and 1970s, the Green Revolution brought new seeds, fertilisers and irrigation, and Punjab became one of India's biggest producers of wheat and rice.

How did India grow enough food?In the 1960s India depended on shiploads of foreign wheat. Within about a decade it was growing enough to feed itself.

Wheat is the everyday grain. Rotis are cooked on a flat iron tawa, and parathas, stuffed with potato, cauliflower or radish, are eaten with white butter for breakfast.

In winter, when mustard plants grow tall and green, families cook sarson ka saag. The leaves are slow-cooked and mashed, then eaten with makki ki roti, a flatbread made from maize flour, topped with butter and jaggery on the side.

The clay oven

A tandoor is a large clay pot, often sunk into the ground or set in a brick frame, with a fire at the bottom. It gets extremely hot. Bakers slap dough onto its inner wall, where it puffs and chars in a minute or two.

Village communities once shared a common tandoor, where women would bring dough to be baked. Today, tandoori rotis, naans and kulchas are mostly made in dhabas and restaurants. Amritsar is famous for its stuffed kulchas, crisp outside and soft inside.

Milk, curd and lassi

Punjabi families traditionally kept buffaloes and cows, and dairy shows up at almost every meal. Curd is served with food, and lassi, curd whisked with water and sugar or salt, is served in tall steel glasses.

Ghee and white butter, churned at home, add richness to dal and rotis. Hard farm work in the fields once burned off all those calories.

The langar

At gurdwaras, Sikh places of worship, there is a free community kitchen called the langar. The custom was started by the first Sikh Guru, Guru Nanak, in the 1500s and spread by later Gurus. Anyone may eat, whatever their religion or background, sitting side by side on the floor.

The langar at the Golden Temple in Amritsar is one of the biggest free kitchens in the world. Volunteers chop vegetables, roll rotis and wash dishes for tens of thousands of visitors a day, and on busy days around one lakh. The food is simple: dal, roti, a vegetable and kheer.

Punjabi food goes to Delhi

In 1947, the Partition of India split Punjab between India and Pakistan. Millions of people were forced to move. Many Punjabi refugees settled in Delhi and opened small eateries.

One such eatery, Moti Mahal, became famous for tandoori chicken. Butter chicken, pieces of tandoori chicken in a buttery tomato gravy, came from Delhi restaurants in the years after Partition. Two restaurant families have argued in court over who deserves the credit.

From Delhi, Punjabi restaurant food spread across India and to cities like London, Toronto and New York. Many people abroad think of it as simply "Indian food".

Myth or fact?

Myth: People in Punjab eat butter chicken and naan every day.

Truth: Everyday Punjabi home food is mostly dal, seasonal vegetables, roti, curd and saag. Butter chicken and naan are restaurant dishes, and naan needs a tandoor that most homes don't have.

Try this

With an adult, make a simple sweet lassi. Blend a cup of plain curd with half a cup of cold water and a spoonful of sugar until frothy. Try a salty version with a pinch of roasted cumin, and decide which you like better on a hot day.
