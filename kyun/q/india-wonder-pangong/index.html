Why does Pangong Lake change colour?

High in Ladakh, one lake can look deep blue at breakfast, bright green by lunch and steel grey under an afternoon cloud.

The short answer

Pangong Tso is a long, narrow lake high in the Himalayas, shared between Ladakh in India and Tibet in China. It sits about 4,250 metres above sea level, higher than many of the clouds you see from a city.

It seems to change colour because light keeps changing. Clear, deep water soaks up red and yellow light and sends back blue. Shallow water over pale mud looks turquoise or green. When a cloud passes, the lake turns grey. At that height the sunlight is very strong and the air is very clear, so every change looks sharper than it would at a lake in the plains.

Surprising but true

Pangong's water is salty, yet the lake still freezes so hard in winter that people have run a half-marathon across it.

Light does the painting

Sunlight is a mix of every colour. When it enters water, red light is soaked up first, then yellow and green. Blue goes deepest and is scattered back out. That is why deep water looks blue, a cousin of the reason the sky is blue.

Near the shore, the water is shallow. Light bounces off the pale bed and fine clay before the red has all been absorbed, so you see green and turquoise. Wind adds another layer. Ripples reflect the sky, and a cloudy sky makes the surface look silver or grey. As the sun moves, the angle of light changes, and so does the whole lake.

A salty lake in the sky

Pangong is about 134 kilometres long, a little shorter than the road from Mumbai to Nashik. Yet in many places it is only a few kilometres wide. Its water is brackish, a little salty, because streams flow in but no river flows out. Water leaves only by evaporation, and the salt is left behind.

Very few fish live in the salty water on the Indian side. The lake still draws life, though. In summer, bar-headed geese, Brahminy ducks and gulls nest on its shores. Bar-headed geese fly over the Himalayas twice a year, at heights where a person would struggle to breathe.

Frozen solid

Salt usually stops water from freezing easily. But Ladakh's winter nights fall well below −20 °C, cold enough to freeze Pangong's surface solid from about January to March. The ice can be thick enough to walk on.

In February 2023, runners took part in a 21-kilometre race across the frozen lake. It was called the Last Run and was billed as the world's highest frozen-lake half-marathon. The organisers wanted to draw attention to how climate change is affecting the Himalayas.

Getting there leaves you breathless

To reach Pangong from Leh, visitors cross the Chang La pass, more than 5,300 metres high. At that altitude, each breath carries only about half the oxygen it would at sea level. Many travellers get headaches, and guides tell them to rest in Leh for a day or two before going higher.

The lake became famous across India after the 2009 Hindi film 3 Idiots filmed its last scene on the shore. Tourist numbers shot up. Today, camps, cafés and a road busy with jeeps line the shore in summer.

A lake made by mountains

Pangong lies in a valley left by the slow crash of India into Asia, which pushed up the Himalayas. Long ago, scientists think, the lake drained into a river. Then the land shifted, the outlet was blocked, and the water had nowhere to go.

The border between India and China runs across the lake, and the two countries disagree about exactly where it lies, so parts are closed to visitors. And the strangest part? Not far away, people walk for days along a frozen river gorge on the Chadar trek.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pangong's water changes colour because it contains magic minerals that shift with time.

Truth: The water itself stays much the same. What changes is the light, meaning the sun's angle, clouds overhead, wind ripples and how deep you are looking. Your eyes see a different mix of reflected and scattered colours as these change.

Try this

Fill a tall clear glass and a shallow white bowl with water. Look at both in bright daylight against a white sheet of paper, then add a pinch of soil to the bowl and stir. Notice how depth and floating bits change the colour you see.
