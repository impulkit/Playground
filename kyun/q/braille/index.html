How does Braille let people read with fingers?

A French teenager, blind since the age of five, invented a way to read with six raised dots. He was 15.

The short answer

Braille is a code of raised dots that you read by touch. Each letter sits in a small space called a cell, which has room for six dots in two columns of three. Different patterns of raised dots stand for different letters, numbers and punctuation marks. One dot in the top left is "a". Two dots down the left side are "b".

A trained reader slides the fingertips lightly along a line, feeling each pattern as a whole rather than counting dots. Fingertips are packed with touch sensors in the skin, and experienced readers move along a line surprisingly quickly.

Louis Braille, a blind French student, created the system as a teenager in 1824.

Surprising but true

Braille grew out of "night writing", a code of raised dots designed so that soldiers could read messages in the dark.

An accident and an idea

Louis Braille was born in 1809 in Coupvray, a village east of Paris. At the age of three, playing in his father's leather workshop, he hurt one eye with a sharp tool. Infection spread to both eyes, and by five he was completely blind.

At ten he won a place at the Royal Institute for Blind Youth in Paris. Its books were printed in large raised letters, pressed into heavy paper. They were enormous, expensive and slow to read, because fingers struggle to tell curvy letters like O and Q apart. The school owned only a small number of them.

From army code to alphabet

In 1821, a former French army officer named Charles Barbier visited the school. He had invented "night writing", a code of raised dots that soldiers could read by touch without lighting a lamp, which might give away their position. Barbier's system used cells of up to 12 dots, and its patterns stood for sounds, not letters. It was too big for a fingertip to feel in one go.

Young Louis worked on the idea for three years. By 1824, aged 15, he had shrunk the cell to six dots, small enough to sit under one fingertip, and matched the patterns to letters. He published his system in 1829 and later added patterns for music and maths.

Six dots, sixty-three patterns

Six dots, each either raised or flat, give 64 combinations. Leave out the blank cell, and 63 patterns remain. That's enough for the alphabet, numbers, punctuation and many common short words. It's the same counting trick a computer uses with its 0s and 1s, which is why braille is sometimes compared to binary code.

Braille has been adapted to many languages. Bharati Braille uses shared patterns for similar sounds across Hindi, Tamil, Bengali and other Indian languages, so a reader who knows one can learn the others more easily. Refreshable braille displays, with tiny pins that pop up and down, let blind people read emails and websites. Like sign language for deaf people, braille opened up schools, jobs and libraries.

A slow road to acceptance

Braille's own school did not officially adopt his system until 1854, two years after he died of tuberculosis at the age of 43. Some sighted teachers had preferred raised print, which they could read themselves. The students kept using his dots anyway.

Today braille appears on lift buttons, on medicine boxes in Europe, and on signs at railway stations and airports, and many countries have laws about access for disabled people. In 1952, a hundred years after his death, Louis Braille's remains were moved to the Panthéon in Paris, where France honours its national heroes. Every 4 January, his birthday, World Braille Day remembers the boy who made books fit under a fingertip.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Braille is a language.

Truth: Braille is a code for writing languages, not a language itself. The same six-dot cells are used to write English, French, Hindi, Tamil and many others.

Try this

Draw a grid of two columns and three rows on paper, about the size of a fingertip, then make a bigger copy. Use dried beans or buttons to "raise" dots and build the braille letters a to e from a braille chart. Close your eyes and ask someone to make a letter for you to read by touch.
