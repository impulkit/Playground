How should we behave online?

In 2018, false messages forwarded on phones led to real harm in parts of India. Soon after, WhatsApp limited how many chats you could forward a message to.

The short answer

Behaving well online is mostly the same as behaving well anywhere: be kind, be honest and think before you act. The internet just makes those choices travel faster and further.

A comment typed in a second can be screenshotted, shared and seen by thousands. A rumour forwarded without checking can cause real harm. A joke that seems funny in your head can hurt someone who can't see your smile.

People sometimes call this being a good digital citizen. It means protecting yourself, respecting others and checking things before you share them. Most of all, it means remembering there is a real person on the other side of the screen.

Surprising but true

Typing in ALL CAPITAL LETTERS has been read as shouting since the early days of the internet, long before smartphones existed.

Why online feels different

Face to face, you see someone's face fall when you say something unkind, and you often stop. Online, you don't. Psychologists call this the online disinhibition effect: people say things on screens they would never say in person.

Being anonymous, or thinking you are, makes it worse. So does speed. A good habit is to pause before posting anything when you are angry, and read it again later. Messages also lose your tone of voice. That is partly why emojis became so popular, to show the feeling behind the words.

Check before you share

False stories spread faster than true ones, because they are often more surprising. In 2018, rumours forwarded on phones in parts of India led to attacks on innocent people. WhatsApp began labelling forwarded messages, and in 2019 it limited forwarding to five chats at a time around the world.

Before you share something, ask: who made this, and how do they know? Search for the same story on trusted news sites. If you can't find it, don't pass it on.

How can you tell if something online is true?Professional fact-checkers can judge a strange website in about a minute, and the first thing they do is leave it.

Your digital footprint

Everything you do online leaves traces: posts, photos, comments, searches and likes. Together they make your digital footprint. Some colleges and employers look at applicants' public posts.

Think about what you share about yourself, and about others. Posting a friend's photo or private message without asking can embarrass them. Keep personal details like your address, school and phone number private, and use strong passwords.

Being a good neighbour online

The internet also lets you do a lot of good. Wikipedia, the free online encyclopedia, is written mostly by volunteers who check each other's work. Young people have used online petitions and videos to raise money for causes and to share skills.

Small habits count too. Credit people when you share their drawing or photo. Say thank you when someone helps you in a game or forum. Disagree with ideas without attacking the person. Online spaces feel like the people in them, so each user shapes what everyone else sees.

When things go wrong

Bullying online, called cyberbullying, can follow a child home, onto their phone, at any hour. If someone is cruel to you, don't reply in anger. Take a screenshot, block them and tell a trusted adult.

If you see someone else being bullied, a private kind message to them can mean a lot. In India, serious online crimes can be reported on the national helpline, 1930, or at the government's cybercrime website, with an adult's help.

The rules behind the apps

Most social apps ask users to be at least 13, because of a 1998 American law called COPPA. It stops companies collecting personal data from younger children without a parent's permission. India's 2023 data protection law asks for a parent's consent for anyone under 18. Rules can protect you, but good habits protect you more, every time you pick up a phone.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If you delete a post or message, it's gone forever.

Truth: Someone may already have taken a screenshot, saved it or forwarded it. Some websites keep copies too. It is safest to post only what you would be happy for a teacher or grandparent to see.

Try this

Write the same short message three ways, once sounding angry, once flat and once kind, without using any emojis. Ask a friend to read them and guess the mood of each. Then talk about how easy it is to be misunderstood when nobody can hear your voice or see your face.
