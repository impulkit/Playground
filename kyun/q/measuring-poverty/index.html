How do you measure poverty?

To know whether poverty is shrinking, you first need a way to count it. But how do you decide who counts as poor, in a country of 140 crore people?

The short answer

The most common way is to set a poverty line: the smallest amount of money a person needs each day for food and other basics. Anyone living on less is counted as poor.

To find out how many people are below the line, governments run big surveys that ask lakhs of families what they spend. Other measures check whether people have things like schooling, clean water, a toilet and electricity.

Surprising but true

The World Bank's extreme poverty line is not about real US dollars. It uses "purchasing power" dollars, adjusted so that $3 means what $3 could buy in the USA, converted into local prices.

How it works

Think of the height line at a fair, the one that says "you must be this tall to ride". A poverty line works the same way, except it measures spending, not height. Everyone below the line is counted as poor.

Drawing the line starts with food. Experts work out how much it costs to eat enough, with enough nutrition, to stay healthy. Then they add other needs, such as clothes, rent, fuel and travel. The total, per person per day, is the line.

Next comes counting. You cannot ask all 140 crore Indians what they spend, so surveyors visit a carefully chosen sample of homes across every state, much as a census team visits homes. They ask what the family spent on rice, milk, soap, school fees, bus tickets and much more.

Finally, experts use statistics to work out the share of people below the line. They look at more than the average, because a high average can hide many families who have very little. Lines must also be updated, because inflation makes the same basket cost more each year.

Where it came from

The first person to try this for India was Dadabhai Naoroji. Using prices from 1867-68, he worked out the least a person needed to survive. His answer was ₹16 to ₹35 a year, depending on the region. In his 1901 book "Poverty and Un-British Rule in India", he argued that British rule was draining India's wealth.

After independence, India's Planning Commission set official lines. In 1993, the Lakdawala committee based the line on calories: enough food for 2,400 calories a day in villages and 2,100 in towns. In 2009, the Tendulkar committee set new lines, worth about ₹27 a day in villages and ₹33 in towns at 2011-12 prices. On that line, about 22% of Indians were poor in 2011-12. A later committee led by C. Rangarajan suggested ₹32 and ₹47, which put the share closer to 30%.

The world needed a common line too. In 1990, the World Bank introduced its famous "dollar a day" line. It has been raised several times as prices and data changed. In June 2025 it became $3 a day, based on the middle value of the national poverty lines of 23 of the poorest countries.

India's story

India's spending survey is huge. The 2023-24 round visited 2,61,953 households, about 1.5 lakh in villages and 1.1 lakh in towns and cities. It found that an average person in a village spent ₹4,122 a month, and in a town ₹6,996.

India also has a measure that looks beyond money. NITI Aayog's multidimensional poverty index checks 12 things, including nutrition, years of schooling, cooking fuel, toilets, drinking water, electricity and bank accounts. By this measure, the share of poor Indians fell from 29.17% in 2013-14 to 11.28% in 2022-23. About 24.82 crore people moved out, including 5.94 crore in Uttar Pradesh and 3.77 crore in Bihar.

In October 2026, the World Bank said India's extreme poverty had fallen from 27.1% to 2.6% between 2011-12 and 2023-24. Most of India's poorest families have long lived in villages, so the rural numbers are watched most closely.

The numbers that matter

2.6%. The share of Indians below the $3 line in 2023-24, down from 27.1% in 2011-12. That is fewer than 3 people in every 100.

16.1%. The share below the higher $4.20 line. So about 1 in 6 Indians still lives on very little, even if not in extreme poverty.

817 million. Roughly how many people in the world lived below $3 a day in 2024. That is about 1 person in every 10 on Earth.

₹4,122. What an average person in rural India spent in a month in 2023-24, about ₹137 a day, for food, clothes, travel and everything else.

2,61,953. The number of homes surveyed in 2023-24. Asking that many families lets experts speak for the whole country with good accuracy.

What people argue about

Some economists, such as Surjit Bhalla, say the World Bank's method is the gold standard. It is checked by experts in each country, and the big fall in poverty is real.

Others, such as Santosh Mehrotra, say India should set its own new poverty line, as it did until 2014. What families need changes over time, they argue, and one global line may miss that.

There is also a debate about the survey itself. The 2022-23 and 2023-24 rounds asked questions in a new way, which may capture spending better. Some experts say this explains part of the fall. Others say the fall is too large to be explained by survey changes alone. A third group points out that even as poverty falls, the gap between rich and poor remains wide.

Myths and mix-ups

"$3 a day means ₹250 or so a day in India." No. The line uses purchasing power dollars, not market exchange rates. In India, it works out to far fewer rupees, because things cost less here than in the USA.

"A lower poverty rate means fewer people are struggling." Usually, but not always. A rate is a percentage. If the population grows fast, the rate can fall while the number of poor people stays the same.

"Anyone above the line is comfortable." A family just above the line can fall back below it after one illness, a bad harvest or a lost job. That is why experts track several lines, not just one.

What it means for you

Poverty numbers decide who gets help. Ration cards, free food grains for about 80 crore people, the mid-day meal at school and housing schemes all depend on knowing who needs support and where they live.

They also shape the government's budget. When surveys show a district is short of toilets or clean water, money can be sent there. And if a surveyor ever knocks on your door, your family's honest answers help paint a true picture of the whole country.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Poverty just means not having much money.

Truth: Money matters, but so do clean water, a toilet, electricity, schooling and healthcare. That is why many experts also use multidimensional measures that check what people can actually get and do, not just what they spend.

Try this

With a grown-up, list what your family spends in a day on food, travel and other needs. Divide by the number of people at home. That is your daily spending per person, the same number poverty surveys try to measure for millions of families.
