How do you measure poverty?

To know whether poverty is shrinking, you first need a way to count it. But how do you decide who counts as poor, in a country of 140 crore people?

The short answer

The most common way is to set a poverty line: the smallest amount of money a person needs each day for food and other basics. Anyone living on less is counted as poor.

To find out how many people are below the line, governments run big surveys that ask lakhs of families what they spend. Some measures also check whether people have things like schooling, clean water and electricity.

Surprising but true

The World Bank's extreme poverty line is not about real US dollars. It uses "purchasing power" dollars, adjusted so that $3 means what $3 could buy in the USA, converted into local prices.

Drawing a line

A poverty line usually starts with food: how much it costs to eat enough food, with enough nutrition, to stay healthy. Then it adds other needs, such as clothes, rent and travel.

The World Bank sets a global line so countries can be compared. Its extreme poverty line is $3.00 a day per person, and a higher line of $4.20 a day is used for countries like India. These are not ordinary dollars. They are adjusted for local prices using purchasing power parity, because a rupee goes further in a village than a dollar does in a big American city.

Prices change too. With inflation, the same line must be updated every few years.

Counting carefully

You cannot ask every person in India what they spend, so experts ask a careful sample, just as a census counts people and an opinion poll asks a few thousand voters. In India, the Household Consumption Expenditure Survey asks families what they spent on food, fuel, schooling and more.

From the answers, experts use statistics to work out the share of people below the line. They look at more than the average, because a high average can hide many families who have very little.

More than money

Some measures go beyond spending. Multidimensional poverty asks about health, education and living standards: Does anyone in the family go hungry? Are the children in school? Is there a toilet, clean water, electricity and cooking gas?

These measures show where help is most needed, from cities to villages. In October 2026, the World Bank said India's extreme poverty had fallen from 27.1% to 2.6% between 2011-12 and 2023-24, while noting that better surveys may explain part of the change. Experts still debate exact numbers, but they agree that how you measure shapes what you see.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Poverty just means not having much money.

Truth: Money matters, but so do clean water, a toilet, electricity, schooling and healthcare. That is why many experts also use multidimensional measures that check what people can actually get and do, not just what they spend.

Try this

With a grown-up, list what your family spends in a day on food, travel and other needs. Divide by the number of people at home. That is your daily spending per person, the same number poverty surveys try to measure for millions of families.
