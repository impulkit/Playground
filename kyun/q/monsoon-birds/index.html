Which birds arrive with the monsoon?

For centuries, people in north India have said the call of one crested bird means the rains are on their way.

The short answer

When the monsoon sweeps across India in June and July, it brings some birds with it, and it sends many others into a frenzy of nest-building.

The most famous monsoon messenger is the pied cuckoo, a black-and-white bird with a crest. In much of north and central India, it appears around the time the rains arrive, and its loud, ringing calls are taken as a sign that the monsoon is near. The Indian pitta, a small, very colourful bird, also arrives in many forests to breed.

Many birds that live in India all year, such as baya weavers, storks and sarus cranes, time their breeding for the rainy months, when food is everywhere.

Surprising but true

The pied cuckoo never builds a nest or raises its own chicks. It lays its eggs in the nests of babblers, which bring up the young cuckoos as their own.

The bird of the rains

The pied cuckoo, also called the Jacobin cuckoo, is known in Hindi and many other languages as the chatak or chataka. In Indian poetry and songs, the chatak waits for the first drops of rain and drinks nothing else. It became a symbol of longing and patient waiting.

Biologists see a different story. Some pied cuckoos live in south India all year. Others appear in north India just before or with the monsoon, then leave once it ends. Many scientists think these northern birds fly in from eastern Africa, crossing the Arabian Sea on the monsoon winds, but the details are still being worked out.

Like many cuckoos, it never raises its own family. It lays its eggs in the nests of babblers, noisy brown birds that bring up the cuckoo chicks as if they were their own.

Nine colours in the forest

The Indian pitta is a little smaller than a myna but painted in green, blue, black, white, yellow and bright red. Its Hindi name, navrang, means "nine colours". It spends winter in southern India and Sri Lanka, then moves north to forests in central India and the Himalayan foothills to breed when the rains come.

Pittas hop on the forest floor, flicking leaves aside to find insects and worms. In the monsoon, the damp leaf litter is full of them.

Builders and dancers

The rains turn many resident birds into builders. Baya weavers, small yellow-and-brown birds, weave hanging nests from long strips of grass, often over water. The male builds the nest. Females inspect it, and if one approves, the male finishes it. The finished nest, with a long entrance tube pointing straight down, is one of the most skilful bird nests in India.

In grasslands of central and western India, the male lesser florican, a rare bustard, leaps high above the grass again and again in the monsoon to attract a mate. Asian openbill storks and egrets gather to nest in trees beside flooded fields.

All this activity runs on food. Rain makes insects hatch in huge numbers, and ponds fill with frogs, snails and fish.

After the rains

Some of the most dramatic arrivals come as the monsoon ends. In October and November, huge flocks of Amur falcons stop in Nagaland, in northeast India, to feed on flying termites. They are on one of the longest migrations of any bird of prey, from eastern Asia to southern Africa.

Around 2012, many of the falcons were being trapped for food. Conservation groups worked with village communities in Nagaland, and hunting fell sharply. Today, villages such as Pangti welcome birdwatchers instead, and the falcons' return has become a celebration. Soon after, winter visitors like ducks and cranes begin arriving from Central Asia and Siberia, as India's bird calendar turns once again.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The chatak bird drinks only raindrops and will die of thirst rather than drink water from the ground.

Truth: This is a beautiful idea from Indian poetry, not biology. The pied cuckoo eats caterpillars and other insects, which supply much of its water. It arrives with the rains because that is when its food is plentiful.

Try this

During the rainy season, sit by a window or in a garden for 15 minutes each morning for a week. Note every bird you hear or see, and whether it is carrying grass, twigs or food. You may spot birds building nests or feeding chicks.
