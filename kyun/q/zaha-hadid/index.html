Who was Zaha Hadid?

For years, Zaha Hadid's designs were called impossible to build. Then she became the first woman to win architecture's biggest prize.

The short answer

Zaha Hadid was an Iraqi-British architect, born in Baghdad in 1950. She became famous for buildings full of sweeping curves and sharp angles, which seem to flow and twist instead of standing still. People called her the "Queen of the Curve".

In 2004, she became the first woman to win the Pritzker Prize, the top award in architecture. In 2016, she became the first woman to receive the Royal Gold Medal of the Royal Institute of British Architects on her own. She died suddenly later that year, at 65, but her firm in London still designs buildings across the world.

Surprising but true

Before she studied architecture, Zaha Hadid studied mathematics at university in Beirut.

From Baghdad to London

Hadid grew up in Baghdad, an old city on the Tigris river not far from the ruins of Mesopotamia, where some of the world's first cities were built. Her family valued education, and as a girl she went to schools in Iraq and abroad.

She first studied mathematics at the American University of Beirut in Lebanon. Then, in 1972, she moved to London to study at the Architectural Association, a famous school for bold ideas. There she was inspired by Russian artists of the early 1900s who painted floating shapes and broken lines. She began making paintings of her own buildings, with walls and floors exploding apart like pieces in a storm.

The paper architect

For years after starting her own firm, Hadid won design competitions but saw very little built. Her plans looked thrilling on paper, but builders and clients worried they were too strange, too expensive or simply impossible.

In 1994 she won a competition to design an opera house in Cardiff, Wales, but the project was cancelled. Some critics called her a "paper architect", someone whose buildings existed only as drawings. Her first major completed building did not come until 1993: a small, sharp-angled fire station for the Vitra furniture company in Germany.

Curves made by computer

Hadid's ideas grew easier to build as computers improved. Her team used powerful design software to model complex curved surfaces and work out exactly how to make each part. Some programs had first been made for designing cars and aeroplanes.

Her training in maths helped too. A building with no straight walls needs careful geometry so it can stand up, keep out the rain and be built at a sensible cost. Her firm became known for working closely with engineers to find a way.

Buildings around the world

From the late 1990s, Hadid's buildings appeared on almost every continent. The MAXXI art museum in Rome opened in 2010, with long concrete galleries that cross over each other like ribbons. The Guangzhou Opera House in China opened the same year. The Heydar Aliyev Center in Baku, Azerbaijan, completed in 2012, rises from the ground like a white wave.

For the 2012 Olympics in London, she designed the Aquatics Centre, with a curving roof shaped like a wave of water. Swimmers and divers there won Olympic medals beneath it.

Praise and criticism

Not everyone loved her work. Some critics said her buildings cost too much and paid more attention to looks than to the people using them. She was also questioned about working conditions for labourers building stadiums for the 2022 football World Cup in Qatar, where she had designed one of the venues.

Hadid was known for being fierce and demanding, and she worked in a field where few women reached the top. She once said that being a woman and an Arab made it harder to be taken seriously. Today, many young architects, especially women, name her as an inspiration.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Zaha Hadid's buildings were only drawings that could never really be built.

Truth: Many of her early designs were not built, which earned her the nickname "paper architect". But by the end of her life her firm had finished dozens of buildings around the world, and many more were completed after she died.

Try this

Take a sheet of paper and try to design a building without using any right angles or straight walls. Bend strips of card into curves and tape them to a base. Notice how hard it is to make a curved wall stand up, a problem Hadid's engineers solved with computers.
