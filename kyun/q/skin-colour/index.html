Why do people have different skin colours?

Almost everyone has about the same number of colour-making cells in their skin. What changes is how hard those cells work.

The short answer

Skin gets its colour mainly from melanin, a pigment made by special cells called melanocytes. More melanin, and especially more of the dark brown kind, makes skin darker. Less makes it lighter.

The amount your body makes depends on your genes. Those genes, in turn, were shaped by sunlight. Over tens of thousands of years, people whose ancestors lived where the sun is fierce, near the equator, kept dark skin. People whose ancestors moved to cloudier, cooler places far from the equator often became lighter. Skin colour is mainly a record of how much sunshine your great-great-great-grandparents lived under.

Surprising but true

The genes for skin colour tell you very little about the rest of a person's DNA. Two people with the same skin tone can be less closely related than two people with very different ones.

Inside your skin

At the bottom of the outer layer of your skin sit the melanocytes. They have long arms, like tiny octopuses, that hand packets of melanin to the skin cells around them. Those packets settle over each cell's centre like a sunshade, guarding its DNA from damage.

There are two main kinds of melanin. Eumelanin is brown to black. Pheomelanin is a lighter red to yellow, and people with red hair and freckles have a lot of it. Everyone's skin colour comes from a mix of the two, plus a little help from blood near the surface, which adds pink.

When strong sun hits your skin, melanocytes speed up and make more melanin. That's a tan. It takes a few days to appear, and it fades once you stay indoors. A tan gives only a little extra protection, roughly like a very weak sunscreen, so it is no excuse to bake in the sun.

A balancing act with sunlight

Sunlight brings both harm and help. Its ultraviolet rays can burn skin and break down folate, a B vitamin the body needs for growing babies and healthy cells. But the same rays let skin make vitamin D, which keeps bones strong.

Scientists such as the American anthropologist Nina Jablonski think skin colour evolved as a balance between the two. Our early ancestors in Africa lost most of their body hair and developed dark skin to protect their folate under a strong sun. As some groups spread into Europe and northern Asia, where the sun is weaker, lighter skin helped them make enough vitamin D.

There are interesting exceptions. The Inuit of the Arctic have fairly dark skin for such a sunless place, possibly because their traditional diet of fish and seal is rich in vitamin D.

Many genes, many shades

Skin colour isn't controlled by one switch. Dozens of genes each nudge it a little lighter or darker, which is why children can have a different shade from either parent, and why brothers and sisters can look quite different.

Skin tone is also a poor guide to ancestry. Scientists have found more genetic variety among people in Africa than among people on all other continents put together. Two people with similar skin may share less DNA than two people whose skin looks completely different.

Shades of India

India has an enormous range of skin tones, from very light to very dark. You can see that range within a single city, a single school or even a single family. Like many countries, it also has a long history of treating lighter skin as more beautiful. In 2020, after criticism, one of the country's best-known fairness creams changed its name to drop the word "fair".

Scientists see no shade as better or worse than any other. Each is a smart answer to a different amount of sunshine. Whatever your colour, sunlight still matters, so shade and sunscreen on very sunny days make sense for everyone.

Myth or fact?

Myth: People with dark skin can't get sunburnt, so they never need sun protection.

Truth: Dark skin has more natural protection, but it can still burn in strong sun, especially at high altitude or near water. Everyone's skin can be damaged by too much ultraviolet light.

Try this

Put two identical sheets of coloured paper in a sunny window, and cover half of one with a book. After a week, compare the covered and uncovered parts. Sunlight fades dyes in paper by breaking them down, and in skin it can break down useful chemicals too, which melanin helps to block.
