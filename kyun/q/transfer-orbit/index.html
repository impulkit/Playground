How does a satellite get from its rocket to its final orbit?

A rocket does not drop a satellite exactly where it needs to be. Often it leaves it on a long, stretched loop, and the satellite has to finish the climb by itself.

The short answer

Many satellites are dropped by their rocket on a stretched, egg-shaped loop around Earth, called a transfer orbit. Its lowest point is a few hundred kilometres up, and its highest point can be about 36,000 km away.

The satellite then fires its own small engine each time it reaches the high point. Every burn makes the loop rounder, until the satellite settles into its final, circular orbit.

Surprising but true

The clever path most satellites use to climb higher was worked out in 1925 by a German engineer, Walter Hohmann, more than 30 years before the first satellite was launched.

Why not go straight there?

Lifting anything takes huge amounts of fuel. A rocket that carried a satellite all the way to 36,000 km and then made its orbit round would need to be much bigger. It is cheaper to let the rocket do the hard first part and let the satellite finish the job.

In 1925, a German engineer, Walter Hohmann, worked out the most fuel-saving way to move between two circular orbits. One push stretches the orbit into a long loop. Another push at the far end makes it round again. Space agencies still use his idea.

How do satellites help us every day?Weather forecasts, maps on your phone, card payments and cricket on TV all depend on machines circling far above your head.

Climbing step by step

At its high point, a satellite moves slowly. A forward push from its engine there raises the low point of its loop. After a few burns, spread over days, the loop becomes a high circle. Engineers on the ground track it with radio dishes and send each command.

Each burn uses thrust from the satellite's own fuel, so every drop counts. Fuel left over is saved for small nudges to keep the satellite in place for years. India's Chandrayaan missions used a similar step-by-step method to stretch their orbits all the way to the Moon.

Satellites that stay in step

At about 36,000 km, a satellite takes exactly one day to go round Earth, the same time Earth takes to spin. This is called a geosynchronous orbit. If it sits above the equator, it seems to hang still in the sky, which is useful for TV, weather and communication.

India's NavIC navigation satellites use these high orbits to watch over India and the area around it. Like GPS, they send precise time signals from atomic clocks. ISRO launches them from Sriharikota on its GSLV rockets, which carry them to a transfer orbit before the satellites make the final climb on their own.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A rocket carries a satellite all the way up to its final orbit.

Truth: Many rockets leave satellites on a lower or stretched orbit to save fuel and weight. The satellite then uses its own small engine over days or weeks to reach its final home.

Try this

Tie a small ball to a string and swing it in a circle. Now let out more string while it spins. The ball needs a push to keep up on the bigger circle. Satellites face the same problem and use their engines to give that push.
