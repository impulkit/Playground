What is the Right to Information?

For ₹10 and a written question, any Indian citizen can make a government office explain itself, and the office has 30 days to answer.

The short answer

The Right to Information, or RTI, is a law that lets citizens ask the government questions and get answers on paper. In India, anyone can write to a public office and ask for records: how much a road cost, why a ration card was refused, how many doctors a hospital has. The office must reply within 30 days.

The idea is simple. Governments spend the people's money, so the people should be able to see how. Information that used to sit in locked files can now be asked for by a farmer, a student or a retired teacher, without hiring anyone.

Surprising but true

India's RTI law grew out of village meetings in Rajasthan, where workers read out government spending records in public and found money paid for work that never happened.

Born in a village meeting

India's RTI movement began in the dusty villages of central Rajasthan in the early 1990s. A group of workers and farmers called the Mazdoor Kisan Shakti Sangathan, co-founded by Aruna Roy, suspected that money for local works was being stolen.

They held jan sunwais, or public hearings. Records of roads, wells and wages were read aloud in front of the whole village. People stood up and said, "I never got that money" or "That wall was never built." The officials had little to say. These meetings made a simple demand catch on across India: if it's our money, show us the accounts. A national law followed in 2005.

How a request works

You write a short letter or fill in a form online, addressed to the office's Public Information Officer, and pay a small fee, usually ₹10 for central government offices. You don't need to say why you want the information. The officer must answer within 30 days, or within 48 hours if a person's life or liberty is at risk.

If the reply is late or unhelpful, you can appeal to a senior officer and then to an Information Commission, which can fine officials who delay without reason. There is a Central Information Commission in Delhi for national offices, and each state has its own commission for state offices. Some records are protected, such as matters of national security, cabinet papers before a decision is made, and personal details that would invade someone's privacy.

Older laws elsewhere

Sweden passed one of the first freedom-of-information laws in 1766, as part of a law on press freedom. The United States passed its Freedom of Information Act in 1966, and President Lyndon Johnson signed it on 4 July, Independence Day. Reporters use it to uncover stories, and historians use it to open old government files. Today well over a hundred countries have laws like these.

Such laws work hand in hand with free speech. The right to say what you think matters more when you can check the facts first.

What it has changed

RTI requests have revealed missing ration grain, fake names on wage lists and delays in pensions. Many people use it for small, personal problems, like finding out why a passport application is stuck, and the act of filing often gets things moving.

It has not been easy for everyone who uses it. Some RTI activists who exposed powerful people have been threatened or attacked, and campaigners have pushed for better protection. There are also arguments about whether recent changes to the law have weakened it. The panchayat office, the city council and the ministry in Delhi all have to answer the same kind of letter.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Only lawyers or journalists can file an RTI request.

Truth: Any Indian citizen can file one, on paper or online, without giving a reason for asking. People below the poverty line don't have to pay the fee.

Try this

With an adult, think of one question you'd like a public body to answer, such as how much was spent repairing your nearest park or how many teachers your local government school has. Write it out clearly in one or two sentences, the way an RTI request would be. You don't have to send it; notice how a precise question is easier to answer.
