How do you start birdwatching?

India's most famous bird scientist, Salim Ali, got hooked as a boy when he brought a strange sparrow to a museum and asked what it was.

The short answer

Starting birdwatching is simple. Pick a quiet spot, sit still and look and listen. Early morning is best, because birds are most active then. Note what you see: its size, colour, shape, beak and how it moves. Then use a field guide, a book of bird pictures, or a free app to work out its name.

You don't need special gear to begin. A notebook and pencil are enough. Later, a pair of binoculars will help you see details like eye colour or wing stripes. The most important skill is patience. Birds hide, flit and fly off, and the more time you spend watching, the more you notice.

Surprising but true

Every February, people in more than 100 countries count birds for at least 15 minutes and send in their lists, making one of the biggest science projects in the world.

What to look for

Experienced birdwatchers, often called birders, start with a few simple questions. How big is it, compared to a sparrow, a myna or a crow? What shape is its beak: short and thick for cracking seeds, long and thin for sipping nectar, hooked for tearing meat? What colours and patterns stand out?

Behaviour helps too. Does it hop or walk? Does it climb up tree trunks or hover in the air? Does it fly in a straight line or in swoops? A bird that wags its tail as it walks on a lawn may be a wagtail. One sitting very still on a wire, then diving into water, may be a kingfisher.

Learning by ear

Often you'll hear a bird long before you see it. Learning a few calls is like getting a second pair of eyes. The rising "ku-oo" of the koel, the bright whistle of the magpie robin and the harsh "kaa" of the crow are easy to start with.

Many apps and websites have recordings to compare. Some birders remember calls as little phrases, because many birds sing in patterns that sound like words. In North America, the barred owl seems to ask "Who cooks for you?" Spend a few mornings just listening, and your city will start to sound very different.

The birdman of India

When Salim Ali was about ten, in Bombay, he shot a small bird with a yellow patch on its throat. Curious, he took it to the Bombay Natural History Society. A staff member there identified it as a yellow-throated sparrow and showed him drawers full of stuffed birds.

That visit changed his life. Salim Ali went on to survey birds across India and wrote The Book of Indian Birds, first published in 1941, which is still read today. He helped protect places like Keoladeo National Park in Bharatpur, a wetland famous for winter birds. India now marks 12 November, his birthday, as National Bird Day.

Counting birds for science

Birders do more than enjoy the view. Their counts help scientists track which birds are thriving and which are in trouble. In the United States, the Christmas Bird Count has run since 1900. The Great Backyard Bird Count began in 1998 and now takes place every February around the world.

In India, schools, colleges and families take part in Campus Bird Counts and Great Backyard Bird Counts, and they upload lists to eBird, a free website and app. These millions of lists help researchers write big reports, such as the State of India's Birds. This kind of shared science is a good example of data science in action.

Being a good birder

The birds come first. Never chase birds, touch nests or get too close to chicks. Stay on paths, keep quiet and don't play recorded calls over and over to lure birds out, as this can stress them, especially in nesting season.

When you're new, it's fine to get things wrong. Even expert birders argue over tricky warblers and distant hawks. Join a local bird walk if you can. They are often free, and an experienced birder can teach you more in a morning than a book can in a month.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You need expensive binoculars and a camera to go birdwatching.

Truth: You can start with nothing but your eyes, ears and a notebook. Many common birds are easy to see in parks and gardens. Binoculars help later, and a simple, cheap pair is fine for beginners.

Try this

Pick one spot near home, like a window, balcony or garden bench, and sit there quietly for 15 minutes each morning for a week. List every bird you see or hear, with a tally of how many. With an adult's help, you can submit your list to eBird or a school bird count and add to real science.
