Why do we learn times tables?

The oldest known decimal times table was written on strips of bamboo in China about 2,300 years ago.

The short answer

Times tables are lists of the answers you get when you multiply small numbers together, like 7 × 8 = 56. Learning them so well that the answers pop into your head saves a lot of brain effort later.

When you do a long division, work out a fraction or solve a word problem, you need dozens of small multiplications along the way. If each one takes you ten seconds of counting, it is easy to lose track of the bigger problem. If the answers come instantly, your mind is free to think about what the problem actually means. It is a bit like knowing the alphabet well enough to read without spelling out each word.

Surprising but true

Because 3 × 7 is the same as 7 × 3, a 12 × 12 table has 144 answers but only 78 different facts to learn, and many of those are easy.

The oldest tables

People have written times tables for thousands of years. Students in Babylon, in what is now Iraq, copied multiplication tables onto clay tablets about 4,000 years ago. Their tables worked in sixties.

In ancient China, someone wrote a times table on strips of bamboo about 2,300 years ago. The strips were bought by Tsinghua University in Beijing in 2008, and researchers announced in 2014 that 21 of them formed a decimal times table, working in tens like ours. It could even multiply halves. It is the oldest decimal table known.

Pahade, rhymes and songs

In India, children have long learned pahade, chanting tables aloud in class. "Do ekam do, do dooni chaar", two ones are two, two twos are four. Many older Indians learned tables up to 20 or beyond, and some learned tables for fractions too, like sawa (1¼), dedh (1½) and dhai (2½).

In Japan, children learn a rhyming chant called kuku, "nine nine", for the tables up to 9 × 9. In England, every child in Year 4, aged 8 or 9, takes an online check of tables up to 12 × 12. In the United States, most children learn tables up to 10 or 12, though how they practise varies a lot between schools.

Why memory helps

Your brain has a small "working memory", a kind of mental notepad that can hold only a few things at once. Researchers who study memory think that when simple facts are stored firmly in long-term memory, they no longer crowd that notepad. That leaves space for harder thinking.

Some educators worry that drilling tables can make children anxious or bored, especially in timed tests. Many suggest learning patterns first, then practising until the facts feel automatic. Both sides agree on the goal. They argue about the best route.

Patterns that help

Times tables are full of shortcuts. Order does not matter, so learning 3 × 8 gives you 8 × 3 for free. Every answer in the fives table ends in 5 or 0. Every answer in the tens ends in 0. In the nines, the digits of each answer up to 9 × 10 add up to 9: 18, 27, 36, 45.

To multiply by 4, double and double again. To multiply by 8, double three times. The elevens up to 9 × 11 just repeat the digit: 33, 44, 55. Once you spot these, only a few dozen hard facts are left, like 6 × 7, 7 × 8 and 8 × 9.

Do we still need them?

Calculators can multiply any numbers in a blink, so some people ask why we bother. One reason is speed in daily life, like checking a bill in a shop or working out how many tiles cover a floor. Another is noticing mistakes. If a calculator says 7 × 8 is 65, you will spot it if you know your tables. Children who learn abacus or Vedic maths tricks usually still begin with a firm grip on their tables.

Myth or fact?

Myth: To learn times tables up to 12 × 12, you must memorise 144 separate facts.

Truth: Since 6 × 8 is the same as 8 × 6, you only need half of them. The ones, twos, fives, tens and elevens follow easy patterns. That leaves only a few dozen tricky facts to practise.

Try this

Try the nines trick on your fingers. Hold up all ten fingers. To work out 9 × 4, fold down your fourth finger from the left. There are 3 fingers to its left and 6 to its right, so the answer is 36. Try it for 9 × 7 and 9 × 9, and work out why it works.
