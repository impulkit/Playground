What was Sputnik?

In October 1957, a metal ball the size of a beach ball began beeping from space, and the whole world stopped to listen.

The short answer

Sputnik 1 was the first artificial satellite, the first object made by humans to go into orbit around Earth. The Soviet Union launched it on 4 October 1957. It was a shiny aluminium ball about 58 cm wide, a bit smaller than a beach ball, with four long thin antennas trailing behind.

Sputnik did one simple thing. It sent out radio beeps as it raced around Earth every 96 minutes. That was enough to amaze and alarm the world. It started the space age, and with it a contest between the Soviet Union and the United States known as the space race.

Surprising but true

Most people who thought they saw Sputnik crossing the night sky were really seeing the much bigger rocket stage that had carried it up.

Fellow traveller

"Sputnik" is a Russian word meaning "fellow traveller" or "companion". The satellite weighed about 84 kg, about as much as a grown man. Inside its sealed ball were batteries, two radio transmitters and a fan to keep them cool.

It was launched from a secret site in the desert of Kazakhstan, now called the Baikonur Cosmodrome, on top of an R-7 rocket. The R-7 had been designed to carry nuclear weapons across the world. The engineer in charge, Sergei Korolev, was so secret that the Soviet public didn't know his name until after he died in 1966.

Beep, beep, beep

Sputnik's beeps used radio waves at frequencies that radio hobbyists everywhere could tune into. Within hours, people in many countries were listening. Newspapers printed times when it would pass overhead.

How does radio travel through the air?Radio waves don't need air at all. They are a kind of invisible light that can cross empty space.

The batteries ran out after 21 days, and the beeping stopped. Sputnik kept circling silently. Each orbit, the thin outer air dragged on it a tiny bit. On 4 January 1958, after about 1,400 orbits, it dropped low enough to burn up like a shooting star.

Scientists still learned from it. By tracking how its orbit shrank, they measured how dense the air is high above Earth. By studying how the radio signal changed, they learned about the electrically charged layer of the atmosphere.

Shock in America

In the United States, Sputnik caused something close to panic. This was the Cold War, a long rivalry between the two countries. Americans worried that a country able to put a satellite in orbit could also send a rocket across the world.

The US responded fast. Its first attempt to launch a satellite, in December 1957, exploded on the launch pad. On 31 January 1958, it succeeded with Explorer 1, which discovered belts of radiation circling Earth, now called the Van Allen belts. In October 1958, the US set up a new space agency, NASA. It also spent much more on science and maths education in schools.

A dog, then a person

Just a month after Sputnik 1, on 3 November 1957, Sputnik 2 carried the first animal into orbit, a stray dog from Moscow named Laika. Her spacecraft was never designed to return, and she died within hours, probably because the cabin overheated. Her flight showed that a living creature could survive launch and float in orbit, but it is still remembered with sadness.

Less than four years after Sputnik 1, the Soviet cosmonaut Yuri Gagarin became the first person in space.

From one satellite to thousands

In 1957 there was one working satellite. Today there are more than 10,000. They forecast weather, beam television, guide ships and phones, and photograph every corner of Earth.

India joined in 1975, when the Soviet Union launched India's first satellite, Aryabhata. Five years later, India launched a satellite on its own rocket for the first time. All of them trace their story back to a beeping ball that went round the world in an hour and a half.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The bright dot people watched crossing the sky in 1957 was Sputnik itself.

Truth: Sputnik was small and hard to see with bare eyes. The bright moving light most people saw was the rocket's large core stage, which was also in orbit, about 26 m long and much more reflective.

Try this

With a friend in another room or across the garden, make up a simple code using a torch or a whistle, such as one beep for yes and two for no. Send each other messages. Sputnik's beeps were a little like this, and scientists even learned about the upper air from how its signal changed.
