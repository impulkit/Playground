What did Mexico give the world?

Without Mexico there would be no chocolate bars, no popcorn and, believe it or not, no green chillies in Indian food.

The short answer

Mexico is a large country between the United States and Central America. It is famous for its food, its ancient cities, its bright festivals and its art. But its biggest gift to the world may be the plants first farmed there.

Maize (corn), chillies, tomatoes, beans, squash and avocados were all grown in or near Mexico thousands of years ago. Cocoa, the bean behind chocolate, was prized there too. Today these foods are eaten everywhere, from a bowl of popcorn in Chicago to a plate of chilli-laced pav bhaji in Mumbai.

Surprising but true

Mexico City is built on top of an old lake, and parts of it sink by more than 20 cm a year.

The grass that became corn

Corn did not exist in the wild. About 9,000 years ago, farmers in southern Mexico began choosing seeds from a scraggly grass called teosinte. Its "cob" held only a handful of hard seeds. Over thousands of years of picking the best plants, those farmers turned it into maize with fat, juicy cobs.

Maize became so important that Maya stories describe the first people being made from maize dough. Today it is one of the most grown crops on Earth, and makka roti with sarson ka saag owes its yellow colour to that grass from Mexico.

Cities on a lake

In 1325, according to tradition, the Aztecs founded a city called Tenochtitlan on an island in Lake Texcoco. They built causeways to the shore and floating garden beds called chinampas. When Spanish soldiers arrived in 1519, Tenochtitlan was one of the largest cities in the world.

The Spanish drained the lake and built Mexico City on top. Today more than 2 crore people live in and around it, which makes it one of the largest cities in the Americas, alongside New York and São Paulo. Right beside its central square, the Zócalo, lie the ruins of the Aztec Great Temple, which electricity workers rediscovered by accident while digging in 1978. Because so much water is pumped out of the soft old lakebed, parts of the city sink every year, and some old churches lean.

A festival for remembering

Every year on 1 and 2 November, many Mexican families celebrate the Day of the Dead (Día de los Muertos). They build altars at home with photos of relatives who have died, along with marigold flowers, candles, sugar skulls and the person's favourite foods.

Paper banners with cut-out patterns hang over the streets, and bakeries sell a sweet bread decorated with strips of dough shaped like bones. Children get sugar skulls with their own names iced across the forehead.

It is a happy, colourful time, not a gloomy one. Families visit graves, tell stories and share meals. The marigolds are thought to guide the spirits home with their scent. UNESCO lists the festival as part of the world's intangible cultural heritage.

The crater under Yucatán

About 66 million years ago, an asteroid around 10 km wide slammed into the sea off what is now Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula. It left a crater, called Chicxulub, about 180 km across, now buried under rock and sea.

The impact threw so much dust and gas into the sky that it helped wipe out the large dinosaurs, as told in the story of extinction. Not far away, the ruins of the Maya city of Chichén Itzá draw visitors who climb its stepped pyramid. Few realise they are standing near the scar of the most famous collision in Earth's history.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Mexico is mostly a hot, dry desert.

Truth: Northern Mexico has big deserts, but the country also has rainforests, pine forests, coral reefs and snow-capped volcanoes. Its highest peak rises over 5,600 m and keeps snow all year.
