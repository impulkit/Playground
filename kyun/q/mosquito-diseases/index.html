Why are mosquitoes so dangerous?

No shark, snake or tiger comes close. Through the diseases they carry, mosquitoes cause more human deaths than any other animal.

The short answer

Mosquitoes are dangerous because of what they carry. When a female mosquito bites someone sick with certain diseases, she can pick up germs with the blood. When she bites the next person, she can pass them on.

That's how malaria, dengue, chikungunya, Zika, yellow fever, Japanese encephalitis and other diseases spread. Malaria alone killed about 600,000 people in 2023, according to the World Health Organization, most of them young children in Africa. Mosquitoes themselves are tiny and weak. It's their role as flying needles that makes them so deadly. Mosquito-borne diseases have even changed the course of history, wrecking armies and halting canal-builders.

Surprising but true

Only female mosquitoes bite. They need the protein in blood to make their eggs. Males live on nectar and plant juices.

Why only females bite

Female mosquitoes need protein from blood to make their eggs. Male mosquitoes never bite. Both eat nectar and plant juices for energy.

A female finds you mainly by smell. She can sense the carbon dioxide in your breath from several metres away, then homes in on body heat and skin chemicals. Some people really do get bitten more than others, probably because of the mix of chemicals on their skin.

Mosquitoes lay their eggs in water. The larvae wriggle in puddles, ponds and containers for about a week before turning into flying adults. That's why mosquito numbers shoot up in the monsoon, when every pothole, drain and forgotten bucket fills with rainwater.

The discovery in Secunderabad

For centuries, people blamed malaria on bad air. Its name comes from Italian words meaning exactly that. In 1880, the French army doctor Alphonse Laveran found the real cause in Algeria: a tiny parasite living in the blood.

But how did it spread? Ronald Ross, a British army doctor born in India, worked in hot, cramped labs in Secunderabad, near Hyderabad. On 20 August 1897, he found the parasite growing in the stomach of an Anopheles mosquito that had fed on a malaria patient. He later showed the whole cycle in birds. Ross won the Nobel Prize in 1902, and 20 August is now marked as World Mosquito Day.

Different mosquitoes, different diseases

Anopheles mosquitoes carry malaria. They mostly bite between dusk and dawn, which is why bed nets treated with insecticide save so many lives.

Aedes mosquitoes, with black-and-white striped legs, carry dengue, chikungunya, Zika and yellow fever. They bite during the day and breed in small amounts of clean, still water in pots, coolers, tyres and flower vases. Dengue cases rise in many Indian cities after the monsoon each year. Culex mosquitoes spread Japanese encephalitis in Asia and West Nile virus, which reached the USA in 1999 and has since spread across the country.

Fighting back

The USA wiped out malaria in the early 1950s by draining swamps, spraying, using screens on windows and treating patients. India has cut malaria cases sharply in recent decades and aims to end it by 2030.

New tools are helping. In 2021, the WHO recommended the first malaria vaccine for children, and in 2023 a second one, R21, which is manufactured in India by the Serum Institute. Scientists are also releasing mosquitoes carrying bacteria called Wolbachia, which stop them from spreading dengue well.

Simple steps still matter most: removing standing water, using nets and repellents, and wearing long sleeves at the times mosquitoes bite. Anyone who gets a high fever, especially during the monsoon, should see a doctor quickly.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Mosquitoes prefer people with "sweet blood".

Truth: Blood sweetness has nothing to do with it. Mosquitoes find people by the carbon dioxide they breathe out, body heat and the chemicals in their skin and sweat, which differ from person to person.

Try this

Do a mosquito survey around your home with an adult. Look for any place where water collects and stands, such as plant saucers, buckets, old containers, blocked drains or a forgotten water cooler. Make a list, then help tip out or cover each one, and check again a week later.
