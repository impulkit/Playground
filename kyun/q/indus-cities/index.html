How did the Indus cities have drains 4,500 years ago?

Long before many cities had sewers, Indus towns had covered drains along their streets.

The short answer

The Indus (or Harappan) civilisation flourished about 4,500 years ago, across today's India and Pakistan. Its cities were planned, with straight streets laid out in a grid, a bit like modern Chandigarh or parts of New York.

Many houses had their own bathing area and toilet, connected to brick-lined drains that ran along the streets under stone or brick covers. Workers could lift the covers to clean out blockages. That is a system many cities in the world did not match for thousands of years.

Nobody knows for sure what they called themselves. Their writing hasn't been decoded.

Surprising but true

Indus bricks followed the same 1:2:4 shape across cities hundreds of kilometres apart.

Rediscovered in the 1920s

For thousands of years, the cities lay buried under mounds of earth. In the 1800s, railway builders even took old bricks from Harappa to lay under their tracks.

In 1921, the archaeologist Daya Ram Sahni began digging at Harappa, in today's Punjab province of Pakistan. In 1922, R.D. Banerji started at Mohenjo-daro, in Sindh. Soon the world realised that a forgotten civilisation, as old as Egypt and Mesopotamia, had been found.

A huge civilisation

Indus cities and towns spread across today's Pakistan and north-west India, from Afghanistan to Gujarat and Haryana. Over a thousand sites are now known. Rakhigarhi in Haryana is one of the largest.

Dholavira, on an island in the Rann of Kutch in Gujarat, had giant stone reservoirs to catch rare rainwater. In 2021 it became a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Lothal, also in Gujarat, has a large brick basin that many experts think was a dock for boats.

Planned to the last brick

Builders used baked bricks of the same 1:2:4 shape everywhere. Merchants used stone weights in a standard set of sizes, doubling from small to large, so a sack of grain weighed the same in Harappa as in Lothal.

Wells were dug all over the cities. Mohenjo-daro may have had hundreds. Houses faced side lanes instead of the main streets, perhaps for privacy and to keep out dust. Many modern sewers still use the same basic idea of sloping, covered channels.

Trade and crafts

Indus people made beads, pottery, cotton cloth and stone seals. Their carnelian beads, drilled with long, fine holes, have been found far away in Mesopotamia, in today's Iraq. Mesopotamian writings mention a land called Meluhha, which many scholars think was the Indus region.

Artists also cast small bronze figures. The most famous, known as the "Dancing Girl", is in the National Museum in New Delhi. A stone bust of a bearded man, nicknamed the "Priest-King", was found at Mohenjo-daro and is now in Pakistan.

Farmers grew wheat, barley, peas and sesame, and some of the world's earliest cotton. They kept cattle, sheep and goats.

Why did the cities empty?

After about 1900 BCE, the big cities slowly emptied, as people moved to smaller villages. Experts still debate why. Rivers may have changed course or dried up, and the monsoon may have weakened, making farming harder. It was probably a mix of causes over many years, not a sudden disaster.

Still a mystery

We can't yet read their writing, so much of their story is told by the cities themselves. Their seals show animals like bulls, elephants and a one-horned creature, next to short rows of signs. Whoever cracks the code will open a door that has been shut for 4,000 years.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Every great ancient civilisation was ruled by kings who built palaces and giant tombs.

Truth: Archaeologists have found no clear palaces, royal tombs or huge statues of rulers in Indus cities. Nobody knows exactly who was in charge, and some think power may have been shared among groups of leaders or merchants.
