How do fireworks get their colours?

Every firework colour comes from a metal. Strontium glows red, barium green, and copper makes the hardest one, blue.

The short answer

A firework's colours come from metals mixed into small pellets inside it, called stars. Each star contains a fuel, a chemical that provides oxygen, and a metal compound, all packed together.

When the stars burn, they get extremely hot. The heat pumps energy into the metal atoms, pushing their electrons up to higher energy levels. A moment later, the electrons drop back down and give off that energy as light. Each metal releases light of particular colours. Strontium glows red, barium gives green, copper gives blue, sodium gives yellow and calcium gives orange. Mixing metals makes other shades, like purple from strontium and copper together.

So the colour in the sky is really a glowing signature of the metal inside.

Surprising but true

Blue is the trickiest firework colour. The copper chemicals that make it break down if the flame gets too hot, so a strong blue is a sign of a skilled maker.

A Chinese invention

Fireworks began with gunpowder, a mixture of saltpetre, charcoal and sulphur. Most historians think Chinese alchemists discovered it around the 800s, possibly while searching for a potion for long life. Instead they found a powder that burned in a flash.

People in ancient China packed it into bamboo tubes to make loud bangs at festivals, which were believed to scare away evil spirits. Over the following centuries, the knowledge spread west along trade routes to the Islamic world, Europe and India. Early fireworks were mostly orange and gold, the colour of burning charcoal and iron sparks. Bright reds, greens and blues only became possible in the 1800s, when chemists learned to make new metal compounds.

Inside a firework shell

A big display firework is a ball or cylinder called a shell. At the bottom is a lift charge of gunpowder. When it is lit, it shoots the shell out of a tube high into the sky. At the same time, a time fuse starts burning inside.

When the fuse reaches the middle, it lights a burst charge, which breaks the shell open and flings the stars outwards. How the stars are arranged inside decides the shape of the burst: a round flower, a ring, a heart or a smiley face. Some stars change colour as they burn, because they are made in layers, like an onion.

Fireworks are made and set off only by trained experts. They can cause serious burns and eye injuries, so families are always safest watching from a distance.

Sivakasi and the festival season

In India, most fireworks are made in and around Sivakasi in Tamil Nadu. The hot, dry climate there helps the chemicals stay dry. Thousands of people work in its factories, many of them preparing for the busy months before Diwali.

In the USA, fireworks are a big part of Independence Day on 4 July. Around the world, cities like Sydney, London and Dubai welcome the new year with huge displays timed to music by computers. Each shell is wired to an electric igniter, so a show can fire hundreds of them to the exact beat.

The smoky side

Fireworks leave behind smoke, tiny dust particles and metal compounds. In Delhi and other cities, air pollution can rise sharply after Diwali, especially when cold, still weather traps the smoke near the ground. Loud bangs also frighten pets, birds and street animals.

Indian scientists at CSIR-NEERI have developed "green crackers" that aim to produce less smoke and fewer harmful particles. Some places now prefer laser light shows or drone shows, where hundreds of glowing drones draw pictures in the sky without any burning at all.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Firework colours come from coloured paper or dye inside.

Truth: Dyes would simply burn away. The colours come from metal compounds that glow with their own particular colour when they get very hot. Each metal always gives off the same colours, a bit like a fingerprint.

Try this

Hold an old CD or DVD, shiny side up, under a white light and tilt it until you see a rainbow. Now look at the reflection of different lights in it, like a tube light, an LED bulb or an orange street lamp seen from a safe place indoors. Some lights spread into a smooth rainbow, others into separate bands of colour. Those bands are clues to which elements are glowing.
