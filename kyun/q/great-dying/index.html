What was the Great Dying, worse than the dinosaur extinction?

About 252 million years ago, life on Earth came closer to being wiped out than at any other time we know of.

The short answer

The Great Dying is the name for the biggest extinction in the history of life. It happened about 252 million years ago, at the end of a time called the Permian, before the first dinosaurs. Somewhere around 80–90 percent of all kinds of sea animals died out, and about 70 percent of kinds of land animals with backbones.

The main suspect is a vast series of volcanic eruptions in what is now Siberia. They poured out lava and gases for a very long time. The gases heated the planet, made the seas more acidic and stole oxygen from the water.

Surprising but true

One of the few large land animals to come through it, a stocky plant-eater called Lystrosaurus, left its bones in West Bengal as well as South Africa and Antarctica.

A world before dinosaurs

Before the Great Dying, almost all the land on Earth was joined into one supercontinent called Pangaea. India sat in its southern part, called Gondwana, next to Africa, Antarctica and Australia. Forests of strange seed plants grew there, and coal from those forests is mined in India today.

On land lived tusked plant-eaters and sabre-toothed hunters, many of them distant relatives of mammals. In the seas, there were reefs, sea lilies, fish, spiral-shelled animals and the last of the trilobites, which had lived for about 270 million years.

Lava for thousands of centuries

In Siberia, cracks in the ground poured out lava again and again. The lava piles, called the Siberian Traps, still cover an area several times the size of Maharashtra. Like India's later Deccan Traps, they formed as layer upon layer of flat sheets, not as a single cone-shaped volcano.

The lava itself was not the main killer. The eruptions released huge amounts of carbon dioxide and other gases. Some lava also baked through thick layers of coal and other rock underground, adding even more gas. Very precise dating suggests that most of the dying happened within about 60,000 years. On the scale of Earth's history, that is a blink.

Hot seas, empty reefs

Carbon dioxide traps heat, which is why it matters for climate change today. At the end of the Permian, temperatures shot up. Studies of fossil teeth suggest that tropical seas may have reached around 40°C at the surface, hot bath temperature, much too warm for most sea life.

Warm water holds less oxygen, so large parts of the ocean became hard to breathe in. Carbon dioxide also dissolves in seawater and makes it more acidic, which makes it harder for animals to build shells. Reef-building animals were among the worst hit. Reefs almost vanished from the fossil record for millions of years. The corals that build today's coral reefs belong to a newer group that appeared afterwards.

Who came through

A few kinds of animals were lucky or tough. The most famous is Lystrosaurus, a pig-sized plant-eater with two tusks and a beak, related to the ancestors of mammals. For a while after the extinction it was one of the commonest land animals on Earth. Its fossils have been found in South Africa, Antarctica, China and the Damodar valley in eastern India. Finding the same animal on lands now far apart helped show that the continents were once joined, as described in India's long journey.

Life took millions of years to recover. For a long stretch after the extinction, there are almost no coal layers anywhere in the world, a sign that big forests had collapsed too. Even insects, which survive most disasters, suffered one of their worst losses ever. When life did bounce back, the world had changed. New groups of reptiles appeared, and among them, within about 20 million years, were the first dinosaurs.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The extinction that killed the dinosaurs was the worst in Earth's history.

Truth: It was the most famous, but not the worst. The Great Dying, about 186 million years earlier, killed a larger share of life in the sea and on land.

Try this

Fill a glass with fizzy water and drop in a small piece of chalk or a crushed eggshell. Watch it over a day. The bubbles are carbon dioxide, which makes water slightly acidic and slowly eats at shells. Large amounts of carbon dioxide did something similar to the seas 252 million years ago.
