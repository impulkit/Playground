How does a solar cooker work?

A black box with a glass lid, left in the sunshine, can cook dal and rice with no fire, no gas and no electricity.

The short answer

A solar cooker catches sunlight and turns it into heat for cooking. The most common kind, a box cooker, is an insulated box painted black inside, with a glass or clear plastic lid and often a mirror on a hinged flap.

Sunlight passes through the glass and hits the black inside and the dark pots. Dark surfaces soak up light and get hot. That heat then has trouble escaping. The glass lid stops warm air from drifting away, and the insulation keeps the walls from losing heat. The mirror bounces extra sunlight in.

Inside, temperatures often reach about 90 to 150 °C. That's enough to boil water, cook rice and dal, or bake a cake, though more slowly than on a stove.

Surprising but true

A solar cooker can work well on a freezing day in Ladakh, because what matters is bright sunlight, not warm air.

Trapping the heat

You've felt this effect in a car parked in the sun with its windows closed. Light streams in through the glass and warms the seats. The warm air can't get out, so the car becomes much hotter than the air outside.

A solar box cooker does the same on purpose. Colour matters a lot. A black pot soaks up most of the light that hits it, while a shiny or white pot reflects much of it away. Lids on the pots help too, keeping steam and heat inside. The Sun sends a surprising amount of energy. On a clear day, each square metre of ground in sunlight receives roughly 1,000 watts, about as much as a small electric heater.

Three kinds of cooker

Box cookers are cheap and safe, and food rarely burns, so you can leave it to cook while you do other things. Panel cookers are even simpler: shiny folded panels, often of card and foil, bounce sunlight onto a dark pot inside a clear plastic bag.

Parabolic cookers use a large curved dish of mirrors, shaped like a satellite dish, to focus sunlight onto one spot. They get very hot, very fast, and can fry food. They must be handled carefully, because the bright spot can burn skin and eyes.

An idea from the 1760s

In the 1760s, a Swiss scientist named Horace-Bénédict de Saussure built a box with layers of glass on top and a black base. He found it got hot enough to cook fruit. Later scientists used similar boxes on expeditions. India has had solar cooker programmes since the 1980s, and the government has long supported cheap box cookers for homes.

Some of India's largest kitchens now use sunlight on a giant scale. Two examples are the Brahma Kumaris centre at Mount Abu in Rajasthan and the Sai Baba temple at Shirdi in Maharashtra. There, rows of large curved mirrors on the roof focus sunlight to make steam. The steam cooks food for thousands of people a day.

Why they matter

Around the world, a very large number of people still cook over wood, dung or charcoal fires. Smoke from these fires fills homes and harms people's lungs, and it is a major cause of indoor air pollution. Collecting firewood takes hours and cuts down trees.

Solar cookers can help, though they have limits. They need clear skies and daytime cooking, they're slow, and they don't suit every dish or every family. In high, sunny places like Ladakh, where firewood is scarce and the sun is strong even in winter, they have been especially useful. Many families use a solar cooker alongside a regular stove, cooking a pot of dal in the sun while saving fuel for the evening meal.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Solar cookers only work on hot days.

Truth: Solar cookers need strong, direct sunlight, not warm air. They work well on clear, cold days in the mountains, and badly on hot but cloudy or hazy days.

Try this

On a sunny day, put two cups of water side by side in the sun. Wrap one in black paper and the other in white paper. After an hour, feel them or measure with a thermometer. Then try covering the black cup with a clear plastic bag and see if it gets warmer still.
