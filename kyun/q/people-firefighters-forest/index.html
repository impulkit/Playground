How do people fight forest fires?

Some American firefighters jump out of planes with parachutes to reach wildfires deep in the mountains, where no road goes.

The short answer

People fight forest fires mainly by taking away what a fire needs to burn. Every fire needs three things: heat, fuel and oxygen. Firefighters cool flames with water, smother them with soil, and above all remove the fuel in front of the fire.

On the ground, crews dig long strips of bare earth called fire lines. From the air, planes and helicopters drop water or a red chemical that slows burning. Sometimes crews deliberately burn a strip ahead of the fire, so the big blaze reaches ground already burned. Modern teams also use weather forecasts and satellites to predict where a fire will run next.

Surprising but true

Firefighters sometimes fight a fire by lighting another one. A carefully set fire burns up the fuel in the path of the big blaze, so it has nothing left to eat.

Digging the line

The most important tool in many wildfires is a hand tool. Crews of about 20 people, called hotshot crews in the USA, cut through brush and scrape the ground down to bare soil. A fire line can stretch for kilometres.

Their favourite tool is the Pulaski, half axe and half hoe. It was designed by Ed Pulaski, a forest ranger in Idaho. In 1910 a giant fire known as the Big Burn swept across the northwestern USA. Pulaski led his crew of about 45 men into an old mine tunnel and refused to let anyone run back out into the flames. Most of them survived.

Jumping from the sky

Some fires start from lightning in wild mountains far from any road. In the USA, smokejumpers reach them by parachute. The first jump onto a fire was in 1940. Today, jumpers leap from planes with tools, food and water dropped alongside them.

Once on the ground, they may work for days before hiking out to a road. A parachute gets them in. Their own legs get them out, often carrying 50 kg of gear.

Water bombers and helicopters

Planes called air tankers swoop low and drop thousands of litres of water or red fire retardant in a single pass. Helicopters carry huge buckets on cables and scoop water from lakes or rivers.

In April 2024, forest fires spread near Nainital in Uttarakhand. Indian Air Force helicopters filled buckets from Bhimtal lake and dropped the water on burning hillsides. Aircraft rarely put a big fire out alone, though. They slow it down so ground crews can finish the job.

Eyes in space

India's forests catch fire most often between February and June, when leaves are dry and the monsoon hasn't arrived. The Forest Survey of India uses satellites that pass overhead and detect hot spots. Alerts go by text message and email to forest staff near each fire, often within hours.

In the Himalaya, chir pine needles carpet the ground and burn very easily. Many villages have their own forest councils. Villagers clear needles, beat out small flames with green branches and keep watch, carrying on a tradition of caring for forests seen in the Chipko movement.

Fighting fire with fire

A backburn is a fire set on purpose by firefighters. They light it along a road or fire line, burning towards the main fire. When the two meet, there is nothing left to burn. It takes skill, because a shift in the wind can make a backburn dangerous. Crews watch the weather constantly, and US wildland firefighters carry a folding fire shelter, a foil tent to crawl under if flames ever come too close.

Some fire is even good. Many Aboriginal Australians and Native American nations have long used small, cool fires to clear undergrowth. Scientists now study these traditions to prevent huge blazes. In the USA, Smokey Bear has reminded people since 1944 that most wildfires are started by humans. Why flames spread the way they do comes down to the chemistry of how fire burns.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Every forest fire is a disaster that should be put out as fast as possible.

Truth: Many forests and grasslands evolved with fire. Small, cool fires clear dead wood and help some seeds sprout. Some pine cones only open after fire. Putting out every small fire can let fuel pile up until a huge one breaks out.

Try this

Draw the fire triangle on a card: heat, fuel and oxygen on its three corners. Next to each corner, write how firefighters remove it, such as water for heat, a firebreak for fuel and soil thrown on flames for oxygen. Use it to explain to your family how fires are put out.
