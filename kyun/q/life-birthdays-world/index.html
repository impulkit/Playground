How do kids celebrate birthdays around the world?

In South Korea, a one-year-old is placed before a table of objects, and whatever the baby grabs is said to hint at their future.

The short answer

Kids almost everywhere now get a cake, a song and a bit of fuss on their birthday. But the details change from country to country. Chinese families serve extra-long noodles for a long life. Korean children eat seaweed soup. In Mexico, kids swing at a piñata full of sweets. In many Indian homes, the day starts with new clothes, a visit to the temple or touching elders' feet for blessings, and a bag of toffees to hand out at school.

Some of these customs are very old. Others, like the Happy Birthday song, are barely 130 years old.

Surprising but true

The tune of "Happy Birthday to You" began as a classroom song called "Good Morning to All", written by two sisters in Kentucky in 1893.

Noodles, soup and a piñata

In China, a classic birthday dish is longevity noodles: very long noodles served without cutting. Biting through them early is said to be bad luck, so people slurp. Noodles stand for a long life because they go on and on.

In South Korea, birthdays come with miyeokguk, a soup made with seaweed. New mothers there traditionally eat it to regain strength after giving birth, so eating it on your birthday is a way to remember your mother. In Mexico, a papier-mâché piñata hangs from a tree or a beam, and blindfolded children whack it with a stick until it bursts.

A baby's first big choice

Korea's first-birthday party, the doljanchi, is a big event. In the past, many babies didn't live to see their first birthday, so reaching it was worth celebrating.

The highlight is doljabi. The baby, dressed in bright traditional clothes, sits before a tray of objects: a pencil, money, thread and more. Whatever the baby grabs first is said to show the future. A pencil means a scholar, thread means a long life. Modern families add a microphone or a football. Everyone laughs, and nobody takes it seriously.

Lucky, unlucky and slightly odd

Some customs are about luck. In Germany, people often avoid wishing someone happy birthday before the actual day, because it is thought to be unlucky. In Denmark, a flag is often hung outside the window to tell the street someone is celebrating. In parts of Atlantic Canada, a birthday child may get a dab of butter on the nose, so bad luck slides off.

In India, many families celebrate using the Hindu calendar as well, so a person's "tithi" birthday can fall on a different day each year. Some families mark both dates.

The song everyone knows

"Happy Birthday to You" started as "Good Morning to All", a song for small children written by sisters Mildred and Patty Hill in Kentucky, USA, published in 1893. People soon sang new words to it at parties.

For decades, a company claimed to own the song and collected money whenever it was used in films. Restaurants made up their own birthday songs to avoid paying. In 2015, a US court ruled that the claim did not hold up, and the song is now free for everyone. It is sung in dozens of languages.

How old are you, really?

Counting age is not the same everywhere. In Korea, people traditionally counted a baby as one year old at birth, and everyone added a year on New Year's Day. In June 2023, South Korea switched official age counting to the international system, which made many people a year or two "younger" overnight.

In Vietnam, people traditionally aged together at Tết, the lunar new year. And the strangest part? Babies born on 29 February get a real birthday only once every four years, thanks to how a leap year works. Myth or fact?

Myth: Every culture has always celebrated birthdays every year.

Truth: For much of history, many people didn't know or mark their exact birthday. In some traditions, birthdays were for kings, gods or saints, and in others, everyone counted a new year of age together at the new year.

Try this

Ask everyone in your class for their birthday month and day, and write them on the board. Check if any two of you share a birthday. With 23 or more people, it happens more often than not, which surprises most people.
