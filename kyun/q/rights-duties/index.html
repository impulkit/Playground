What are rights and duties?

India's Constitution promises you rights you can take to court. It also lists duties that nobody can force you to do. Why the difference?

The short answer

Rights are freedoms and protections that every person should have, such as the right to be treated equally, to speak freely, to follow your own religion and to go to school. Duties are responsibilities you have towards others and towards your country, such as obeying the law, respecting other people and protecting the environment.

The two go together. Your right to walk safely down the street depends on other people's duty not to harm you, and theirs depends on yours. In India, rights are written into the Constitution and can be defended in court. Duties are listed there too, but mostly as goals to live by.

Surprising but true

India's Constitution had no list of duties at all when it began in 1950. The duties were added in 1976.

Rights you can take to court

Part III of India's Constitution lists the Fundamental Rights. They include the right to equality, the right to freedom of speech and movement, and protection against forced labour and child labour. There is freedom of religion, and cultural and educational rights for minorities. Finally, there is the right to go to court to protect all of these.

That last one matters most. If the government breaks your rights, you can go straight to the Supreme Court or a High Court. B. R. Ambedkar, who led the drafting of the Constitution, called this right its "heart and soul". He knew that rights on paper mean little if nobody can enforce them.

Rights that changed

Rights can be added or removed. The right to property was a Fundamental Right until 1978, when it was moved out of that list. In 2002, India added the right to free education for children aged 6 to 14. Courts have also widened the meaning of existing rights. The Supreme Court has said that the right to life includes the right to clean air and water and, since 2017, the right to privacy.

Duties, added later

When India's Constitution began in 1950, it had no list of duties. In 1976, the 42nd Amendment added ten Fundamental Duties, and an eleventh was added in 2002. They include respecting the national flag and anthem, protecting forests, rivers and wildlife, developing a scientific temper, and, for parents, giving their children the chance to go to school.

Courts cannot usually punish you just for ignoring a duty. Some critics say that makes them weak. Others say duties work best as shared values, taught at school and at home, rather than as rules backed by fines.

The American way

The United States Constitution of 1787 at first said little about individual rights. Many Americans objected, so in 1791 ten amendments were added, called the Bill of Rights. They protect freedom of speech, religion and the press, and the right to a fair trial. The US Constitution has no list of duties like India's, though citizens are expected to obey laws, pay taxes and serve on juries when called.

Rights for everyone, everywhere

After the Second World War, the United Nations wrote the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, adopted on 10 December 1948. Hansa Mehta, an Indian delegate, is credited with changing a line about "all men" to "all human beings", so it clearly included women.

Why was the United Nations created?After two world wars in thirty years, the world's countries agreed to try something new, a place where every nation could talk before it fought.

Countries do not always keep these promises. But the declaration gave people everywhere a shared language for saying when they were being treated unfairly, and it is still quoted in courts and protests around the world.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Having rights means you can do whatever you want.

Truth: Rights have limits, because they must fit with other people's rights. Freedom of speech in India and the USA does not protect, for example, lying about someone in a way that harms them, or calling for violence.

Try this

Make two lists on a sheet of paper, "My rights at home" and "My duties at home". For each right, see if there is a matching duty, like "I have a right to a clean room" and "I have a duty to help keep it clean". Ask a family member to add one to each list.
