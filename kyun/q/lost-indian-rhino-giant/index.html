What was Paraceratherium?

Millions of years ago, a hornless rhino taller than a double-decker bus browsed treetops in what is now Pakistan and China.

The short answer

Paraceratherium was a giant relative of the rhinoceros that lived in Asia between about 34 and 23 million years ago. It had no horn. Instead, it had long legs, a long neck and a head held so high it could browse leaves from the tops of trees, like a giraffe.

It stood up to about 4.8 m at the shoulder, higher than a double-decker bus, and weighed perhaps 11 to 20 tonnes, as much as three or four elephants. Its fossils have been found in Pakistan, China, Mongolia and Kazakhstan. It is one of the biggest land mammals ever known.

Surprising but true

Its skull alone was about 1.3 m long, longer than many 8-year-olds are tall.

A rhino with no horn

Today's rhinos, like the one-horned rhinos of Kaziranga, are heavy, low and horned. Paraceratherium belonged to a branch of the rhino family that went a different way. Its legs were long and pillar-like, and its feet had three toes, as rhino feet still do.

Its skull was about 1.3 m long, yet quite small for its huge body. Scientists think it had a flexible upper lip, perhaps a short trunk-like snout, for stripping leaves. Two big, tusk-like front teeth pointed downwards, probably for snapping branches.

Bones in Balochistan

In the early 1900s, the British palaeontologist Clive Forster Cooper dug in the Bugti Hills of Balochistan, then part of British India and now in Pakistan. He found bones of an enormous animal and named it Paraceratherium in 1911.

Soon other giant bones turned up. Forster Cooper named a second set from Balochistan Baluchitherium in 1913. A Russian scientist studied bones from Kazakhstan and called them Indricotherium. In 1922, the American Roy Chapman Andrews led an expedition to Mongolia that found a huge skull. For decades, books used all three names, and many old dinosaur books show "Baluchitherium" towering over the other beasts. Most experts now place them in one group, with Paraceratherium as the oldest name.

Life in the treetops

When Paraceratherium lived, the dinosaurs had been gone for more than 30 million years, and mammals had grown big. Much of Asia was open woodland with scattered trees. A tall browser had the high leaves to itself, the same trick the giraffe uses today.

An animal this size would have needed hundreds of kilograms of leaves every day. Its sheer bulk protected adults from almost every predator. Babies were probably at risk from big crocodiles and other hunters. Like elephants today, a mother may have carried her calf for well over a year before it was born, and guarded it closely.

Its feet were probably wider than dinner plates, and each step of an adult must have landed with a heavy thud.

A long walk across Asia

In 2021, Chinese scientists described a new species from Gansu in north-west China. They suggested these giants moved between Mongolia and South Asia, possibly through lowlands where the Tibetan Plateau now stands. At that time, the Himalaya and Tibet were still rising, as India pushed slowly into Asia.

Why did Paraceratherium die out? The climate was cooling and drying, forests shrank, and big animals need big supplies of food. Other plant-eaters, including early elephants spreading from Africa, may also have competed with it for trees.

Bigger than a rhino, smaller than a dinosaur

Paraceratherium is often called the largest land mammal ever. That title is now in doubt. Some scientists estimate that an extinct straight-tusked elephant whose fossils were found in India's Narmada valley may have been heavier still, though its remains are few.

Either way, the giants of the dinosaur age were in another league. Argentinosaurus, a long-necked sauropod from South America, may have weighed 70 tonnes or more, about as much as four Paraceratheriums put together.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Paraceratherium was the biggest land animal that ever lived.

Truth: Giant sauropod dinosaurs were several times heavier. Paraceratherium is one of the largest land mammals known, and some scientists think an extinct Indian elephant may have been even bigger.

Use a measuring tape and a long string to mark out 4.8 metres, the height of Paraceratherium at the shoulder. Stretch it up the side of a building with an adult's help, or lay it on the ground next to a car. Its head would have stood even higher.
