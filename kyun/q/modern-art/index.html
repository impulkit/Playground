Why does modern art look so strange?

Once cameras could copy the world perfectly, painters started asking a new question. If a picture doesn't have to look real, what else can it do?

The short answer

Modern art is a name for art made roughly between the 1860s and the 1970s, when many artists stopped trying to make pictures look like real life. Instead they experimented with colour, shape, feelings and ideas.

One big reason was the camera. Once photography could capture a face or a landscape exactly, painters no longer needed to. They began asking new questions. Can a painting show a feeling? Can it show a face from every side at once? Can it be just colour, with nothing recognisable at all? Their answers often looked strange to people at the time. Many still do.

Surprising but true

In 1917 the artist Marcel Duchamp bought an ordinary bathroom urinal from a shop, signed it with a made-up name and sent it to an art exhibition.

Breaking the old rules

For hundreds of years European art schools taught artists to use perspective, careful shading and realistic detail. The Impressionists began to loosen those rules in the 1870s, with quick brushstrokes and bright colour.

Others went much further. Vincent van Gogh used colour to express emotion. Pablo Picasso and Georges Braque broke objects into flat, angular pieces in a style called Cubism. Each new group pushed a little harder, and audiences often reacted with laughter or anger.

Pictures of nothing

Around 1910, a few painters made pictures that showed no objects at all. This is called abstract art. The Russian painter Wassily Kandinsky believed colours and shapes could work like music, stirring emotions without telling a story.

The Dutch painter Piet Mondrian reduced his pictures to black lines and blocks of red, yellow and blue. In the United States in the 1940s, Jackson Pollock laid huge canvases on the floor and dripped and flicked paint across them, moving around like a dancer. The result was a record of movement as much as a picture.

Is it even art?

In 1917 Marcel Duchamp sent a shop-bought urinal, turned on its side and signed "R. Mutt", to an exhibition in New York. He called it Fountain. The exhibition refused to show it. Duchamp's point was that an artist's idea and choice could matter more than skill with a brush.

People are still arguing about that idea. Some think it opened art to anyone with something to say. Others think it let people call almost anything art. Both views are reasonable, and the debate is part of what modern art is about.

Modern art in India

In India, modern art grew alongside the freedom movement. Amrita Sher-Gil blended European training with Indian subjects in the 1930s. In 1947, the year of independence, young painters in Bombay formed the Progressive Artists' Group. They included M.F. Husain, F.N. Souza and S.H. Raza.

Raza later became famous for paintings built around the bindu, a dot or circle that in Indian thought can stand for the starting point of everything. His work is abstract, but it grows from Indian ideas, which shows that modern art did not belong to Europe alone.

How to look at it

Modern art can feel confusing because it does not always try to show something. A good start is to ask simple questions. What colours did the artist choose? Does the picture feel calm, angry or joyful? What would change if one shape were moved?

Many museums, like the Museum of Modern Art in New York, which opened in 1929, put labels beside each work to explain what the artist was trying to do. Even so, you are allowed not to like something. Most modern artists expected people to argue.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Modern art is so simple that any child could make it.

Truth: Some modern art looks simple, but it came from artists who usually trained for years and made deliberate choices. In a 2011 study, people could tell professional abstract paintings from children's more often than chance.

Try this

Put on a piece of music you like and paint or draw only shapes and colours that match how it feels, with no people, houses or objects. Then swap with a friend and see if they can guess whether your music was happy, sad, calm or exciting.
