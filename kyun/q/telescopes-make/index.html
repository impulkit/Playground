Can you make a telescope at home?

Galileo's telescope was a tube with two lenses, and with it he found four moons circling Jupiter. You can build something similar from a pair of lenses.

The short answer

Yes. A simple telescope needs only two lenses and a tube to hold them in line.

At the front goes a large, gently curved lens with a long focal length, the distance at which it brings light to a point. It collects light from something far away and makes a small, upside-down picture of it inside the tube. At the back goes a small, strongly curved lens, the eyepiece. You look through it like a magnifying glass, enlarging that little picture.

To work out how much the telescope magnifies, divide the focal length of the front lens by that of the eyepiece. A front lens of 50 cm and an eyepiece of 5 cm gives ten times.

Surprising but true

For seeing faint stars and galaxies, how wide a telescope is matters far more than how much it magnifies.

Galileo's tubes

In 1608, spectacle makers in the Netherlands applied for a patent on a device for seeing faraway things as if they were close. When news reached the Italian scientist Galileo Galilei in 1609, he quickly worked out the idea and began grinding his own lenses.

His first telescope magnified about three times. Within months he had one that magnified about 20 times. In January 1610, he pointed it at Jupiter and saw four small points of light beside it. Night after night they moved. He realised they were moons orbiting Jupiter, proof that not everything circled the Earth. Today they are called the Galilean moons, and Jupiter's four big moons are still among the easiest sights for a small telescope.

Building your own

Many children's science kits and astronomy clubs offer simple telescope kits with cardboard tubes and two lenses. In 2009, the International Year of Astronomy, scientists designed a cheap kit called the Galileoscope so students around the world could build one and see Jupiter's moons for themselves.

You can also try with lenses you have at home. A pair of weak reading glasses can serve as the front lens, and a strong magnifying glass as the eyepiece. Hold them in line and move them apart until a distant object comes into focus. You'll notice the view is upside down. That's normal for this design, and it doesn't matter for looking at the Moon.

Width beats magnification

Many people think a telescope's power is all about magnification. Astronomers care more about its width, called the aperture. A wider lens or mirror collects more light, like a bigger bucket in the rain. More light means you can see fainter stars and finer detail.

Magnify too much with a small lens, and the picture just gets bigger, dimmer and blurrier. That's why serious astronomers build huge mirrors. In 1668, Isaac Newton made a telescope using a curved mirror instead of a front lens. Mirrors can be made much bigger than lenses without sagging or bending colours.

Telescope makers

In the 1920s, an American engineer named Russell Porter helped start a club in Vermont where amateurs ground their own telescope mirrors by hand. The meeting, called Stellafane, still happens every summer, and amateur telescope making spread across the USA.

In India, the Indian Institute of Astrophysics runs the Vainu Bappu Observatory at Kavalur in Tamil Nadu. Its main telescope, named after the astronomer M. K. Vainu Bappu, has a mirror about 2.3 metres wide. Many Indian cities also have astronomy clubs and planetariums that hold star parties, where people bring telescopes to share. A home-made two-lens telescope won't show galaxies, but it will show the Moon's craters, which is where Galileo started too.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The best telescope is the one with the biggest magnification.

Truth: Magnification just enlarges what the telescope collects. A wider front lens or mirror gathers more light, so it shows fainter stars and sharper detail. A small telescope pushed to huge magnification shows a big, dim, blurry blob.

Try this

Hold a magnifying glass close to your eye with one hand. Hold a second, weaker lens (an old pair of long-sight reading glasses works) at arm's length with the other. Look at a faraway building through both and slide the far lens slowly until the view is sharp. Never point it at the Sun.
