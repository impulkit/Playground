Which famous cities were lost and found?

Cities have vanished under jungle, sand and volcanic ash, and some were "lost" only to outsiders while local people knew exactly where they were.

The short answer

Many great cities were abandoned over time and then forgotten by most of the world. Some were swallowed by forests, like Maya cities in Central America. Others were buried by sand or soil, like Mohenjo-daro in the Indus valley, or by a volcano, like Pompeii in Italy. Wars destroyed some, like Hampi in India.

Centuries later, travellers, archaeologists and sometimes farmers found them again. The word "lost" can mislead, though. Local people often knew the ruins well. What was lost was the wider world's memory of them, and often the city's own history.

Surprising but true

In 2018, lasers fired from aircraft revealed tens of thousands of hidden Maya buildings, roads and farms under the jungle of northern Guatemala.

Machu Picchu

High in the Andes mountains of Peru, the Inca built Machu Picchu around 1450, probably as an estate for an emperor. When Spanish invaders conquered the Inca in the 1500s, they never found it, so its stone buildings survived.

In 1911, Hiram Bingham, a lecturer from Yale University, was led there by local people. His photos in National Geographic made it world-famous. Today it is one of the most visited sites in South America, and Peru limits visitor numbers to protect it.

Mohenjo-daro

For thousands of years, Mohenjo-daro lay under mounds in Sindh, now in Pakistan. In the 1920s, archaeologists, including R.D. Banerji of the Archaeological Survey of India, began digging. They found a planned city of baked brick with straight streets, a great bath and covered drains.

It was part of the Indus civilisation, about 4,500 years old. Before these digs, nobody knew that people in South Asia had built such cities so early. Their writing has still not been deciphered.

Hampi

Hampi, in Karnataka, was the capital of the Vijayanagara empire. In the 1400s and 1500s, visitors from Persia and Portugal described huge markets selling rubies, horses and spices. After a defeat in battle in 1565, the city was looted and abandoned.

Hampi was never fully forgotten. Its Virupaksha temple has stayed in worship. But its palaces and bazaars fell into ruin among giant boulders. Today it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Cities in the jungle

In Cambodia, the temples of Angkor were partly covered by forest after the Khmer capital moved away. Monks still used Angkor Wat. A French naturalist, Henri Mouhot, wrote about it in the 1860s, and his books made it famous in Europe.

What is Angkor Wat?The largest religious monument on Earth was built for the Hindu god Vishnu, and it stands not in India but in Cambodia.

In Central America, Maya cities with stone pyramids were swallowed by rainforest. Exploring them on foot was slow. In 2018, researchers used lidar, lasers fired from aircraft, to map the ground beneath the trees in Guatemala. They found tens of thousands of hidden structures, showing the Maya world was far larger than thought.

Legends that turned out real

Ancient Greek poems told of Troy, a city besieged in a great war. Many thought it was only a story. In the 1870s, Heinrich Schliemann dug at a mound called Hisarlik in Turkey and found layers of ruined cities, one on top of another. Most archaeologists now accept it was Troy, though Schliemann's rough digging destroyed a lot of evidence.

In Jordan, the rock-carved city of Petra was home to local Bedouin people. In 1812 a Swiss traveller, Johann Ludwig Burckhardt, disguised himself to visit it and told Europe about it.

Some lost cities may still be out there, under forests, deserts or the sea, waiting for the right tool or a lucky farmer.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Explorers like Hiram Bingham discovered lost cities nobody knew about.

Truth: Most "lost" cities were known to local people, who farmed nearby, used the stones or told stories about them. Explorers brought them to the attention of the wider world, often with local guides.
