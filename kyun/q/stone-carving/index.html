How do sculptors carve stone?

Michelangelo carved his David from a block of marble that other sculptors had started, given up on and left lying in a yard for about 25 years.

The short answer

Carving stone is the art of taking away, not adding. A sculptor starts with a solid block and removes everything that isn't part of the figure.

The work goes in stages. First comes rough shaping with heavy tools. Then smaller chisels bring out the form. Last come files, rasps and sandpaper to smooth and polish the surface.

Sculptors have to plan carefully. Once a piece of stone is knocked off, it can't be put back. A crack in the wrong place can ruin months of work.

Surprising but true

About 90% of the rock removed to carve the faces on Mount Rushmore in the USA was blasted away with dynamite. Fine chisel work came only at the end.

Choosing the stone

Every stone behaves differently. Soapstone is so soft you can scratch it with a fingernail. Alabaster and some sandstones are soft too. Marble is a little harder and has a fine, even grain that takes detail and polish beautifully.

Granite and basalt are among the hardest. Carving them by hand takes huge patience. Many Indian temple sculptors still work in granite, and the temple sculptures of south India show what that patience can do.

Carvers look for hidden faults before they start. They tap a block and listen. A clear ring means solid stone. A dull thud can mean a crack inside.

The tools

Most hand carvers use a mallet and a set of steel chisels. A point chisel, sharpened to a spike, knocks off big chunks. A claw chisel, with a row of teeth, shapes the surface and leaves little grooves. A flat chisel smooths it. Then come rasps, which are rough files, and finally sandpaper or polishing stones.

Today many sculptors also use air-powered hammers and electric grinders with diamond blades, especially for hard stone. Big stone yards may use computer-controlled machines to cut the rough shape before an artist finishes by hand.

Michelangelo's giant

In 1501, a young sculptor in Florence, Italy, named Michelangelo was handed a huge, tall block of marble. Other sculptors had begun working on it decades earlier, then given up. It had been lying in a workshop yard for about 25 years.

Over about three years, he carved it into David, the young hero from the Bible. The statue is about 5.17 m tall, around three times the height of an adult. People in Florence called the block "the Giant" before it was ever carved.

Michelangelo liked to say that the figure was already inside the stone. His job was only to set it free.

Why are presidents carved into Mount Rushmore?Four faces, each about as tall as a six-storey building, were blasted out of a granite mountain with dynamite. Not one worker died in 14 years.

India's carved giants

At Shravanabelagola in Karnataka stands a statue of Gommateshwara, also called Bahubali, a figure honoured in the Jain faith, which Mahavira taught. Carved around 983 CE from a single granite rock on top of a hill, it is about 17 m tall. That is about as tall as five floors of a building.

Every twelve years or so, a ceremony called the Mahamastakabhisheka is held. Priests pour milk, saffron and other offerings over the statue's head from a high platform.

The white marble of the Taj Mahal came from Makrana in Rajasthan, where marble has been quarried for centuries. Carvers there still cut inlay and lattice screens by hand, many of them from families who have done the work for generations.

A craft for patient hands

Big stone projects have often used whatever tools work best. At Mount Rushmore, in South Dakota, workers between 1927 and 1941 blasted away about 90% of the rock with dynamite before finishing with drills and hand tools.

Smaller works are still mostly done by hand. A carver might spend a whole day on one fold of a robe or the curl of a lip. The sound of steel on stone, tap after tap, is the same in a workshop in Mamallapuram, in Carrara in Italy, or in a sculpture studio in New York.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ancient marble statues were always pure white.

Truth: Many ancient Greek and Roman statues were brightly painted. The paint wore off over the centuries, and later artists copied the bare white stone, thinking that was how they were meant to look.

Try this

Carve a big raw potato using only a teaspoon and a lolly stick. Draw a simple shape on it first, like a fish or a face. Scoop away big chunks, then smaller ones, then smooth the surface with the back of the spoon. Work from big shapes down to small details, just as stone carvers do, and notice how one careless scoop can't be undone.
