What strange medicines did people once use?

For about 200 years, European chemists sold powder made from ground-up Egyptian mummies, and doctors told patients to eat it.

The short answer

For most of history, nobody knew what germs were. Doctors guessed at why people got sick and tried whatever seemed to help. Some cures were useless, some were harmful, and a few accidentally worked.

People ate powdered mummies, drained blood out of the sick, swallowed potions with 60 ingredients and blew smoke into very unusual places. Strange as these sound, a few old remedies led straight to medicines still in your cupboard. Please don't try any of them. Even the ones that partly worked were risky, and a doctor today has far better tools.

Surprising but true

Leeches never fully left medicine. Surgeons still use them today to help reattached fingers and ears heal.

Eating mummies

In the 1500s, European chemists stocked a brown powder called mumia. It was made by grinding up bodies taken from Egyptian tombs. People swallowed it for bruises, headaches and almost anything else.

The idea started with a mix-up. An Arabic word, mumiya, meant a natural black tar that was used as a medicine. Ancient Egyptian mummies looked as if they were coated in the same dark stuff, so traders began selling the mummies themselves. Demand grew so high that some sellers made fakes from the bodies of recently dead people. The fashion faded in the 1700s.

Bleeding and leeches

For over 2,000 years, many doctors believed the body held four fluids, and illness meant one was out of balance. The cure was to let some blood out, by cutting a vein or by putting hungry leeches on the skin.

When George Washington, the first US president, fell ill with a throat infection in 1799, his doctors took about 2.4 litres of his blood in less than a day. That is around 40 percent of what an adult body holds. He died that night. Most historians think the bleeding made things worse.

Leeches have made a comeback, though. Surgeons use them after reattaching fingers or ears, because their spit stops blood clotting and keeps it flowing through fragile new veins.

Potions with everything in them

One of the most famous medicines in Europe and the Middle East was theriac. Some recipes had more than 60 ingredients, including opium, honey, spices and dried snake meat. Cities like Venice made it in public, so buyers could watch and be sure it wasn't fake. Theriac was meant to cure almost anything, from snakebite to plague. Some doctors in Europe still prescribed it in the 1800s.

In the 1920s, some Americans paid for "radium water", which contained a radioactive metal. People thought the glow meant energy. It actually caused terrible illness, and it helped scientists understand how dangerous radiation is.

The cures that worked

Not every old cure was a dud. For thousands of years, people in Egypt, Greece and India chewed willow bark for pain and fever. In the 1800s, chemists found the active chemical inside. In 1899, the company Bayer turned a version of it into aspirin.

Ancient Egyptian doctors smeared honey on wounds. Honey makes it hard for bacteria to grow, and some hospitals still use specially cleaned medical honey on hard-to-heal cuts. In ancient India, the surgeon Sushruta described using leeches and careful stitching, and his writings show a sharp eye for how wounds heal.

Why bad cures seemed to work

Many patients felt better after weird treatments. Partly that is because most illnesses get better on their own. Partly it is the placebo effect, where believing in a cure can make you feel better even if it does nothing. Without fair tests, nobody could tell the difference.

Fair tests, where one group gets the medicine and another doesn't, only became normal in the 1900s. Around the same time, a scientist noticed mould killing bacteria on a dish, and the age of antibiotics began.

Myth or fact?

Myth: People in the past were foolish to believe in such strange cures.

Truth: They were working with the best ideas they had, before anyone knew about germs or could test medicines fairly. Some old cures, like willow bark and honey, turned out to contain real medicine.
