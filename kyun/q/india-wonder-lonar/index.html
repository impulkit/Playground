How did a meteor make Lonar Lake?

Long ago, a space rock slammed into Maharashtra at many times the speed of a bullet and left a round lake that once turned pink overnight.

The short answer

Thousands of years ago, a rock from space came screaming through the sky over what is now Maharashtra. It was travelling at perhaps 20 km every second, fast enough to cross Mumbai in under two seconds. When it hit the ground, it blasted out a bowl about 1.8 km wide and about 150 m deep.

Over time, rain and underground springs filled the bottom of the bowl with water. Today that water is Lonar Lake, ringed by forest, old temples and steep crater walls. It's one of the best-preserved young meteorite craters on Earth.

Surprising but true

In June 2020, Lonar Lake's green water turned pink in a few days, coloured by salt-loving microbes.

A punch harder than a bomb

The space rock was probably only a few dozen metres across, smaller than a cricket ground. Size isn't the scary part. Speed is. At those speeds, the rock carried more energy than a large bomb, and much of it turned into heat and pressure in a split second.

The rock vaporised. Ground rock was squeezed so hard that its crystals were changed in ways ordinary forces can't manage. Some of it melted into small glassy beads. Finding these "shocked" minerals in the 1970s helped prove that Lonar was not a volcano at all.

The blast also flung rock outwards in every direction. Look at the crater today and you can still see a raised rim about 20 to 30 m high. It is made of rock layers that were peeled back and flipped over like the pages of a book. Smaller pieces landed far beyond the rim as a blanket of broken stone.

A crater in lava rock

Lonar sits on the Deccan Traps, layers of basalt from enormous lava flows about 66 million years ago. That is the same time a much bigger space rock struck Mexico and the dinosaurs died out, which makes scientists argue about what really killed them.

Basalt matters because the Moon and Mars are covered in it. Lonar lets scientists walk around a basalt impact crater on Earth and compare it with ones seen by spacecraft. Researchers from NASA, ISRO and Indian universities have all studied it.

How old is Lonar? Here, scientists disagree. Earlier estimates said about 50,000 years. A 2011 study using argon dating suggested about 5,70,000 years. New dating methods may settle it.

The day the lake turned pink

Lonar's water is very salty and very alkaline, like water with lots of soap dissolved in it. Few fish can live in it, but tiny microbes thrive.

In June 2020, people living nearby noticed something odd. Within a few days, the green lake had turned pink. Scientists tested the water and found huge numbers of salt-loving microbes called haloarchaea, which make a pink pigment. A hot summer had evaporated water and made the lake even saltier, giving them a boom. The same thing tints pink lakes in Australia and Senegal.

Temples on the rim

People have lived around Lonar for a very long time. Ruined temples stand on the slopes, and the Daitya Sudan temple in the town is about 800 years old. A local story says the crater was where the god Vishnu defeated a demon named Lonasur, who hid underground.

In 1823, a British officer named C. J. E. Alexander described the crater in writing, which is often counted as its first scientific mention. Today Lonar is a Ramsar wetland and a national geological monument. Peafowl call from the forest on its rim, and many kinds of birds feed along the shore, as they do in other wetlands across India.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Lonar Lake was made by a volcano, like most round lakes.

Truth: For a long time some people thought so, because the Deccan is old volcanic land. But Lonar has shocked minerals and glassy bits of melted rock that only a high-speed impact can make.
