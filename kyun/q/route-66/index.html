What is Route 66?

Route 66 was officially removed from America's highway map in 1985, yet people from all over the world still fly in just to drive it.

The short answer

Route 66 is a highway in the United States that opened in 1926. It ran about 3,940 kilometres (2,448 miles) from Chicago in the Midwest to Santa Monica on the Pacific coast, passing through eight states. That is a little more than the distance from Srinagar to Kanyakumari.

For decades it was the main road west. Farm families escaping hard times drove it, then holidaymakers in shiny new cars. Diners, motels and petrol stations with glowing signs sprang up beside it. When faster motorways replaced it, the old road became something new: a symbol of travel and freedom. Its 100th birthday falls on 11 November 2026.

Surprising but true

The road crosses only about 20 kilometres of Kansas, the shortest stretch of any of its eight states.

Joining up the dirt roads

In the early 1900s, most American roads were dirt tracks that turned to mud in the rain. As cars became cheaper, people demanded better ones. In 1926, the government created a national system of numbered highways.

A businessman from Oklahoma, Cyrus Avery, pushed hard for one road to link Chicago with Los Angeles. Unlike older roads that ran straight east to west, it curved south-west through small towns, giving farmers a way to get crops to market. Much of it was still gravel or dirt at first. It was paved from end to end by 1938.

The Mother Road

In the 1930s, America was hit by the Great Depression, when banks failed and millions lost their jobs. On the southern plains, years of drought and poor farming turned fields to dust. Huge dust storms rolled across Oklahoma and Texas.

Hundreds of thousands of people packed their belongings onto old cars and trucks and headed west to California, hoping for work. Most of them took Route 66. The writer John Steinbeck told their story in his 1939 novel The Grapes of Wrath, where he called it "the mother road, the road of flight". The name has stuck ever since.

What was the Great Depression?In October 1929 share prices in New York crashed. Within three years, about one in four American workers had no job, and the trouble had spread around the globe.

Kicks, neon and giant dinosaurs

After the Second World War, Americans had more money and more cars. Families took road trips on Route 66 for fun. A 1946 song told listeners to "get your kicks on Route 66", and it became a hit.

Businesses competed for travellers' attention. There were motels shaped like wigwams, giant concrete dinosaurs and towering neon signs. Diners served burgers and milkshakes to people who had driven all day across the desert. One famous detour leads to the Grand Canyon, about an hour's drive north of the road in Arizona.

Bypassed by the interstate

In 1956, the US began building the interstate highways: wide, fast motorways that skip around towns instead of going through them. Bit by bit, new interstates replaced Route 66. The last town on the old road, Williams in Arizona, was bypassed in 1984. The next year, Route 66 was removed from the official list.

Many towns that had lived off passing travellers faded. Motels closed and signs went dark. It's a pattern seen in many countries: when a new highway or railway goes elsewhere, the towns on the old route can lose their customers almost overnight.

A road that came back

Fans of the old road started campaigning to save it. States put up brown "Historic Route 66" signs, and old motels and diners were restored. Visitors from Europe, Japan and India now rent cars to drive it.

The 2006 Pixar film Cars is set in Radiator Springs, an imaginary town on a forgotten stretch of the road. Its makers drove Route 66 to research it, and many details come from real places along the way. Engineers who studied how roads are built in 1926 would barely recognise today's interstates. The Mother Road still runs past the same small towns.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Route 66 doesn't exist any more.

Truth: It was taken off the official US highway list in 1985, but much of the old road is still there. Long stretches are signed as "Historic Route 66", and you can still drive most of the way from Chicago to the Pacific.
