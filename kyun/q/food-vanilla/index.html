Why is vanilla so expensive?

Almost every vanilla flower on Earth is pollinated by a human hand, using a trick a 12-year-old boy worked out in 1841.

The short answer

Vanilla is the seed pod of an orchid, and it is one of the hardest crops on Earth to grow. Each flower opens for only one day and must be pollinated by hand. Then the green pod takes about nine months to ripen and several more months to dry and "cure" before it smells of vanilla at all.

So every bean has been touched by people many times over about a year. Most of the world's vanilla comes from one place, the island of Madagascar, so a single bad storm can send prices flying. That is why vanilla is often called the second most expensive spice, after saffron.

Surprising but true

Each vanilla flower opens for only a single day. If nobody pollinates it in those few hours, it wilts and no bean grows.

A flower with no bee

The vanilla orchid grows wild in the forests of Mexico. There, the Totonac people grew it long ago, and the Aztecs flavoured their chocolate drink with it. A local bee and perhaps hummingbirds pollinate it naturally.

When Europeans took vanilla vines to other tropical places, the vines grew happily but almost never made pods. The right bees weren't there. For about 300 years, Mexico was nearly the only place that could produce vanilla.

Who pollinates our food?Every vanilla pod in the world owes something to a 12-year-old boy on an island in the Indian Ocean in 1841.

The boy who solved it

In 1841, on the French island of Réunion in the Indian Ocean, an enslaved 12-year-old named Edmond Albius found a way. Using a thin stick and his thumb, he lifted a small flap inside the flower and pressed the male and female parts together. It took a few seconds.

His method spread to Madagascar, Indonesia, India and beyond, and it is still how almost all vanilla is pollinated today. Albius was freed when slavery ended on the island in 1848, but he never became rich from his discovery. Today there is a statue of him on Réunion.

Sweating and sunbathing beans

A fresh green vanilla pod has almost no smell. The flavour comes from curing. Farmers dip the pods in hot water, wrap them in blankets to "sweat", then lay them in the sun each day and wrap them up again each night. This goes on for weeks. Then they dry slowly for months.

During curing, the pods turn dark brown and wrinkly, and a chemical called vanillin forms inside. Good beans may even grow tiny white crystals of vanillin on their skin. One kilogram of cured beans needs about five to six kilograms of green pods.

Boom and bust

Because so much vanilla comes from one island, prices jump and crash. After a cyclone hit Madagascar in 2017 and harvests fell, cured beans sold for around 500 to 600 US dollars a kilogram, more than silver at the time. High prices tempted thieves, and some farmers branded their beans with codes, like dabba marks, so stolen ones could be traced. Since then, prices have fallen a long way, which is hard on the farmers too, a pattern explained by supply and demand.

India grows some vanilla too, mainly in Kerala and Karnataka. Farmers there rushed into it in the early 2000s when prices were high, then many gave up when prices fell.

Fake vanilla everywhere

Real beans could never meet the world's demand. So most "vanilla" flavour, in biscuits, cakes and ice cream, is vanillin made in factories. It can be made from substances in wood pulp, from clove oil or from chemicals made from oil. It tastes close, but real vanilla has hundreds of other flavour chemicals that the factory version lacks.

And here's the oddest twist: the "plain" flavour of ice cream is the one that took the most work to grow. It shares an ancient story with the Aztec drink that became chocolate.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The vanilla flavour in most ice creams and biscuits comes from vanilla beans.

Truth: Most of it is vanillin, the main flavour chemical in vanilla, made in factories from cheaper materials such as chemicals from wood or oil. Only a small share of the world's vanilla flavour comes from real beans.

Try this

Smell a vanilla biscuit or a little vanilla essence, then check the label for the word "vanillin" or "artificial flavour". Most cheap vanilla flavour is made in a factory. If you can, compare it with the smell of a real vanilla pod and see if you notice a difference.
