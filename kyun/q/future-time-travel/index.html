Is time travel possible?

Astronaut Sergei Krikalev spent 803 days in orbit and came home about a fiftieth of a second younger than if he had stayed on Earth.

The short answer

Travelling into the future is possible. In fact it happens all the time, just in tiny amounts. According to Einstein's theories of relativity, time passes more slowly for anything moving very fast, and for anything in strong gravity. Astronauts on the space station age very slightly slower than people on the ground.

Travelling into the past is a different story. No experiment has ever shown it, and it causes deep puzzles about cause and effect. A few equations of relativity seem to allow strange shortcuts through time, but most physicists suspect some unknown law forbids them.

Surprising but true

In 2009, Stephen Hawking threw a party for time travellers and sent the invitations only after it was over. Nobody came.

Clocks that disagree

In 1971, two scientists, Joseph Hafele and Richard Keating, took four atomic clocks on ordinary passenger flights around the world, first heading east, then west. When they compared the clocks with ones left behind in Washington, the times differed by billionths of a second, almost exactly as Einstein predicted.

You rely on this effect every time a phone map shows where you are. GPS satellites carry atomic clocks. They move fast, which slows their time, but they are farther from Earth's gravity, which speeds it up more. Overall, their clocks gain about 38 microseconds a day. Without correction, your position would be wrong by about 10 km by tomorrow.

The astronaut who jumped ahead

Russian cosmonaut Sergei Krikalev spent a total of 803 days in orbit. Because he was moving at about 28,000 km/h, his clock ran a little slow. By the time he retired, he had travelled about 0.02 seconds into the future compared with everyone on Earth.

That doesn't sound like much. But the faster you go, the bigger the effect. A spaceship moving at 99.5 percent of the speed of light would run about ten times slower than Earth. A year aboard would equal about ten years at home. Nobody knows how to build such a ship, but the physics is solid.

Gravity bends time too

Strong gravity slows time as well. Clocks at the top of a mountain tick slightly faster than clocks at sea level. The best atomic clocks can now detect a difference across a height of just a few centimetres.

Near a black hole, the effect becomes enormous. The film Interstellar showed a planet where one hour equalled seven years on Earth. The physicist Kip Thorne, who later won a Nobel Prize, advised the filmmakers so the idea would be based on real equations, though such a planet may not be able to exist.

Going backwards

Going into the past is where science gets slippery. Some solutions to Einstein's equations include wormholes, shortcuts through space and time. A wormhole could, in theory, connect two times as well as two places. But it would need a kind of "exotic matter" with negative energy that nobody has found in usable amounts.

Then there are the paradoxes. If you went back and stopped your grandparents from meeting, you would never be born, so you couldn't go back. Some physicists think the universe would simply prevent such loops. This is the kind of puzzle explored in paradoxes.

Hawking's empty party

Stephen Hawking, the famous British physicist, liked to test ideas with a sense of humour. On 28 June 2009 he held a party at the University of Cambridge, with balloons and champagne. Only after the date did he send out the invitations. If time travel into the past were possible, he reasoned, someone in the future would read them and drop in. He waited. No guests arrived.

Hawking thought this was a small piece of evidence that backward time travel isn't possible, though he admitted it wasn't a proper experiment. Forward travel is real, but backward travel remains in the land of films and books. Moving yourself across space instantly turns out to be almost as tricky, as you will find in teleportation.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Time passes at exactly the same rate for everyone, everywhere.

Truth: Einstein showed that time runs slower for things moving very fast and for things deep in strong gravity. The differences are tiny in everyday life but are measured every day by atomic clocks and GPS satellites.
