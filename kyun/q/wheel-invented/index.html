Who invented the wheel?

The wheel came surprisingly late. People were farming, making pots and building towns for thousands of years before anyone rolled a cart.

The short answer

Nobody knows who invented the wheel. There was no single inventor whose name was written down. What archaeologists do know is when wheels for carrying things started to appear: around 3500 BCE, about 5,500 years ago.

At almost the same moment, wheels show up in Mesopotamia, the land of the first cities in present-day Iraq, in the grasslands north of the Black Sea, and in central Europe. A clay pot found at Bronocice in Poland, made about 3500 BCE, has a drawing of what looks like a four-wheeled wagon. Some experts think the idea spread very quickly from one place. Others think it was worked out more than once.

Surprising but true

Peoples of ancient Mexico made little wheeled toys, but never used wheels for carts, probably because they had no horses or oxen to pull them.

Why it took so long

A log rolls on its own, so the wheel can seem obvious. The tricky part is the axle, the rod the wheel turns around. The axle has to be perfectly round and smooth. The hole in the wheel must fit snugly but loosely enough to spin. Both must be strong enough to carry a heavy load over bumpy ground.

Making that fit needed good tools, and people needed animals to pull carts. Cattle had been tamed thousands of years earlier, but people still had to learn to yoke them to heavy loads. Copper tools, which made fine woodwork easier, were spreading at about the same time. Once all the pieces were ready, the wheel seems to have spread fast.

What was Mesopotamia?Every time you check a clock, you use an idea from Mesopotamia, where people counted in sixties more than 4,000 years ago.

The oldest wheel ever found

In 2002, archaeologists working in the Ljubljana Marshes in Slovenia found a wooden wheel preserved in the wet ground. It is about 5,000 years old and as wide as a child's arm span, made from ash planks. The axle found with it was made from oak. Waterlogged mud kept out the bacteria that would normally rot wood away.

Early wheels were solid discs, heavy and slow. Around 2000 BCE, people near the Ural Mountains began building light wheels with spokes for fast war chariots. Spoked wheels soon spread across Asia and Europe. A spoked wheel is far lighter than a solid one of the same size, so a pair of horses could pull a chariot at a gallop. Iron rims came later, to stop the wooden edges wearing away on stony tracks.

Wheels in ancient India

At Harappa, Mohenjo-daro and other Indus cities, archaeologists have dug up small clay models of bullock carts, along with tiny clay wheels. They show that people in the Indus Valley were using carts by around 2500 BCE. Carts very much like them still roll along Indian village roads, pulled by bullocks at a walking pace.

Wheels also turned up in art and faith. The Sun Temple at Konark in Odisha, built in the 1200s, is designed as the chariot of the Sun god, with 24 huge stone wheels carved into its sides. India's national flag carries the Ashoka Chakra, a wheel with 24 spokes.

More than carts

The potter's wheel may be even older than the cart wheel, though it turned flat, like a spinning table. Later came water wheels for grinding grain, the spinning wheel for thread, and gears inside clocks.

Wheels did not suit everyone. In the deserts of the Middle East, camels replaced carts for many centuries, because they needed no roads. Yet today wheels are almost everywhere, from your school bus to the spinning parts inside a hard disk. Each one depends on the same old trick: cutting down friction by rolling instead of dragging.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The wheel was one of the first things humans invented, back in the Stone Age.

Truth: Farming, pottery, woven cloth and even boats all came first. Wheeled vehicles appear only about 5,500 years ago, long after people had settled in villages.

Try this

Test the wheel's big idea. Push a heavy book across a table and feel how hard it is. Then lay four round pencils under it and push again. Finally, tape two pencils as axles under a shoebox lid, slide on cotton reels or bottle caps with holes as wheels, and see how far it rolls with one gentle push.
