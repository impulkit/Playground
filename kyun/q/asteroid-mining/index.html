Could we mine asteroids?

Japan and the USA have already brought home small scoops of asteroid. The hard part is turning a few grams into a mine.

The short answer

In principle, yes. Asteroids are leftover building blocks of the solar system, and many contain useful materials. Some are rich in iron and nickel, with small amounts of precious metals like platinum. Others hold water locked in clay minerals or as ice.

Space agencies have already visited asteroids and brought back samples. Japan's Hayabusa2 returned about 5 grams from asteroid Ryugu in 2020. NASA's OSIRIS-REx brought back about 120 grams from asteroid Bennu in 2023. But those missions cost hundreds of millions of dollars to collect a few spoonfuls.

Turning that into real mining would need cheaper rockets, robots that work on their own and a way to make money from what they dig. Nobody has done it yet.

Surprising but true

For the first space miners, water could be worth more than gold, because it can be turned into rocket fuel far from Earth.

What is up there

Most asteroids orbit the Sun between Mars and Jupiter, in the asteroid belt. Some, called near-Earth asteroids, come closer to us and are easier to reach.

Asteroids come in different types. Dark, carbon-rich ones like Ryugu and Bennu hold water and the chemicals of life. Stony ones are like the rocks of Earth's crust. A few are mostly metal. The best-known metal-rich asteroid is 16 Psyche, about 280 km across at its widest. NASA launched a spacecraft called Psyche in October 2023 to study it. It should arrive in 2029.

Many meteorites that fall on Earth are broken bits of asteroids, which is how scientists first learned what asteroids are made of.

Water beats gold

The first useful thing to mine in space may be water, not metal. Launching water from Earth is extremely expensive because it is heavy. Water can be drunk, split into oxygen to breathe, and turned into hydrogen and oxygen rocket fuel.

A fuel station made from asteroid water could refill spacecraft far from Earth, the way a petrol pump on a highway saves a truck from carrying all its fuel from the start. Metals could be used to build things in space instead of being shipped down to Earth.

How would it work?

Mining in near-zero gravity is strange. A small asteroid's gravity is so weak that a robot could bounce off with one hard push. When OSIRIS-REx touched Bennu in 2020, its sampling arm sank in as if the surface were a ball pit. The asteroid turned out to be a loose pile of rubble, not solid rock.

Ideas for future mining include wrapping a small asteroid in a bag, heating rocks to drive water out as steam, or using magnets to collect metal grains. Cheaper reusable rockets make these plans seem less far-fetched than they did 20 years ago, but none has been tested on a real asteroid.

In the 2010s, two American start-ups promised to mine asteroids. Both ran short of money and were taken over by other companies before they ever launched a mining mission.

Who owns an asteroid?

The Outer Space Treaty, agreed through the United Nations in 1967, says no country can claim the Moon, planets or asteroids as its own territory. It doesn't say clearly whether a company can own material it digs up.

In 2015, the USA passed a law saying its citizens can own resources they collect in space. Luxembourg passed a similar law in 2017. Some other countries argue that space resources should be shared by all of humanity. The debate is still open, and so is the question of whether asteroid mining will ever be more than an exciting idea.

Myth or fact?

Myth: One metal asteroid is worth more than the whole world's economy, so mining it would make everyone rich.

Truth: Those huge price tags come from multiplying the asteroid's mass by today's metal prices. Nobody can bring an asteroid to Earth, and if anyone did, metal prices would crash. The numbers make headlines but don't describe a real business.

Try this

Mix some small metal paperclips into a bowl of dry rice or sand. Try to "mine" them out using only a fridge magnet wrapped in a plastic bag, without touching the rice. Think about how much harder this would be with no gravity and a robot doing it millions of kilometres away.
