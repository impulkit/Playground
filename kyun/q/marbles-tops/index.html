Why have children played marbles for thousands of years?

The glass ball sealing a bottle of goli soda is a marble, and the drink is named after it.

The short answer

Marbles have lasted because they are cheap, small and endlessly flexible. A pocket of little balls and a patch of flat ground are enough for dozens of games. You can flick them into a hole, knock other marbles out of a ring, trade them or simply admire the swirls of colour inside.

Children in many ancient societies played with small round stones, clay balls or nuts. Spinning tops are almost as old. Both toys use simple physics that feels like magic: a flick that sends a marble rolling true, a twist that makes a top stand on its point. Each generation teaches the next, often without any adult involved.

Surprising but true

In ancient Rome, children played with nuts instead of marbles, and the Latin saying "to leave the nuts behind" meant to grow up.

Balls in the dust

Small clay and stone balls have been found at many ancient sites, including the Indus cities of Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, more than 4,000 years old. Archaeologists can't always be sure what they were for, but many were probably used in games.

In ancient Rome, children played similar games with nuts, rolling them at targets or into holes. When Romans said someone had "left the nuts behind", they meant that person had grown up. It's one of the oldest clues that toys like these marked childhood.

Glass takes over

For centuries, marbles were made from clay, stone or agate. In the 1800s, German glassmakers learned to make glass marbles in bulk, twisting coloured glass inside clear balls. In the early 1900s, factories in the USA built machines that could turn out millions.

Making a glass marble starts like any glass: sand, soda and lime melted together. A blob of molten glass, often streaked with colour, is cut and rolled between turning rollers until it cools into a perfect sphere.

How is glass made from sand?Glass is sand that has been melted and then cooled so quickly that its atoms never get the chance to line up neatly again.

Goli, kancha and the bottle

In India, marbles are called kanche, goli or other local names, and games are played in lanes and school grounds everywhere. One common game has players flicking their marble with a bent finger at others scattered on the ground, winning any they hit.

In the USA, a classic game is ringer. Thirteen marbles are set out in a cross inside a ring about 3 metres across, and players kneel at the edge and shoot to knock them out. The English phrase "playing for keeps" comes from marble games where winners kept the marbles they won. Many children carry one favourite, heavier marble, the shooter, which they never risk.

A glass marble also seals the famous goli soda bottle. In 1872 an English inventor, Hiram Codd, designed a bottle where the fizz pushes a marble up against a rubber ring, closing the neck. You press the marble down to open it. Codd bottles vanished from most countries, but they're still filled and sold in parts of India and Japan.

Tops that spin

Spinning tops have been made from clay, wood, bone and plastic for thousands of years. In India, wooden tops called lattu in Hindi and bambaram in Tamil are wound with a string and thrown so they land spinning. Karnataka's Channapatna toys include brightly lacquered tops.

A spinning top stays up because of angular momentum: a spinning object resists tipping. Friction from the floor and air slowly steals its spin. Only a tiny point touches the ground, which keeps that friction low. Tops with more weight near the rim tend to spin longer, for the same reason a heavy potter's wheel keeps turning after a push. In the 2000s, Japanese battle tops called Beyblades made the old toy a global craze. In the USA, a National Marbles Tournament for young players has been held almost every year since 1922. The toys change shape. The urge to flick and spin does not.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Marbles are made of marble stone.

Truth: Some early ones were, which gave them the name. Today almost all are glass, and many older ones were clay, stone or even agate.

Try this

Make a top from a cardboard circle about the size of a biscuit with a short pencil pushed through the middle. Spin it on a table and time how long it lasts. Then tape two coins near the edge of the card and try again. Which spins longer, and why might the extra weight at the rim help?
