Why did ISRO carry a rocket on a bicycle?

India's space age began in 1963 in a fishing village in Kerala, with a church for a workshop and rocket parts wheeled to the launch pad on bicycles.

The short answer

In the early 1960s, India's space programme was tiny and short of money. Its first launch site was a strip of sandy coast at Thumba, near Thiruvananthapuram in Kerala. There were no big buildings and few vehicles. The scientists worked out of a church and the bishop's house next door.

Many of the early rocket parts were small and light, so the team carried them around on bicycles. A famous black-and-white photo shows part of a rocket strapped to the back of a bicycle near the launch pad. It became a symbol of how India's space journey began, with very little except clever people.

On 21 November 1963, the first rocket rose from Thumba: a small American Nike-Apache, used to study the upper atmosphere.

Surprising but true

The church where India's first rocket parts were put together is now a space museum.

Why Thumba?

The site was chosen for a scientific reason. Thumba lies very close to Earth's magnetic equator, an invisible line where the planet's magnetic field runs flat. Over this line, a river of electric current flows high in the atmosphere, called the equatorial electrojet. Scientists around the world wanted to study it with rockets.

The physicist Vikram Sarabhai picked the spot. The land belonged to a fishing community, with a church dedicated to St Mary Magdalene in the middle. The local bishop agreed to give the church for science, and the villagers agreed to move to a new village with a new church nearby.

A church, a cattle shed and a bishop's house

The church became the workshop where rockets were put together. The bishop's house became the office. Cattle sheds were used as stores and laboratories. Scientists sometimes cycled to the nearest town for meals.

The rockets arrived from abroad in parts. NASA supplied the first ones, and France and the Soviet Union helped too. A young engineer named A. P. J. Abdul Kalam was part of the team, later writing about these days in his book Wings of Fire.

Thumba was also offered to the whole world. In 1968 it was dedicated to the United Nations, and scientists from many countries flew their own experiments from the Kerala coast. The rockets were sounding rockets. They did not go into orbit. They climbed straight up, sometimes more than 150 km, measured the air and fell into the sea a few minutes later.

From borrowed rockets to Indian ones

Within four years, India built its own. On 20 November 1967, the RH-75, an Indian-made sounding rocket only about 75 mm wide, flew from Thumba. RH stood for Rohini. Over the next decades, the Rohini family grew bigger and more powerful.

Thumba grew into the Vikram Sarabhai Space Centre, one of ISRO's largest. The old church still stands inside it. Today it is a space museum, full of rocket models and old equipment, and you can visit it.

The bullock cart satellite

There is a second famous photo. In 1981, ISRO built an experimental communications satellite called APPLE. Engineers wanted to test its antenna in an open field. A metal truck might have disturbed the radio signals, so they put the satellite on a wooden bullock cart instead.

The picture of a satellite riding behind two bullocks went round the world. Some saw it as a sign of how poor India was. Many Indians saw something else: smart engineers solving a problem cheaply.

Those humble beginnings did not last. Within a few decades, ISRO went on to send missions to the Moon, Mars and the Sun. And India's very first satellite, launched in 1975, was built in tin sheds in Bengaluru, with a data room squeezed into a converted toilet.

Myth or fact?

Myth: ISRO used bicycles and bullock carts because it had no vehicles at all.

Truth: Money was tight, but the choices were also practical. Early rocket parts were light, roads near the launch site were narrow, and in 1981 a wooden cart was picked because metal could spoil an antenna test.

Hold a fridge magnet over a paper clip, then slide sheets of paper, a wooden ruler and a steel spoon between them one at a time. Which ones stop the magnet's pull? Metal can get in the way of magnetic and radio effects, which is why ISRO once used a wooden cart.
