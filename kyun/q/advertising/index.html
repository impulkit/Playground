How do adverts try to persuade you?

The first official TV advert in the USA, in 1941, showed a watch ticking over a map of the country for about ten seconds. It cost the company $9.

The short answer

An advert is a message paid for by someone who wants you to buy something, use a service or believe an idea. Its job is to persuade, not just to inform.

Adverts use a set of well-tested tricks. They repeat a brand name until it feels familiar. They link a product with happy feelings, music, cute animals or famous faces. They create urgency with "limited offer" or "only three left". They show "everyone" using something, so you feel left out if you don't. Knowing these tricks doesn't make adverts stop working, but it helps you think before you buy.

Surprising but true

People who say adverts don't work on them are often influenced anyway. Researchers call our habit of thinking others are more easily persuaded than we are the "third-person effect".

From painted walls to TV

Advertising is old. Shop signs and painted notices were common in ancient Roman towns. After the printing press spread in Europe, printed notices and newspaper ads followed.

On 1 July 1941, a New York TV station showed what is usually called the first official television commercial in the USA. It was for Bulova watches: a clock over a map of America, lasting about ten seconds. In India, adverts appeared on Doordarshan in the 1970s. By the 1980s, jingles for soaps, cold drinks and toothpastes were being hummed by children all over the country.

How did farmers build Amul?In 1946, angry dairy farmers in Gujarat refused to sell their milk for two weeks. Out of that strike grew one of India's best-known brands.

A little girl in polka dots

Since 1966, the Amul girl has appeared on Indian billboards, making jokes about the week's news. She is a lesson in how a simple character, seen again and again, can make a brand feel like an old friend. Familiarity is one of advertising's strongest tools. People tend to trust names they have seen many times.

Most of us believe we see through all this. In 1983, the sociologist W. Phillips Davison described what he called the "third-person effect": people tend to think adverts and media messages sway others more than themselves. Studies since then have often found that people are influenced more than they admit.

Colours are chosen carefully too. Many fast-food chains use red and yellow, which are bold and easy to spot, and many banks use blue, a colour often linked with calm and trust.

Ads that don't look like ads

Today, many adverts don't look like adverts. A video-maker might use a product in a video because a company paid them. A brand might pay to place its drink in a film. On social media, apps use what they know about you to choose which ads you see.

Rules try to keep this honest. In India, the Advertising Standards Council of India, a watchdog set up by the industry in 1985, says that since 2021, influencers must clearly label paid posts. The US Federal Trade Commission has similar rules. Ads aimed at children face extra limits in many countries, because younger children often can't tell an advert from a programme.

Reading an advert like a detective

When an advert makes a claim, ask who paid for it and what they want. Look for the small print. Words like "up to 50% off" or "helps fight germs" can promise less than they seem.

Numbers can be slippery too. "Nine out of ten people prefer it" may come from a tiny or carefully chosen group. Learning how statistics can mislead is one of the best defences. Adverts also pay for much of what you enjoy for free, from television channels to video apps, which is why they are everywhere.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Adverts only work on people who aren't paying attention.

Truth: Many adverts are designed to work even when you notice them, through repetition, feelings and familiar faces. Simply seeing a brand many times can make people like it more, even if they don't remember the ads.

Try this

Watch three adverts on TV or a video app with an adult. For each one, note the trick it uses, such as a celebrity, a catchy song, a cute animal, a "today only" offer or a big claim with numbers. Then decide whether the advert told you anything useful about the product itself.
