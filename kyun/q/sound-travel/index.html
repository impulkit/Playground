How does sound get from a drum to your ear?

Sound is invisible. But it's really air being pushed and pulled.

The short answer

Sound travels about 343 metres every second in air, much slower than light.

When a drum is hit, its skin wobbles back and forth. Each wobble squeezes the air next to it, then lets it spread out again. Those squeezes pass from one bit of air to the next, like a push moving through a queue of people, until they reach your ear. The air itself doesn't travel from the drum to you. Only the push does.

In water it goes over four times faster, because water molecules are packed closer together.

That's why you see lightning before you hear thunder.

Surprising but true

In space, nobody can hear you shout. There's no air to carry the sound.

High and low, loud and soft

The number of wobbles each second is called the frequency. A fast wobble gives a high note, like a whistle. A slow wobble gives a low note, like a big dhol. People can hear from about 20 to 20,000 wobbles a second. A sitar's thin strings shake faster than its thick ones, so they sound higher.

How hard the air is squeezed decides loudness. Loudness is measured in decibels. Quiet breathing is about 10 decibels, and a jet engine close up is well over 120, loud enough to hurt your ears.

Counting the thunder

Light from lightning reaches you almost instantly, but its thunder plods along at sound speed. Count the seconds between the flash and the rumble, then divide by three. That's roughly how many kilometres away the storm is.

Sound is faster in water and fastest in solids. Whales can hear each other's calls across hundreds of kilometres of ocean. Whale songs travel especially far through a layer of water where sound bounces along without spreading out much.

Why do humpback whales sing?Humpback whale songs spread from one ocean to the next, like a hit song moving around the world.

Whispering walls

In Vijayapura, in Karnataka, stands the Gol Gumbaz, a tomb built in the 1600s with one of the biggest domes in the world. Whisper against the wall of the gallery running round inside it, and someone on the far side can hear you clearly. The curved wall guides the sound round the edge instead of letting it spread out.

The US Capitol in Washington has a similar effect in its old hall of the House of Representatives, now called Statuary Hall. Tour guides love to show it off to visitors. In both buildings, the trick comes from the shape of the curved walls and ceiling.

Breaking the sound barrier

In 1947, the American pilot Chuck Yeager flew the rocket plane Bell X-1 faster than the speed of sound. A plane that fast outruns its own noise. The squashed air piles up into a shock wave, heard on the ground as a sudden double bang called a sonic boom.

A crack of a whip is a tiny sonic boom too. The tip of the whip moves faster than sound.

Echoes and bats

When sound hits a hard surface, it bounces back as an echo. Shout towards a cliff that's 170 metres away and the echo returns in about one second. Bats use echoes to hunt in the dark. They send out high squeaks and listen for the echoes, building a sound picture of the moths around them.

Silent space

In space, there's no air at all, so there's nothing to squeeze. Astronauts on a spacewalk talk by radio, which uses waves that don't need air. If one tapped the other's helmet with a tool, though, the sound would travel through their touching suits, because solids carry sound too.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Explosions in space make a huge boom, like in the movies.

Truth: Space is almost empty, with nothing to carry the squashes and stretches of a sound wave. An explosion out there would be completely silent to anyone floating nearby. Film-makers add the boom because silence feels strange to audiences.

Try this

Lay a long wooden table or bench bare and ask a friend to tap gently on one end with a pencil while you stand at the other. Listen. Then press your ear flat against the table and ask them to tap just as gently again. The sound is much louder through the wood than through the air.
