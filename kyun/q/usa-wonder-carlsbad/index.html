What is inside Carlsbad Caverns?

Every summer evening, hundreds of thousands of bats pour out of a cave mouth in New Mexico like a spiralling cloud of smoke.

The short answer

Carlsbad Caverns is a national park in the Guadalupe Mountains of New Mexico, in the southwestern USA. Beneath its dry, cactus-covered hills lie about 120 known caves. The most famous is Carlsbad Cavern itself, which holds the Big Room, about 3.3 hectares in size. It is the largest cave chamber in North America that visitors can easily reach.

Inside are stone icicles, giant columns and delicate formations like soda straws. Every summer evening, hundreds of thousands of bats fly out of the entrance to hunt. The caves were carved in an unusual way, by acid, inside the remains of a reef that grew in a sea about 265 million years ago.

Surprising but true

Carlsbad's caves were not dug out mainly by flowing water. They were dissolved by acid that formed when gas from deep oil deposits rose and mixed with groundwater.

A reef in the desert

Hard to believe, but this desert was once underwater. About 265 million years ago, a shallow inland sea covered the region. Along its edge, sponges, algae and shellfish built a reef hundreds of kilometres long, much as corals build reefs today.

The sea dried up and the reef was buried. Much later, the land was pushed upwards, and the old reef became the Guadalupe Mountains. Fossils of ancient sea creatures can still be spotted in the cave walls, as the story of how fossils form explains.

Carved by acid

Most limestone caves are made by rainwater, which is slightly acidic and slowly eats away rock. Carlsbad is different. Deep below, there are large deposits of oil and gas. Gas called hydrogen sulphide, which smells like rotten eggs, seeped upwards from them.

When that gas met oxygen-rich groundwater, it formed sulphuric acid, a strong acid. The acid dissolved huge spaces in the limestone, mostly between about 4 and 6 million years ago. The oil far underground, in other words, helped hollow out the caves.

The Big Room

Visitors can walk down through the natural entrance, a steep trail dropping about 230 m, or ride an elevator. The elevator descends about 230 m (750 feet), as deep as a 75-storey building is tall.

The Big Room path is about 2 km long. Along it are formations with names like the Rock of Ages and the Bottomless Pit, which actually has a bottom about 40 m down. The stone icicles hanging from the ceiling are stalactites. The ones growing up from the floor are stalagmites. When the two meet, they form a column.

The bat cloud

About 400,000 Brazilian free-tailed bats spend summers in one part of the cavern. At sunset, they spiral out of the entrance and head off to hunt moths and other insects. They use echolocation, clicking and listening for echoes, to catch prey in the dark.

The park has seats near the entrance for watching the bat flight. Phones and cameras are not allowed during it, because noise and light can confuse the bats.

A teenage cowboy explorer

Local people had long known the cave was there, and Native Americans left pictures near its entrance. Around 1898, a teenager named Jim White saw what looked like smoke rising from the ground. It was bats. He explored the cave with a homemade wire ladder and a kerosene lamp.

White later guided visitors and told everyone about its wonders. Miners dug out bat droppings, called guano, to sell as fertiliser. In 1923 the cave became a national monument, and in 1930 a national park.

The secret cave next door

In 1986, cavers digging in a nearby pit broke through into Lechuguilla Cave. It has since been mapped for more than 225 km and goes 489 m deep. Inside are gypsum crystals several metres long and pools of perfectly clear water. Microbes living in it, cut off for millions of years, turned out to resist many antibiotics. Lechuguilla is closed to tourists to protect it. How such underground worlds begin is told in how caves form.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Stalactites and stalagmites are made of ice.

Truth: They are made of stone, mostly a mineral called calcite. Each drip of water leaves a thin film of mineral behind, and over thousands of years the films build up into stone icicles and columns.

Try this

Grow your own stalactite. Dissolve lots of Epsom salt or washing soda in two jars of warm water. Set the jars apart with a plate between them, and drape a woolly string from one jar to the other with its middle sagging over the plate. Over a week, crystals drip and grow from the string.
