How do oysters make pearls?

A pearl is an oyster's way of dealing with an itch, wrapped in thousands of shiny layers over several years.

The short answer

When something unwanted gets inside an oyster's shell and can't be pushed out, the oyster covers it up. Special tissue called the mantle, the same tissue that builds the shell, wraps the intruder in a pouch. Then it coats it with thin layers of nacre, a hard, shiny material made of a mineral called calcium carbonate held together with protein.

Layer after layer builds up, thousands of them, each far thinner than a human hair. Over months and years, the lump grows into a smooth, gleaming pearl. Oysters are the best known, but freshwater mussels and some other shellfish can make pearls too.

Surprising but true

Pearls are made of almost the same mineral as chalk and limestone. A drop of vinegar can damage one.

Why pearls shine

A pearl's glow, called lustre, comes from its layers. Light passes into the outer layers, bounces off the ones beneath, and comes back out. The reflections overlap and create soft rainbow colours, the same shimmer you see inside many seashells.

That is also why pearls need care. Nacre is close to the mineral in chalk, so acids such as vinegar or lemon juice can dull it, and hard grit can scratch it. A real pearl rubbed gently against the front teeth often feels slightly gritty, while a plastic copy feels smooth.

The pearl coasts of India

For more than two thousand years, the Gulf of Mannar, between southern India and Sri Lanka, was one of the world's great pearl fisheries. Divers held their breath and sank to the sea bed with a stone for weight, filling baskets with oysters before shooting back up.

Greek and Roman writers mentioned the pearls of southern India. In the late 1200s, the traveller Marco Polo described pearl divers off this coast on his way home from China. The port of Thoothukudi is still nicknamed the Pearl City. Far inland, Hyderabad grew into a famous centre for drilling, sorting and selling pearls, a trade that continues in its old bazaars.

How people learned to grow pearls

Natural pearls are rare. Divers might open hundreds of oysters to find one good pearl. In the 1890s, a Japanese noodle-seller's son named Kokichi Mikimoto began farming oysters and placing small beads inside them by hand. His first success, in 1893, was a half-round pearl. Over the next few decades, Japanese researchers worked out how to grow fully round pearls.

Today, a pearl farmer slips a polished bead and a tiny square of mantle tissue from another oyster inside each shell. The oyster coats the bead with nacre, just as it would an intruder. After one to three years or more, the pearl is harvested. Almost all pearls sold today are cultured like this, mostly from China, Japan, Indonesia, Australia and French Polynesia.

A pearl in every colour

Pearls are not all white. Depending on the kind of oyster and the water it lives in, they can be cream, gold, pink, silver, grey or nearly black. The large dark pearls of Tahiti come from black-lipped oysters.

Unlike gems such as diamonds, which are dug out of rock and cut into shape, a pearl comes out of the shell ready to wear. People in Japan, India and many other places have treasured that natural finish for centuries. Diving for pearls was once a way of life from the Persian Gulf to the coasts of Australia. In Bahrain, an old pearling trail through the town of Muharraq is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pearls form when a grain of sand gets stuck inside an oyster.

Truth: Sand rarely causes pearls. Most natural pearls begin with a parasite or a bit of misplaced tissue. Cultured pearls start with a bead and a small piece of tissue placed inside the oyster by a person.

Try this

Find an empty mussel or oyster shell from a beach or a fish market and wash it well. Tilt the inside under a lamp and watch the rainbow shimmer move. That shiny lining is nacre, the same material as a pearl, and the colours come from light bouncing between its thin layers.
