How did chai become India's favourite drink?

A hundred and fifty years ago most Indians had never tasted tea. Then a British company set out to change that, and Indians changed the tea.

The short answer

Chai feels ancient, but it is quite new. In the 1800s, Britain wanted tea it could grow itself instead of buying from China. It set up huge plantations in Assam and the hills of Bengal, mostly to sell tea in Britain. Most Indians did not drink it.

In the early 1900s, the tea companies tried to win Indian drinkers too. They gave away cups and set up stalls at stations. Indians took to it, but on their own terms. They boiled the leaves with milk, lots of sugar and spices such as ginger and cardamom. That strong, sweet mix became chai, and today India drinks more tea than almost any country on Earth.

Surprising but true

Tea companies once sent teams to Indian railway stations and factories to hand out free cups and show people how to brew it.

A plant hiding in Assam

For centuries China was the only big supplier of tea to the world, and Britain was hooked. It paid huge sums in silver, then sold opium into China to win the silver back. That ugly trade led to war.

In the 1820s, British officials learned that a tea plant was already growing wild in Assam. The Singpho people there had been brewing it for a long time. The East India Company first tried Chinese plants, then switched to the tougher Assam variety. By the late 1800s, India was sending more tea to Britain than China was.

What were the Opium Wars?Britain wanted Chinese tea, China wanted only silver, and a drug grown in India filled the gap. The result was two wars that China still remembers.

Selling tea to India

By 1900, the plantations grew more tea than Britain could drink. So the Indian Tea Association, a group of mostly British planters, turned to India itself. Its teams handed out free tea in cities, at fairs and in mills. Tea stalls popped up on platforms as the railways spread, and passengers bought a cup at every stop.

The companies wanted people to brew tea lightly, with a splash of milk. Many Indians ignored this. Street sellers known as chaiwalas boiled cheap tea dust with milk, sugar and spices in one pot. A little tea went a long way that way.

The machine that changed the cup

In the 1930s, a planter named William McKercher came up with the CTC method. CTC stands for crush, tear, curl. Leaves pass between metal rollers and come out as hard little grains instead of long leaves.

CTC tea is strong, fast and cheap, and it stands up to boiling in milk. It suited chai perfectly. Today most tea sold in India is CTC. Long, loose leaves like Darjeeling are mostly sold abroad or sipped by tea lovers.

Cha, chai and tea

The word itself has a story. In parts of China, tea is called "cha". Places that got tea over land, along trade routes through Central Asia and Persia, kept a word like it: chai in Hindi, Persian and Russian. Places that got tea by sea from Dutch traders, who bought it in a port where people said "te", ended up with "tea", "thé" or "tee".

So when a café in the United States sells a "chai tea", it is really selling tea tea. In the US, "chai" usually means the spiced, milky drink, not plain tea.

A glass on every corner

India is the world's second-largest tea grower after China, producing about 1.4 billion kilograms in 2023. Most of it is drunk inside India. A small glass of cutting chai, half a full cup, can cost less than a bus ticket.

Every region has its own spin. Kashmir drinks pink noon chai with salt. Hyderabad loves Irani chai with cream. In Kolkata, chai still comes in little clay cups called kulhads, which are thrown away after one use and crumble back into soil. The spices in that cup carry a story of their own, and cardamom was once so valued that traders crossed oceans to get it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Indians have been drinking chai for thousands of years.

Truth: People in parts of Assam drank tea long ago, and some ayurvedic drinks used spices. But milky, sweet chai as a drink for almost everyone only spread in the 20th century, after tea companies pushed it hard.

Try this

With an adult in charge of the stove, make two small cups of tea. Add only milk and sugar to the first. Add a crushed cardamom pod and a thin slice of ginger to the second before it boils. Taste both side by side and decide which spice you noticed first.
