What is khadi?

A simple spinning wheel became one of the most powerful symbols of India's fight for freedom.

The short answer

Khadi is cloth that is both spun into thread by hand and woven by hand. It's usually made from cotton, though silk and wool khadi exist too. Because every thread is spun on a wheel and woven on a handloom, no two lengths are exactly alike.

In the early 1900s, khadi became much more than cloth. Mahatma Gandhi asked Indians to spin their own thread and wear khadi instead of machine-made cloth from British mills. Spinning became a daily act of protest that anyone could join, rich or poor, young or old. The charkha, or spinning wheel, became a symbol of India's struggle for freedom.

Surprising but true

When Gandhi decided India should spin its own thread, he had trouble even finding a spinning wheel. A friend finally tracked one down in a village in Gujarat in 1917.

Why cloth mattered so much

For centuries India had been famous for its cotton, as you can read in How did Indian cloth amaze the world?. Under British rule, raw cotton was shipped to factories in Britain, turned into cloth by machines, and sold back to India. Millions of Indian spinners and weavers lost their work.

Gandhi saw this as a trap. Every time an Indian bought foreign cloth, money left India and village workers stayed poor. If Indians made their own cloth, he argued, villages would earn again and the country would depend less on Britain. This idea of buying Indian goods was called swadeshi, "of one's own country".

Hunting for a spinning wheel

Hand spinning had faded so much that when Gandhi wanted to start, he could not find a charkha or anyone to teach him. In his autobiography he tells how a friend, Gangaben Majmudar, searched for one. In 1917 she finally found spinning wheels in Vijapur, a village in Gujarat, stored away in people's lofts.

Soon spinning was being taught at Gandhi's ashram in Ahmedabad. Gandhi himself spun every day, even in prison and on long journeys.

What was the Salt March?In 1930, a 60-year-old man walked 385 km to the sea, bent down and picked up a lump of salty mud. It shook the British Empire.

A uniform for freedom

From about 1920, khadi became the uniform of the freedom movement. Leaders, students and volunteers wore white khadi kurtas, dhotis and saris, and the khadi cap became a familiar sight. During the protests of the early 1920s, people made huge bonfires of foreign cloth in cities like Bombay.

Wearing khadi was a quiet but visible statement. A person in khadi was saying, without any words, that they supported the freedom struggle. The Congress party's flag of 1931 even placed a spinning wheel at its centre.

Spinning and weaving

Making khadi starts with cleaning and fluffing raw cotton. The spinner draws the fibres out while the charkha twists them into thread, winding it onto a spindle. Gandhi later used a small folding box charkha that could be carried around like a briefcase.

The hand-spun thread is then woven on a handloom, much like the looms used for sarees. Hand-spun thread is slightly uneven, which gives khadi its texture. The loose weave lets air through, so khadi feels cool in summer and, in thicker weaves, warm in winter.

Khadi today

After independence, the government set up the Khadi and Village Industries Commission to support khadi spinners and weavers, many of them women in villages. Khadi shops, called Khadi Bhandars, still sell cloth, clothes, soap and honey made by village workers.

For decades, the official rules said India's national flag should be made of hand-spun khadi. The rules were changed in 2021 to also allow machine-made cloth, a decision some khadi workers were unhappy about. Today many fashion designers use khadi for modern clothes, and on Gandhi Jayanti every 2 October, khadi shops often hold big sales.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Khadi is just plain, rough, old-fashioned cloth.

Truth: Khadi can be coarse, but it can also be very fine, and it comes in many colours and weaves. Designers use it in modern clothes, and its loose weave lets air through, so it stays cool in summer.

Try this

Take a ball of cotton wool from a first-aid box. Pinch a little and pull it out gently while twisting it between your fingers. Keep pulling and twisting, and you'll see the loose fibres turn into a thin thread. This is the basic idea of spinning, the first step in making khadi.
