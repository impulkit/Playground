What are Cheriyal scrolls?

Long before cinema reached Telangana's villages, storytellers unrolled painted scrolls as long as four autorickshaws and sang the pictures to life at night.

The short answer

Cheriyal scrolls are long, painted cloth scrolls from Cheriyal, a town in Telangana about 90 kilometres from Hyderabad. Each scroll is about a metre wide and can stretch 12 to 14 metres, about as long as a big school bus. It is split into about 50 scenes, one above the other, divided by flowery borders.

The scrolls were never meant to sit quietly in a museum. Travelling storytellers hung them up in village squares and sang the story while a helper pointed to each scene. The bright red backgrounds and big-eyed figures made the pictures easy to see by lamplight. Today the art survives mainly through a few families of painters in Cheriyal.

Surprising but true

A single Cheriyal scroll can hold about 50 painted scenes, one after another, like a comic strip as tall as a four-storey building.

Picture by picture, like a comic

Looked at from top to bottom, a scroll works much like a comic. Each panel shows one moment: a hero sets out, a demon attacks, a wedding takes place. Faces are drawn in profile with large eyes. Men, women, gods and animals are painted flat and bold, with no shadows.

The background is almost always red, with blues, yellows and greens on top. People living in the villages knew the stories already, so the pictures acted as a reminder. They followed the scenes, laughing and cheering at their favourite parts.

Painting a scroll the old way

The painters, called Nakashis, start with khadi cotton cloth. They coat it several times with a paste of rice starch, white clay, boiled tamarind seeds and gum. Each coat dries first. Then they sketch the outlines straight onto the cloth.

Traditionally, every colour came from nature. White came from crushed seashells, black from lamp soot and yellow from turmeric. Brushes were made from squirrel hair tied to sticks. Many painters now buy ready-made paints, but the method of building up layers is the same. One scroll can take weeks or months. Many scrolls open with a panel of Ganesha, painted to bless the story before it begins, often followed by Saraswati, the goddess of learning. Then the community's own heroes take over, sometimes for dozens of scenes.

The singers who carried the scrolls

The paintings belong to the storytellers as much as to the painters. A community of balladeers called the Kaki Padagollu travelled with them. A team of about five would arrive in a village, tie the scroll to a frame of poles and perform with a harmonium, a drum and small clappers.

Different communities had their own stories, often about their own ancestors. Toddy tappers, leather workers and weavers each had a favourite tale. Mixed in were scenes from the Ramayana, the Mahabharata and tales of Krishna. A full performance could last several nights.

Cousins across India

India has other painted scrolls with storytellers too. In Rajasthan, priests sing the tale of the folk hero Pabuji in front of a Phad painting. In Bengal, patuas sing as they unroll their scrolls. Each tradition looks different, but the idea is the same: a long picture and a singing voice.

Saving a fading art

As television and cinema spread, fewer villages invited the storytellers, and long scrolls became rare. To survive, Cheriyal painters began making smaller panels, single scenes and even key chains and masks for tourists. Their light masks, moulded from sawdust and tamarind seed paste, sell well too.

In 2008, Cheriyal paintings got a GI tag, a legal label that protects the name for art made in the region. And the strangest part? The way a scroll is read, one box after another, is the same trick used by every modern graphic novel, centuries later.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Cheriyal scrolls were made to hang on walls like posters.

Truth: They were made for performance. Storytellers carried them from village to village, unrolled them scene by scene and sang the stories, often all night. Wall-sized panels for buyers came much later.

Try this

Tape four or five sheets of paper end to end to make your own scroll. Draw a story from your family or a favourite book, one scene per sheet, with a strip of flowers between scenes like Cheriyal artists do. Roll it up, then unroll it slowly while you tell the story to someone.
