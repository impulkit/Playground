What is concrete made of?

Concrete does not harden by drying out. It hardens through a chemical reaction with water, so it can even set underwater.

The short answer

Concrete is a mix of four simple things: cement, water, sand and gravel or crushed stone. Cement is a fine grey powder. When it meets water, it reacts and forms tiny crystals that grow and tangle together. Those crystals glue the sand and stones into an artificial rock.

The stones and sand make up most of the volume, and they give concrete its strength. The cement is the glue. Freshly mixed, concrete can be poured into any shape, like a thick grey batter. Within hours it stiffens. Within weeks it is strong enough to hold up a flyover. People pour so much of it that it is the most used building material on Earth.

Concrete keeps getting stronger for a long time. Builders usually test samples after 28 days, when they have reached most of their strength, but the reaction carries on slowly for years. That's why fresh concrete is kept damp, often under wet sacks, while it cures.

Surprising but true

The dome of the Pantheon in Rome, finished nearly 1,900 years ago, is still the largest dome in the world made of concrete without steel inside.

How cement is made

Cement starts as rock. Limestone, often formed from the shells of ancient sea creatures, is crushed and mixed with clay. The mix is heated in a huge rotating kiln, a long tilted tube, to about 1,450 °C. The heat changes the rock into hard grey lumps called clinker.

The clinker is ground into powder with a little gypsum, a soft mineral that slows the setting so builders have time to work. The result is Portland cement, patented in England in 1824 and named after a pale building stone from the Isle of Portland. India is now the world's second-largest cement maker, after China. Many of its plants sit close to limestone deposits, in states such as Rajasthan, Andhra Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh and Chhattisgarh.

Roman secrets

The Romans built with concrete about 2,000 years ago. They mixed lime with volcanic ash from near Naples. Their harbour walls, soaked by the sea for centuries, have grown stronger as seawater reacted with the ash.

The Pantheon in Rome has a concrete dome about 43 metres across, wider than two cricket pitches laid end to end, with a round hole open to the sky at the top. Roman builders used lighter stones near the top so the dome would not crush itself. It was finished around 125 CE and is still in use. Engineers keep studying ancient Rome's recipes for ideas.

Adding steel

Concrete is very good at being squashed but bad at being stretched or bent. A concrete beam with nothing inside can crack in the middle under a heavy load.

The fix, worked out in the 1800s, was to bury steel rods inside. Steel is good at being stretched. Together they make reinforced concrete, which can span wide gaps and hold up tall buildings. Most bridges, metro stations, dams and skyscrapers today rely on it. If water seeps in and the steel rusts, it swells and cracks the concrete from the inside, which is why old buildings sometimes shed chunks.

The carbon problem

Making cement releases a lot of carbon dioxide. The fuel for the kiln gives some off. Heating limestone releases even more, because limestone is partly made of carbon dioxide locked in rock. Altogether, cement causes about 8% of the world's emissions, which feeds climate change.

Scientists are working on fixes. Some mixes swap part of the cement for fly ash from power stations or slag from steel works. Others capture the carbon dioxide before it escapes, or press it back into fresh concrete. Since the world will keep building homes, roads and schools, a greener grey could matter a great deal.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Concrete hardens because the water dries out of it.

Truth: The water is part of the reaction. If concrete dries too fast, it actually ends up weaker, which is why builders spray new concrete with water for days.

Try this

Make two mini blocks in paper cups. In one, mix a few spoons of sand and small pebbles with water. In the other, mix the same sand and pebbles with white school glue and a little water. Leave both overnight, then tear off the paper. The glue plays the part of cement, holding everything together.
