What fuel do rockets use?

A rocket carries its own oxygen into space, because there's nothing up there to breathe, not even for a fire.

The short answer

A rocket burns two things together: a fuel and an oxidiser. On Earth, a fire gets its oxygen from the air. In space there is no air, so a rocket has to bring its own oxygen supply along, usually as an icy liquid.

Burning the two makes a huge amount of hot gas, which shoots out of the nozzle at several kilometres per second. That backwards rush pushes the rocket forwards. The force is called thrust. Rockets use many different recipes, from rubbery solid fuel to kerosene, liquid hydrogen and methane. Engineers choose them for power, price and how easy they are to store.

Surprising but true

Liquid hydrogen in a rocket tank is kept at about minus 253 °C, more than 160 degrees colder than the coldest temperature ever measured in Antarctica.

Solid fuel, like a giant firework

The oldest rockets were solid. Chinese inventors were firing gunpowder rockets by around the 1200s. In the 1700s, the armies of Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan in Mysore used rockets with iron casings, which flew much farther than earlier ones. British engineers studied captured ones and built their own.

Modern solid fuel is a thick, rubbery mix of fuel and oxidiser packed into a tube. India's PSLV starts with a big solid stage, and NASA's Space Shuttle used two giant solid boosters. Solid rockets are simple and powerful. Once lit, though, they can't be switched off, a bit like a lit cracker.

Kerosene, hydrogen and methane

Liquid rockets keep fuel and oxidiser in separate tanks and pump them into the engine. They can be throttled up and down, and some can be restarted in space.

Kerosene, a refined cousin of the fuel in old lamps, burned with liquid oxygen in the first stage of the Saturn V that sent astronauts to the Moon. SpaceX's Falcon 9 uses the same pair today.

Liquid hydrogen gives the most push for its weight, but it is fussy. It must be kept at about minus 253 °C and leaks through the tiniest gaps. Fuels this cold are called cryogenic. India's CE-20 cryogenic engine powers the top stage of the LVM3, which carried Chandrayaan-3 from Sriharikota in 2023.

Methane is the newest favourite. It is easier to store than hydrogen, burns cleaner than kerosene, and might one day be made on Mars. SpaceX's Starship runs on it.

How do rockets land themselves?A rocket booster taller than a 12-storey building can fall back from the edge of space and land upright on a ship's deck about the size of a football field.

Fuels that light themselves

Some pairs catch fire the instant they touch, with no spark needed. They are called hypergolic. ISRO's Vikas engine, used on the PSLV's second stage, burns a pair like this. They are very reliable, which matters when an engine must fire after months in space. They are also poisonous, so the ground crew wear protective suits.

Mostly fuel

A big rocket is mainly a flying fuel tank. Around 85 to 90 per cent of its weight at launch is propellant. The Saturn V weighed about 2,900 tonnes at liftoff, about as much as 500 elephants, and most of that was gone within minutes.

This is why rockets drop empty stages as they climb. Every kilogram of empty tank thrown away means less weight to push. The PSLV, for example, has four stages, and it alternates solid and liquid fuel as it climbs. Burning fuel is only half the story. The other half is getting rid of what you no longer need.

A famous mistake

In 1920 a newspaper, The New York Times, mocked the American rocket pioneer Robert Goddard. It said a rocket could not work in space because it would have nothing to push against. On 17 July 1969, as Apollo 11 flew to the Moon, the paper printed a short correction. It said that a rocket can work in a vacuum as well as in air, and that it regretted the error.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Rockets fly by pushing against the air behind them.

Truth: Rockets push against nothing. They throw hot gas backwards very fast, and the gas pushes the rocket forwards. They actually work better in empty space, where there is no air to slow them down.

Try this

Make a balloon rocket. Thread a long string through a straw and tie it tight between two chairs. Blow up a balloon, pinch the neck, tape it under the straw and let go. The air rushing out the back pushes the balloon forwards, just like rocket exhaust.
