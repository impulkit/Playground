Why is the sky blue?

Sunlight looks white. So where does all that blue come from?

The short answer

Sunlight is a mix of all colours. When it hits the tiny gas particles in air, blue light gets bounced around much more than red.

So blue light comes at you from all over the sky. That's why the sky looks blue.

At sunset, light travels through much more air. Most of the blue is bounced away, so red and orange reach you.

Surprising but true

In 1921, on a ship, C.V. Raman wondered why the sea is blue. His research on light won India its first science Nobel Prize.

White light is a mix

In 1666, the young English scientist Isaac Newton passed sunlight through a glass prism and split it into a band of colours, from red through to violet. Then he used a second prism to mix them back into white. White light, he showed, is all the colours together.

Each colour is light with a different wavelength. Red has long waves, and blue and violet have short ones. That difference is the key to the blue sky. It's also why raindrops can spread sunlight into a rainbow.

Why blue bounces more

Air is mostly nitrogen and oxygen molecules, far smaller than the wavelength of light. When sunlight hits them, some of it gets scattered in every direction. Short waves get scattered much more than long ones. In the 1870s, the British scientist Lord Rayleigh worked out the rule. Blue light is scattered about five times more strongly than red.

So as sunlight crosses the sky, blue light is thrown around everywhere. Wherever you look, away from the Sun, some of that scattered blue is heading towards your eyes.

The same effect makes faraway hills look hazy and bluish, because there is so much air between you and them. Painters copy this to make their landscapes look deep, a trick called aerial perspective. Climb higher, and there is less air above you to scatter light. That's why the sky looks a darker, deeper blue from a high mountain or an aeroplane window.

Why not violet?

Violet light scatters even more than blue. But sunlight has less violet in it, some is absorbed high in the air, and our eyes are more sensitive to blue. So we see blue.

White clouds, red sunsets

Cloud droplets are much bigger than air molecules. They scatter all colours about equally, so clouds look white or grey. How clouds form.

At sunrise and sunset, sunlight takes a long, slanting path through the air. Most of the blue is scattered out along the way, leaving reds and oranges. Dust and smoke can make sunsets even redder. After the volcano Krakatoa erupted in Indonesia in 1883, its fine ash made skies glow red around the world for months.

On Mars, it's the opposite. Fine red dust in its thin air makes the daytime sky look butterscotch, and its sunsets look blue.

C.V. Raman

Raman studied how light changes colour slightly when it scatters off molecules. The ‘Raman effect’ is now used to identify chemicals, even in old paintings.

In 1921, sailing to Europe, Raman looked at the deep blue Mediterranean and doubted the usual explanation that the sea just reflects the sky. Back in Calcutta, he and his student K.S. Krishnan studied how light scatters in liquids. On 28 February 1928, they announced that a tiny part of scattered light changes colour. India now celebrates that date as National Science Day. In 1930, Raman became the first Asian to win a Nobel Prize in science.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The sky is blue because it reflects the ocean.

Truth: It's the other way round, mostly. Air scatters blue sunlight, which makes the sky blue even over deserts. The sea looks blue partly because it reflects the sky, and partly because water itself soaks up red light, which is what Raman showed.

Try this

Fill a clear glass with water and stir in a few drops of milk. In a dark room, shine a torch through the side of the glass. From the side, the water looks faintly blue. Now look through the glass straight at the torch. The light looks yellow-orange. The milk specks scatter blue light sideways, just as air does at sunset.
