What is chikankari embroidery?

In Lucknow, embroiderers stitch white flowers onto thin white cloth, and some of the best stitches are done from the back so they glow through like shadows.

The short answer

Chikankari is a style of hand embroidery from Lucknow, in Uttar Pradesh. Traditionally, embroiderers sew white cotton thread onto very fine, almost see-through white cloth. The patterns are vines, flowers, paisleys and leaves, built from dozens of different stitches.

What makes chikan special is its delicacy. Some stitches pull the threads of the cloth apart to make tiny holes like a net. Others are sewn from the back so the design glows softly through. A finished kurta looks light and cool, perfect for Lucknow's hot summers.

Surprising but true

Before any stitching starts, the pattern is printed on the cloth with wooden blocks and a blue ink that is designed to wash away completely.

How a piece is made

Making chikan is a team job. First a carver cuts flower patterns into wooden blocks. A printer dips the blocks in a blue dye and stamps the design onto the cloth. The dye is made to wash out, so it disappears once the stitching is done.

Next the cloth goes to the embroiderers, mostly women working from home in Lucknow and nearby villages. Many learnt from their mothers. Then comes washing, often by washermen who scrub the cloth to remove every trace of blue and starch it for crispness. Finally it is ironed and sold. One fine kurta may pass through five or six pairs of hands.

Thirty kinds of stitch

Chikankari has around 30 or more named stitches. Bakhiya, shadow work, is sewn on the back of the cloth so it shows as a soft outline from the front. Phanda and murri are tiny knots shaped like grains of rice or millet. Jaali stitches tease apart the threads of the cloth to make little windows, without cutting a single thread.

Some pieces use only one or two stitches. Masterpieces may use dozens. Expert embroiderers can make stitches so tiny and even that the back looks almost as neat as the front. Craftspeople say you can judge a chikan piece by turning it inside out. Thread matters too. Embroiderers often split cotton or silk thread into finer strands, so the stitches lie flat and soft. A thick thread would pucker the delicate cloth.

An empress, or a mystery?

A popular story says Nur Jahan, the wife of the Mughal emperor Jahangir, loved white embroidery and brought the art to India in the 1600s. It is a lovely tale, but there is no firm proof. White embroidery on fine cotton existed in Bengal and other places, and the craft probably grew from many influences.

What is certain is that chikan flourished in Lucknow under the Nawabs of Awadh in the 1700s and 1800s. They loved fine clothes, and Lucknow's craftspeople supplied them. In 2008, Lucknow chikan received a GI tag, so only work made in the Lucknow region can carry the name.

From muslin to film sets

The first chikan was sewn on fine muslin, a cotton cloth so thin it was said to pass through a ring. Today it appears on cotton, silk, georgette and chiffon, in many colours besides white. Pastel shades and even bright colours are common.

Film costumes and designer weddings have made chikankari popular again. But most embroiderers are poorly paid, and machine-made copies sell for a fraction of the price. Groups in Lucknow now help women sell directly to buyers, so more of the money reaches the hands that did the work.

The love of light white clothes in hot places makes scientific sense. Pale cloth reflects sunlight, and thin cotton lets air move and sweat evaporate. Cotton itself has a long journey from fluffy boll to shirt, the story of cotton. And Lucknow has another famous needle art, zardozi, sewn with glittering metal thread fit for royalty.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Chikankari is always done by machine now.

Truth: Cheap machine copies are everywhere, but the real craft is still stitched by hand, mostly by women working at home in and around Lucknow. A single kurta can take days or weeks.

Try this

Lay a thin white handkerchief or muslin cloth over a drawing made with a dark felt pen. Notice how the lines show through softly, like shadow work. Then try drawing a flower on paper using only dots, loops and crosses, the way chikan stitches build a pattern.
