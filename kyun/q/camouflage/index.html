How do animals hide in plain sight?

A butterfly in India folds its wings and becomes a dead leaf, complete with a fake stalk and fake veins.

The short answer

Animals hide in plain sight using camouflage: colours, patterns and shapes that make them hard to spot. Some match their background, like a sand-coloured lizard on sand. Some have bold patterns that break up their outline, so a hunter's eye doesn't see the shape of an animal at all. Others pretend to be something else entirely, such as a twig, a leaf or a bird dropping.

Camouflage works both ways. Prey animals use it to avoid being eaten. Predators use it to creep close to their meals. Over many generations, the animals that were hardest to spot survived and passed on their colours, which is why camouflage can be so precise.

Surprising but true

A tiger's bright orange coat works as camouflage because deer, its main prey, can't tell orange from green.

Leaves, twigs and droppings

Some of the best hiders are insects. The orange oakleaf butterfly of India has bright blue and orange on the top of its wings. When it lands and closes them, the undersides look exactly like a dried brown leaf, down to a line like a central vein and a tail like a leaf stalk.

Stick insects look like twigs and sway gently, like a twig in the breeze. Leaf insects have flat green bodies with ragged edges, as if a caterpillar had nibbled them. Some caterpillars look like bird droppings, which few predators want to eat.

Hiding in stripes and shadows

A tiger's stripes break up its outline among tall grass and shadows. Its orange coat seems a poor choice, but deer and most other mammals see fewer colours than people do. To a deer, orange and green look much the same, a bit like how some forms of colour blindness work in humans.

Many animals, from sharks to deer, are darker on the back and paler underneath. Sunlight from above brightens the back and leaves the belly in shadow, so the two even out and the animal looks flat. This is called countershading. The American painter Abbott Thayer wrote about it in the 1890s, and it is sometimes called Thayer's law.

Colour on demand

Some animals change colour as they move. Octopuses, squid and cuttlefish have thousands of tiny colour sacs in their skin, each opened and closed by muscles. An octopus can change its colour, pattern and even the bumpiness of its skin in under a second.

Other animals change with the seasons. The Arctic fox and the snowshoe hare turn white in winter and brown in summer. High in the Himalaya, the snow leopard's smoky grey coat with dark rosettes blends into rock and snow all year.

A moth that changed colour

In England in the 1800s, most peppered moths were pale and speckled, and blended into lichen-covered tree bark. As factories burned coal, soot blackened the trees. Dark moths, once rare, became common near industrial cities, because birds found pale moths on dark bark more easily.

When air pollution laws cleaned the air in the 1900s, pale moths became common again. Scientists still use the peppered moth as a clear example of how living things change over time.

People borrow the idea

During the First World War, armies asked artists to help hide guns and soldiers. The French called them camoufleurs, from a French word meaning to disguise, and that is how the word camouflage spread. Britain painted some ships in bold zigzags, called dazzle, to make it harder to judge their speed and direction. Today, soldiers in India, the United States and elsewhere wear patterns designed for forests, deserts and snow.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Chameleons change colour to match their background.

Truth: Mostly, they don't. Chameleons change colour mainly to send messages to other chameleons and to warm up or cool down. Their normal greens and browns already blend in well among leaves.

Try this

Cut out a few small paper moths, some plain white and some coloured with patterns from a newspaper. Stick them on a newspaper page and on a plain white wall. Ask a friend to find them in ten seconds. You will see which ones vanish best, and why matching the background matters.
