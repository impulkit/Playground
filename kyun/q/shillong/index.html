Why is Shillong called the Scotland of the East?

Rolling green hills, pine forests, misty lakes and rain that drifts in on the wind reminded British visitors so much of home that they nicknamed Shillong "the Scotland of the East".

The short answer

British officials and soldiers who lived in Shillong in the 1800s thought it looked like the Scottish Highlands. Its gentle hills are covered in pine forests, with streams, waterfalls and lakes. The weather is cool, misty and often rainy. To homesick Britons, it felt like a little piece of Scotland in the hills of north-east India.

Shillong sits about 1,500 m above sea level, high in the Khasi Hills. It was the capital of British Assam from 1874, and when Meghalaya became a separate state in 1972, it became Meghalaya's capital. Today it's famous for much more than its scenery: for music, football, food, and the culture of the Khasi people.

A hill station in the clouds

The British built Shillong as a hill station, a cool place to escape the heat of the plains. They added churches, a golf course and bungalows with sloping tin roofs. Ward's Lake, a small lake with a bridge and gardens, was made in the city centre in the 1890s.

Shillong is only about 50 km from Cherrapunji, one of the wettest places on Earth. It gets much less rain than that, but still enough to keep its hills green. The Elephant Falls tumble down in three steps just outside the city, and Shillong Peak gives views over the whole valley.

Khasi families and traditions

Most people in Shillong are Khasi, one of Meghalaya's main communities, though many others live there too. The Khasi are matrilineal: children take their mother's clan name, and family property usually passes down the female line. The youngest daughter, the khadduh, traditionally inherits the ancestral house and cares for her parents.

This makes Khasi families different from most others in India. It's one of the many ways families are organised around the world. Khasi men still play important roles in clan councils and community life. A mother's brother, the maternal uncle, has traditionally been an important guide for her children.

The rock music capital

Shillong is often called India's rock music capital. Many homes have a guitar, many churches have choirs, and the city is full of bands. Its musicians have played in festivals across India and abroad. Christian missionaries from Wales arrived in the Khasi Hills in the 1840s, and their hymn singing helped give the region its strong love of music.

The best-known is Lou Majaw, who started holding a concert on Bob Dylan's birthday, 24 May, in 1972, and kept doing it for decades. Western rock, blues and country songs have been popular here since the 1960s, and many young Khasi musicians now write songs in their own language too.

Arrows, football and food

Shillong has an unusual daily game called teer. Members of archery clubs shoot hundreds of arrows at a straw target in a field. The number of arrows that hit is counted, and people bet on the last two digits of that number. It is a legal, licensed lottery, and results are posted every afternoon.

Football is the other big sport. Local clubs have produced players for India's national team, and big matches fill the stadium with noise. After the game, people head for jadoh, a Khasi dish of rice cooked with pork, or tungrymbai, made from fermented soybeans. In autumn, cherry trees bloom pink across the hills, and Shillong holds a cherry blossom festival, adding one more surprise to a city that already feels a long way from the plains.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Shillong was named after a Scottish town.

Truth: The name comes from U Shulong, or U Shyllong, a deity the Khasi believe lives on Shillong Peak, the highest point near the city. The Scottish link is only a nickname.
