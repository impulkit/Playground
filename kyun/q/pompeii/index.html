What happened at Pompeii?

In 79 CE a volcano buried a Roman town so quickly that bread was left in its ovens, and it stayed hidden for about 1,700 years.

The short answer

Pompeii was a busy Roman town in southern Italy, near the Bay of Naples. In 79 CE, the volcano Mount Vesuvius, about 10 km away, erupted violently. Within about a day, the town was buried under metres of ash and pumice, a light stone full of holes.

Many people escaped, but more than a thousand died there. The ash sealed the town like a time capsule. Houses, paintings, shops, graffiti and even food survived. When digging began in 1748, Pompeii opened a window onto ordinary Roman life that no history book could match.

Surprising but true

Archaeologists at Pompeii have found about 80 fast-food counters, called thermopolia, where Romans bought hot snacks from big clay pots set into the counter.

A town that didn't know

Pompeii had perhaps 10,000 to 20,000 people. It had temples, a large amphitheatre for games, public baths, bakeries and a forum, the main square. Rich families had houses with gardens and painted walls. Water arrived through an aqueduct and ran to public fountains on street corners. Stepping stones crossed the streets so people could keep their feet dry, while carts rolled between them. It was a fairly ordinary town of ancient Rome, which is exactly what makes it so valuable.

The people did not know Vesuvius was a volcano. It looked like a green mountain covered in vineyards. There had been a strong earthquake in 62 CE, and some buildings were still being repaired when the eruption came.

The day the mountain exploded

Around midday, Vesuvius blasted a column of ash and rock high into the sky. For hours, pumice rained down on Pompeii, piling up on roofs until some collapsed. Many people fled.

Early the next morning, fast-moving clouds of searing hot gas and ash, called pyroclastic flows, swept down the mountain. They reached Pompeii and the nearby seaside town of Herculaneum. People who had stayed behind did not survive.

An eyewitness

A teenager named Pliny the Younger watched from Misenum, across the bay, about 30 km away. Years later he described it in two letters. He compared the ash cloud to an umbrella pine tree, rising up on a tall trunk and then spreading out.

His uncle, Pliny the Elder, a famous writer and the commander of the Roman fleet, sailed towards the eruption to help rescue people. He died on the shore. Today, scientists call powerful eruptions like this one "Plinian".

Frozen in time

In 1748 diggers began uncovering Pompeii. Early excavators often hunted for treasure. Later, archaeologists worked more carefully.

They found bakeries with ovens, one still containing a round loaf of bread turned to charcoal. Walls were covered in graffiti: election slogans, jokes and messages like "I was here". There were about 80 counters selling hot food to people who had no kitchen at home, a Roman version of street food.

In the 1860s Giuseppe Fiorelli began making plaster casts from hollows left in the ash, showing people and animals in their last moments. These casts are a reminder that the ruins were once someone's home.

Still digging

About a third of Pompeii has not yet been excavated. New finds keep appearing. In 2018, a charcoal scribble on a wall, apparently dated mid-October, raised doubts about the traditional August date of the eruption.

Pompeii is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site, visited by millions of people each year. Vesuvius is still active. It last erupted in 1944, and hundreds of thousands of people live on its slopes today, one reason scientists watch it constantly. Many cities around the world share that risk.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pompeii was destroyed by flowing rivers of lava.

Truth: Lava did not reach the town. Pompeii was buried first by falling ash and pumice, then by fast, scorching clouds of hot gas and ash called pyroclastic flows.

Try this

With an adult, press a small toy or a leaf into a block of clay or play dough, then carefully remove it. Fill the hollow with a little wet plaster of Paris or thick flour paste and let it set. When you peel away the dough, you'll have a copy, made the same way as Pompeii's casts.
