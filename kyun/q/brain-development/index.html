How does a child's brain grow?

A newborn's brain is about a quarter of its adult size. By the time a child starts school, it has reached about 90%.

The short answer

A baby is born with almost all the brain cells, or neurons, it will ever have, about 86 billion in an adult. What changes most is the wiring. In the first years of life, the brain builds connections between neurons at an astonishing speed.

Then the brain trims away connections that aren't used and strengthens the ones that are. At the same time, nerve fibres get wrapped in a fatty coat that makes signals travel faster. This building, pruning and coating goes on through childhood and the teenage years, and the last parts aren't finished until the mid-20s.

Surprising but true

Around ages 4 to 5, the brain may use about two-thirds of all the energy the body burns at rest, according to a 2014 study. That may be one reason children grow taller more slowly at that age.

A fast start

A newborn's brain weighs about 350–400 grams, roughly a quarter of an adult's. By age two, it is about three-quarters of adult size. By about six, it is close to 90%.

All that growth costs energy. Scientists at Northwestern University in the USA calculated in 2014 that around ages 4 and 5, the brain uses about two-thirds of the energy the body burns while resting. They noticed that this is also when children's growth in height slows down, and suggested the body may be saving energy for the brain.

Use it or lose it

Harvard's Center on the Developing Child describes the young brain as being built like a house, from the foundations up. Early experiences, such as being talked to, played with and comforted, lay down the foundations.

Babies have far more connections than they need. As they grow, connections that get used often become stronger, while those that are rarely used fade away. Scientists call this pruning. It isn't a loss. It makes the brain faster and more efficient, like clearing a jungle into a few well-used paths.

Why is it easier to learn languages when you're young?At six months old, a baby can hear the difference between speech sounds from almost any language. By their first birthday, much of that skill has faded.

Sounds of every language

Research in the USA, especially by Patricia Kuhl at the University of Washington, found that very young babies can tell apart speech sounds from any language. By about their first birthday, they get better at the sounds of the languages around them and worse at others.

Children can still learn languages easily for years, which is lucky in a country like India, where many children grow up speaking two or three. Growing up with several languages doesn't confuse the brain. Children sort them out naturally.

The teenage rebuild

During puberty, the brain goes through another big round of change. Areas linked to emotion and reward become very active, while the prefrontal cortex, behind the forehead, is still being wired up. That front area helps with planning, patience and judging risk.

Scientists think this mismatch may help explain why teenagers often enjoy excitement, new experiences and time with friends. It also makes the teenage years a great time for learning new skills, from music to coding to sport.

What helps a brain grow

There are no magic tricks, but the basics matter a lot. Sleep is when the brain sorts and stores what it learned during the day, which is why children need more of it than adults. Food matters too, since the brain is built from the nutrients you eat. Playing, reading, talking with family, moving outdoors and trying new things all give the brain fresh work to do.

The brain also keeps changing all through life. Scientists call this plasticity. An adult learning a new language or instrument still grows new connections. It just tends to take more effort than it does for a child.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You only use 10% of your brain.

Truth: Brain scans show that people use virtually all of their brain, just not every part at full power at once. Even during sleep, many areas stay busy.

Try this

Learn something new with your hands that you've never done, like juggling two balls or writing your name with the other hand. Practise for five minutes a day for a week and note how it feels on day one and day seven. You are watching your brain build and strengthen new connections.
