What is Tex-Mex food?

Nachos were invented in a hurry, in 1943, by a waiter named Nacho who had to feed some hungry visitors with whatever was in the kitchen.

The short answer

Tex-Mex is the cooking of Texas that grew out of Mexican food. It was created mainly by Tejanos, Texans of Mexican descent, and then shaped by American tastes and American shops.

You can usually spot Tex-Mex by a few clues. There is plenty of melted yellow cheese, a lot of beef, a warm, earthy flavour of cumin, and soft wheat-flour tortillas or crunchy fried shells. Famous dishes include chili con carne, nachos, fajitas, crispy tacos and a bowl of melted cheese dip called queso. It is a border cuisine, born where two countries, two languages and two kitchens rub against each other.

Surprising but true

Texas was part of Mexico until 1836, so many Tex-Mex families did not cross the border. The border crossed them.

When Texas was Mexico

Before 1836, Texas belonged to Mexico. After a war that year it became an independent republic, and in 1845 it joined the United States. Many families who had lived there for generations suddenly found themselves in a new country without moving an inch.

They kept cooking what they knew, using what they could find. Beef was cheap on the great cattle ranches of Texas, and wheat flour was easy to buy. So beef crept into dishes that might have used pork or chicken, and flour tortillas sat beside corn ones.

The chili queens of San Antonio

In the 1800s, women in San Antonio set up tables in the city's plazas each evening. They cooked pots of chili con carne, a stew of meat and dried chillies, over open fires and served it by lantern light. Visitors called them the "chili queens".

Travellers wrote home about them, and chili became famous across the United States. City health rules shut the stalls down in the 1930s and 1940s. By then, canned chili and chili powder were sold in grocery stores. In 1977, Texas made chili its official state dish.

A nacho for the visitors

In 1943, a group of American women from a nearby army base crossed the border into Piedras Negras, a Mexican town across the river from Eagle Pass, Texas. The cook was away. The head waiter, Ignacio Anaya, went into the kitchen and grabbed what he had.

He cut tortillas into triangles, fried them, added cheese and slices of jalapeño chilli, and warmed them until the cheese melted. He called the plate "Nacho's especiales". The dish crossed back over the border and is now eaten in cinemas from Mumbai to Miami.

Fajitas and the cowboys

Fajitas began on Texas ranches, where Mexican cowboys, called vaqueros, were often paid partly in the cheapest cuts of beef. One was skirt steak, a thin strip from below the ribs. Grilled fast over fire and wrapped in a tortilla, it became a meal. Restaurants made fajitas popular in the 1970s, served sizzling on a hot iron plate.

Fusion food everywhere

Tex-Mex is a kind of fusion: a new cuisine made when two food traditions meet. India has its own famous example. Indo-Chinese food, with dishes such as gobi Manchurian, grew up in Kolkata and Mumbai and is quite unlike food in China. Both were made by people far from their original homes, cooking for neighbours with new tastes.

Indian visitors to a Tex-Mex restaurant often notice familiar things: rajma-like beans, green chillies and the smell of jeera, which is cumin. It turns out that a plate of nachos and a plate of chole have more in common than you might guess.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Tex-Mex food is the same as Mexican food.

Truth: Tex-Mex grew up in Texas and has its own habits, such as lots of yellow cheese, beef, cumin and wheat-flour tortillas. Many dishes from different regions of Mexico taste quite different and are rarely seen in Tex-Mex restaurants.
