What is life like at an Antarctic research station?

At the South Pole station, the Sun sets once a year, and the winter team works on through six months of darkness.

The short answer

Life at an Antarctic research station is a mix of careful science, hard outdoor work and long days indoors with the same small group. Around 70 stations, run by about 30 countries, are spread across the ice. In summer the continent holds a few thousand people. In winter, only about a thousand stay on.

Everything has to be shipped or flown in, from fresh vegetables to fuel. Waste is carried out again. People sleep in heated rooms, eat in a shared dining hall and dress in several layers before stepping outside, where the wind can freeze bare skin in minutes.

Surprising but true

The coldest air temperature ever measured on Earth, minus 89.2°C, was recorded at a research station, Russia's Vostok, in 1983.

India on the ice

India's first station, Dakshin Gangotri, was set up in 1983 and was slowly buried by snow until it was closed. Maitri opened in 1989 beside a freshwater lake in an ice-free patch of rock. Bharati followed in 2012, on a rocky headland near the coast. It is made partly from shipping containers and looks a bit like a silver spaceship parked on a hill.

Each year an Indian expedition sails south from Goa or Cape Town. Some members stay only for the summer. A smaller winter team keeps the station running through the dark months, when no ship can get through the frozen sea. Doctors, cooks, engineers and radio operators go too, because a station is really a tiny village that has to fix its own pipes, generators and vehicles.

Science you can only do here

Scientists come because Antarctica holds records that exist nowhere else. Ice cores, long tubes of ice drilled out of the ice sheet, trap tiny bubbles of ancient air. They show how the climate changed through the ice ages over hundreds of thousands of years.

Others watch the aurora and the sky, measure the ozone layer, study rocks and track how fast the ice is melting into the sea. Biologists count seals, krill and penguins. The clean, dry air is also good for telescopes, which is one reason the United States runs a station right at the South Pole.

A winter day

At the South Pole, the Sun sets once a year and stays down for about six months. Stations nearer the coast get a few hours of dim light in midwinter. People keep to a strict timetable for meals and work, partly to keep their body clocks steady.

Cooking duty, cleaning and snow clearing are shared. Showers are often short, because water comes from melting snow, which takes fuel. Fresh fruit runs out early, so frozen and tinned food fills the menu. Birthdays and festivals become big events, and many stations celebrate Midwinter Day on 21 June with a special dinner. Some teams make their own films, hold quiz nights or grow lettuce and herbs under lamps in a small indoor garden. A few leaves of something green can feel like a feast after months of tinned peas.

Living with risk

The nearest hospital may be thousands of kilometres away, so most stations have a doctor, and everyone learns first aid. In winter, an emergency flight can be almost impossible. Teams practise fire drills often, because the air is so dry that a fire spreads fast.

Living with the same 20 or 25 people for months can be tough. Space agencies study winter crews to learn how astronauts on long missions to a space station might cope with being shut in together.

A continent for science

Early visitors, like the explorers who raced to the Pole in 1911, came for glory. Today the rules are different. The Antarctic Treaty, signed in 1959 and joined by India in 1983, bans military bases and nuclear tests, and a later agreement from 1991 bans mining. Countries share their results, and visitors from one station often drop in on another for dinner, which may be the only party within 500 kilometres.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Anyone who wants to can go and live in Antarctica.

Truth: Nobody lives there permanently. Almost everyone on the continent is a scientist or support worker sent by a national programme for a few months or a year, and the Antarctic Treaty says the land can be used only for peace and science.

Try this

Fill two identical cups with warm water. Wrap one in a woolly sock or a few layers of cloth, and leave the other bare. Check them with your finger every ten minutes. Station buildings are built like the wrapped cup, with thick layers that trap warmth inside.
