How is a film made?

A two-hour film can take years to make, hundreds of people to build, and be shot completely out of order, with the ending sometimes filmed first.

The short answer

A film is made in five big steps. First someone has an idea and writes a script. Then the team plans everything: actors, places, costumes, sets and money. Next comes the shoot, when cameras roll. After that, editors cut the footage together and add music, sound and effects. Finally the film is released in cinemas or online.

A big film can involve hundreds of people and take years. Even a short scene of two friends talking may need a day of filming, a dozen camera positions and lots of repeated takes.

Surprising but true

Film crews often shoot ten times more footage than ends up on screen, and sometimes far more.

From idea to script

Every film starts as words on a page. A screenplay lists what we see and hear, scene by scene. One page of script usually comes out to about one minute on screen, so a two-hour film's script is about 120 pages.

Many directors then draw a storyboard, a comic-strip version of the film showing each camera shot. It helps the whole team picture the scene before anyone builds a set or books an actor.

Planning the impossible

This stage is called pre-production, and it is mostly organising. Who plays which part? Where will each scene be shot? Can a desert scene be filmed near Jaisalmer, or does it need a studio?

Planners break the script into pieces and group them. If five scenes happen in the same school corridor, they're shot together, even if they appear at the start, middle and end. That is why films are almost always made out of order.

How do films make special effects?One of cinema's first famous special effects began, so the story goes, with an accident. A camera jammed in a Paris street, and a bus seemed to turn into a hearse.

Lights, camera, action

On shooting days, a crew might include camera operators, lighting technicians, sound recordists, make-up artists, costume designers and drivers. Each shot is repeated several times, called takes, until the director is happy.

Before each take, someone snaps a clapperboard in front of the camera. The sharp clap helps editors line up the sound, which is recorded separately, with the picture. The board also shows the scene and take number.

A person called the script supervisor watches tiny details. Was the glass half full or empty? Was the actor's collar up? Mistakes slip through anyway, and fans love spotting them.

The editing room

Once filming ends, the editor may have hundreds of hours of footage for a two-hour film. Crews often shoot ten times more than they use, and sometimes far more. Choosing the best moments and putting them in order is where a film really takes shape. A single scene can change mood completely depending on which shots are used.

Then come special effects, colour correction and sound. Many sounds you hear, such as footsteps, punches or a horse's hooves, are recorded later by Foley artists in a studio, using clever tricks like coconut shells for hooves. Finally a composer adds the music.

Why it looks like it moves

A film is really a series of still pictures shown very fast, usually 24 every second. Your brain blends them into smooth motion. It is related to the tricks behind many optical illusions.

The Lumière brothers held one of the first paid film shows in Paris in December 1895. Some viewers were startled by a train seemingly rushing towards them. Eighteen years later, in 1913, Dadasaheb Phalke released Raja Harishchandra, India's first full-length feature film. He did almost every job himself, and men played the female roles.

India, the film factory

Today India makes films in more than 20 languages, from Hindi and Tamil to Telugu, Bengali, Malayalam, Kannada and Marathi. In most years it produces more feature films than any other country, far more than Hollywood.

Films also travel the other way. Many Indian films use American visual-effects studios, and many Hollywood films send their effects work to studios in Mumbai, Chennai and Hyderabad. When a superhero leaps off a building on screen, an artist in India may have made that jump look real. Or a trained performer may really have jumped, which is where the careful science of stunts comes in.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Films are shot in the same order you watch them.

Truth: Almost never. Shooting is planned around places, actors and weather. Editors put the scenes in order afterwards, which is why keeping track of details like a character's haircut is a real job on set.

Try this

Make a flipbook. Draw a stick figure in the corner of 20 pages of an old notebook, moving its arm a tiny bit on each page. Flip the pages fast with your thumb. Films work the same way, only with 24 pictures every second.
