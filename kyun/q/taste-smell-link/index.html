Why does food taste bland when you have a cold?

Pinch your nose and eat a jelly sweet, and you can tell it's sweet but not whether it's lemon or strawberry. Most of what we call taste is really smell.

The short answer

When you have a cold, your tongue still works. What stops working is your sense of smell, and smell supplies most of the flavour in food.

Your tongue can only sense a handful of basic tastes: sweet, salty, sour, bitter and umami (a savoury taste). Everything else, like the difference between mango and banana or the aroma of a biryani, comes from smells. As you chew, scent molecules float up from the back of your mouth into your nose. A cold blocks that route with swollen tissue and mucus, so food seems flat.

Surprising but true

The famous "tongue map", showing sweet at the tip and bitter at the back, is wrong. It came from a mistranslated German paper from 1901, and every part of the tongue can sense every basic taste.

Two senses working as one

Smell reaches your nose in two ways. When you sniff a flower, the scent goes in through your nostrils. When you eat, smells travel the back way, from your mouth up through the throat into the nose. Scientists call this retronasal smell.

Your brain blends this back-door smell with signals from your tongue, and you experience one thing: flavour. It feels like it's all happening in your mouth, which is why most people are surprised to learn how much their nose is doing.

Why a cold shuts it down

At the very top of each nostril is a patch of tissue about the size of a postage stamp. It contains millions of smell-sensing cells with about 400 different kinds of receptors. Odour molecules have to reach this patch and dissolve in its moist lining to be detected.

During a cold, the inside of your nose swells and fills with mucus. Air, and the smells it carries, can't get to the smell patch. Once the swelling goes down, flavour usually comes back within days. Some viruses, including the one that causes COVID-19, can affect smell more directly and for longer.

The five tastes

Sweet, salty, sour and bitter have been recognised for centuries. In 1908, the Japanese chemist Kikunae Ikeda studied why a broth made from kelp seaweed tasted so savoury. He found the cause was glutamate, and named the taste umami, roughly "delicious taste". It's strong in tomatoes, cheese, mushrooms and soy sauce.

Taste buds sit mostly on the small bumps on your tongue, with some on the roof of the mouth and throat. You have a few thousand, and they are replaced every week or two. Scientists are still testing whether there might be more basic tastes, such as one for fat.

Why does chilli feel hot?Chilli does not actually burn you. It tricks the same nerves that warn you about a hot stove.

Heat is not a taste

The burn of green chillies or a fiery curry isn't a taste at all. It comes from a chemical called capsaicin, which triggers the same nerves that sense heat and pain. That is why spicy food can make you sweat and your eyes water, and why it stings if you touch your eyes after cutting chillies.

The cool feeling from mint works the same way in reverse. Menthol triggers cold-sensing nerves, even though the mint isn't actually cold.

A sense we notice when it's gone

Most people don't think about smell until they lose it. People who lose their sense of smell for a long time, a condition called anosmia, often say food becomes dull and they miss warning signs like smoke or spoiled milk.

Your nose also shapes your memories. A whiff of a particular food can bring back a holiday or a grandparent's kitchen in a flash. Smell signals travel to brain areas linked to emotion and memory more directly than signals from the other senses.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Different parts of the tongue sense different tastes, with sweet at the tip and bitter at the back.

Truth: All areas of the tongue that have taste buds can detect all the basic tastes. The idea came from a 1901 German study that was misread and turned into a neat but wrong diagram in textbooks.

Try this

Do a blindfold flavour test with a friend. Use small pieces of foods with similar textures, such as apple and pear, or two flavours of jelly sweets. Have your friend taste each one with their nose pinched, then again with it open. Keep score of how many they guess right each way.
