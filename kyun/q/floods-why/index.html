Why do floods happen?

On 26 July 2005, almost a metre of rain fell on one part of Mumbai in a single day.

The short answer

A flood happens when more water arrives than the land can hold or drain away. Usually that water is rain. It soaks into the soil until the soil is full, then it runs over the surface into streams and rivers. When rivers can't carry it all, they spill over their banks.

Some floods build up over days, as a big river slowly rises. Others, called flash floods, arrive in minutes after a cloudburst in hilly country. Along coasts, the sea itself can flood the land when a cyclone pushes it ashore.

Surprising but true

Just 1 cm of rain falling on one square kilometre of land adds up to about 10,000 tonnes of water.

When the sky empties

On 26 July 2005, a weather station at Santacruz in Mumbai recorded about 944 mm of rain in 24 hours. That is more than London gets in a whole year. Trains stopped, roads became rivers, and many people walked home through water for hours.

The numbers explain why. One centimetre of rain over one square kilometre is about 10,000 tonnes of water. Mumbai got nearly a hundred times that in a day. No drain system on Earth is built for it.

India gets most of its rain in a few months of monsoon, so river floods are a yearly event in places like Assam and Bihar. In Assam, the Brahmaputra floods so often that people on Majuli, a river island, build houses raised on bamboo stilts.

Flash floods are different. In June 2013, days of extreme rain fell over the mountains of Uttarakhand. Swollen streams and a burst glacial lake sent a wall of water and boulders down the valley at Kedarnath. Steep slopes leave water no time to soak in, so it rushes downhill, gathering mud and rocks as it goes.

Concrete, mud and sponges

Soil, grass and forests drink rain like a sponge. Roads, roofs and car parks don't. In a city, almost all the rain runs straight off hard surfaces into drains, which fill fast. When drains get blocked by plastic and rubbish, the water has nowhere to go.

Marshes and swamps help in the opposite way. Wetlands soak up floodwater and let it out slowly, which is why many cities are now trying to protect the lakes and marshes that are left.

Why are wetlands important?In Kolkata, a marsh quietly cleans a big share of a giant city's dirty water, and grows fish while it does it.

Rivers that build land

Floods aren't only a disaster. Every time a river spills over, it drops fine mud called silt across the fields. For thousands of years, farmers in Egypt waited for the Nile to flood each summer, because the silt made the soil rich. The great plains of north India and Bangladesh were built the same way.

That changed in Egypt after the Aswan High Dam was finished in 1970. Now the Nile doesn't flood, but farmers must use fertiliser instead. Dams can hold back floods, but they also trap the silt.

When the sea comes in

On coasts, the biggest floods often come from the sea. A cyclone's winds can pile ocean water into a dome several metres high, called a storm surge. In 2005, Hurricane Katrina's surge broke the flood walls of New Orleans in the USA, and most of the city ended up under water.

In the Mississippi valley, the Great Flood of 1927 covered land in several states for months. Afterwards the US government built huge walls and spillways along the river, and engineers still argue about how best to let a big river breathe.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Shallow floodwater is safe to walk or drive through.

Truth: Fast water only a shin deep can sweep a person off their feet, and it can hide open drains and broken roads. Weather services in India and the USA give the same advice. If water covers a road, turn around.
