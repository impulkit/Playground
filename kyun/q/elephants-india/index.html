How do elephants and people share land in India?

An adult elephant eats around 150 kg of plants a day, about the weight of two grown men, and a field of ripe rice is very tempting.

The short answer

India has more wild Asian elephants than any other country, about 22,446 according to a DNA-based count released in 2025. Most live in the forests of the south and the northeast, in states like Karnataka, Assam, Tamil Nadu and Kerala.

But India is also crowded with people, farms, roads and railways. Elephants need huge areas to find enough food and water, and their old paths now cross villages and fields. When elephants and people meet, both can get hurt. About 500 people die in encounters with elephants each year, and many elephants are killed by electric fences, trains and poisoning. Forest officers, scientists and villagers are trying new ways to help both sides live alongside each other.

Surprising but true

Elephants are scared of bees. Farmers in parts of India and Africa hang beehives along the edges of their fields, and the buzzing helps keep hungry elephants away.

Big appetites, long walks

An adult Asian elephant eats around 150 kg of grass, leaves, bark and fruit a day and drinks up to about 200 litres of water. To find all that, a herd walks many kilometres, following routes passed down from older females.

These routes link forests together. Scientists call them corridors. A 2023 government report identified 150 elephant corridors across India. Many are now narrow strips squeezed between farms, towns and highways.

Elephants also shape the forest for other animals. They open paths, knock down trees to let light in and spread seeds in their dung. That's why scientists call them a keystone species. Many Hindus also link the elephant with Ganesha, and elephants have walked in temple festivals and royal processions for centuries. That gives many Indians a deep fondness for them, even when they are a nuisance.

When paths cross

When an elephant herd finds a field of ripe rice, sugarcane or bananas, it's like finding a buffet. A single night can wipe out a farmer's crop for the whole season. Farmers try to drive elephants away with shouting, drums, firecrackers and torches, and sometimes with dangerous illegal electric fences.

Frightened or chased elephants can charge. Many deaths happen in the dark, when people walking along paths or through fields don't see a herd until it is too close. Government figures show more than 2,500 people died this way between 2019 and 2024. In the same years, hundreds of elephants died from electrocution, train collisions and poisoning.

Trains and elephants

Railway tracks often pass through elephant corridors, especially in Assam, West Bengal and Kerala. Trains can't stop quickly, and elephants crossing at night are sometimes hit.

To reduce this, railways have marked danger zones where trains must slow down. In the northeast, a system using fibre-optic cables along the tracks can sense the heavy footsteps of elephants and warn train drivers and station staff. Some lines have underpasses and ramps so elephants can cross safely.

Bees, phones and patience

Some of the best ideas are simple. In Kodagu, Karnataka, a project started in 2021 hung boxes of honeybees along forest edges. Elephants seem to hate bee stings, especially around their eyes and trunks, and many turned back. A similar idea had already worked in Kenya.

In the tea and coffee estates of Valparai, in Tamil Nadu, conservationists set up an early-warning system. When someone spots elephants nearby, local people get text messages and calls, and red lights flash in the area. Since it began, deaths there have fallen sharply.

Many villagers also grow crops elephants don't like, such as chillies, lemongrass or ginger, near forest edges. Fast compensation payments for damaged crops reduce anger. There are no perfect answers, but the elephants that roam India's tea gardens and coffee estates today show that people and giants can, with care, share the same land.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Elephants that raid crops are bad-tempered "rogue" elephants.

Truth: Most crop-raiding elephants are simply hungry and following old routes through land that used to be forest. Ripe crops are easy, nutritious food. Fear, stress and being chased can make any elephant dangerous.

Try this

Pick a room in your home and draw it as a "forest". Mark where the food, water and resting places are. Now block one doorway with a chair and see how the path between food and water changes. Elephant corridors work the same way. When roads and fields cut them, elephants have to find new routes.
