What robots do people have at home?

The robot most likely to live in your house is not a metal butler. It's a flat disc that bumps into sofas and remembers where they are.

The short answer

Most robots in homes today do one job, and do it over and over. Robot vacuum cleaners are by far the most common. Others mop floors, mow lawns, scrub swimming pools and clean windows. Some toys and learning kits are robots too.

What makes them robots, and not just machines, is that they sense the world, decide what to do and then act on their own. A ceiling fan spins whether or not someone is under it. A robot vacuum notices a chair leg and steers around it. That loop of sensing, deciding and moving is the basic idea behind every robot.

Surprising but true

The word "robot" comes from a 1920 Czech play, and it grew from a Czech word for forced, boring labour.

The disc that learned the house

The robot vacuum called Roomba went on sale in the US in 2002. Early models were fairly simple. They bumped into something, turned a little and tried again, and their wandering path slowly covered most of a room.

Newer ones are cleverer. Many spin a laser on top to measure distances to walls, or use a camera pointed at the ceiling. From those readings they draw a map, then clean in neat rows like a farmer ploughing a field. Underneath, "cliff sensors" fire infrared light at the floor so the robot stops at the top of the stairs.

How does a vacuum cleaner suck up dust?A vacuum cleaner doesn't really suck. It lowers the air pressure inside, and the air of the room pushes the dust in.

Where the word came from

In 1920, the Czech writer Karel Čapek wrote a play called R.U.R. about factory-made workers who eventually rebel. He called them robots, a word his brother Josef suggested. It comes from the Czech robota, meaning forced or dull labour.

That origin is a good fit for today's home robots. They take on the chores nobody enjoys: sweeping under the bed, trimming grass in the afternoon heat, scrubbing algae off pool walls.

Čapek's robots were made of something like flesh, not metal. The clanking metal robot came later, from films, comics and magazine covers. Real home robots look like neither. Most are low and round so they can slide under beds, and lawn robots look more like small turtles than people.

Why there's no robot butler yet

Here is an odd truth. Computers beat world chess champions in 1997, yet picking up a sock and folding a shirt remain hard for robots. Researchers noticed this long ago. Skills that feel effortless to people, like seeing, grasping and balancing, took millions of years of evolution and turn out to be very difficult to program.

A shirt can crumple into endless shapes. A plate can be wet, heavy or slippery. Your brain handles these surprises without you thinking. A robot has to be shown, or trained on huge amounts of practice, and still drops things.

Cost matters too. A robot vacuum is cheap because it only needs wheels, a brush, a few sensors and a small chip. A robot with two arms, fingers and cameras needs dozens of motors, and every one of them can break. In many Indian homes, a family member or a paid helper still does the jobs a robot cannot.

Robots that talk, and robots that listen

Smart speakers can answer questions and switch on lights, but most engineers wouldn't call them robots, because they cannot move. Many homes now link lights, locks and fridges together on the Internet of Things, which lets a robot vacuum start cleaning when everyone leaves.

Companies in the US, China, Japan and India are testing humanoid robots with arms and legs for homes. A few are already sold to researchers. For now, the robot most families own is still the humble disc under the sofa, patiently learning where the furniture lives.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Robots that can do all the housework, like the ones in films, are just about to arrive.

Truth: Single-job robots, such as vacuums and lawn mowers, work well today. Robots that can fold clothes, wash dishes and tidy up an untidy room are still mostly experiments, because handling soft, messy objects is very hard for machines.

Try this

Play "robot vacuum" with a friend. One person is blindfolded and can only move forward until they touch something with an outstretched hand, then turn. The other person times how long it takes to cross a room clear of anything breakable. Notice how many bumps it takes to learn the room.
