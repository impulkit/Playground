What is lightning?

A giant spark, hotter than the surface of the Sun.

The short answer

Inside a storm cloud, bits of ice crash into each other. This separates electric charges, like rubbing a balloon on your hair.

When enough charge builds up, it jumps as a giant spark: lightning.

The heat makes the air explode outwards. That boom is thunder. Count seconds between flash and boom, divide by 3: that's how many km away it is.

Surprising but true

Lightning heats the air to about 30,000 °C, five times hotter than the Sun's surface.

Charging up a cloud

A thunderstorm cloud is a tower of rising air, often more than 10 km tall. Near the top, it's far below freezing. Tiny ice crystals are carried upwards, while heavier soft hail pellets fall through them. As they bump past each other, electric charge gets rubbed off, a bit like static electricity when you shuffle across a carpet.

The lighter ice crystals, carrying positive charge, gather near the top of the cloud. The heavier pellets, with negative charge, collect lower down. Soon the cloud is like a giant battery, with hundreds of millions of volts between its parts and the ground.

The spark

Air normally stops electricity flowing. But when the charge gets strong enough, a faint, branching path called a leader zigzags down from the cloud in steps. When it gets close to the ground, a spark shoots up from a tree, a pole or a building to meet it. The path is complete.

Then a huge current rushes along the channel. That's the bright flash we see. It often flickers because several strokes travel down the same path in less than a second. Most lightning, though, never touches the ground. It jumps inside a cloud or between clouds.

Thunder

The flash heats the air in the channel to about 30,000 °C in a few millionths of a second. The air expands so fast that it creates a shock wave, which we hear as thunder. Light reaches you almost instantly, but sound travels only about 1 km in 3 seconds. That gap tells you how far away the storm is.

Lightning in India

India gets a lot of lightning, especially in the weeks before and during the monsoon. States like Odisha, Jharkhand, Bihar and West Bengal see some of the most. Lightning kills more people in India each year than any other natural hazard, including floods and cyclones. Many of them are farmers working in open fields.

To help, scientists at the Indian Institute of Tropical Meteorology in Pune built a network of lightning sensors. The government's Damini app warns people on their phones when lightning is likely nearby, so they can get to shelter in time.

Franklin's famous kite

In 1752, the American inventor Benjamin Franklin is said to have flown a kite in a thunderstorm to show that lightning is electricity. It was extremely dangerous, and a scientist in Russia was later killed trying a similar experiment. Franklin's idea led to the lightning rod, a metal spike on top of a building, connected to the ground by a thick wire. It gives lightning a safe path, and it still protects buildings today, including skyscrapers in New York.

Staying safe

When thunder roars, go indoors. A proper building or a car with a metal roof is safe. In a car, it's the metal shell that protects you, not the rubber tyres. Don't shelter under a lone tree, and stay away from open water. If you can hear thunder, you're close enough to be struck.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Lightning never strikes the same place twice.

Truth: It often does, especially tall, pointed things. The Empire State Building in New York is struck about 20 to 25 times in a typical year. Lightning goes for whatever offers the easiest path to the ground.

Try this

Blow up a balloon and rub it quickly on a woolly jumper or your dry hair for 20 seconds. Hold it just above some tiny bits of torn paper and watch them jump up. In a dark room, bring the rubbed balloon close to a metal door handle and you may see a tiny spark. It's the same kind of charge that builds up in a storm cloud, just much smaller.
