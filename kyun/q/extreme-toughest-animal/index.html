What is the toughest animal on Earth?

An animal smaller than a full stop has survived the open vacuum of space, been frozen close to the coldest temperature possible, and come back to life.

The short answer

Most scientists would hand the title to the tardigrade. It is a plump, eight-legged animal usually less than 1 mm long, about the size of a grain of salt. It waddles slowly through water films on moss, which is why people call it a water bear.

When its home dries up, a tardigrade pulls in its head and legs, loses almost all its water and curls into a dry ball called a tun. In that state it can survive deep freezing, boiling heat, crushing pressure and blasts of radiation that would kill a person many times over.

Surprising but true

In 2007, scientists sent dried tardigrades into orbit and exposed them to open space for 10 days. Some survived and later had healthy babies.

A body that switches off

Living things need water to run the chemistry of life. Tardigrades can lose about 97% of theirs. Their bodies fill up with special sugars and proteins that hold cells in shape, a bit like packing glass in bubble wrap.

In the tun state, their life processes slow to less than 0.01% of normal. They are not quite dead, but not really living either. Add water and, within minutes to hours, they puff back up and start walking.

The tardigrade survival list

In laboratories, tuns have survived being cooled to about −272°C, just one degree above absolute zero, the coldest anything can get. They have lived through short heating to about 150°C, hotter than boiling oil in a kadhai.

They have survived pressure about six times greater than at the bottom of the Mariana Trench, the deepest point of the ocean. Some species shrug off radiation about 1,000 times stronger than what would kill a human. One has a protein, named Dsup, that seems to shield its DNA like an umbrella.

Which animals have gone to space?The first animals in space weren't dogs or monkeys. They were fruit flies, launched from New Mexico in 1947, and they came back alive.

Into space and onto the Moon

In 2007, a European experiment called TARDIS put dried tardigrades on the outside of a satellite for 10 days. They faced the airless vacuum of space and the Sun's harsh radiation. Back on Earth, some woke up, and a few later laid eggs that hatched.

In 2019, an Israeli lander named Beresheet crashed onto the Moon carrying thousands of dried tardigrades. Nobody knows if any survived. Later, scientists fired tuns from a special gun at a target. They died when they hit faster than about 900 m per second. A hard crash on the Moon was probably too much even for them.

Frozen for 30 years

In 1983, Japanese scientists collected moss in Antarctica and stored it in a freezer at −20°C. In 2014, they thawed it. Two tardigrades woke up, after more than 30 years on ice. One laid eggs, and the babies hatched healthy.

Other tough contenders

Tardigrades are not alone. Tiny animals called bdelloid rotifers were revived in 2021 after about 24,000 years frozen in the Siberian permafrost. The wood frog of North America lets its body freeze solid in winter, its heart stopping until spring.

The naked mole-rat is another kind of tough. It can live more than 30 years, almost never gets cancer and survives about 18 minutes with no oxygen at all.

Why it matters

Tardigrades were first described in 1773 by a German pastor, Johann Goeze, who peered at them through an early microscope and called them little water bears. Today scientists study them to learn how to protect things that hate drying out, from vaccines that need no fridge to future astronauts' bodies. Their DNA-shielding protein has already been tested in human cells in the lab.

They also change how we think about alien life. An animal smaller than a grain of salt can ride outside a satellite and wake up later. So life elsewhere in the universe might be far harder to kill than we once thought.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Tardigrades are indestructible and can survive anything at any time.

Truth: They are only super-tough when dried out into a tun. An active, wet tardigrade is fragile. Studies show a few days at about 37°C, the temperature of your body, is enough to kill many of them.

Try this

Go tardigrade hunting with an adult. Collect a small clump of dry moss, soak it in a dish of rainwater overnight, then squeeze the water into a clear lid. Look with a strong magnifying glass or a cheap clip-on phone microscope for slow, chubby, eight-legged crawlers.
