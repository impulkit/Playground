Why do some birds fly thousands of kilometres?

Every October, lakhs of tiny falcons stop in Nagaland, halfway along one of the longest journeys of any bird of prey.

The short answer

Birds migrate to follow food and good weather. In winter, insects become hard to find in northern Asia, but in southern Africa it is summer and insects are everywhere. So the Amur falcon, a bird weighing about 150 g, flies from China, Mongolia and Russia to southern Africa every autumn and back again in spring.

On the way, lakhs of them stop in Nagaland in Northeast India to rest and feast on insects. Then they cross India and fly out over the Arabian Sea, non-stop for days, without landing. Scientists learned their route by fixing tiny satellite tags on a few birds.

Surprising but true

A bird about as heavy as a cricket ball flies around 22,000 km in a year.

Finding the way

Migrating birds use several clues to find their way. They watch the Sun by day and the stars by night. They remember coastlines, rivers and mountains. Many can also sense Earth's magnetism, though exactly how is still being worked out. Young birds of many species make their first trip without a guide, so some of the route must be built in. Some birds may even use smell. Experiments with seabirds called shearwaters suggest they find their way across open ocean partly by sniffing.

In 2013, scientists from the Wildlife Institute of India fitted three Amur falcons in Nagaland with satellite tags. The signals showed the birds crossing India and the Arabian Sea on their way to southern Africa.

A village that changed its mind

Around 2012, conservationists reported that huge numbers of Amur falcons were being trapped for food near Doyang lake in Nagaland's Wokha district. The news spread around the world. Local villages, especially Pangti, decided to protect the birds instead. Village councils banned hunting, children joined eco-clubs, and the state government and conservation groups helped.

Now the falcons roost safely, and visitors come each October to watch clouds of birds settle into the trees at dusk. Some people call the area the falcon capital of the world.

Winter visitors to India

India is a winter home for millions of migrating birds. From October, ducks, cranes, storks and waders arrive from Central Asia, Siberia and Europe. In the village of Khichan in Rajasthan, thousands of demoiselle cranes land each winter, and villagers have fed them grain for decades. Siberian cranes once wintered at Keoladeo National Park in Bharatpur, but the last ones were seen there in 2002.

Before setting off, many birds eat huge amounts and store the energy as fat. Some small songbirds nearly double their weight. During the flight their bodies burn this fat like fuel, and some even shrink parts of their gut to save weight. When they arrive, they are thin and very hungry.

Other record breakers

Amur falcons are not the only great travellers. Bar-headed geese fly over the Himalayas, as you can read in how geese cross the mountains. Arctic terns fly from the Arctic to the Antarctic and back each year, covering about 70,000 km and chasing a second summer. In 2022, a young bar-tailed godwit tracked by scientists flew about 13,500 km non-stop from Alaska to Tasmania, without eating or resting.

Insects migrate too. Some scientists think Amur falcons may feed on swarms of migrating dragonflies over the Arabian Sea, which you can read about in insect flight. Nobody has proved it yet, but it would explain how such small birds keep going so far from land.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Birds migrate mainly to escape the cold.

Truth: Many birds can survive cold well if they have enough to eat. They migrate mainly to follow food, such as insects, which become scarce in winter.

Try this

Track a migration on a map. Take a world map or globe and mark Mongolia, Nagaland, the Arabian Sea and South Africa. Join them with string or a pencil line to follow the Amur falcon's route. Then measure it with the map's scale and add up the distance.
