Why are blackbucks so fast?

A blackbuck can sprint at about 80 km/h, as fast as a car on a highway, and bounce two metres into the air while doing it.

The short answer

Blackbucks are fast because they evolved on open grasslands, where there was nowhere to hide. On flat plains, the best defence against wolves, and long ago against cheetahs, was to see danger early and outrun it. Over countless generations, the quickest blackbucks survived and passed on their long, slim legs and light bodies.

A blackbuck can reach about 80 km/h in short bursts and keep up a fast pace for longer than most predators. It is one of the fastest land animals in India. Only a few antelope in Africa, and the cheetah, run much faster.

Surprising but true

Blackbucks were taken to Texas in the 1930s for hunting ranches, and today many thousands roam there, far from their Indian grasslands.

Built for open plains

A blackbuck is light, weighing about 20 to 57 kg as an adult male, roughly as much as a teenage child. Its legs are thin and long, with most of the muscle packed high up near the body. That keeps the lower legs light, so they can swing back and forth very quickly, like a pendulum.

Big eyes on the sides of the head give the blackbuck a wide view of the plains. It can spot movement across a field while still grazing. Herds keep watch together, and when one animal bolts, the rest follow at once.

Leaps and spiral horns

When chased, blackbucks often bound high into the air, sometimes about 2 metres up, before racing off. Scientists call this kind of jump stotting or pronking. One idea is that it tells a predator, "I'm strong. Don't bother." It may also warn the rest of the herd.

Adult males are striking. Their backs turn dark brown or nearly black, with a white belly and white rings around the eyes. Their horns twist upward in a corkscrew spiral, reaching up to about 75 cm, longer than a cricket bat's blade. Females are fawn-coloured and usually have no horns. Males clash horns over territory, but rarely hurt each other badly. A male's horns also hint at his age. Young males have short, straight spikes. The twists build up as he grows, and an old buck may have three to five full turns.

Protected by a promise

Blackbucks once roamed in huge herds across India's plains. Hunting and the loss of grasslands to farms made them disappear from much of the country. Today they live mostly in protected areas and in places where local people guard them.

The Bishnoi community of Rajasthan is famous for protecting blackbucks and chinkara. Their faith forbids harming animals and green trees. In 1730, in the village of Khejarli, many Bishnoi people are said to have given their lives to stop trees being cut, a story that later inspired the Chipko movement. Today Bishnoi families still rush injured blackbucks to rescue centres. Blackbuck hunting is illegal in India, and well-known cases have ended up in court.

From India to Texas

Some of the best places to see blackbucks are Velavadar National Park in Gujarat and Tal Chhapar in Rajasthan. The blackbuck is the state animal of Punjab, Haryana and Andhra Pradesh. Thanks to protection, its numbers have grown, and the IUCN now lists it as Least Concern.

In the 1930s, ranchers took blackbucks to Texas in the USA. They thrived on the dry grasslands there, and many thousands now live on ranches across the state. Argentina has a wild population too. So an Indian antelope now races across American and South American plains. Long ago, the blackbuck's main hunter in India was the Asiatic cheetah, which vanished from the country by the 1950s. Now that India has brought cheetahs back from Africa, the old race between the two could return. Both are built for open grasslands, and both need them.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The blackbuck is a kind of deer.

Truth: It is an antelope. Deer grow new antlers every year and shed them, but blackbucks keep the same hollow horns their whole lives.

Try this

Mark a starting line outside and do a standing long jump, then measure it. A blackbuck's bounding leaps can carry it several metres forward and up to two metres high. Count how many of your jumps it takes to match one.
