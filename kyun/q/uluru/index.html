What is Uluru?

In the red centre of Australia, a single rock rises 348 m above flat desert, taller than the Eiffel Tower, and turns orange, red and purple as the sun sets.

The short answer

Uluru is a giant sandstone rock in the middle of Australia's Northern Territory, about 450 km by road from the town of Alice Springs. It stands 348 m above the flat desert around it, a little taller than the Eiffel Tower. Walking all the way round its base is about 9.4 km and takes most people three to four hours.

To scientists, Uluru is the tip of an ancient rock layer pushed up on its edge. To the Anangu, the Aboriginal people whose land it is, Uluru is a sacred place full of stories, laws and ancestors. Both ways of seeing it shape how the rock is looked after today.

Surprising but true

Uluru is grey underneath. Its famous red colour comes from a thin coat of rust on the surface.

A rock standing on its edge

About 550 million years ago, rivers washed sand and pebbles off old mountains into a basin. Over time the sand was buried, squeezed and cemented into a rock called arkose, a kind of sandstone full of feldspar crystals. Then the Earth's crust buckled. The layers that had been lying flat were tipped up until they stood almost straight up.

Softer rock around Uluru wore away over millions of years. The hard arkose stayed. You can still see the tilted layers as stripes running down its sides. Most of the formation is hidden underground, like the bottom of an iceberg.

Why it changes colour

The rock's red skin is rust, a thin coating on the surface of the stone. Iron in its minerals reacts with oxygen and water, just as an old bicycle chain does. Freshly broken rock from inside is grey.

At sunrise and sunset, sunlight passes through more of the atmosphere, and the blue light is scattered away. Only orange and red light reach the rock, so Uluru seems to glow and change shade by the minute. That is the same reason the sky turns red at sunset. After rare heavy rain, the rock turns silvery and waterfalls pour off its sides.

The Anangu and their land

Aboriginal people have lived in this part of Australia for tens of thousands of years. For the Anangu, Uluru is part of Tjukurpa, the knowledge that explains how the world was made and how people should live. Caves around its base hold old paintings. Some places on the rock are so sacred that visitors are asked not to photograph them. Anangu rangers lead walks around the base, pointing out plants used for food and medicine. At the foot of the rock, the Mutitjulu waterhole holds water for much of the year and has long drawn people and animals.

In 1873, an English surveyor named William Gosse saw the rock and called it Ayers Rock, after a South Australian politician. For about a century that was its official name. In 1993 it got a double name, Ayers Rock / Uluru. In 2002 the order was swapped, and today almost everyone simply calls it Uluru.

Giving the land back

On 26 October 1985, the Australian government handed the land back to the Anangu. They leased it to the national parks service, and the two now run the park together.

For decades, tourists climbed a steep path to the top, holding a chain. The Anangu asked people not to, because the route follows a sacred path, and because dozens of climbers had died in falls or from heat. On 26 October 2019, exactly 34 years after the handback, the climb closed for good.

The park's other famous sight is Kata Tjuta, a cluster of 36 rounded domes about 25 km away. Like Uluru, it is sacred, and like the deserts of the world around it, it can swing from freezing nights to scorching afternoons in a single day.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Uluru is the biggest single rock in the world.

Truth: Mount Augustus in Western Australia is often said to be more than twice as large. Geologists also point out that Uluru is not a lone boulder. It is the top of a huge tilted layer of rock that continues deep underground.

Try this

Leave an iron nail or a steel wool pad (ask an adult) in a saucer with a little water for a few days. Watch it turn orange. That is the same chemistry that paints Uluru red, only far faster.
