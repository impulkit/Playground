How did LEGO bricks begin?

A carpenter in a small Danish village started by making wooden ducks. His company now makes plastic bricks so precise that a brick from 1958 still fits one made today.

The short answer

LEGO began in 1932 in Billund, a village in Denmark, when a carpenter named Ole Kirk Christiansen started making wooden toys. In 1934 he named his company LEGO, from the Danish words "leg godt", meaning "play well".

In 1949 the company began making plastic building bricks, based on a British design. In 1958 it patented a new brick with tubes inside that made models hold together firmly. That design has barely changed since, which is why bricks from the 1950s still fit with ones made today.

Surprising but true

A mathematician worked out that six standard 2×4 LEGO bricks of the same colour can be put together in 915,103,765 different ways.

Wooden ducks and hard times

Christiansen was a carpenter who made furniture and houses. In the early 1930s, during a worldwide economic slump, farmers could no longer afford new buildings. To keep his workshop going, he began making wooden toys, including yo-yos, trucks and pull-along ducks.

His motto was "only the best is good enough". A famous family story says his son once saved money by using only two coats of varnish on some toy ducks instead of three. Christiansen sent him back to the railway station to fetch them and finish the job.

From wood to plastic

After the Second World War, plastic was a new and exciting material. In 1947 Christiansen bought a plastic injection-moulding machine, which squirts hot liquid plastic into a metal mould.

In 1949 the company released Automatic Binding Bricks, based on Kiddicraft self-locking bricks invented in England by Hilary Fisher Page. They had studs on top but were hollow underneath, so models fell apart easily. Many shops thought plastic toys would never replace wooden ones.

The brick that clicks

In 1958 Ole Kirk's son Godtfred Kirk Christiansen and his team found the solution: hollow tubes inside the brick. The studs of one brick press between the tubes and walls of the brick above, gripping firmly. The patent was filed on 28 January 1958, and the design is still the basis of LEGO bricks.

Soon after, a fire destroyed the company's wooden toy warehouse. LEGO decided to concentrate on plastic bricks, and by 1960 it had stopped making wooden toys.

Built to a fraction of a hair

Since 1963, LEGO bricks have been made from a tough plastic called ABS. The moulds are made so accurately that the bricks differ by only a few thousandths of a millimetre, much less than the width of a human hair. That precision is why billions of bricks, made in different factories and decades apart, all fit together.

In 2005 the Danish mathematician Søren Eilers used a computer to count how many ways six 2×4 bricks can be joined. The answer, 915,103,765, shows how fast possibilities grow, an example of big numbers hiding in a toy box.

Minifigures, robots and recycling

The smiling minifigure arrived in 1978. Themed sets based on space, castles and later films followed. The first LEGOLAND park opened in Billund in 1968.

Around 2003 the company nearly went bankrupt after expanding too fast into other products. It recovered by focusing on its bricks again. Today LEGO kits are used in schools to teach coding and robots, and in competitions where teams build machines.

LEGO is trying to make bricks from recycled or plant-based plastic, but it has proved hard to match the strength of ABS. Old bricks rarely end up in the bin. They are handed down to younger cousins and siblings, which makes LEGO one of the most passed-on toys in history, and slows the need for recycling.

Myth or fact?

Myth: LEGO was named after a Latin word meaning "I put together".

Truth: The name comes from the Danish "leg godt", meaning "play well". The company later noticed that "lego" in Latin can mean "I gather" or "I put together", but that was a happy coincidence.

Try this

Take two LEGO-style bricks and press them together, then pull them apart. Now try with one stud overlapping, then two, then four. Feel how the grip grows with each stud. Build the tallest tower you can that stands on just one stud.
