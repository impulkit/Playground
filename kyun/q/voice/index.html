How does your voice work?

Every word you speak starts as two small folds of tissue in your throat, slapping together hundreds of times a second.

The short answer

Your voice is made in your larynx, or voice box, which sits at the top of your windpipe. You can feel it as the bump at the front of your throat.

Inside are two bands of muscle and tissue called the vocal folds, or vocal cords. When you breathe, they lie open. When you speak, they close together. Air pushed up from your lungs forces its way between them, making them vibrate very fast. Those vibrations make a buzzing sound.

On its own, that buzz doesn't sound like much. Your throat, mouth, nose, tongue, teeth and lips shape it into words, songs and laughter.

Surprising but true

Your recorded voice sounds strange to you because, when you speak, you also hear your voice through the bones of your head, which makes it sound deeper.

Tiny folds, fast vibrations

The vocal folds are small: in adults, only about 1.25 to 2.5 centimetres long, shorter in women than men. Yet they move astonishingly fast. In an adult man speaking, they open and close roughly 100 to 120 times every second. In women it is around 200 times. In children, it is higher still.

Each vibration sends a puff of air up the throat. Those puffs create a pressure wave, which travels to other people's ears as sound. The faster the folds vibrate, the higher the note.

Changing the pitch

To sing higher, tiny muscles in your larynx stretch the vocal folds and make them thinner and tighter, like tightening a guitar string. To go lower, they relax and thicken. Pushing more air through makes the sound louder.

Trained singers learn to control these muscles and their breathing very precisely. Some opera singers can sing a note that makes a thin wine glass vibrate so hard it cracks, though it takes a loud, perfectly pitched note.

Shaping sounds into words

Say "ee", then "oo", and notice how your lips and tongue move. The buzz from your larynx stays the same, but the shape of your mouth changes which sounds are strongest. This shaping turns a buzz into vowels.

Consonants are made by blocking or squeezing the air. Your lips close for "p" and "b". Your tongue touches the ridge behind your teeth for "t" and "d". For "m" and "n", air goes through your nose instead. Languages use different sets of these sounds. Hindi, for example, has sounds such as the retroflex "ṭ", made with the tongue curled back, that English doesn't use.

Why voices change

A baby's larynx is tiny, so babies cry in high voices. As children grow, the larynx grows too. During puberty, hormones make it grow quickly, especially in boys. Their vocal folds get longer and thicker, and their voices drop, often by about an octave.

While this happens, a boy's voice may crack or jump unexpectedly between high and low. This is called the voice breaking. The larynx also tilts forward, making the bump called the Adam's apple. Girls' voices deepen a little too, but less.

Hearing yourself

Most people dislike hearing their own voice on a recording. When you speak, you hear your voice in two ways: through the air into your ears, and through vibrations in the bones of your skull. The bone route makes your voice sound deeper and fuller to you. A recording captures only the air route, which is how everyone else hears you.

Shouting, screaming or talking for hours can make your voice hoarse, because the vocal folds get swollen. Resting your voice and drinking water usually helps. If hoarseness lasts more than a couple of weeks, a doctor should check it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Breathing helium makes your vocal folds vibrate faster, which is why your voice goes squeaky.

Truth: Your vocal folds vibrate at the same speed. Sound travels much faster through helium than through air, which changes how your throat and mouth shape the sound, so your voice sounds thin and cartoonish. Breathing helium is also dangerous and should never be tried.

Try this

Rest your fingertips gently on the front of your throat. Hum a low note, then a high note, then whisper the same words. Feel how the buzz changes, and how it disappears when you whisper. Then try saying "ah" while pinching your nose, and then "mmm", and notice which sound needs your nose.
