How did quiz shows like KBC become so popular?

When Kaun Banega Crorepati first aired in July 2000, a single crore of rupees was a prize most viewers had never even heard of anyone winning.

The short answer

People have loved quizzes for as long as there have been questions to ask. Radio stations in the USA ran quiz programmes in the 1930s. Television made them far bigger, because viewers could see the sweat on a contestant's face and hear the clock ticking.

In India, Kaun Banega Crorepati became a sensation from its first episode on 3 July 2000. It was based on a British show, Who Wants to Be a Millionaire?, which began in 1998. With Amitabh Bachchan as host, a top prize of a crore and ordinary people in the hot seat, it turned general knowledge into a national obsession.

Surprising but true

In the 1950s, some of America's biggest TV quiz shows were secretly fixed, and the scandal was so big that Congress held hearings about it.

The first TV quiz craze

American television tried quizzes early. In 1955, The $64,000 Question became a hit on CBS. That sum was a fortune at the time, and families gathered around the TV to watch people climb towards it, question by question.

Within three years, things went badly wrong. It turned out that producers of some shows, especially one called Twenty-One, had secretly given favourite contestants the answers to keep viewers hooked. When this came out in 1958, the scandal reached the US Congress. A 1960 law made fixing a quiz show a crime, and big-money quizzes almost vanished from American screens for years.

India's link with the Millionaire format later went global in another way. A novel by the Indian diplomat Vikas Swarup imagined a boy from Mumbai's streets answering every question on a quiz show. It became the film Slumdog Millionaire, which won eight Oscars in 2009.

Quizzing on Doordarshan

India's first quiz stars were on Doordarshan, when the country had just one TV channel. Quiz Time, hosted by Siddhartha Basu from the 1980s, pitted school and college teams against each other. The Bournvita Quiz Contest, which started on radio, later moved to TV with Derek O'Brien as quizmaster. Clever students became small celebrities, and schools held tryouts.

These shows were serious and clever. KBC kept the brainpower but added something new: money, music and drama.

Why KBC worked

The KBC set was dark, with a glowing hot seat in the middle and music that rose as the questions got harder. The format made every question feel like a cliffhanger. Contestants had lifelines, such as asking the audience or phoning a friend. Viewers at home played along and shouted answers at the screen.

The host mattered too. Amitabh Bachchan spoke to contestants with warmth and gentle humour. Lines like "lock kiya jaye?" (shall we lock it?) became everyday phrases. In 2000, Harshvardhan Navathe became the first person to win one crore on the show, and the news made front pages across India.

Stories of the people in the chair

Over the years, KBC has become as much about contestants as questions. A schoolteacher, a farmer's son or a railway clerk can tell their story to the whole country. Many winners have used their prize to build a house, pay for studies or help their village. By 2025, the top prize had grown to ₹7 crore.

The brain behind the buzzer

Quiz shows test more than memory. Contestants must judge how sure they really are. Using a lifeline too early wastes it, while guessing at the wrong moment can cost a fortune. That is a lesson in probability: with four options and no clue, you have only a one-in-four chance of being right.

In America, Jeopardy! has run since 1964. In 2004, a software engineer named Ken Jennings won 74 games in a row on the show, a record that still stood in 2025. He later became its host, proof that the person who knows the most answers can end up asking the questions.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Contestants on big quiz shows are secretly given the answers.

Truth: Some American shows in the 1950s were fixed, and the scandal led to a US law in 1960 making it a crime to rig a quiz show. Big shows today use strict rules, sealed questions and outside checkers to keep the game fair.

Try this

Run a family quiz night. Write ten questions on slips of paper, getting harder as they go, and give each player one lifeline, such as asking another person for help. Keep score, and see whether the hardest questions really are the ones people get wrong.
