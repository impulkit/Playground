Is the ocean noisy?

Put a microphone underwater and the "silent" sea erupts with crackles, whistles, groans and songs.

The short answer

The ocean is very noisy, but our ears are not built to hear it from above the surface. Sound travels through water about four times faster than through air, and it can go a long way. Waves crash, rain patters, ice cracks and underwater earthquakes rumble. On top of that, animals use sound to find food, find each other and stay safe.

Whales sing, dolphins click and whistle, fish grunt and drum, and tiny shrimp crackle like bacon frying in a pan. Scientists listen with underwater microphones called hydrophones. Since the 1900s, ships have added their own constant hum of engines and propellers.

Surprising but true

Snapping shrimp, no longer than your finger, make so much noise together that submarines in the Second World War hid in the crackle.

Light fades, sound carries

Underwater, light fades fast. Below about 200 m, it is dim, and below 1,000 m there is no sunlight at all. Sound behaves differently. It can travel for tens or even thousands of kilometres.

That is why so many sea animals rely on it. Dolphins and toothed whales use echolocation, sending out clicks and listening for the echoes, to find fish in murky or dark water. Humpback whales sing long, repeating songs that can last for hours. The deep calls of blue whales are some of the loudest sounds made by any animal, and are low enough that many people couldn't hear them even if they were there.

The crackling reef

Swim near a healthy coral reef with a hydrophone and you hear a constant sizzling. Most of it comes from snapping shrimp. Each has one oversized claw that snaps shut so fast it shoots out a jet of water and a tiny collapsing bubble. The pop is loud enough to stun small prey.

During the Second World War, US Navy submarines sometimes hid among these shrimp beds, because the crackle drowned out their own noise. Reef sound also helps young fish find a home. In 2019, scientists working on Australia's Great Barrier Reef played recordings of healthy reefs over damaged ones, and more young fish settled there.

A highway for sound

About 1 km down in many parts of the ocean, there is a layer where sound gets trapped and bounces along like light in a glass fibre. Scientists call it the SOFAR channel. Sound in this layer can travel across a whole ocean. During the Second World War, the American scientist Maurice Ewing showed that a small explosion set off in the channel could be heard thousands of kilometres away. Navies saw at once how useful that could be.

The US Navy built networks of hydrophones to listen for submarines. They also picked up strange sounds. In 1997, scientists at NOAA recorded a deep, rising noise in the South Pacific that they called "the Bloop". Rumours spread about a giant unknown creature. Years later, they concluded it was probably ice cracking and breaking off from Antarctica.

Too much noise?

Ships, sonar, oil and gas surveys and building work at sea all add sound to the ocean. Large ships make a low rumble that overlaps with the calls of big whales. Scientists worry that this noise can make it harder for whales to hear each other, a bit like trying to talk at a loud wedding.

When shipping slowed down during the COVID-19 lockdowns in 2020, researchers in several places noticed the sea had become quieter. That gave them a rare chance to compare. Some ports and shipping lanes now ask ships to slow down near whale areas, which makes them quieter and safer for whales at the same time.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The ocean is a silent world.

Truth: A famous 1956 film about the sea was even called "The Silent World". In fact, the ocean is full of sound, from waves, rain, cracking ice and earthquakes to animals clicking, singing, grunting and drumming.

Try this

Fill a sink or bucket with water. Put one ear against the side of the bucket, or just under the water surface in a bath, while a friend gently taps two spoons together under the water. Then try it with the spoons tapped in the air. The underwater taps sound sharper and louder than you might expect.
