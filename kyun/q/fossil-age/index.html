How do scientists know how old a fossil is?

The rocks around a fossil are like a stack of chapatis, with clocks hidden inside.

The short answer

Rock forms in layers, like a stack of chapatis. The bottom ones were made first, so they're the oldest.

Layers of volcanic ash contain special atoms that work like a clock. Scientists can measure their age very precisely.

If a fossil is sandwiched between two dated ash layers, its age must be somewhere in between.

Surprising but true

Scientists dated the asteroid layer to within about 11,000 years, out of 66 million.

Older below, younger above

The first clue is simply the order of the layers. Mud and sand settle on top of what was there before, so going down means going back in time. Geologists call this relative dating. It tells you which fossil is older, but not by how many years.

Fossils themselves help match layers from place to place. In the early 1800s, an English canal digger named William Smith noticed that each rock layer held its own set of fossils, always in the same order. Using this, he made the first geological map of a whole country, Britain, in 1815.

Some fossils make excellent markers because the animals spread widely and changed quickly. Ammonites, coiled sea animals, are a favourite. Each kind lived for only a short time, so finding a particular ammonite tells a geologist roughly when that rock formed, whether it's in England, Spiti or Madagascar.

What were ammonites, the spiral shells of the ancient seas?Ammonites floated through the seas for more than 300 million years, and in Nepal their fossils are honoured as sacred stones.

Clocks in the ash

To put real numbers on the layers, scientists need atom clocks. When a volcano erupts, its ash contains tiny crystals. As the crystals form, they trap certain unstable atoms, such as a type of potassium. Over time these slowly change into another element, at a steady and known rate. Measuring how much has changed tells you when the crystal formed.

Fossils rarely contain these clock atoms themselves, so scientists look for ash layers above and below them. That gives a range, like knowing a letter arrived sometime between Monday's post and Wednesday's.

Different clocks suit different ages. Carbon-14, the famous one, runs out after about 50,000 years, so it is used for things like mammoth bones, ancient seeds and charcoal from old campfires. For dinosaurs and older fossils, scientists use slow clocks such as potassium and uranium, which keep ticking for billions of years.

A date to 11,000 years

The layer that marks the end of the dinosaurs is one of the most carefully dated rocks on Earth. It is full of iridium, a metal common in asteroids but rare in Earth's crust. That iridium layer is found all over the world.

In 2013, a team led by Paul Renne measured ash just above and below it in Montana, USA. They got about 66 million years, give or take only about 11,000 years. That is like measuring the length of a 66 km road and being off by just 11 metres.

Dating India's dinosaurs

India has its own natural clocks: the Deccan lava flows. About 66 million years ago, huge eruptions in western and central India poured out layer upon layer of lava. Scientists have dated many of those layers using atom clocks.

Dinosaur eggs and bones found at places like Jabalpur and in Gujarat lie in rocks just below or between these lava flows. So the lava acts like a set of dated bookends. Those fossils tell the story of the last dinosaurs that lived in India, right up to the time the asteroid struck.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Scientists use carbon dating to find out how old dinosaur fossils are.

Truth: Carbon dating only works for things up to about 50,000 years old. After that, almost no carbon-14 is left. Dinosaur fossils are tens of millions of years old, so scientists date the volcanic rock around them using other atoms, like potassium and uranium.

Try this

Make your own layer clock. Each evening for a week, put a sheet of scrap paper on a pile and write the day on it, then add a small drawing of something that happened. At the end, "dig" down through the pile. The bottom sheet is the oldest, just as in rock. Now slip a sheet out from the middle and see if a friend can work out which day it came from.
