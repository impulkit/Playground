Why is there a leap year?

In 1752, people in Britain and its colonies went to bed on 2 September and woke up on 14 September.

The short answer

The Earth takes about 365 days, 5 hours and 49 minutes to go once round the Sun. Calendars can only count whole days, so a normal year has 365. That leaves almost a quarter of a day over each year.

If we ignored it, the calendar would slip by about one day every four years. After a century, it would be nearly a month out. Summer holidays would slowly drift into the rains, and festivals tied to the seasons would land at the wrong time.

So every four years we add an extra day, 29 February. That year has 366 days and is called a leap year.

Surprising but true

The year 2000 was a leap year, but 1900 was not, and 2100 won't be either.

Caesar's simple rule

Julius Caesar's calendar, started in 45 BCE, used this simple rule: one leap year every four years, no exceptions. That makes the average year exactly 365¼ days.

But the real year is about 11 minutes shorter than that. Eleven minutes doesn't sound like much. Over 128 years it adds up to a whole day. By the 1500s, the calendar had drifted about ten days away from the seasons. Easter, which depends on the spring equinox, was moving out of place.

Pope Gregory's fix

In 1582, Pope Gregory XIII introduced a corrected calendar, worked out by a team of astronomers. It did two things.

First, it jumped forward ten days to catch up. In Italy, Spain and a few other countries, Thursday 4 October 1582 was followed by Friday 15 October.

Second, it made a small change to the leap year rule. Century years would only be leap years if they divided exactly by 400. That removes three leap days every 400 years. The average year becomes 365.2425 days, very close to the real one. The Gregorian calendar only drifts by about one day in 3,000 years.

The year September went missing

Not every country switched at once. Protestant Britain was suspicious of a calendar made by a pope and waited 170 years. By then the gap had grown to 11 days.

In 1752, Britain and all its colonies, including the American colonies and the territories ruled by the British East India Company in India, made the jump. Wednesday 2 September 1752 was followed directly by Thursday 14 September.

Some countries switched even later. Russia changed only in 1918, after its revolution. The Russian Orthodox Church still uses the older Julian calendar for its festivals, so its Christmas falls on 7 January.

Born on 29 February

A baby born on 29 February only gets a real birthday every four years. Such people are sometimes called "leaplings". The chance of being born on that day is about 1 in 1,461, because there are 1,461 days in every four-year cycle.

In common years, most leaplings celebrate on 28 February or 1 March. Some joke that they are only a quarter of their real age.

Leap seconds and other fixes

Leap days keep the calendar in step with the Sun. A different problem is the length of a single day. The Earth's spin is not perfectly steady, and over long periods it is slowly getting longer.

Since 1972, timekeepers have occasionally added a "leap second" to the world's clocks to keep them matched with the Earth's spin. These extra seconds cause headaches for computers, so scientists have agreed to stop adding them by 2035. Other calendars solve the same kind of problem in their own way. Many Indian calendars add a whole extra month every few years instead of a single day.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In 1752, angry crowds in England rioted, shouting "Give us our eleven days!"

Truth: Historians have found little real evidence of such riots. The slogan appears on a banner in a famous painting by William Hogarth about an election, and the riot story seems to have grown from that.
