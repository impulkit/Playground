How do sleeper trains work?

Board a train after dinner, go to sleep in a proper bed, and wake up in another city hundreds of kilometres away. The journey happens while you dream.

The short answer

A sleeper train has beds, called berths, instead of only seats. You board in the evening, sleep through the night, and arrive in the morning.

The beds are stacked in tiers along the coach, often two or three high. In the daytime, the lower berth works as a seat. At night, everyone unfolds their bed and the lights go down.

Surprising but true

The world's first proper sleeping cars ran in the USA in the 1860s. George Pullman's coaches had seats that folded out into beds at night.

Beds on wheels

The idea is more than 150 years old. In the 1860s, an American named George Pullman built railway coaches with seats that folded out into beds. People could cross huge distances without stopping at hotels.

Indian Railways has run overnight trains for well over a century, since not long after India's first train ran in 1853. Its sleeper classes range from simple Sleeper coaches with open berths, to AC Three-Tier and Two-Tier, to First AC with closed cabins.

A fast train you can sleep on

Older long-distance trains are pulled by a single engine. Vande Bharat trains are different: their motors sit under many coaches, so more wheels grip the rails with friction. They can speed up, which is called acceleration, and slow down faster. That saves minutes at every stop, which add up over a long trip.

The first Vande Bharat trains had only chair cars, good for trips of a few hours. In January 2026, India started its first Vande Bharat sleeper, between Howrah and Guwahati. Each coach has cushioned berths, reading lights, charging points, automatic doors and toilets that work without being touched.

How do trains stay on the tracks?Train wheels aren't flat. They are slightly cone-shaped, and that small slope steers a train around every curve.

Sleeping while moving

Berths run along the length of the train, the same way it travels. When the train brakes, you slide gently towards your feet or head instead of rolling off the side. Coaches have springs and dampers under them to soak up bumps and vibration, so the ride feels smooth even at speed.

Your body clock does the rest. As night falls, it tells you to sleep, wherever you are. Some high-speed lines, like bullet trains in Japan, mostly run by day. But for long trips across a big country like India, travelling while you sleep is hard to beat.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You cannot sleep properly on a moving train.

Truth: Many people sleep very well on trains. Berths run along the direction of travel, so the gentle sway rocks you side to side, and the steady sound of the wheels can work a bit like a lullaby.

Look up the timetable of an overnight train near your home. Divide the distance by the hours it takes to find its average speed. Then work out how many of those hours you would spend asleep.
