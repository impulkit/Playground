How does the US Congress work?

Wyoming has about 6 lakh people and California has nearly 4 crore. In the US Senate, they get exactly the same number of votes.

The short answer

Congress is the part of the US government that makes federal laws, the laws for the whole country. It has two chambers. The House of Representatives has 435 voting members, with more seats for states that have more people. The Senate has 100 members, two from each of the 50 states.

A bill must pass both chambers in exactly the same words. Then it goes to the President, who can sign it into law or reject it with a veto. The rules come from Article I of the US Constitution, written in 1787.

Surprising but true

The first Congress did not meet in Washington, D.C. It met in New York City in 1789, because the capital city had not been built yet.

A deal between big and small

When the Constitution was being written, small states feared that big states would outvote them every time. Big states thought it unfair for a tiny state to have the same say as a huge one. The compromise, often called the Great Compromise, gave Congress two chambers. The House counts people. The Senate counts states.

That is why Wyoming, with about 6 lakh people, and California, with nearly 4 crore, each have two senators. In the House, California has 52 representatives and Wyoming has one. Six states have only one representative each.

Counting people every ten years

House seats are shared out by population, so the country must know how many people live in each state. Every ten years, the US carries out a census, a count of every person. After each one, states that have grown may gain seats, and states that have shrunk may lose them. Texas and Florida gained seats after the 2020 count.

Different jobs for each chamber

Members of the House face voters every two years, so they must stay close to what people at home think. Bills about raising money through taxes must start in the House. The House can also impeach, or formally accuse, a president or a judge of serious wrongdoing.

Senators serve six years, and only a third of the seats are up for election at a time. The Senate holds the trial if someone is impeached. It also approves treaties with other countries and confirms the people a president picks for top jobs, including Supreme Court judges. The Vice President presides over the Senate and can cast a vote only to break a tie.

Inside the Capitol

Congress meets in the Capitol building in Washington, D.C., under a white dome topped by a bronze statue called Freedom. The House sits in the south wing and the Senate in the north. People living in Washington, D.C., which is not a state, elect a delegate to the House who can speak but cannot vote on final bills.

Much of the real work happens away from the big chambers, in committees. Small groups of members study bills on subjects like farming, defence or education. They hold hearings, question experts and rewrite the wording. Most bills are quietly dropped at this stage and never reach a full vote. Visitors can watch debates from public galleries, and both chambers are shown live on television.

Slow on purpose

Getting the House, the Senate and the President to agree is hard, and most bills never become law. The writers of the Constitution designed it that way, so no single group could grab too much power quickly. Critics say it can make Congress slow to act even when most people want change. Myth or fact?

Myth: The President of the United States makes the country's laws.

Truth: Congress writes and passes federal laws. The President can sign a bill or veto it, but Congress can override a veto if two-thirds of both chambers vote to do so.
