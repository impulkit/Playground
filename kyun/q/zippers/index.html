How does a zip work?

Two rows of tiny teeth, a little sliding wedge, and a problem that took inventors about twenty years to solve.

The short answer

A zip has two strips of cloth tape, each with a row of small teeth along one edge. Every tooth has a little bump on its top and a hollow on its bottom. When the bump of one tooth fits into the hollow of the tooth above it on the other side, the two rows lock together like fingers clasped tight.

The teeth can't do this on their own. They need to meet at exactly the right angle. That is the job of the slider, the small metal piece you pull. Inside it is a Y-shaped channel. As you pull it up, the two arms of the Y guide the teeth in from each side and press them together one at a time. Pull it down, and a small wedge in the middle pushes the teeth apart again.

Surprising but true

The zip was first used on boots and tobacco pouches. It took until the 1930s for it to catch on in trousers.

A shaky start in Chicago

In 1893, the American inventor Whitcomb Judson patented a "clasp locker". He is often said to have shown it at the World's Fair in Chicago that year. It used hooks and eyes that a slider joined together, and it was meant to save people the trouble of buttoning their high boots.

It was clever, but it didn't work well. The hooks popped open at awkward moments, and they rusted. Very few people bought one. A clothing fastener that might fly open in the street was not something many people wanted to risk. Judson's company kept trying, and in 1906 it hired a young Swedish-born engineer named Gideon Sundback.

Sundback's "hookless fastener"

Sundback worked on the problem for years. In 1913 he came up with the design we still use, with small cupped teeth instead of hooks. More teeth packed closer together made a fastener that held firmly but could still bend. He also designed a machine to stamp out the teeth and fix them to the tape, so it could be made cheaply.

In 1923, the B.F. Goodrich company used it on rubber boots and called them "Zippers", after the sound they made. The name stuck. Americans say zipper, while people in India and Britain usually say zip.

From boots to blue jeans

Through the 1920s, zips appeared on boots, purses and tobacco pouches. Tailors still preferred buttons for clothes. In the 1930s, fashion designers and makers of children's clothing began promoting zips as easier to use, and men's trousers started using them.

Today zips are everywhere, from jeans to school bags, tents and sleeping bags. Some are made of metal, some of plastic and some are a tight coil of nylon. A Japanese company, YKK, makes a very large share of them. Check the slider of a zip near you and there's a fair chance you'll see those three letters.

When a zip goes wrong

If one tooth is bent or missing, the teeth below it can't hook properly and the zip splits open behind the slider. Sometimes the slider has simply worn loose, and the Y-channel no longer squeezes hard enough. A tailor can often fix it by gently pinching the slider tighter or fitting a new one.

Engineers still work on zips. Divers' drysuits and spacesuits have needed special airtight zips, with rubber seals so no water or air can get through. The basic idea is the same Sundback worked out more than a century ago: get each tooth in at the right angle, and let friction and shape hold it there.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The zip was invented for trousers.

Truth: Early zips were used on boots, gloves, money belts and tobacco pouches. Tailors were slow to trust them in clothes. Zips in trousers became common only in the 1930s.

Try this

Look closely at a zip on a bag or jacket, using a magnifying glass if you have one. Find the bump on top of each tooth and the hollow underneath. Open the zip halfway, then slowly close it while watching how the teeth swing in from each side and hook one by one inside the slider.
