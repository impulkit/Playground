Who was the Rani of Jhansi?

She first asked the British, politely and in writing, to let her rule. When that failed, she ended up leading an army in battle.

The short answer

Rani Lakshmibai was the queen of Jhansi, a small kingdom in central India. She became one of the best-known leaders of the revolt of 1857 against the British East India Company.

After her husband died in 1853, the Company refused to accept her adopted son as heir and took over Jhansi. She protested in writing for years. When the great uprising spread in 1857, Jhansi joined, and she took charge of its defence. In 1858 British forces stormed the city. She escaped, fought on with other rebel leaders and was killed in battle near Gwalior in June 1858. A British general who fought her praised her courage. In India she became a symbol of bravery and resistance.

Surprising but true

In 1943, Subhas Chandra Bose named an all-women regiment of his Indian National Army after her, the Rani of Jhansi Regiment.

Manu from Varanasi

Lakshmibai was born Manikarnika Tambe, probably in 1828, in Varanasi. Her family called her Manu. Her mother died when she was small, and she grew up at the court of the Peshwa in exile at Bithoor, near Kanpur, where her father worked.

There, unusually for a girl of the time, she learned to ride, fence and shoot. Her childhood companions are said to have included Nana Sahib and Tatya Tope, who would later become leaders of the revolt too.

A queen without a kingdom

In 1842 Manu married Gangadhar Rao, the Raja of Jhansi, and took the name Lakshmibai. Their son was born in 1851 but died as a baby. In 1853, the ailing Raja adopted a young relative, renamed him Damodar Rao, and died the next day.

The Governor-General, Lord Dalhousie, used a rule called the Doctrine of Lapse to take over Jhansi. The Rani was given a pension and told to leave the fort. She sent petitions and hired an Australian-born lawyer, John Lang, to plead her case. The Company would not budge.

1857 comes to Jhansi

In May 1857, Indian soldiers rose up against the Company at Meerut, and the revolt spread across north India. In June, soldiers at Jhansi rebelled and killed British officers and their families. Historians still debate how much the Rani knew or controlled at that moment.

Afterwards she governed Jhansi, raised troops and strengthened its walls. In March 1858, a British force under Sir Hugh Rose besieged the city. For about two weeks the defenders fired back from the fort.

Escape and the last battle

When Jhansi fell in April 1858, the Rani escaped at night on horseback, with her son, according to popular tradition, tied to her back. She joined Tatya Tope at Kalpi, and together they seized the great fort of Gwalior.

British forces soon attacked. On 17 or 18 June 1858, she was fatally wounded while fighting near Gwalior, dressed as a cavalry soldier. She was probably not yet 30. Her son survived and lived quietly for many years afterwards.

Khoob ladi mardani

In the 1930s, the Hindi poet Subhadra Kumari Chauhan wrote a stirring poem about her. Its refrain says she fought bravely, and generations of Indian children have learned it by heart.

During the Second World War, Subhas Chandra Bose named the women's regiment of his army after her. Statues of the Rani on horseback, sword raised, stand in Jhansi, Gwalior and many other cities. Her fort at Jhansi is still there, high on a rocky hill, and visitors can see the spot from which, the story goes, she leapt on horseback to escape.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Rani wanted to fight the British from the start.

Truth: At first she sent petitions and hired a lawyer to argue for her adopted son and her right to rule. She joined the revolt only later, after 1857 had begun and her position became impossible.
