What did Marco Polo see?

A young Venetian spent about 17 years in China, and Europeans refused to believe half of what he told them, including that people paid with paper money.

The short answer

Marco Polo was a merchant from Venice who travelled across Asia to China in the 13th century. He lived there for about 17 years at the court of the Mongol emperor Kublai Khan.

He saw huge, well-run cities, paper money, coal used as fuel, a postal service with relay stations and the busy port of Quanzhou, full of ships. On his way home by sea, he passed through Southeast Asia, Sri Lanka and the coasts of south and west India. Back in Europe, he described it all in a book that became one of the most famous travel books ever written. For centuries, it shaped what Europeans imagined about Asia, and it inspired later explorers, including Columbus.

Surprising but true

Marco Polo told his story while sitting in a prison in Genoa, to a cellmate who happened to be a writer of adventure stories.

A teenage traveller

Marco's father Niccolò and uncle Maffeo were merchants who had already visited Kublai Khan. In 1271 they set out again, taking 17-year-old Marco with them.

They travelled overland along the Silk Road, through Persia, across the high Pamir mountains and along the edge of the Gobi Desert. The journey took over three years. Kublai Khan welcomed them at his summer palace at Shangdu, which English poets later called Xanadu.

Marvels of the Mongol empire

Marco says Kublai Khan sent him on missions across China. He was amazed by its size and wealth. Hangzhou, he wrote, was the finest city in the world, with canals, bridges and markets.

What surprised him most was ordinary to the Chinese. People paid with paper notes stamped by the emperor, while Europe still used coins. They burned "black stones" for heat, which was coal. Messengers changed horses at relay stations and could carry news hundreds of kilometres in a day.

Pearls, diamonds and a queen in India

In 1292, the Polos left by sea, escorting a Mongol princess to Persia. Their fleet stopped in Sumatra and Sri Lanka, then sailed along India's coast.

Marco described pearl divers off the Tamil coast and a kingdom on the east coast, the Kakatiya kingdom, which he said was ruled wisely by a queen. Historians identify her as Rudrama Devi, one of the few women rulers in Indian history. He also told a tall tale about how diamonds were collected from deep valleys by tricking eagles into carrying meat with diamonds stuck to it.

A story told in prison

Marco reached home in 1295. A few years later he was captured in a war between Venice and its rival Genoa. In prison he met Rustichello da Pisa, a writer of romances, and told him his adventures.

Rustichello wrote them down, adding a few flourishes of his own. The book was copied by hand into many languages, and each copy changed a little. Italians nicknamed it Il Milione, perhaps because Marco described everything in millions.

Did he really go?

Some readers have doubted Marco. His book never mentions tea, chopsticks or the Great Wall. In 1995, historian Frances Wood argued he might have collected stories from Persian and Arab traders instead.

Most historians disagree. In 2013, Hans Ulrich Vogel showed that Marco's descriptions of Chinese paper money and salt production were detailed and accurate, matching Chinese records. A later story says that, on his deathbed in 1324, friends asked him to admit he'd made things up, and he replied that he had not told half of what he saw.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Marco Polo brought pasta from China to Italy.

Truth: Italians were eating pasta before Marco Polo returned. The story became popular in the 20th century, partly through an American pasta industry magazine.
