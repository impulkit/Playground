Why is the Nile so important to Egypt?

Almost all of Egypt is desert. Yet about 95% of its people live on a thin green ribbon along one river, just as their ancestors did 5,000 years ago.

The short answer

Egypt gets almost no rain. Without the Nile, it would be almost entirely desert. The river brings water for drinking and farming, a road for boats, and in the past, rich black mud spread by its yearly floods. The ancient Greek historian Herodotus called Egypt "the gift of the Nile".

Today Egypt has more than 10 crore people, and about 95% of them live within a short distance of the river or its delta. All that life is squeezed onto about 5% of the country's land. From space, the Nile looks like a green line drawn across a sandy page.

Surprising but true

The Nile flows north. It starts deep in Africa and runs about 6,650 km to the Mediterranean Sea.

Two rivers become one

The Nile has two main branches. The White Nile flows from the region of Lake Victoria in East Africa. The Blue Nile starts at Lake Tana in the highlands of Ethiopia. They meet at Khartoum in Sudan and flow north together through the desert to Egypt and the Mediterranean Sea.

Every summer, monsoon rains fall on the Ethiopian highlands. The Blue Nile swells and carries huge loads of mud downstream. That is what used to cause Egypt's yearly flood.

Black land and red land

For thousands of years, the Nile flooded Egypt every summer, from about June to September. When the water went down, it left a layer of dark, fertile mud. Farmers planted wheat and barley in it. The ancient Egyptians called their country Kemet, "the black land", after this soil. The desert around it was Deshret, "the red land".

The flood was so important that the Egyptians measured it carefully with nilometers, stone steps or columns marked with levels. A good flood meant a good harvest, and taxes were set to match. Too little water meant hunger. Too much washed away villages.

A river that shaped a civilisation

The Nile helped build ancient Egypt. Boats sailed north with the current and south with the wind, which usually blows from the north. Huge stone blocks for temples and pyramids were floated on barges. Papyrus reeds growing along the banks were made into paper and boats.

How were the pyramids of Giza built?About 2.3 million stone blocks, stacked 4,500 years ago, without engines or cranes.

The flood even shaped their calendar. Egyptians noticed that the bright star Sirius reappeared before dawn at about the time the flood began. They divided the year into three seasons: flood, growing and harvest.

Taming the flood

In 1970, Egypt finished the Aswan High Dam. It holds back Lake Nasser, one of the largest human-made lakes in the world. The dam ended the yearly floods, provided water all year round and makes a large amount of electricity.

There were costs. The rising lake would have drowned ancient temples. In the 1960s, UNESCO led a worldwide effort to save them. The great temples of Abu Simbel were cut into more than 1,000 huge blocks and rebuilt about 65 m higher. Without the flood's mud, farmers now need fertilisers, and the delta on the coast is slowly shrinking.

Sharing the river

The Nile passes through or drains parts of 11 countries. In 2011, Ethiopia began building a large dam on the Blue Nile, the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam, to make electricity for its people, and officially opened it in 2025. Egypt and Sudan worry that it could reduce the water reaching them. The three countries have held many talks, and they still disagree on how the river should be shared.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Rivers always flow south.

Truth: Rivers flow downhill, in whatever direction the land slopes. The Nile, Russia's Ob and Canada's Mackenzie all flow north.

Try this

Make a river valley. In a baking tray, pile sand or soil high at one end and press a winding channel down the middle. Slowly pour water at the high end and watch where it flows and where mud settles. Notice the fan of mud that forms at the low end, like the Nile delta.
