How does an eraser rub out pencil?

Before erasers, people rubbed out pencil marks with balls of soft bread. Some artists still do.

The short answer

When you write with a pencil, tiny flakes of graphite stick to the fibres on top of the paper. They don't soak in. An eraser is made of a soft, slightly sticky material that grips graphite more strongly than paper does. Rub it over the mark, and the flakes cling to the eraser instead.

The rubbing also wears the eraser away. Little crumbs roll up, carrying the graphite with them, and fall off. That keeps a clean surface touching the paper. So the grey crumbs you brush off the page are the pencil line, wrapped up and removed.

Surprising but true

The English word "rubber" for an eraser comes from the scientist Joseph Priestley, who wrote in 1770 that this new substance from South America was good for rubbing out pencil.

Bread before rubber

For centuries, people who used graphite or chalk rubbed out mistakes with soft, fresh bread, squeezed into a ball. It picked up the dust well enough. Some drawing artists still use bread or a soft, putty-like eraser that works in the same way.

Others used wax, or rough stones like pumice to scrape marks off parchment. Scribes who wrote in ink on animal skin sometimes shaved off mistakes with a knife.

A word from a scientist

Natural rubber comes from the milky sap, called latex, of the rubber tree, which grows wild in the Amazon rainforest of Brazil. People in Central and South America had used it for thousands of years to make balls and waterproof cloth. Europeans began bringing samples home in the 1700s.

In 1770, the English scientist Joseph Priestley, better known for discovering oxygen, noted that a small cube of this gum was excellent for rubbing out pencil marks. He added that a London instrument maker, Edward Nairne, sold cubes about a centimetre across for three shillings each, a high price at the time. Because it "rubbed", people started calling it rubber. The name spread to the material itself. In Britain and India, people still say "rubber" for an eraser, while Americans say "eraser".

Better rubber

Early rubber had problems. It went sticky and smelly in summer and hard and cracked in winter. In 1839, the American inventor Charles Goodyear found that heating rubber with sulphur made it tough and stable. This process, called vulcanisation, made rubber useful for erasers, tyres, boots and much more.

In 1858, an American named Hymen Lipman patented a pencil with an eraser fitted into one end. In 1875, the US Supreme Court ruled that the patent was invalid, because joining two existing things wasn't a true invention. Pencils with erasers on top became common anyway, especially in American schools.

What erasers are made of now

Most erasers today aren't natural rubber. Many are made of vinyl, a kind of plastic, mixed with softeners. Vinyl erasers are usually white, rub out cleanly and leave fewer smudges. Others use synthetic rubber made in factories from chemicals that come from oil. Fancy erasers shaped like fruit or animals are popular in schools, but they are often harder and smear more than plain ones.

Pink erasers often contain a little fine grit to help lift graphite. Kneaded erasers, used by artists, are soft and stretchy like dough. You press them onto a drawing to lighten areas without rubbing at all.

Why ink is harder

Pen ink is a liquid that soaks into the paper's fibres and dries there. A soft eraser can't reach it. Ink erasers contain rough grit that grinds away the top layer of paper along with the ink. They often leave a thin or fuzzy patch, and can tear the page.

Some school pens use special erasable ink that fades when it's warmed by friction. Rubbing creates heat, and the heat makes the ink turn clear. Put the paper in a freezer, and some of the "erased" writing can come back.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Erasers work by scraping away the top layer of paper.

Truth: A soft eraser mostly lifts graphite off the paper's surface because graphite sticks to it better than to paper. Rough ink erasers do grind away paper, which is why they can leave a thin, scuffed patch.

Try this

Draw three short pencil lines on paper. Try to rub out one with a clean eraser, one with a small ball of soft bread and one with your finger. Compare which works best and look at what is left on the bread and on your finger.
