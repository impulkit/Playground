Why do cities want to host big events?

Montreal hosted the 1976 Olympics and took about 30 years to pay off the debt. Locals nicknamed its stadium the "Big Owe".

The short answer

Cities compete to host the Olympics, the football World Cup and other giant events because they hope to gain fame, tourists and new buildings. A big event puts a city on television screens around the world for weeks. Governments often use the deadline to build metro lines, airports and roads that might otherwise take decades.

But hosting is enormously expensive. Stadiums, athletes' villages, security and transport can cost billions of dollars, and studies show the costs almost always end up higher than planned. Whether it is worth it depends a lot on how the city plans, and on what it does with everything afterwards.

Surprising but true

The 1984 Los Angeles Olympics made a profit of over 200 million dollars, largely by reusing old stadiums and selling sponsorships.

The dream

Barcelona is the success story people often point to. Before the 1992 Olympics, it rebuilt a run-down industrial waterfront into beaches, a marina and new neighbourhoods. Tourism grew enormously in the following decades, and the city became one of Europe's most visited.

For India, the 1982 Asian Games in Delhi brought new flyovers, stadiums and hotels. They also brought colour television: Doordarshan began broadcasting in colour that year so that people could watch the Games. Before the 2010 Commonwealth Games, Delhi rapidly expanded its metro network.

The hangover

Montreal spent so much on the 1976 Olympics that the province of Quebec took about 30 years to pay off the debt, finishing in 2006. Its stadium's tower and roof ran years late. Many venues from the 2004 Athens Games sat empty and crumbling for years afterwards. Football's World Cup has the same problem. For the 2014 tournament, Brazil built a large stadium in Manaus, deep in the Amazon, for just four group matches. The city's own clubs rarely draw crowds anywhere near big enough to fill it.

The 2010 Delhi Commonwealth Games were hit by delays, cost overruns and corruption charges against officials, which embarrassed the country. Studies led by Bent Flyvbjerg at Oxford University found that every Olympics since 1960 for which data exist went over budget. Fixed deadlines are part of the problem: a stadium cannot be delayed for a year when the opening ceremony date is already set.

A different way to host

Los Angeles took another path in 1984. Few other cities wanted the Games after Montreal's troubles, so LA could set its own terms. It used existing stadiums, including the Los Angeles Memorial Coliseum, the main stadium of the 1932 Olympics, and paid for the rest with corporate sponsors and TV money. The Games ended with a surplus of over 200 million dollars.

LA plans to repeat the idea for 2028, using venues that already exist. The International Olympic Committee now encourages cities to reuse buildings and spread events across a region, partly because fewer cities have been willing to bid.

India's ambitions

India hopes to host a Summer Olympics, and in 2024 it formally told the International Olympic Committee it was interested in the 2036 Games. Meanwhile, Ahmedabad has been named as the host of the 2030 Commonwealth Games. Supporters say big events could build sports facilities, inspire young athletes and boost tourism. Sceptics warn about costs and ask whether the money would do more good spent on schools, hospitals and local playgrounds.

Good urban planning may matter more than the event itself. A stadium used every week by local clubs, or a metro line still crowded twenty years later, is the kind of legacy that makes the cost easier to defend.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Hosting the Olympics always makes a city richer.

Truth: Some hosts gained new transport and a better image, but many spent far more than they earned. Studies of the Games since 1960 found that every one with good data went over budget, and some left empty stadiums behind.

Try this

Plan a pretend sports day for your school on paper. List everything you would need, guess the cost of each item, and add it up. Then ask an adult to help you check real prices. How far off was your estimate? Most people guess too low, just like Olympic planners.
