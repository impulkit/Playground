What is ball lightning?

People have reported glowing balls drifting through storms, rooms and even aeroplanes. In 2012, scientists in China caught one by accident.

The short answer

Ball lightning is a rare glowing sphere that sometimes appears during thunderstorms. People describe it as about the size of a grapefruit or a football, glowing white, yellow, orange or blue. It floats or drifts sideways, often for a few seconds, and then fades away or vanishes with a pop.

For centuries, scientists weren't sure it was real, because nobody could predict where it would show up. Then in 2012, researchers in China recorded one by chance. It proved that the glowing balls exist. What makes them is still argued over.

Surprising but true

When Chinese scientists measured a ball of lightning in 2012, its light showed traces of silicon, iron and calcium, the same elements found in soil.

Thousands of eyewitnesses

Reports go back hundreds of years. In 1638, during a great thunderstorm in the village of Widecombe-in-the-Moor in England, people described a huge ball of fire crashing into the church during a service.

Modern accounts are calmer. People have seen glowing balls float through open windows, roll along fences or bounce off the ground. Pilots have described balls appearing inside aircraft cabins and drifting down the aisle. Most witnesses say it moved slowly and made little heat.

In a 1960s survey of about 2,000 NASA workers, roughly one in twenty said they had seen it. Since it is so brief, almost nobody has a camera ready.

Caught on camera

In July 2012, a team from Northwest Normal University in China was studying ordinary lightning on the Qinghai Plateau. They had high-speed cameras and spectrometers pointed at a storm. A spectrometer splits light into colours, a bit like a prism, showing which elements are glowing.

A bolt struck the ground. Then a glowing ball rose from the spot and moved for about a second and a half. Its light carried the fingerprints of silicon, iron and calcium, which are common in soil. The scientists published their results in 2014.

The soil theory

That matched an idea from two New Zealand scientists, John Abrahamson and James Dinniss. In 2000 they suggested that when lightning hits the ground, it can boil a little soil into a cloud of tiny silicon particles.

As those particles meet oxygen in the air, they slowly burn, giving off light. They stick together in a loose fluffy ball, which glows until the fuel runs out. In 2007, researchers in Brazil zapped thin wafers of silicon with electricity and made glowing balls that lasted several seconds.

Other ideas

The soil theory doesn't explain everything. It's hard to see how burning soil could appear inside a closed aeroplane. So scientists have other ideas.

One suggests ball lightning is a bubble of microwave energy trapped inside a shell of plasma, the glowing electrified gas that makes up lightning. Another, from physicists in Austria in 2010, proposed that some sightings aren't outside the eye at all. Strong magnetic fields from repeated lightning strikes might make the brain see a glowing spot. Doctors can produce flashes of light in people's vision with magnets, so it is possible, explained more in how your brain builds what you see.

Why it's so hard to study

Ball lightning can't be ordered on demand. It appears in storms, which are dangerous places to stand with instruments, and it lasts only seconds. Scientists rely on lucky recordings and careful lab experiments.

They also need to rule out other things. Some reports are probably sparks from power lines, St Elmo's fire glowing on ship masts, or the afterimage left in your eyes by a bright flash.

Safety first

Treat any storm with respect. Government records show that lightning kills more people in India than any other natural hazard, mostly people caught outdoors in the monsoon. If you hear thunder, head indoors and stay away from tall trees, open fields and water.

Buildings with lightning rods are much safer, because the rod gives the electricity an easy path to the ground. Meanwhile the mystery keeps its fans. Over a century ago, the inventor Nikola Tesla even claimed he had made glowing fireballs in his own lab.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ball lightning is just a story, because nobody has ever measured it.

Truth: For a long time that was a fair worry, since it relied on eyewitnesses. But in 2012 a natural ball was recorded on video and its light was measured, so scientists now know at least some reports are real.

Try this

On a dry day, rub a balloon on a woollen jumper and slowly bring it near a friend's hair or a small piece of tissue paper. You are seeing static electricity, the same kind of charge that builds up in thunderclouds, just millions of times weaker.
