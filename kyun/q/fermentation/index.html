How do tiny microbes make food tasty?

Curd, idli, bread, cheese and chocolate all need help from creatures far too small to see.

The short answer

Fermentation is what happens when microbes, living things too small to see, feed on the sugars in food. As they eat, they give off waste. Luckily for us, that waste is often delicious.

Some microbes make acids, which give curd and pickles their tang and stop harmful germs from growing. Some make bubbles of gas, which lift bread and idli. Some make alcohol, which is how grape juice becomes wine. People have used fermentation for thousands of years, long before anyone knew microbes existed.

Surprising but true

Chocolate starts with fermentation. Cacao beans are left in heaps for several days so microbes can work on them before they are dried and roasted.

Curd: milk with company

Put a spoon of old curd into warm milk and leave it overnight. By morning the milk has set. The spoon of curd carried millions of lactic acid bacteria. They feed on lactose, the natural sugar in milk, and make lactic acid.

The acid makes milk proteins clump together into a soft, wobbly gel, and it gives curd its tangy taste. Acid also makes life hard for many harmful germs, which is one reason curd keeps longer than fresh milk. Yogurt in the United States is made the same way, with closely related bacteria.

Idli, dosa and bread

South Indian idli and dosa batter is a mix of ground rice and urad dal, left to sit overnight. Bacteria and wild yeasts living on the grains get to work. They puff the batter with gas and give it a light sourness.

Bread works the same way. Bakers mix yeast into dough, and as it feeds, it breathes out carbon dioxide that makes the dough rise. In sourdough bread, made in San Francisco and many other places, wild yeasts and bacteria work together, like a tiny team that has been passed from loaf to loaf for years.

Who spotted the tiny cooks?

For most of history, nobody knew why milk turned to curd. In the 1670s Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, a Dutch cloth trader, built small, powerful lenses and saw "little animals" swimming in drops of water. These were the first microbes anyone had seen.

How did microscopes reveal a hidden world?In the 1670s, a Dutch cloth seller peered through a lens smaller than a pea and saw tiny creatures swimming in a drop of pond water. Nobody had ever seen them before.

Two hundred years later, the French scientist Louis Pasteur showed that fermentation was caused by living microbes, not by a chemical reaction on its own. His work helped explain why wine went sour and why some foods spoiled.

A kitchen full of microbes

Fermented foods are everywhere. Korean kimchi, German sauerkraut, Japanese soy sauce, vinegar, many cheeses and the kanji drink of north India all rely on microbes. Even chocolate starts this way: cacao beans are piled up and left to ferment for days, which builds up the flavours that later become chocolate.

Not every sour, long-lasting food is fermented, though. Many Indian pickles, or achar, keep for months mainly because of salt, oil and spices, which make it hard for spoiling microbes to grow. Others, like some carrot and mustard pickles, are left in the sun so that friendly bacteria can sour them slowly. Cooks learned which method suited which vegetable by trial and error over many generations.

The same idea shows up in cheese. Cheese makers add bacteria, and sometimes moulds, to milk curds, then leave them to age for weeks or years. Each mix of microbes gives a different smell and flavour. Paneer is different: it is set with lemon juice or vinegar and eaten fresh, with no ageing at all.

Scientists are now studying how eating fermented foods affects the friendly microbes in our gut. Early results are interesting but not settled. What is clear is that people across the world, with no microscopes at all, learned to team up with creatures they could not see.

Myth or fact?

Myth: All bacteria are harmful germs.

Truth: Only a small share of bacteria make people ill. Many are harmless, and some are useful, like the ones that turn milk into curd or that help digest food in your gut.

Try this

Mix two spoons of wheat flour with two spoons of water in a clean glass jar. Cover it loosely with a cloth, then add a spoon of each every day. Within a few days you should see bubbles and smell something sour. Do not eat it; just watch the microbes at work.
