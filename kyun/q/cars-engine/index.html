How does a car engine work?

A petrol engine makes thousands of tiny controlled bursts of burning every minute, and each one shoves a piston down.

The short answer

Most car engines are internal combustion engines, which means the fuel burns inside the engine itself. The engine has several metal tubes called cylinders. Inside each one, a tightly fitting piston slides up and down.

A tiny mist of petrol mixed with air is let into a cylinder. The piston squeezes it, and a spark plug lights it. The fuel burns in a flash, the hot gases expand and they shove the piston down hard. Each piston is joined to a crankshaft, a bent metal rod that turns the up-and-down push into spinning, a bit like your legs pushing bicycle pedals round. Through gears, that spin turns the wheels.

A four-cylinder engine at ordinary speed fires its cylinders thousands of times every minute.

Surprising but true

Only about a quarter of the energy in a car's petrol ends up moving the car. Most of the rest escapes as heat, from the exhaust and the radiator.

Suck, squeeze, bang, blow

Each cylinder goes through four steps, called strokes, over and over.

First comes intake. The piston slides down and a valve opens, drawing in air and fuel. Mechanics call this "suck". Then compression: the valves close and the piston moves up, squeezing the mixture into a small space. That's "squeeze". Next comes power. The spark plug fires, the fuel burns and the expanding gas pushes the piston down. That's "bang". Last is exhaust. Another valve opens and the piston moves up, pushing the burnt gases out to the exhaust pipe. That's "blow".

Only the power stroke actually pushes. The spinning crankshaft and the other cylinders carry the piston through the other three.

Otto, Benz and Bertha

In 1876, the German engineer Nikolaus Otto built a working four-stroke engine. Others turned it into transport. In 1886, Karl Benz patented a three-wheeled car with a small petrol engine. People weren't sure it was practical.

In August 1888, his wife, Bertha Benz, settled the question. Without telling her husband, she drove the car about 100 kilometres from Mannheim to her mother's home in Pforzheim with their two teenage sons. She bought fuel from a chemist's shop and fixed problems along the way. The trip showed the car could really be used, and the route is now a tourist trail.

Who built the first car?The first long drive in a petrol car was made in secret, in 1888, by a woman and her two teenage sons. They refuelled at a chemist's shop.

Where the energy goes

A car engine is not very efficient. Of all the energy stored in the petrol, only around a quarter actually moves the car. Most of the rest becomes heat. Some leaves through the exhaust, and a lot is carried away by the radiator, which pumps water around the engine to stop it overheating.

Burning petrol and diesel also makes carbon dioxide and other gases. Some of them add to air pollution and smog in cities like Delhi and Los Angeles. Carbon dioxide adds to climate change. Catalytic converters in exhaust pipes clean up some of the harmful gases, but they can't remove the carbon dioxide.

Petrol, diesel and beyond

Diesel engines, invented by Rudolf Diesel in the 1890s, have no spark plugs. They squeeze air so hard it becomes hot enough to light the fuel the moment it is sprayed in. They use less fuel than petrol engines, which is why most trucks, buses and many trains use them.

Many cities in India run buses and autorickshaws on CNG, compressed natural gas, which burns more cleanly than diesel. Hybrid cars pair an engine with a battery and an electric motor. And electric cars skip the burning entirely. Their motors have far fewer moving parts, and no pistons at all.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Petrol explodes inside a car engine.

Truth: The fuel and air burn extremely fast, but in a controlled way that spreads smoothly through the cylinder. A true explosion would damage the engine. When fuel does go off too suddenly, mechanics call it knocking, and engines are designed to prevent it.

Try this

Act out the four strokes with three friends. One person is the piston going down and drawing in air (suck), the next moves up to squeeze it (squeeze), the third claps for the spark and jumps down (bang), and the fourth pushes upwards to clear the exhaust (blow). Repeat the cycle faster and faster to feel how an engine speeds up.
