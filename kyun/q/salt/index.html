Why do we need salt?

Every message your brain sends to your muscles depends on salt, and in 1930 a handful of it helped shake the British Empire.

The short answer

Your body needs salt because it needs sodium, one of the two things salt is made of. Sodium helps nerves send electrical signals, so your brain can talk to your muscles. It helps your muscles, including your heart, squeeze. It also helps control how much water stays in your blood and cells.

You lose some sodium every day in sweat and urine, so you must replace it. But the body needs only a little. Most people eat far more than they need, mostly from packaged and restaurant food. Too much salt over many years can raise blood pressure, which strains the heart.

Surprising but true

Gujarat produces about three quarters of India's salt, much of it raked by hand from the flat, salty land of the Little Rann of Kutch.

How much is enough

The World Health Organization advises adults to eat less than 5 g of salt a day, about one level teaspoon. Worldwide, adults eat about twice that on average. Children need even less, depending on their size.

Your kidneys do the balancing. If you eat a salty snack, you feel thirsty, drink water, and your kidneys flush the extra sodium out. That's also why sweat tastes salty: it carries sodium out of your skin. Athletes and labourers working in the heat sometimes need extra salt to replace what they lose.

Where salt comes from

Most salt comes from the sea. Seawater is about 3.5% salt, which is why the sea tastes salty. In shallow pans near the coast, sunshine evaporates the water and leaves salt behind. In the Little Rann of Kutch in Gujarat, families called Agariyas pump salty groundwater into pans and rake the crystals by hand through the dry season.

Salt is also mined from underground layers left by ancient seas that dried up. The Khewra mine in Pakistan produces the pinkish rock salt sold as "Himalayan salt". In the USA, huge salt mines lie beneath Michigan, Ohio and Louisiana, and the Great Salt Lake in Utah is several times saltier than the ocean.

The march to the sea

In British India, the government held a monopoly on salt. Indians were not allowed to make or sell it freely, and they paid a tax on every bit they bought. Because everyone needs salt, the tax hit the poorest hardest.

On 12 March 1930, Mahatma Gandhi set out from his ashram near Ahmedabad with 78 followers. They walked for 24 days, about 385 km, to the coastal village of Dandi. On 6 April, Gandhi picked up a lump of natural salt from the shore, breaking the law. Across India, thousands followed his lead, and tens of thousands were arrested.

Salt as a preserver

Before fridges, salt was one of the best ways to keep food. Germs need water, and salt pulls water out of food and out of the germs themselves. Indian pickles, dried fish on the Konkan coast and salted meat on old sailing ships all relied on it.

Salt was so valuable that it was traded across deserts and taxed by kings in many lands. The word "salary" comes from the Latin salarium, which is related to salt. Some say Roman soldiers were paid in salt, but historians have found little evidence for that.

Iodine in the salt

In parts of India, especially the Himalayan foothills, many people once had goitre, a swelling of the thyroid gland in the neck caused by too little iodine. Iodine shortage can also slow children's growth and learning.

India began a national programme to fight it in 1962, and by the 1990s most salt sold for eating had to be iodised. A packet of ordinary iodised salt from a corner shop is one of the cheapest health measures in the world.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pink Himalayan salt is far healthier than ordinary salt.

Truth: Pink salt is about 98% sodium chloride, just like white salt. Its colour comes from traces of iron and other minerals, in amounts too small to make a difference. It usually has no added iodine.

Try this

Dissolve two spoons of salt in a cup of water and pour a thin layer onto a dark plate. Leave it in a sunny spot for a day or two. When the water has evaporated, look at the crystals with a magnifying glass. Many of them will be tiny cubes.
