How is writing turned into art around the world?

On the Taj Mahal, the carved letters around the great arch get bigger as they go up, so from the ground they all look the same size.

The short answer

Calligraphy is writing done as art. The word comes from Greek and means "beautiful writing". In many parts of the world, people have treated the shape of letters as seriously as painters treat colour.

The tools and rules change from place to place. Chinese calligraphers use a soft brush. Arabic calligraphers use a pen cut from reed. Monks in medieval Europe used quills made from feathers. In India, scribes wrote on palm leaves and paper in dozens of scripts. Wherever it grew, calligraphy turned something useful into something people hang on walls, carve into buildings and practise for a lifetime.

Surprising but true

Chinese calligraphy and Arabic calligraphy are both on UNESCO's list of the world's living heritage, the first in 2009 and the second in 2021.

The brush in China and Japan

In China, writing with a brush has been one of the highest arts for about 2,000 years. Each of the Chinese characters is made of strokes in a set order, and the calligrapher controls every one: pressing down for a thick line, lifting for a thin tail.

The most famous calligrapher, Wang Xizhi, lived in the 300s. A short piece he wrote at a poetry party by a stream became the most copied work in Chinese art. Japan learned brush writing from China and calls it shodo, "the way of writing". Japanese schoolchildren still practise it, kneeling over long strips of paper with black ink.

Children learn by copying model characters again and again. Some practise with plain water on special cloth that darkens when wet and fades as it dries, so one sheet lasts for years. In parks in Chinese cities, older people write huge characters on the pavement with water and long sponge brushes. The words vanish in minutes, and that is part of the pleasure.

The reed pen and the arch

Arabic calligraphy grew around copying the Quran. Islam traditionally avoids pictures of people and animals in sacred places, so mosques are often decorated with words instead. Over centuries, calligraphers developed many styles of Arabic script, from blocky Kufic to the flowing Thuluth.

That art came to India with Persian and Mughal rulers, who loved a graceful hanging style called nastaliq. On the Taj Mahal, verses of black stone are set into white marble around the arches. Amanat Khan, the calligrapher, signed his work there, which was rare for an artist of his time.

What is a font?We still say "upper case" and "lower case" because printers once kept capital letters in a wooden case above the case for small letters.

Monks, scribes and palm leaves

Before printing, every book in Europe was copied by hand, often by monks. The Book of Kells, made in Ireland around 800, fills its pages with twisting letters, painted animals and knots.

In India, scribes wrote on dried palm leaves with a metal stylus, scratching the letters and then rubbing in soot to darken them. Rounded scripts like Odia and Malayalam may have grown partly because straight lines along the leaf's grain could split it. Sikh scribes copied the Guru Granth Sahib by hand in Gurmukhi for centuries, and some still do.

From pens to screens

Calligraphy shaped the letters you read every day. In 1916 a calligrapher named Edward Johnston designed the lettering for the London Underground, still used today. Many computer fonts are based on letters first made with a pen or brush.

Steve Jobs, who started Apple, later said that a calligraphy class he took in college made him care about beautiful type on computers. Modern artists mix old and new, painting giant letters on walls or blending Devanagari with graffiti. In India, shop signs, wedding cards and film posters often still use hand-drawn Devanagari, Tamil or Bengali lettering, and calligraphy classes have become popular in many cities. The brush strokes may be digital now, but the rules about balance and space are centuries old.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Calligraphy is just very neat handwriting.

Truth: Neatness is only the start. Calligraphers plan spacing, rhythm and the thickness of every stroke, and some styles, like Chinese "grass script", flow so freely that even fluent readers find them hard to read.

Try this

Tape two sharpened pencils side by side with their points level. Write your name holding them at a slant, so both points touch the paper. You'll get hollow letters with thick and thin parts, just like a broad calligraphy pen. Try different angles and see which looks best.
