Why do some people sleepwalk?

A sleepwalker can open doors, walk downstairs and even make a sandwich, all with their eyes open, and remember none of it in the morning.

The short answer

Sleepwalking happens when part of the brain wakes up while the rest stays deeply asleep. The parts that control walking and simple actions switch on. The parts that handle thinking, judgement and memory stay switched off.

So a sleepwalker can sit up, walk around, open doors and even talk, often with their eyes wide open. But they are not really aware of what they are doing, and they usually remember nothing the next morning.

It is much more common in children than adults. Most children who sleepwalk grow out of it by their teenage years.

Surprising but true

Sleepwalking happens during deep sleep, not while dreaming. Most sleepwalkers are not acting out a dream at all.

Stuck between sleep and waking

Sleep comes in cycles, each about 90 minutes long. In each cycle, you drift from light sleep into deep sleep, then into dreaming sleep, called REM sleep, when your eyes flick around under your lids.

Sleepwalking almost always happens in deep sleep, usually in the first few hours of the night. Something partly wakes the brain, perhaps a noise or a full bladder, but it fails to wake up fully. The brain gets stuck in between, one foot in sleep and one in waking.

During REM sleep, your body is mostly paralysed, so you cannot act out dreams. That's why sleepwalkers are usually not acting out a dream. They are doing simple, automatic things.

What sleepwalkers do

Most sleepwalking is calm. Children might sit up, look around and lie down again. Some walk to another room, or to the kitchen, or try to get dressed. Many mumble or talk, though what they say often makes little sense.

Their eyes are usually open but glassy, and they may look straight through you. They move more clumsily than when awake. An episode can last from a few seconds to half an hour, then the person goes back to bed, often on their own.

Because they are not fully aware, sleepwalkers can hurt themselves on stairs, doors or windows. Families of regular sleepwalkers often lock outside doors, put gates on stairs and clear toys from the floor at night.

Why some people and not others

Sleepwalking runs in families. If a parent sleepwalked as a child, their children are much more likely to as well. Scientists think genes affect how easily the brain gets stuck in between sleep and waking.

Children sleepwalk more partly because they spend more time in deep sleep than adults. Their brains are still developing the switches that move smoothly from one sleep stage to another.

Several things make episodes more likely. The biggest is not getting enough sleep, followed by going to bed at irregular times. Fever, stress, noisy bedrooms and a full bladder at night can trigger it too.

A famous sleepwalker

Writers have used sleepwalking in stories for centuries. In William Shakespeare's play Macbeth, written around 1606, Lady Macbeth walks in her sleep, rubbing her hands as if trying to wash off a stain only she can see. A doctor and a servant watch her in secret. It is one of the most famous sleepwalking scenes ever written.

Real sleepwalking is usually far less dramatic. Still, doctors have recorded rare cases of sleepwalkers who cooked whole meals or even drove a car.

When to see a doctor

For most children, sleepwalking is harmless and fades with age. Simple things often help: a regular bedtime, enough sleep, a calm routine before bed and going to the toilet before lights out.

It is worth talking to a parent and a doctor if sleepwalking happens very often, if the person gets hurt or leaves the house, or if it starts in adulthood. A doctor might check for problems like snoring or breathing pauses that disturb sleep. Humans are built to be still and safe in the dark, unlike the nocturnal animals that do their busiest living at night.

Myth or fact?

Myth: It is dangerous to wake a sleepwalker. It could give them a heart attack or shock.

Truth: Waking a sleepwalker does not harm them. They may be confused, upset or grumpy for a moment, so it is best to do it gently, or simply guide them back to bed.

Try this

Keep a sleep diary for a week. Each morning, write what time you went to bed, what time you woke up, and how rested you feel from 1 to 5. Look for patterns. Many sleepwalkers find episodes happen after short or late nights.
