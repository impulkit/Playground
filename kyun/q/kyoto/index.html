What is Kyoto known for?

For over a thousand years this city was home to Japan's emperors, and a card company founded here later made Mario.

The short answer

Kyoto is a city in western Japan, ringed by green hills. It was Japan's capital and the home of its emperors from 794 until 1868, which is longer than the United States has existed, four times over. During those centuries it became the centre of Japanese art, religion, poetry and fine crafts.

Today it is a modern city of about 14 lakh people, with trains and offices. But it still has around 1,600 Buddhist temples and 400 Shinto shrines, along with old wooden houses, Zen gardens and teahouses. Seventeen of its temples, shrines and castles are together a UNESCO World Heritage Site. People come for the cherry blossoms in spring, red maple leaves in autumn, and a feeling of stepping back into old Japan.

Surprising but true

Nintendo, the company behind Mario and Pokémon games, was founded in Kyoto in 1889 to make hand-painted playing cards.

Built on a grid

When the emperor moved the capital here in 794, the new city was called Heian-kyō, meaning "capital of peace and tranquillity". Its planners copied Chang'an, the great capital of Tang China, laying out straight streets in a chessboard grid with the palace at the north.

That grid still shapes Kyoto's centre, and addresses are often given as directions: go up (north) or down (south) from a crossing. Around the year 1000, a court lady named Murasaki Shikibu wrote The Tale of Genji here, about life among the nobles. It is often called one of the world's first novels.

Gold, gates and bamboo

Kinkaku-ji, the Golden Pavilion, is covered in real gold leaf and reflected in a still pond. In 1950 a young monk set fire to it and it burned down. It was rebuilt in 1955, as close as possible to the old one.

At Fushimi Inari shrine, thousands of bright orange torii gates line paths that climb a whole mountain. Each gate was given by a person or business, with their name painted on it. On the western edge of the city, the paths of the Arashiyama grove run between stems of bamboo many metres tall that creak and click in the wind.

The city that was spared

During the Second World War, American bombers destroyed large parts of many Japanese cities. Kyoto was largely spared. In 1945, it was on an early list of targets for the atomic bomb. Henry Stimson, the US Secretary of War, had visited Kyoto as a tourist years earlier. He argued that destroying it would be a terrible loss and had it removed from the list.

Because of this, Kyoto still has a great many buildings that are hundreds of years old, more than almost any other big Japanese city.

Cards, games and a climate treaty

In 1889, a craftsman named Fusajiro Yamauchi started a small business in Kyoto making hanafuda, Japanese playing cards decorated with flowers. The company was called Nintendo. Many decades later it turned to toys and then to video games, and Mario, Zelda and the Nintendo Switch were born. Nintendo still has its headquarters in Kyoto.

In 1997, countries from around the world met in Kyoto and signed the Kyoto Protocol, one of the first big international agreements to cut the gases that cause climate change. So the name of an old imperial city is known by scientists and diplomats everywhere.

In the old Gion district, you may glimpse a young woman in a bright kimono and white make-up walking quickly to an appointment. In Kyoto, geisha are called geiko, and their trainees are called maiko. They spend years learning dance, music and the art of conversation.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Tokyo has always been Japan's capital.

Truth: Kyoto was the emperor's home for over a thousand years. Only in 1868–69 did the young Emperor Meiji move to Edo, which was renamed Tokyo, meaning "eastern capital".

Kyoto was laid out on a neat grid, copied from an old Chinese capital. Draw a grid of streets on squared paper, number the north–south streets and name the east–west ones. Then write directions to a friend's "house" using only the street names, the way Kyoto addresses still work.
