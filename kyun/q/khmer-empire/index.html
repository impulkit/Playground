Who built Angkor?

Angkor Wat, the largest religious monument on Earth, was built in Cambodia about 900 years ago as a Hindu temple to Vishnu.

The short answer

Angkor was built by the Khmer, the people of Cambodia. From about 802 CE to the 1400s, their kings ruled a powerful empire across much of South-East Asia, covering parts of today's Cambodia, Thailand, Laos and Vietnam.

Their capital, Angkor, grew into one of the largest cities in the world before modern times. Its kings built hundreds of stone temples there. The most famous is Angkor Wat, built in the early 1100s by King Suryavarman II.

Angkor Wat was first a Hindu temple to the god Vishnu. Later it became a Buddhist temple, which it still is. It is so important to Cambodians that it appears on the national flag.

Surprising but true

In 2012 and 2015, scientists flew over the jungle with laser scanners and found the outlines of whole lost neighbourhoods, roads and ponds hidden under the trees around Angkor.

Ideas from India

Long before Angkor, traders and priests from India sailed to South-East Asia. They brought Sanskrit, Hindu gods and Buddhist teachings, and stories like the Ramayana and the Mahabharata. Local rulers blended these ideas with their own traditions.

So Khmer kings took Sanskrit names ending in "varman", meaning protector. Their temples were built as mountains for the gods, like Mount Meru in Hindu and Buddhist stories, the centre of the universe. The walls of Angkor Wat are carved with Hindu scenes, including the gods and demons churning the ocean of milk.

Building Angkor Wat

Angkor Wat covers a huge area. A moat about 190 m wide surrounds it, so wide it looks like a lake. A long stone causeway crosses the moat to the gates. Inside, galleries and courtyards rise towards five tall towers shaped like lotus buds.

The builders used sandstone from quarries in the Kulen hills, about 40 km away. Blocks were probably floated along canals on rafts. Archaeologists estimate that several million blocks were used, some weighing over a tonne. They were cut to fit together tightly, often without mortar.

The walls are covered with carvings. One gallery alone has carved panels hundreds of metres long showing battles, gods and the king himself.

A city of water

Angkor depended on water. The monsoon brought heavy rain for part of the year, then months of dry weather. To keep rice growing, the Khmer dug a vast system of canals and giant reservoirs called barays. The largest, the West Baray, is about 8 km long and over 2 km wide.

In 2012 and 2015, scientists used lidar, a way of mapping with lasers fired from aircraft, to see through the forest. They found the faint outlines of streets, houses, ponds and fields spreading for many kilometres around the temples. Angkor had been far bigger than anyone thought.

The Buddhist king

Around 1181 King Jayavarman VII came to power. He was a Buddhist, and he built a new walled city, Angkor Thom. At its heart is the Bayon temple, with more than 200 huge calm stone faces gazing in every direction.

He also built roads, rest houses and hospitals across the empire. Another of his temples, Ta Prohm, has been left partly wrapped in the roots of giant trees, which makes it one of the most photographed places in Asia.

Decline and rediscovery

From the 1300s, the Khmer Empire grew weaker. Wars with the Thai kingdom of Ayutthaya, changes in trade, and long droughts followed by floods that damaged the canals all probably played a part. By the 1400s the capital had moved south, towards Phnom Penh.

Monks kept worshipping at Angkor Wat. In the 1860s the writings of a French traveller, Henri Mouhot, made it famous in Europe. Since then many countries have helped repair the temples. From 1986 to 1993, experts from the Archaeological Survey of India worked on cleaning and strengthening Angkor Wat. Today crowds of visitors from around the world walk its causeway at dawn to watch the sun rise behind the towers.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Angkor was a lost city until a French explorer discovered it in the 1860s.

Truth: Cambodians never forgot Angkor, and Buddhist monks kept using Angkor Wat. Henri Mouhot's writings in the 1860s made it famous in Europe, but it was not lost to the people who lived nearby.
