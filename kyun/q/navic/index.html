What is India's NavIC navigation system?

India built its own version of GPS so it would never have to depend on another country's satellites to know where it is.

The short answer

NavIC is India's own satellite navigation system. Like the American GPS, it uses satellites that send out precise time signals. A receiver on the ground, in a phone, ship or truck, picks up those signals and works out where it is.

The difference is size. GPS covers the whole Earth with about 30 satellites. NavIC was designed to cover only India and a zone around 1,500 km beyond its borders, using a planned seven satellites. That smaller job means fewer satellites, placed very high above the Indian Ocean so they always stay in view. The system was built by ISRO, and the Indian government controls it, so it cannot be switched off by anyone else.

Surprising but true

In Hindi and several other Indian languages, "navik" means sailor or navigator, so NavIC's name is a small pun.

Why India wanted its own

Satellite navigation became part of everyday life in the 1990s and 2000s. Ships, planes, banks, power grids and armies all came to rely on it. Yet the best-known systems belonged to the USA and Russia.

A story often told in India is that during the Kargil conflict in 1999, India asked for detailed GPS data and did not get it. Whatever the exact details, the lesson was clear to planners: a country that leans on someone else's satellites can be left in the dark. India approved its own regional system in 2006.

How it works

Every NavIC satellite carries atomic clocks, which keep time to within a few billionths of a second. Each satellite keeps broadcasting the exact time and its own position.

A receiver on the ground hears these signals a tiny moment after they were sent. Radio travels at the speed of light, so a delay of one millionth of a second means the satellite is 300 metres further away. Measuring the delay from several satellites tells the receiver how far it is from each one. With four, it can work out its position, height and the exact time. The maths is the same as with GPS, only with different satellites.

How does GPS know where you are?Your phone finds your place on Earth by timing radio signals from satellites 20,000 km away, to within billionths of a second.

Who uses it

Indian fishing boats can carry NavIC receivers that show their position and receive messages about storms and sea borders, useful far from mobile phone towers. Trucks, buses and trains use it for tracking. Since 2020, several phone chips can pick up NavIC signals alongside GPS, and the Indian government has pushed phone makers to support it.

Banks, telecom networks and power grids also use its precise timing to keep their computers in step.

A rocky road

NavIC has had real trouble. Its first satellite went up in July 2013. Soon after, atomic clocks on several satellites began to fail, and one launch in 2017 failed when the rocket's nose cone did not open. A new generation began with NVS-01 in 2023, which carried an atomic clock made in India.

In January 2025, ISRO's 100th launch from Sriharikota carried NVS-02, but a valve problem left it stuck in the wrong orbit. By July 2026, the government told Parliament that only three satellites were working. That is one short of the four needed for a position fix, so NavIC could provide accurate timing but not standalone positioning. On 30 October 2026, a GSLV rocket carried a new satellite, NVS-03, into orbit. Once it is tested and in place, it should bring the count back to four, with more launches planned. For now, phones in India mostly mix NavIC with GPS and other systems, and users hardly notice.

A crowd of systems

Several countries now run their own navigation satellites. Russia has GLONASS, Europe has Galileo and China has BeiDou. Japan, like India, has a regional system. A modern phone may listen to dozens of satellites from four or five systems at once, picking whichever give the cleanest signals. That is one reason a map app can find you on a narrow lane in Old Delhi or a Chicago street lined with skyscrapers.

Myth or fact?

Myth: NavIC and GPS are the same thing with different names.

Truth: GPS belongs to the USA and covers the whole planet. NavIC is India's own regional system, covering India and the area around it. Many phones can listen to both, plus Russia's, Europe's and China's systems.

Try this

Stand three friends in a playground and give each a piece of string of a different length. Hold the free ends together and find the only spot where all three strings reach without slack. That is trilateration, the geometry that satellite navigation uses.
