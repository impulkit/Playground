Who is Sherlock Holmes?

In 1893 Arthur Conan Doyle killed off Sherlock Holmes at a Swiss waterfall. Readers were so upset that, ten years later, he brought him back.

The short answer

Sherlock Holmes is a made-up detective, one of the most famous characters in any book. The Scottish doctor and writer Arthur Conan Doyle created him in 1887. Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street in London and solves crimes that baffle the police.

His method is simple to say and hard to do. He notices tiny details other people miss, such as a scuff on a boot, a stain on a sleeve or a smell of tobacco. Then he reasons his way from those clues to the one explanation that fits them all.

His friend Dr John Watson, an army doctor, narrates almost all the stories. Watson is clever too, but slower, which lets readers see how surprising Holmes's thinking really is.

Surprising but true

Sherlock Holmes never says "Elementary, my dear Watson" in any of the original stories.

A doctor's idea

Conan Doyle was a young doctor with few patients and time to spare. He had studied at Edinburgh under a surgeon named Joseph Bell, who loved to guess where patients came from and what work they did just by looking at them. Doyle said Bell was a model for Holmes.

The first Holmes story, A Study in Scarlet, appeared in a Christmas magazine in 1887 and drew little notice. Things changed in 1891, when short Holmes stories began appearing in The Strand Magazine. People queued at newsstands for the next one. Each story was complete in itself, so a reader could pick up any issue and follow along. That was new, and it made both the magazine and its detective famous across Britain and the USA.

The death that didn't last

Conan Doyle grew tired of his detective. He wanted to be known for serious historical novels. So in "The Final Problem", published in 1893, Holmes wrestles his enemy Professor Moriarty and both seem to fall to their deaths at the Reichenbach Falls in Switzerland.

Readers were furious. Doyle held out for years. Then came The Hound of the Baskervilles, a novel set before the fall, in 1901 and 1902. In 1903 Holmes properly returned, explaining that he had never gone over the edge at all. In the end there were four novels and 56 short stories.

Science on the page

Holmes was written at a time when science was changing police work. He carries a magnifying glass, studies soil and tobacco ash, and runs chemistry experiments in his sitting room. He even talks about how footprints and handwriting can betray a criminal.

Real detectives at the time were only beginning to use such methods. A few years after the early stories, police in Bengal and then Britain began using fingerprints to identify people.

The deerstalker hat

Picture Holmes and you probably see a cape and a cap with flaps over the ears, called a deerstalker. Conan Doyle never named either one in the stories. The look came from Sidney Paget, the artist who drew Holmes for The Strand, and stage actors made it stick. The curved pipe came from the theatre too. In the books, Holmes plays the violin while he thinks, sometimes for hours.

Detectives everywhere

Holmes inspired a whole family of detective stories. In Bengal, Sharadindu Bandyopadhyay created Byomkesh Bakshi in the 1930s, a thinker who called himself a "truth-seeker" rather than a detective. Satyajit Ray later wrote about Feluda, whose friend Topshe tells the stories much as Watson does, and Feluda openly admires Holmes.

Holmes has been played by hundreds of actors on stage, film and television, from Hollywood to Russia. Every week, a museum at 221B Baker Street, an address that didn't exist when Doyle wrote, welcomes visitors who come to see the home of a man who never lived.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Sherlock Holmes often says "Elementary, my dear Watson."

Truth: The exact phrase never appears in Conan Doyle's stories. Holmes says "Elementary" once and "my dear Watson" often, but never together. The line became famous through later plays and films.

Play a Holmes game with a friend. Look carefully at their shoes, hands and school bag for one minute, then make three guesses about what they did that morning, giving a clue for each. Swap roles and see whose deductions hold up best.
