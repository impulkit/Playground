How is cotton turned into cloth?

Your T-shirt began as fluffy fibres wrapped around seeds on a plant, and people in India were spinning them into cloth around 7,000 years ago.

The short answer

Cotton grows on a bushy plant. After it flowers, it forms a green pod called a boll. When the boll ripens, it splits open and a ball of soft white fibres puffs out, clinging to the seeds inside.

Farmers pick the fluffy bolls. A machine called a gin pulls the seeds out. The fibres are cleaned and combed straight, then twisted into long threads, which is called spinning. The threads are woven or knitted into cloth, then dyed or printed. A pair of jeans or a school uniform is the end of that long chain.

Surprising but true

Some people in medieval Europe believed cotton came from a plant that grew tiny sheep on its branches.

Seeds with fluffy coats

Each cotton fibre is a single plant cell, grown long and thin from the skin of a seed. In the wild, the fluff helps seeds float away on the wind or drift on water.

The fibres are mostly cellulose, the same stuff that makes paper. Under a microscope, a dried cotton fibre looks like a flat, twisted ribbon. Those natural twists help fibres grip each other when they are spun, which is why cotton makes strong, soft thread that soaks up sweat.

An Indian invention

People in South Asia were among the first to spin cotton. Archaeologists at Mehrgarh, in present-day Pakistan, found cotton threads inside a copper bead about 7,000 years old. In the Indus cities, people grew cotton and wore woven cloth.

For thousands of years, Indian cotton cloth was famous around the world. Fine muslin from Dhaka was so light that it was compared to woven air. Calico from Calicut and chintz prints were traded to Rome, Arabia and later Europe.

From fluff to thread

For most of history, all this work was done by hand. A small roller gin, called a charkhi in India, squeezed seeds out of the fibre. Then spinners twisted fibres into yarn on a spindle or a spinning wheel.

The thread was woven on a loom. Long threads called the warp are stretched tight, and the weaver passes another thread, the weft, over and under them. The pattern of over and under decides whether the cloth is plain, twilled like denim, or patterned.

Machines take over

In the 1700s, British inventors built spinning machines, and factories in Lancashire began making cotton cloth by the mile. This was a key part of the Industrial Revolution. Cheap factory cloth flooded India and hurt millions of hand-spinners and weavers.

What was the Industrial Revolution?About 250 years ago, machines began doing work that hands had done for thousands of years, and the way people lived changed faster than ever before.

In the United States, Eli Whitney's cotton gin of 1794 cleaned cotton far faster. Cotton farming spread across the South, and so did slavery, because plantation owners forced enslaved people to grow and pick it. The story of American cotton is tied to the story of slavery and freedom.

Spinning for freedom

In the 1920s, Mahatma Gandhi asked Indians to spin their own thread on a charkha and wear handwoven khadi instead of British mill cloth. Spinning became a sign of self-reliance during the freedom struggle. The charkha even appeared on an early version of the Indian flag.

Cotton today

India and China are now the world's largest cotton growers, and Texas grows the most in the United States. Growing cotton can use a lot of water and pesticides, so some farmers are trying new methods. A cotton T-shirt is simple to wear, but it carries a very long story.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Cotton grows on plants that sprout tiny lambs.

Truth: Medieval Europeans had never seen a cotton plant and heard only travellers' tales of "wool" growing on trees. Some imagined a lamb on a stalk. The idea lives on in German, where cotton is called Baumwolle, meaning tree wool.

Try this

Take a small tuft of cotton wool from a first-aid kit. Gently pull and twist it between your fingers, adding more fluff as you go, until it becomes a thin thread. Test how strong it is, then try with an untwisted tuft. Twisting is the whole secret of spinning.
