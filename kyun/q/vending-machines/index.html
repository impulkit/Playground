How does a vending machine know your coin?

Drop a coin into a vending machine and, in under a second, it weighs the coin, measures it and checks its metal.

The short answer

A coin rolls down a short track inside the machine, and on the way it is tested several times. Gaps and slots check that it is the right size and thickness. A sensor checks its weight. Then comes the clever part: magnets and electric coils check what metal it is made of.

Every kind of coin is made of a particular mix of metals, called an alloy. Each alloy reacts to magnetic fields in its own way, almost like a fingerprint. If all the tests match a real coin, a little gate flips and sends the coin into the cash box. If not, the gate sends it to the return slot.

Surprising but true

The first known coin-operated machine was built about 2,000 years ago in Egypt. It sold holy water.

A splash of holy water

The idea is very old. Hero of Alexandria, an engineer in Roman Egypt, described a coin machine in the 1st century CE. A visitor dropped a bronze coin into a slot at a temple. The coin fell onto a little tray attached to a lever. Its weight tipped the lever, which opened a valve, and holy water trickled out. When the coin slid off the tray, the lever sprang back and the water stopped.

Coin machines came back in the 1800s. In London in the 1880s, machines sold postcards. Soon there were machines selling chewing gum in New York and chocolate in railway stations.

The magnet test

Many coins aren't magnetic at all, yet magnets can still test them. When a metal coin moves past a strong magnet, tiny swirls of electric current form inside the coin. These currents push back against the magnet and slow the coin down.

How much it slows depends on the metal. Copper slows a lot, while some steel and nickel alloys behave very differently. Some coins also stick to the magnet. A sensor times the coin as it passes, and a fake made of the wrong metal moves at the wrong speed. A metal detector uses a similar trick to find coins buried in sand.

Modern machines often use electric coils instead of fixed magnets. The coil sends out a magnetic field and measures how the coin changes it. A small computer compares the result with a stored list of real coins.

How does a metal detector find coins?A metal detector never touches the coin. It makes the coin send back a tiny magnetic echo, and listens for it.

What about notes?

Paper money is harder to check. A note reader pulls the note in with rollers and shines light through it and onto it. Sensors look at the patterns, the watermark and the size. Many notes, including Indian rupees, have special inks and security threads that react to ultraviolet light or magnets. If anything looks wrong, the rollers push the note back out.

Japan and India

Japan is famous for its vending machines. Even in 2025, after years of decline, it had close to 2 million machines selling drinks alone. Some serve hot soup, umbrellas or fresh eggs. Part of the reason is that crime is low, so machines can stand on quiet street corners without being broken open.

In India, vending machines have spread in a different way. Railway stations have ticket machines, and offices have tea and coffee machines. Many newer machines barely bother with coins. You scan a QR code and pay with UPI on your phone, and the machine checks with the bank before dropping your snack.

Making a fair machine

A good vending machine has to be honest both ways. It must reject fake coins, but it mustn't swallow real ones. Coins get scratched, dented and dirty over years, so engineers allow a little wiggle room in every test. When a country brings out a new coin, machine owners have to update their checkers, or the machines will refuse it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If a snack gets stuck, rocking or shaking the machine is a harmless way to get it out.

Truth: Vending machines are very heavy, often several hundred kilograms. Machines rocked by people have tipped over and caused serious injuries. It's safer to tell the owner.

Try this

Collect a few different coins. Line them up by size with a ruler, then weigh them on a kitchen scale if you have one. Finally, hold a fridge magnet near each. Some will stick and some won't, and a vending machine notices all three differences.
