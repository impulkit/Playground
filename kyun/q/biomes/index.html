What are biomes?

Climb a tall mountain near the equator and you pass through rainforest, cool forest, grassland and icy tundra in a single day, like walking from the tropics to the Arctic.

The short answer

A biome is a large area of the Earth with a similar climate, plants and animals. Rainforests, deserts, grasslands and tundra are all biomes.

The two biggest things that shape a biome are temperature and rainfall. Together they decide which plants can grow. The plants then decide which animals can live there, since animals need them for food and shelter.

Biomes are a way of grouping places that are alike, even when they are far apart. The Thar Desert in India and the Mojave Desert in the USA are on opposite sides of the world. Yet both are deserts, with spiky plants and animals that can go a long time without water.

Surprising but true

Deserts and the Arctic tundra both get very little rain or snow, which is why some scientists describe parts of the frozen Arctic as cold deserts.

The main land biomes

Scientists split the land up in slightly different ways, but most lists include these.

Forests are among the biggest biomes, covering nearly a third of Earth's land. There are hot, wet rainforests, forests that lose their leaves in a dry season or in winter, and cold northern forests of pine and spruce.

Grasslands get enough rain for grass but not usually for many trees. Africa's savannas, America's Great Plains and India's own grasslands, such as Banni in Gujarat, are examples.

Deserts get very little rain. Some are hot, like the Sahara and the Thar. Others, like the Gobi and parts of Ladakh, are cold.

Tundra is cold, treeless land in the far north and high on mountains, where the summer is short.

Life fits the biome

Each biome has its own tricks for survival. Desert plants like cactuses store water in thick stems and have spines instead of leaves. Camels can go days without drinking.

Rainforest plants grow huge leaves to catch light under the thick canopy. In grasslands, grazing animals such as bison, zebras and blackbuck live in herds, partly for safety in the open.

In the tundra, plants grow low and small, and animals like Arctic foxes have thick fur. Many tundra birds fly south for winter.

Which land animals migrate?Every year, more than a million wildebeest walk in a giant loop across Tanzania and Kenya, chasing the rain.

A mountain is many biomes

In 1802 a German explorer named Alexander von Humboldt climbed high up Chimborazo, a volcano in Ecuador near the equator. He noticed that the plants changed in bands as he climbed.

At the bottom were tropical forests. Higher up came cooler forests, then grasses and shrubs, then mosses and lichens, and finally snow. Climbing a few kilometres up had the same effect as travelling thousands of kilometres towards the poles.

Humboldt drew a famous picture of the mountain with each band of plants labelled. It helped scientists see that climate shapes life in the same patterns everywhere. You can see the same thing in the Himalayas, going from Sal forests in the foothills to alpine meadows and bare rock.

Biomes under water

Water has biomes too. Freshwater biomes include rivers, lakes and ponds. Marine biomes cover the oceans, from coral reefs to the deep sea. Where rivers meet the sea, mangroves and wetlands form special places between the two, like the Sundarbans on the coast of West Bengal and Bangladesh.

India, a land of many biomes

Few countries have as many biomes as India. On a long trip across the country you can pass from the cold desert of Ladakh to the green rainforests of the Western Ghats. Further east lie the grasslands of central India and the mangroves of the Sundarbans.

The USA is varied too, with Alaska's tundra, Arizona's deserts, the prairies of the Midwest and the damp forests of the Pacific Northwest. As the climate warms, scientists are watching biome edges creep: trees moving up mountains and north into the tundra, and some dry lands spreading.

Myth or fact?

Myth: All deserts are hot and sandy.

Truth: A desert is defined by how little rain or snow it gets, not by heat. Ladakh and the Gobi have freezing winters, Antarctica is the biggest desert of all, and most deserts are covered in rock and gravel rather than sand dunes.

Try this

Make a biome in a jar. Put a layer of small stones in a clear plastic jar, then soil, then a few small plants or seeds such as mustard. Keep one jar damp and sunny and another dry. After two weeks, compare which plants thrived, just as different plants win in different biomes.
