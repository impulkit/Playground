Why are fairy tales so old?

Some scientists think a few fairy tales are older than writing, older even than the wheel.

The short answer

Fairy tales are old because they are easy to remember and fun to retell. They have simple shapes: three brothers, three wishes, a forest, a wicked giant, a clever youngest child. Anyone can carry one in their head and pass it on, without a book.

Many of the tales we know were written down only in the last 300 years. But that is just when collectors caught them. The stories themselves had been told aloud, by grandparents and travelling storytellers, for much longer. Some may be thousands of years old.

Surprising but true

A story very like Cinderella, with a lost shoe and a king searching for its owner, was written down in ancient times about a girl called Rhodopis in Egypt.

Caught on paper

In 1697 a French writer called Charles Perrault published tales including Cinderella, Puss in Boots and Little Red Riding Hood. In 1812 two brothers in Germany, Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm, published the first volume of their collection. It included Hansel and Gretel, Rapunzel and Snow White.

The Grimms were language scholars. They believed old tales held clues to the past of the German language and people. They gathered stories from friends, neighbours and older books, then rewrote them over many editions.

In Bengal, Dakshinaranjan Mitra Majumder collected tales from village grandmothers and published them in 1907 as Thakurmar Jhuli, "Grandmother's Bag". It is still a favourite, with its demons, princes and talking birds. India has many such collections, and an even older tradition of telling stories aloud.

A family tree of stories

Folklorists, people who study traditional stories, sort tales into "types". Cinderella is a type. So is the dragon-slayer, and the clever animal who tricks a stronger one. One famous catalogue lists more than 2,000 types found around the world.

In 2016, a folklorist, Sara Graça da Silva, and an anthropologist, Jamshid Tehrani, tried something new. They treated tales like living things that evolve, and compared versions in many related languages. They looked for which tales were shared by languages that split apart long ago.

Their study suggested that Beauty and the Beast and Rumpelstiltskin may be around 4,000 years old. A tale called The Smith and the Devil, about a blacksmith who outwits an evil being, might go back 6,000 years. Other experts are cautious. Stories jump easily between neighbours, so a shared tale does not always mean a shared ancestor.

Why the same stories turn up everywhere

There are two main explanations, and both are probably true. Some stories spread from place to place with traders, travellers and marriages. A tale heard in a market could cross a sea in one lifetime. The Arabian Nights and the Panchatantra both travelled this way, through many languages.

Other stories may grow up separately because people everywhere share the same hopes and fears. Losing a parent, being bullied by bigger siblings, getting lost, or rising from poverty to riches are worries in every culture.

Dark beginnings

Early versions were often much darker than today's films. In some old Cinderella stories the stepsisters are punished cruelly. In Perrault's Little Red Riding Hood, nobody comes to the rescue. The Grimms softened many tales in later editions, because by then parents were buying the books for children.

Writers keep inventing new fairy tales too. Hans Christian Andersen in Denmark wrote The Little Mermaid and The Ugly Duckling in the 1830s and 1840s. They felt so traditional that many readers assumed they were centuries old.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Brothers Grimm made up their fairy tales.

Truth: The Grimms collected tales from storytellers and older books. They did edit them heavily, and later editions became gentler and more suitable for children.

Try this

Ask the oldest person in your family to tell you a story they heard as a child. Write it down, then compare it with a fairy tale you know. Look for the same shapes, such as three tries, a magic helper or a cruel stepmother.
