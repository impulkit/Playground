Who was Susan B. Anthony?

In 1872, Susan B. Anthony was arrested for voting in a US election. She was fined 100 dollars and refused ever to pay it.

The short answer

Susan B. Anthony was an American campaigner who spent more than 50 years fighting for women's rights, especially the right to vote. She gave speeches across the country, wrote petitions, ran a newspaper and lobbied Congress year after year.

In 1872, she voted in a presidential election, which was illegal for women then, and was arrested. She died in 1906, before she could see women win the vote. But in 1920, the 19th Amendment to the US Constitution gave American women that right. It was nicknamed the "Susan B. Anthony Amendment" in her honour.

Surprising but true

Susan B. Anthony died in 1906, 14 years before American women won the vote. The amendment that finally gave it to them was nicknamed the "Susan B. Anthony Amendment".

A Quaker childhood

Anthony was born in 1820 in Massachusetts into a Quaker family. Quakers believed men and women were equal before God, and girls were expected to be educated. Her father, a cotton mill owner, was against slavery and alcohol.

She became a teacher and soon noticed that she was paid far less than male teachers for the same work. She began speaking out, first against slavery and against the harm caused by heavy drinking. When she wasn't allowed to speak at a temperance meeting because she was a woman, she started to focus on women's rights.

A famous partnership

In 1851, Anthony met Elizabeth Cady Stanton, who had helped organise the Seneca Falls Convention, the first big women's rights meeting in the US, in 1848. The two became close friends and partners for the next 50 years.

Stanton, a mother of seven, was the brilliant writer. Anthony, who never married, was the organiser and traveller. Stanton once said that she forged the thunderbolts and Anthony fired them. Together, they founded the National Woman Suffrage Association in 1869.

Voting and arrest

After the Civil War, the US Constitution was amended to protect the rights of formerly enslaved people. Anthony argued that these amendments, which spoke of the rights of "citizens", already gave women the vote, since women were citizens.

On 5 November 1872, she and about 15 other women in Rochester, New York, cast ballots in the presidential election. Two weeks later, a marshal arrested her. At her trial in 1873, Judge Ward Hunt did not let her testify and ordered the jury to find her guilty. He fined her 100 dollars. She told him she would never pay, and she never did.

Disagreements in the movement

The fight for rights was not always united. After the Civil War, Congress proposed the 15th Amendment to give Black men the vote, but not women. Anthony and Stanton opposed it unless women were included. Others, including Frederick Douglass, who had long supported women's rights, believed Black men urgently needed the vote first.

The split was bitter, and some of Anthony and Stanton's arguments at the time used racist language. Historians today note this as part of her record. The two sides of the women's movement reunited in 1890.

The vote, at last

Anthony kept travelling and speaking into her 80s. In 1906, at her last birthday celebration, she said "Failure is impossible". She died a month later.

Fourteen years after her death, in 1920, the 19th Amendment became part of the US Constitution. It says that the right to vote cannot be denied because of sex. Many Black women in the South, though, were still blocked from voting by unfair local rules until the 1960s.

In 1979, Anthony became the first real woman to appear on a regularly circulating US coin, the Susan B. Anthony dollar. Her house in Rochester is now a museum. On election days, voters in the city sometimes leave their "I Voted" stickers on her gravestone. In India, women won the right to vote with the first constitution in 1950, on the same terms as men.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Susan B. Anthony won women the right to vote in her lifetime.

Truth: She campaigned for over 50 years but died in 1906. Women across the US won the vote in 1920, after a new generation of campaigners carried on her work.
