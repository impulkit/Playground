What does it take to be an umpire or referee?

Football's yellow and red cards were dreamed up by an English referee who was sitting at a traffic light in London.

The short answer

Umpires and referees are the people who apply the rules in a game. In cricket, tennis and baseball they are usually called umpires. In football, basketball and hockey they are referees. Either way, their job is to see what happened, decide in a split second, and stick to the decision while thousands of people shout at them.

To get there, officials study thick rulebooks, pass exams, stay fit and work their way up from school and local matches. Top football referees train like athletes. Top cricket umpires stand for six or seven hours a day, focusing on every ball. Most have one thing in common: they love a sport but never played it at the very highest level.

Surprising but true

A top football referee often runs more than 10 km in a single match, about as much as some of the players.

Knowing the laws by heart

Every sport has its rulebook. Cricket's Laws have been looked after by the Marylebone Cricket Club in London since 1788. Football's Laws of the Game are set by the International Football Association Board, formed in 1886. Officials must know these rules well enough to apply them without thinking, from the tricky leg before wicket law in cricket to offside in football. The rulebooks change too, so officials have to keep relearning them.

New umpires and referees take courses and written tests. In India, cricket umpires climb through state and national exams before they officiate in big matches. Referees are watched and graded after games, and the best are promoted. A single mistake in a big match can follow them for years.

Fit, focused and brave

Football referees need to keep up with play. In a top match, a referee often runs more than 10 km, with lots of short sprints. They take regular fitness tests, and many train with sports scientists.

Cricket umpires need a different kind of stamina. They stand in sun or cold for a whole day, judging whether a ball would have hit the stumps or brushed the bat. Calm matters just as much. Officials learn to explain decisions clearly and to stay steady when players, coaches and crowds disagree.

Signals the whole ground can read

Officials speak with their hands as much as their voices. A cricket umpire raises one finger for out and lifts both arms high for a six. A football referee points to the spot for a penalty. In baseball, the home plate umpire calls a strike with a sharp punch or point of the right arm, so even fans in the farthest seats can follow.

Football's coloured cards came from a moment at a traffic light. At the 1966 World Cup, there was confusion about which players had been warned. Afterwards, English referee Ken Aston, stopped at a traffic light in London, realised that colours speak every language. Yellow and red cards were first used at the 1970 World Cup in Mexico.

Help from cameras

In November 1992, in a Test match in Durban, Sachin Tendulkar became the first batter given out by a third umpire watching TV replays. Since then, technology has grown fast. Cricket added a full review system with ball-tracking around 2008 and 2009. Tennis began using Hawk-Eye at big tournaments in 2006, and in 2025 Wimbledon replaced its line judges with electronic calling.

How do computers see?In 1966, a professor in Massachusetts thought teaching computers to see would make a good summer project for students. Experts are still working on it.

Football introduced video assistant referees, or VAR, at the 2018 men's World Cup. In 2022, Stéphanie Frappart of France became the first woman to referee a men's World Cup match. Cameras catch things eyes can miss, but people still have to decide what a replay means, and arguments about fairness haven't gone away. Players, fans and officials still argue about handballs and offside lines measured in centimetres, much as they did before any camera arrived.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ball-tracking systems like Hawk-Eye are never wrong.

Truth: They are very accurate, but they still estimate where the ball would have gone, with a small margin of error of a few millimetres. That is why cricket's review system has "umpire's call", which keeps the on-field decision when the replay is too close to be sure.

Try this

Watch a few minutes of any sport with the sound off and try to referee it yourself. Write down every decision you would make. Then watch the replay with the commentary on and see how many calls you got the same as the real official, and how many you missed completely.
