What is courage?

Brave people feel fear just like everyone else. Courage is what they do while their heart is still pounding.

The short answer

Courage is doing something good or necessary even though it scares you. A firefighter running into smoke has it. So does a child who tells a teacher that a classmate is being bullied, knowing the bully might turn on them next.

The key word is even though. Courage doesn't mean the fear disappears. Your heart still races and your hands still sweat. Courage is the part of you that decides what to do anyway. Without fear there would be nothing to be brave about.

Surprising but true

In one brain-scanning experiment, people who chose to bring a live snake closer despite their fear showed extra activity in a part of the brain that seems to help override panic.

Two kinds of brave

People often split courage into two kinds. Physical courage means facing danger to your body: climbing a dangerous mountain, pulling someone out of a flooded river, or jumping into the ring for your first boxing match.

Moral courage means standing up for what is right when it could cost you friends, money or comfort. In 1955, Rosa Parks refused to give up her bus seat to a white passenger in Montgomery, Alabama. She was arrested, but her quiet act helped spark the American civil rights movement. In Pakistan, Malala Yousafzai spoke out for girls' schooling even after she was attacked for it.

What happens in your brain

When something frightens you, a small almond-shaped part of your brain called the amygdala raises the alarm. Your heart pumps faster, your breathing speeds up and your muscles get ready to run.

Other parts of the brain, toward the front, can weigh that alarm against what you want to do. In a 2010 experiment in Israel, scientists asked people who were afraid of snakes to pull a live snake closer on a moving belt. When people chose to bring it nearer despite feeling scared, a region in the front of their brain became more active. The fear was still there. Something else was overruling it.

Practice helps too. Firefighters, soldiers and astronauts rehearse frightening moments again and again, in drills and simulators, until their hands know what to do. The fear doesn't vanish, but it gets smaller and stops freezing them. The same thing happens when a shy child gives a second, third and fourth speech in class. Each time, the alarm rings a little more quietly.

A story from Karachi

On 5 September 1986, armed men took over Pan Am Flight 73 on the ground at Karachi airport. Neerja Bhanot, a 22-year-old flight attendant from Mumbai, warned the pilots. Over the next long hours she helped keep passengers calm and hid passports so the hijackers could not pick out Americans.

When the attackers began shooting, she opened the emergency doors and helped people escape. She was killed while helping children get out. India awarded her the Ashoka Chakra, its highest award for bravery away from war. Pakistan and the USA honoured her too.

Courage in small sizes

Most acts of courage never make the news. Owning up to a mistake takes courage, especially when nobody saw you make it and you could easily stay quiet. So does sitting with a new student at lunch, or trying a sport you're bad at while others watch.

The ancient Greek thinker Aristotle said courage sits between two faults. Too little, and you are a coward who runs from every risk. Too much, and you are reckless, taking silly risks for no good reason. A brave person, he thought, fears the right things for the right reasons.

India has given National Bravery Awards to children since 1957, and in the USA the Carnegie Hero Fund has honoured ordinary rescuers since 1904. Many winners say the same thing afterwards: they were terrified, and they did it anyway.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Brave people don't feel afraid.

Truth: Fear is a normal alarm that everyone has. Courage is choosing to act well while you feel it. Someone who feels no fear at all isn't brave, they just don't see the danger.

Try this

Think of one small brave thing you could do this week, like asking a question in class or saying sorry first after a fight. Write it on a slip of paper, do it, then write down how you felt before and after. Most people find the "before" feeling was the biggest part.
