What is Bihu dance?

In April 2023, 11,304 dancers and drummers filled one stadium in Guwahati and danced Bihu together, setting a Guinness World Record.

The short answer

Bihu is the folk dance of Assam, done to celebrate spring and the start of the Assamese new year in mid-April. Young men and women dance in groups, the women swaying their hips and moving their hands in quick, rippling patterns, the men leaping and drumming. The beat is fast, and it gets faster.

The dance belongs to Rongali Bihu, also called Bohag Bihu, the happiest of Assam's three Bihu festivals. Rongali comes from rong, meaning joy. It arrives when the fields are ready for sowing, the trees are blooming and the first rains are near. For a week or more, villages and cities in northeast India fill with drums.

Surprising but true

One of the instruments in a Bihu band, the pepa, is a pipe made from a buffalo's horn.

A band you can hear from the next village

The heartbeat of Bihu is the dhol, a barrel-shaped drum slung from the shoulder and played on both ends, one with a stick and one with the hand. Good dhol players can make it sound like galloping horses.

Then there is the pepa, a pipe made from buffalo horn. Its high, wailing note cuts through the drums. The gogona is a small jaw harp made from bamboo, held against the lips and twanged. Cymbals called taal keep time, and a split bamboo clapper called the toka adds a dry crack.

All of this is played live. Bihu was a village dance long before it was a stage show, so the musicians and dancers feed off each other, speeding up together.

Dancing in the fields

Bihu began as a dance of farmers. Spring was the time to pray for good rain and a good harvest, and also, a little shyly, a time to find someone to marry. Many Bihu songs, called bihugeet, are about love, the monsoon, the Brahmaputra river and work in the fields.

Kings of the Ahom kingdom, which ruled Assam for about 600 years, are said to have invited Bihu dancers to perform at their royal grounds. Village dancing slowly became royal entertainment as well.

In villages, groups of men called husori still go from house to house during Rongali Bihu. They sing in each courtyard, bless the family for the coming year and are given betel nut, a gamosa or a few coins. It is a bit like carol singers at Christmas, with drums.

Today Bihu is danced in schools, on television and in big city stages called Bihutoli. Communities of Assamese families in Delhi, Bengaluru and the United States hold their own Bihu nights every April.

Gold silk and red flowers

Women dancers usually wear the mekhela sador, a two-piece outfit. The finest ones are woven from muga, a golden silk made almost only in Assam. Muga cloth is famous for growing shinier with every wash. Dancers often tuck a kopou phul, the foxtail orchid, into their hair.

Men wear a dhoti, a gamosa tied around the head or waist, and sometimes a short jacket. The gamosa is a white cotton towel with red borders. It is so much a part of Assam that it is offered to guests as a sign of respect.

The biggest Bihu ever

On 13 April 2023, at a stadium in Guwahati, 11,304 dancers and drummers performed Bihu together. Guinness World Records counted it as the largest Bihu dance ever, and a second record was set for the biggest Bihu dhol performance. From the stands, the field looked like a moving carpet of red and gold.

It was a long way from a few dancers under a tree. Yet the steps were the same ones grandmothers teach in village courtyards, still timed to the rains, the rice and the turning year. And if the tea gardens of Assam are its most famous export, Bihu might be its loudest.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Bihu is a single festival that happens once a year.

Truth: Assam celebrates three Bihus. Rongali Bihu comes in spring, Kongali Bihu in autumn and Bhogali Bihu in January. The famous dance belongs mostly to the spring one.

Try this

Make a simple gogona sound. Hold a strip of stiff card or a lolly stick at one end against a table edge, with most of it sticking out, and twang the free end. Now try holding it near your open mouth and changing the shape of your mouth. Listen to how the note changes, just as it does with a real bamboo gogona.
