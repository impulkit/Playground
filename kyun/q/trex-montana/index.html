Where was the first T. rex dug up?

In the badlands of Montana, a fossil hunter found bones so big they needed a new name.

The short answer

In 1902, a fossil hunter named Barnum Brown dug up giant bones in the Hell Creek rocks of Montana, USA. In 1905 the animal was named Tyrannosaurus rex, "tyrant lizard king", by Henry Fairfield Osborn of the American Museum of Natural History in New York.

T. rex lived about 68 to 66 million years ago, right until the asteroid hit. It was a theropod as long as a city bus and as heavy as an elephant. Since then, every known T. rex has come from western North America, from Montana and the Dakotas up into Canada.

Surprising but true

Stegosaurus lived so long before T. rex that T. rex is closer in time to you than to Stegosaurus.

A hunter of bones

Barnum Brown worked for the American Museum of Natural History. He spent decades searching the American West for dinosaurs and was famous for his skill at spotting bones in rock.

The Hell Creek skeleton was only partial: parts of the skull, backbone, hips and limbs. In 1908 Brown found a much better one, with a nearly complete skull. Its bones still stand in the museum in New York. During the Second World War, the museum sold the first skeleton to the Carnegie Museum in Pittsburgh, partly to keep the two T. rex fossils in different cities, safe from bombing.

Some T. rex bones had been found even earlier, but nobody recognised them. In 1892, Edward Drinker Cope described two worn backbones from South Dakota under a different name. Much later, scientists realised they probably came from a T. rex.

Why Montana?

Hell Creek was once a warm, swampy coast with rivers running to a shallow sea. When animals died near the rivers, floods buried them fast in mud. That mud slowly turned to rock, and the bones turned to fossils.

Today the land is dry badlands, with bare hills and gullies. Wind and rain wear the soft rock away, so bones poke out of the hillsides. That makes it a good place to look, though finds are still rare.

How does a bone turn into stone?It takes the right accident, lots of mud, and millions of years.

Sue, the most complete T. rex

In 1990, Sue Hendrickson spotted bones sticking out of a cliff in South Dakota. The skeleton was about 90% complete, one of the most complete T. rex ever found. It was named Sue after her.

Then came a long argument over who owned it. The land belonged to a rancher, but was held in trust by the US government for him, and the fossil collectors had paid him for permission to dig. The FBI seized the skeleton in 1992. After years in court, Sue was sold at auction in 1997 for about 8.4 million US dollars, to the Field Museum in Chicago, with help from companies.

What Sue's bones tell

Sue's bones show a tough life. There are healed broken ribs and an injured shoulder. Holes in the jaw may have come from an infection, similar to one that affects birds today.

Scientists can count growth rings in T. rex bones, like rings in a tree. Sue was about 28 years old when she died, old for a T. rex. Nobody knows whether Sue was female or male. The name stuck anyway.

A cousin in India

India had its own big hunter at the same time: Rajasaurus. It belonged to a different family, but it ruled the same last chapter of the dinosaurs.

Today, real T. rex fossils sit in museums in New York, Chicago, Pittsburgh and other places. Many more museums show casts. Each one began as bones poking out of a hillside.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Most dinosaur skeletons in museums are complete sets of real bones.

Truth: Most are partly or wholly casts, copies made from moulds. Real fossil bones are heavy and fragile, and very few skeletons are found complete. Sue is unusual because most of her displayed bones are real.

Try this

Be a fossil hunter in a tray of sand. Ask an adult to bury a few clean chicken bones or small toys at different depths. Use a paintbrush and a spoon to uncover them slowly, and draw a map of where each piece lay before you lift it. Real fossil hunters record positions the same way.
