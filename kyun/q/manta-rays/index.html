How do manta rays feed?

One of the biggest fish in the sea lives on food too small to see.

The short answer

Manta rays are filter feeders. They eat plankton: tiny shrimp-like animals, fish eggs and larvae drifting in the water. A manta swims with its huge mouth wide open, scooping up water like a moving net. Two flaps on either side of its head, called cephalic fins, unroll to funnel water and food into its mouth.

Inside, the water flows out through the gills, but the food is caught by filter plates and swallowed. Because plankton is tiny, a manta has to filter enormous amounts of water to get enough to eat. The giant oceanic manta, the biggest kind, can measure about 7 m from wingtip to wingtip, about as wide as a minibus is long. Yet it never bites prey.

Surprising but true

Every manta ray has its own pattern of dark spots on its belly, and scientists use these like fingerprints to recognise individuals.

A filter that doesn't clog

When you sieve something, the holes often get blocked. Mantas seem to avoid that. In 2018, scientists in California studied the filter plates of manta rays and built models of them. They found that food particles bounce, or ricochet, off the plates and stay in the mouth, while water flows out through the gaps.

This trick lets mantas catch bits of food even smaller than the gaps in their filter, without getting clogged. Engineers are now interested in copying the idea for water filters. The biggest animal on Earth, the blue whale, is also a filter feeder, though it uses comb-like plates of baleen instead.

Somersaults and feeding chains

Mantas have clever ways of eating. Sometimes they swim in tight backward loops, turning somersault after somersault through a thick patch of plankton. Sometimes many mantas line up nose to tail and swim in a circle, forming a feeding chain that may concentrate the food.

They often feed where currents push plankton together. In Hanifaru Bay in the Maldives, dozens or even more than a hundred mantas can gather to feed at once. In Kona, Hawaii, dive boats shine bright lights into the water at night. The lights attract plankton, and mantas glide in to feed, doing slow somersaults just in front of the divers. Mantas have long been part of Hawaii's ocean life and stories.

Big brains and spotted bellies

Manta rays have large brains for fish, with especially big areas linked to learning. Some scientists think they may be among the smartest fish, though testing intelligence in a fish is hard. They also visit cleaning stations on coral reefs, where small fish nibble off parasites and dead skin, and they seem to remember where these stations are.

Each manta has a unique pattern of dark spots on its white belly. Like human fingerprints, no two are the same. Divers photograph these spots and share them with scientists, who use them to recognise and track individual mantas over many years.

Gentle giants in trouble

Mantas and their relatives are cartilaginous fish, like sharks: their skeletons are made of bendy cartilage, not bone. Unlike many stingrays, they have no sting. Their name comes from the Spanish word for a cloak or blanket.

Mantas grow slowly and usually have just one pup every two years or more, so their numbers recover slowly. They are caught in fishing nets and hunted for their gill plates, which are sold for use in some traditional remedies. In 2018, the USA listed the giant oceanic manta as a threatened species. International trade in mantas is controlled, and many countries, including the Maldives, protect them.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Manta rays are dangerous and can sting you.

Truth: Mantas have no stinging barb, unlike many of their stingray cousins. They are gentle filter feeders and pose no danger to divers, though their size means divers should give them space.

Try this

Make a plankton filter. Stir a spoon of tea leaves or ground spices into a jug of water. Pour it slowly through a tea strainer or a piece of thin cloth into another jug. The water passes through, but the bits stay behind, which is how a filter feeder collects its food.
