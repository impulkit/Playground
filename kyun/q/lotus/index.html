Why does water roll off a lotus leaf?

The lotus grows in muddy ponds, yet its leaves never stay dirty. Scientists have copied the trick to make self-cleaning paint.

The short answer

A lotus leaf looks smooth, but it isn't. Under a microscope, its surface is covered in tiny bumps, each far thinner than a hair. The bumps are coated with even smaller crystals of wax.

Water hates wax, and it can't settle into the spaces between the bumps. So a raindrop sits on the very tips of the bumps, with pockets of air underneath. It pulls itself into a ball and rolls off at the slightest tilt. A surface this good at shedding water is called superhydrophobic, meaning "very afraid of water".

As the drops roll, dust and dirt stick to them and are carried away. So the lotus, rising out of muddy water, keeps its leaves spotless. Scientists call this the lotus effect.

Surprising but true

A lotus seed about 1,300 years old, found in a dry lake bed in China, sprouted and grew when scientists planted it.

Rough is cleaner

On a smooth surface, a water drop spreads out and dirt stays stuck. On the lotus leaf, dirt only touches the tips of the bumps, so it barely holds on. The rolling drops collect it easily.

This matters for the plant. A clean leaf catches more sunlight to make food. Dirt and fungus spores get washed away before they can cause disease. The lotus is even unusual in having its breathing pores on the top of its leaves, which would be a problem if water stayed sitting there.

Copying the lotus

The German botanist Wilhelm Barthlott studied plant leaves under a powerful electron microscope in the 1970s. He noticed that the lotus, and some other plants, stayed clean however dirty their surroundings were. In 1997 he and a colleague explained why, and named it the lotus effect.

Companies have since copied it. There are self-cleaning paints for buildings that shed rain and grime, coatings for glass and fabrics that repel stains. Designers love borrowing ideas from nature like this, a habit you can read about in design.

A flower rising from mud

The lotus grows in ponds and lakes across Asia. Its roots creep through the mud at the bottom, while its leaves and flowers rise on long stalks well above the water. Water lilies look similar, but their leaves and flowers usually float flat on the surface.

Lotus flowers can even warm themselves. On cool days, the flower keeps its centre at around 30 to 35°C, which may attract insects looking for a cosy place to rest.

Almost every part of the lotus is useful. The crunchy stems, called kamal kakdi or bhein, are cooked as vegetables. The seeds are eaten raw or roasted. The leaves are used as plates in some places.

A symbol of purity

Because it rises clean and beautiful out of mud, the lotus has long stood for purity. In Hindu art, the goddesses Lakshmi and Saraswati are often shown seated on a lotus. In Buddhism, it represents a mind rising above the troubles of the world, and the Buddha is often pictured on a lotus throne. Jain art uses it too.

The lotus is India's national flower, one of its national symbols. In Delhi, the Bahá'í House of Worship, opened in 1986, is shaped like a white lotus with 27 marble petals and is known as the Lotus Temple.

A seed that waited 1,300 years

Lotus seeds have very tough coats. In the 1990s, an American scientist, Jane Shen-Miller, planted lotus seeds from a dried-up lake bed in northeast China. Tests showed one was about 1,300 years old, the oldest seed known at that time to grow into a healthy plant.

Lotus ponds are under pressure from drainage and pollution in many places. Protecting wetlands protects the lotus, and the frogs, fish and birds that live among its leaves.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Makhana, the puffed snack, is lotus seeds.

Truth: Makhana are often sold as "lotus seeds", but they come from a different water plant, the prickly water lily, grown mostly in Bihar. True lotus seeds, called kamal gatta, are eaten too, but they are a different food.

Try this

Hold a leaf of a lotus, colocasia (arbi) or nasturtium flat and drip a few drops of water onto it. Watch them bead up and roll. Now try the same on a sheet of paper and on a smooth plate. Sprinkle a little chalk dust or cocoa powder on the leaf first and see what happens to it as the drops roll.
