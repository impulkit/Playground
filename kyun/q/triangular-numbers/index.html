What are triangular and square numbers?

Ten pins in a bowling alley and fifteen red balls on a snooker table are both triangular numbers. So is every total you get by adding 1 + 2 + 3 and onwards.

The short answer

Some numbers can be drawn as neat shapes made of dots. Triangular numbers are the totals you can arrange in a triangle: 1, 3, 6, 10, 15 and so on. You get each one by adding the next counting number: 1, then 1 + 2, then 1 + 2 + 3.

Square numbers are the totals you can arrange in a square grid: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25. Each is a number multiplied by itself, so 5 × 5 = 25. Ancient Greek thinkers called these "figurate numbers", numbers with a figure or shape.

Surprising but true

In 1796 a teenage Carl Friedrich Gauss proved that every whole number can be made by adding at most three triangular numbers, and wrote "Eureka" in his diary.

Triangles you already know

Look at a set of bowling pins: 1 in front, then 2, then 3, then 4. That's 10, the fourth triangular number. Snooker players start with 15 red balls packed in a triangle of five rows.

Fruit sellers often stack oranges or apples in pyramids on a cart. Each layer of a triangle-based pyramid is a triangular number, so the whole pile is a sum of triangular numbers. The Indian mathematician Aryabhata, writing in 499 CE, gave rules for adding up series like these, including sums of squares and cubes.

The schoolroom shortcut

A famous story says that when Carl Friedrich Gauss was a young boy in Germany, his teacher told the class to add up every number from 1 to 100. Gauss wrote the answer almost at once: 5,050. The story may have grown in the telling, but the trick is real.

Pair the numbers from each end: 1 + 100, 2 + 99, 3 + 98. Every pair makes 101, and there are 50 pairs. So the total is 50 × 101 = 5,050. That gives a formula for any triangular number: multiply n by n + 1, then halve it.

Where triangles meet squares

Here is a neat link. Add any two triangular numbers that sit side by side in the list, and you always get a square. 1 + 3 = 4. 3 + 6 = 9. 6 + 10 = 16.

You can see why with dots. Draw a staircase-shaped triangle of 6 dots, then a slightly bigger one of 10 dots, flip it and fit it against the first. Together they fill a 4 × 4 square.

Squares hide another surprise. Add up odd numbers in order: 1, then 1 + 3, then 1 + 3 + 5. You get 1, 4, 9, 16. Each new odd number is an L-shaped strip wrapped around the previous square. This is one of many number patterns you can see with your eyes rather than work out on paper.

A teenager's discovery

On 10 July 1796, Gauss, then 19, wrote in his notebook: "Eureka! num = Δ + Δ + Δ". He had proved that every whole number can be written as the sum of at most three triangular numbers. For example, 20 = 10 + 10, and 25 = 15 + 10.

The ancient followers of Pythagoras also treasured shape numbers. They gave special meaning to the tetractys, a triangle of ten dots in rows of 1, 2, 3 and 4.

Why squares rule geometry

Square numbers sit underneath much of maths. The area of a square floor 4 metres on each side is 16 square metres. The Pythagorean theorem is a statement about squares on the sides of a triangle. Undoing a square, so going from 49 back to 7, is called taking a square root, and it is one of the first steps into algebra.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Squaring a number always makes it bigger.

Truth: Only for numbers bigger than 1. Squaring 1 gives 1, and squaring a fraction makes it smaller, because half of a half is a quarter (0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25).

Try this

Use coins, buttons or bottle caps. Build triangles with rows of 1, then 1 and 2, then 1, 2 and 3, and count the totals. Now push two triangles next to each other, one the size of the other plus a row, and see if they make a square.
