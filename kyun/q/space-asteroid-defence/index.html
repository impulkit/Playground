Can we stop an asteroid hitting Earth?

In 2022 NASA deliberately crashed a spacecraft the size of a car into an asteroid, and it changed the asteroid's orbit by 32 minutes.

The short answer

Yes, probably, if we see it coming early enough. In 2022, NASA's DART mission crashed a spacecraft into a small asteroid called Dimorphos on purpose. The hit changed its orbit, proving for the first time that humans can move a natural object in space.

The real challenge is finding dangerous asteroids years or decades before they arrive. A small push far in advance is all it takes to turn a hit into a miss. Telescopes around the world now track more than 40,000 near-Earth asteroids, and none of the known large ones is set to hit us in the next century.

Surprising but true

In early 2025, a newly found asteroid called 2024 YR4 briefly had about a 3 percent chance of hitting Earth in 2032, the highest ever recorded for an asteroid that size, before new observations ruled it out.

The car that hit an asteroid

Dimorphos is about 160 m across, roughly as wide as a cricket stadium. It orbits a bigger asteroid called Didymos. Neither was ever a threat, which made them a safe target. On 26 September 2022, the DART spacecraft, about the size of a small car, slammed into Dimorphos at about 22,000 km/h.

Before the crash, Dimorphos took 11 hours and 55 minutes to circle Didymos. Afterwards it took about 32 minutes less. The impact blasted out a huge plume of rock and dust, and that spray pushed the asteroid even harder than the spacecraft itself. The full story is told in DART.

A follow-up visit

The European Space Agency launched a spacecraft called Hera in 2024 to inspect the scene. It is due to reach Didymos and Dimorphos in late 2026. Hera will measure the crater, the asteroid's mass and its shape, to work out exactly how well the push worked. That will help engineers plan a real rescue mission if one is ever needed.

When rocks do hit

Small space rocks hit Earth all the time, mostly burning up harmlessly as shooting stars. Bigger ones are rarer. In February 2013, a rock about 18 m wide exploded over the Russian city of Chelyabinsk. The shock wave shattered windows across the city and injured around 1,500 people, mostly from flying glass. Nobody had seen it coming, because it approached from the direction of the Sun.

In 1908, a larger object exploded over Tunguska in Siberia and flattened about 80 million trees across 2,000 km². And 66 million years ago, an asteroid about 10 km wide helped end the age of the dinosaurs, though scientists still argue about how much giant volcanoes added to the dinosaurs' extinction.

The 2032 scare

In December 2024, astronomers found an asteroid about 40 to 90 m across, named 2024 YR4. By February 2025, its chance of hitting Earth in December 2032 had climbed to about 3 percent. Space agencies began planning. Then new telescope observations pinned down its path, and the chance of hitting Earth fell to almost zero.

For a while it still had a small chance, about 4 percent in mid-2025, of hitting the Moon in 2032. Then, in 2026, new observations by the James Webb Space Telescope ruled out a Moon hit too.

Other ways to push

Hitting an asteroid isn't the only idea. A "gravity tractor" would fly a heavy spacecraft alongside an asteroid for years, using the spacecraft's tiny gravity to slowly tug it. Lasers or mirrors focusing sunlight could boil rock off one side, pushing it like a slow rocket. For a huge asteroid found late, a nuclear device exploded nearby might be the last option.

All of these work better the earlier they start. That is why NASA is building NEO Surveyor, a space telescope due to launch around 2027, to hunt asteroids in infrared light.

A close shave in 2029

On 13 April 2029, an asteroid called Apophis, about 340 m wide, will pass about 32,000 km from Earth, closer than the satellites that beam TV signals. It will be visible to the naked eye from parts of the world. Scientists are sure it will miss. Space agencies, including ESA and ISRO, have discussed sending spacecraft to study it during the visit. Knowing exactly where a rock like that will be in 20 years is a puzzle of probability as much as rockets.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The best way to stop an asteroid is to blow it into pieces with a nuclear bomb, like in the films.

Truth: Breaking an asteroid apart could send many big chunks towards Earth. Scientists prefer to nudge it gently, years ahead, so it misses. Nuclear devices are studied only as a last resort, mainly to push an asteroid rather than shatter it.

Try this

Roll a big ball slowly across the floor and try to make it miss a target, like a shoe, by tapping it with a small ball. Try tapping it early in its roll, then late. You will find a tiny tap early works better than a hard hit at the last moment. That is the key idea of asteroid defence.
