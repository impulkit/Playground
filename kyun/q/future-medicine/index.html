How will doctors treat us in the future?

In 2023, Britain and the US approved a medicine that edits a patient's own DNA to treat sickle cell disease, an illness people are born with.

The short answer

Future medicine is already arriving. Doctors can now edit faulty genes inside a patient's cells to treat some inherited diseases. Vaccines can be designed in days using a molecule called mRNA. A patient's own immune cells can be trained in a lab to fight cancer. Robots help surgeons make tiny, precise cuts, and AI helps read scans.

Other ideas are still being tested. Scientists are trying to 3D-print living tissue, grow replacement organs and build microscopic robots that deliver medicine. Some of these may take decades. A few may never work. But medicine is changing faster now than at almost any time in history.

Surprising but true

India's first home-grown CAR-T cancer therapy, NexCAR19, was approved in 2023 and costs a fraction of the price of similar treatments abroad.

Fixing the code

Every cell carries DNA, a long code of instructions. A single wrong letter can cause disease. Sickle cell disease, which affects millions of people in Africa, India and among African Americans, comes from one such error. It bends red blood cells into a crescent shape, causing pain and damage.

In 2023, Britain and then the US approved Casgevy, the first medicine made with the gene-editing tool CRISPR. Doctors take stem cells from the patient's bone marrow, edit them in a lab and put them back. Early results show many patients free of painful crises. It is very expensive, though, and treatment takes months.

Vaccines at speed

Vaccines once took many years to make. During COVID-19, scientists made mRNA vaccines in less than a year. An mRNA vaccine carries a short set of instructions that tell your cells to make a harmless piece of a virus. Your immune system learns to recognise it.

Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman won the 2023 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the research behind them. Scientists are now testing mRNA vaccines against flu, cancer and other diseases.

How does your body fight germs?Right now, an army of cells is patrolling your blood, eating invaders whole and remembering the faces of germs you met years ago.

Training cells to hunt cancer

CAR-T therapy turns a patient's own white blood cells into cancer hunters. The cells are collected, given new instructions in a lab, multiplied and put back. For some blood cancers, it can work when nothing else has.

In the US, this therapy can cost hundreds of thousands of dollars. In 2023, India approved NexCAR19, developed by IIT Bombay and Tata Memorial Hospital in Mumbai. It costs far less, which could make the treatment possible for many more families.

Robots and AI in the room

Surgeons in many big hospitals, including in India, use robotic systems. The surgeon sits at a console and controls thin robot arms that hold tiny tools and a camera. The robot doesn't decide anything. It steadies the surgeon's hand and lets them work through very small cuts, so patients often heal faster.

AI systems are learning to spot signs of disease in X-rays, eye scans and skin photos. In trials, some match expert doctors at certain tasks. They're best used as a second pair of eyes, with a doctor making the final decision. In places with few specialists, like many rural districts in India, such tools could help spot problems early.

The wilder ideas

Researchers are printing layers of living cells to make patches of skin and cartilage. Whole working organs, like a printed kidney, are still far off. Scientists are also growing organs in pigs whose genes are edited so the human body won't reject them. In 2024, surgeons in the US transplanted gene-edited pig kidneys into living patients.

And "nanobots", robots smaller than a cell, swimming through blood to deliver medicine? Labs have made tiny moving particles, but they are still experiments. Medicine has always had bold dreamers, though. More than 2,000 years ago, the Indian surgeon Sushruta described ways to rebuild a damaged nose using skin from the cheek, an idea modern plastic surgeons still recognise.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Gene editing means doctors can change anything about a person, like making them taller or smarter.

Truth: Today's approved gene treatments fix specific faults that cause serious diseases, in certain cells only. Traits like height or intelligence depend on hundreds of genes and on life experience, and editing them is neither possible nor allowed.
