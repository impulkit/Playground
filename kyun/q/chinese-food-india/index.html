How did Indo-Chinese food begin?

Gobi Manchurian has never been near Manchuria. It was most likely invented in Mumbai, by a chef born in Kolkata.

The short answer

Indo-Chinese food was created by Chinese families who settled in India, mostly in Kolkata, and cooked for Indian customers. Over many decades they adjusted their recipes, adding green chillies, garlic, ginger, coriander and plenty of spice. The result is a cuisine of its own, quite different from food in China.

Its famous dishes include chilli chicken, hakka noodles, fried rice, sweet corn soup, American chop suey and Manchurian of every kind. Today you can find it at every level, from a cart near a college gate to a big restaurant. Many Indians think of it as a comfort food, and some have never tasted food from China at all.

Surprising but true

Indian-style Chinese food is now so loved that Indo-Chinese restaurants have opened in the United States, Canada and Britain, serving it to Indians abroad and everyone else.

Kolkata's Chinatown

Chinese people have lived in Kolkata for about 250 years. The first settler usually named is Tong Achew, who is said to have set up a sugar mill near the Hooghly River in the late 1700s. The area is still called Achipur, after him, and his grave is there.

Over the next century, more people came, many from the Hakka and Cantonese communities of southern China. They worked as carpenters, dentists, shoemakers and leather tanners. Two Chinatowns grew up, one in the old city around Tiretta Bazaar and one at Tangra, among the tanneries.

What makes Kolkata special?Kolkata has India's oldest metro, a giant bridge held together by rivets instead of bolts, and a festival so grand that UNESCO honoured it.

Cooking for new neighbours

Early Chinese restaurants in Kolkata served food close to what Cantonese families ate at home. Indian customers wanted more heat and more flavour. Cooks began frying chillies with garlic and ginger, using more soy sauce and thickening sauces with cornflour so they clung to every piece.

Dishes slowly drifted from their roots. Hakka noodles in India are stir-fried with vegetables, vinegar and chilli sauce. "Schezwan" sauce is named after the Sichuan province of China, but the Indian version is a fiery paste of red chillies and garlic, without the numbing Sichuan peppercorn of the original.

The Manchurian story

The best-known story belongs to Nelson Wang, a chef born in Kolkata to Chinese parents. He moved to Mumbai and cooked at the Cricket Club of India. He said that in 1975 a customer asked for something new, not on the menu. So he fried pieces of chicken in a cornflour batter. Then he tossed them in a sauce of garlic, ginger, green chilli and soy, the way an Indian cook might start a masala.

He called it chicken Manchurian. Vegetarian versions with cauliflower and other vegetables soon followed and spread across the country. Wang later opened a well-known restaurant called China Garden in Mumbai.

Hard times

During the war between India and China in 1962, Chinese Indians suffered greatly, even though most had been born in India. Many were sent to a camp in Deoli, Rajasthan, and some were forced to leave the country. Others later moved to Canada, Australia and the United States.

Kolkata's Chinese community is now much smaller than it once was. Yet on Sunday mornings, stalls near Tiretta Bazaar still sell steamed buns, dumplings and fish-ball soup, a breakfast that has run for generations.

A fusion that travelled

Indo-Chinese food is a lot like Tex-Mex in America: a cuisine born when cooks from one country fed people in another. It has now travelled with the Indian diaspora. Restaurants in New Jersey, Toronto and London serve chilli paneer and gobi Manchurian, and diners of every background order them.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Gobi Manchurian is a traditional dish from Manchuria in China.

Truth: It was created in India, most likely in the 1970s. The name sounds Chinese, but the flavours of garlic, green chilli, ginger and coriander are very Indian.
