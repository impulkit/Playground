How was soap invented?

The first soap was made more than 4,000 years ago from fat and wood ash, and it was often used to clean wool and cloth rather than people.

The short answer

Soap is made by mixing fat or oil with an alkali, a strong substance such as lye made from wood ash. When the two are heated together, they react and turn into soap. Chemists call this reaction saponification, from the Latin word for soap.

People in Mesopotamia knew how to do this more than 4,000 years ago. Clay tablets describe boiling fats with ash. For a long time, soap was used mainly for cleaning wool and cloth, and for treating skin. Washing yourself with soap every day became common only in the last couple of hundred years.

Surprising but true

The English word "shampoo" comes from the Hindi word chāmpo, meaning to press or massage.

Recipes on clay

The oldest written soap recipes come from Mesopotamia, in what is now Iraq. Clay tablets more than 4,000 years old describe mixing water, alkali and cassia oil. In ancient Egypt, a medical scroll called the Ebers Papyrus, from about 1550 BCE, describes mixing animal and vegetable oils with alkaline salts for washing and treating skin.

The Romans used soap less than you might expect. Wealthy Romans cleaned themselves by rubbing oil on their skin and scraping it off with a curved metal tool.

How soap works

A soap molecule has two ends. One end loves water. The other end hates water but loves oil and grease. When you wash your hands, the oily ends dig into grease and dirt, while the watery ends face outwards.

The molecules gather around each blob of grease, forming tiny balls. Running water then carries the balls away. Soap can also break apart the fatty outer layer of some germs, including many viruses. That is why it works so well, as explained in how soap fights germs.

Fine soaps and Indian cleansers

In the Middle Ages, soap makers in Syria, Spain and Italy made hard bars from olive oil. Aleppo soap, with olive oil and laurel oil, has been made in Syria for centuries.

In India, people often washed with natural cleansers. Reetha, or soapnut, makes a gentle lather when soaked in water. Shikakai pods were used to wash hair. In the early 1800s, Sake Dean Mahomed, a traveller from Bihar, opened a bathhouse in Brighton, England, offering "shampooing", an Indian head massage. The word stuck in English.

Washing hands saves lives

In 1847 a Hungarian doctor named Ignaz Semmelweis worked in a hospital in Vienna. Many mothers were dying of fever after giving birth. He made doctors wash their hands in a chlorine solution before examining patients, and deaths fell sharply.

Other doctors mocked him at first. Only later, when scientists proved that germs cause disease, did handwashing become a basic rule of medicine. Today the World Health Organization calls handwashing with soap one of the simplest ways to prevent illness.

Soap for everyone

In the 1800s, factories began making soap cheaply. In 1884 Lever Brothers in England launched Sunlight soap, and soon shipped it to India and around the world.

In 1916 the Maharaja of Mysore, Krishnaraja Wadiyar IV, set up a government soap factory in Bengaluru to use the region's sandalwood oil. Mysore Sandal Soap is still made there.

Soap or detergent?

Modern detergents, like washing powder, work the same way as soap but are made from different chemicals. They clean better in hard water, which leaves soap scum. But a simple bar of soap still does the most important job: washing away germs with nothing more than a lather and a rinse.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Soap is named after Mount Sapo near Rome, where melted animal fat ran down into the river.

Truth: This story is often repeated, but historians have found no evidence that a Mount Sapo ever existed. The Latin word sapo probably came from a Germanic word for a kind of grease or dye.

Try this

Fill a plate with water and shake ground black pepper over the surface. Dip a clean finger in, and not much happens. Now put a drop of liquid soap on your fingertip and touch the middle. Watch the pepper rush away to the edges as the soap breaks the water's surface.
