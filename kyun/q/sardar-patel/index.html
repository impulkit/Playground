How did Sardar Patel unite India?

In 1947 India was a patchwork of more than 560 kingdoms. In about two years, one man stitched almost all of them into a single country.

The short answer

Vallabhbhai Patel was a lawyer from Gujarat who became one of the toughest leaders of India's freedom struggle. After independence in 1947 he was Deputy Prime Minister and Home Minister.

His biggest task was a puzzle. British India was only part of the subcontinent. The rest was made up of more than 560 princely states, kingdoms ruled by maharajas, nawabs and nizams. When the British left, each ruler could in theory stay independent. Patel and his secretary V.P. Menon persuaded, bargained with and sometimes pressured nearly all of them to join India. Without this, India's map could have looked like a quilt with holes.

Surprising but true

The title "Sardar", meaning chief, was given to Patel by the farmers, many of them women, of Bardoli in Gujarat after he led their protest in 1928.

A farmer's son who became chief

Patel was born in 1875 into a farming family in Nadiad. He studied law, saved money for years, and finally went to London to qualify as a barrister. He finished the course early and came top of his class. Back home he became a successful lawyer in Ahmedabad, sharp, well dressed and impatient with what he saw as idealistic talk.

That changed when he met Gandhi. Patel organised farmers in Kheda in 1918 to refuse taxes after their crops failed. In 1928 he led farmers in Bardoli against a steep tax rise. The government backed down, and the people of Bardoli began calling him "Sardar", chief.

The 1947 puzzle

Some princely states were tiny, just a few villages. Others were larger than many European countries. Hyderabad alone had more people than Britain's Scotland and Wales put together.

Patel offered the rulers a deal called the Instrument of Accession. They would hand over defence, foreign affairs and communications, and keep their titles and a yearly allowance. He appealed to their patriotism, met many of them himself, and made it clear that India's people wanted unity.

By 15 August 1947, almost every state had signed. Over the next two years, many small states were also merged into larger units, which later became today's states.

The hard cases

Three states held out. The Nawab of Junagadh in Gujarat chose Pakistan, though most of his people were Hindu and the state was surrounded by India. After a public vote in February 1948, Junagadh joined India.

Hyderabad, ruled by the Nizam, wanted to stay independent. In September 1948 Indian troops entered in an operation that lasted about four days, and Hyderabad joined India.

The Maharaja of Kashmir signed the Instrument of Accession in October 1947 as raiders from Pakistan attacked. Indian troops were flown in to defend Srinagar. The dispute over Kashmir continues to this day, and Nehru, more than Patel, handled it in the years that followed.

The steel frame

Patel also defended the civil services, the officers who run districts and departments. Some leaders wanted to scrap a system the British had built. Patel argued that free India needed trained, neutral officers. He called them the country's "steel frame", and today's IAS and IPS grew from his decision.

Remembering the Iron Man

Patel died on 15 December 1950, only three years after independence. He is remembered as the Iron Man of India, firm and practical. Since 2014 his birthday, 31 October, has been marked as National Unity Day.

His face now looks out over the Narmada river from the Statue of Unity, the tallest statue on Earth. A quieter memorial and museum to him stands in the Moti Shahi Mahal palace in Ahmedabad.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Every princely state joined India happily and peacefully.

Truth: Most rulers signed after talks, but a few held out. Junagadh joined after a public vote in 1948, and Hyderabad only after Indian troops moved in that September.
