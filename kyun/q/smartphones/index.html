What's inside a smartphone?

The phone in your pocket holds a computer, a camera, a compass, a radio, a torch and dozens of different chemical elements, including a little gold.

The short answer

A smartphone is a small, powerful computer with a phone, camera and many sensors packed inside a slim case.

Behind the screen sits a circuit board holding the main chip, which contains the processor, graphics unit and more. Memory chips hold apps and photos. Several antennas send and receive radio signals for calls, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth and satellite navigation. Cameras, microphones and speakers are fitted in tiny gaps. Most of the space is taken by a flat battery.

Put together, it's a pocket-sized combination of devices that once filled a whole desk, or a whole room.

Surprising but true

A modern smartphone has far more computing power than the computer that guided Apollo 11 to the Moon in 1969.

The brain on a chip

The main chip in a phone is called a system-on-a-chip, because it combines many parts that once needed separate chips. It includes the processor that runs apps, a graphics unit for games and videos, and often a special unit for artificial intelligence tasks like recognising faces in photos.

These chips contain billions of transistors, each so small that thousands would fit across the width of a human hair. Making them takes some of the most precise factories on Earth.

The Apollo 11 guidance computer, which helped land astronauts on the Moon in 1969, had only a tiny fraction of a modern phone's memory and speed.

A box of sensors

Your phone is always sensing its surroundings. An accelerometer feels movement and tilt, which is how the screen knows to rotate and how step counters work. A gyroscope senses turning. A magnetometer acts as a compass.

A proximity sensor near the top notices when you hold the phone to your ear and switches off the screen so your cheek doesn't press buttons. A light sensor adjusts the brightness. A satellite receiver listens for signals from navigation satellites, so maps can show where you are.

How does GPS know where you are?Your phone finds your place on Earth by timing radio signals from satellites 20,000 km away, to within billionths of a second.

Screen, cameras and battery

The screen is a sandwich of layers: tough glass, a touch-sensing layer, and a display made of millions of tiny coloured dots. Many phones use OLED displays, where each dot makes its own light.

Most phones now have several cameras, each with a different lens for wide, zoomed or close-up shots. Software combines their pictures.

The battery is lithium-ion, the same kind used in laptops and electric cars. It stores energy by moving charged lithium atoms, called ions, between two layers.

What it's made of

A phone contains several dozen chemical elements. Silicon makes the chips. Lithium and cobalt go into the battery. Copper wires carry electricity. Tiny amounts of gold coat connectors because gold doesn't rust. Rare earth elements help make speakers, vibration motors and screen colours.

Many of these materials are mined in just a few countries, sometimes in difficult or unsafe conditions. That is one reason people encourage repairing phones and recycling old ones, so those metals can be recovered.

How phones changed the world

The IBM Simon of 1994 is often called the first smartphone, with a touchscreen and simple apps. The iPhone, launched in 2007, and phones running Android, launched in 2008, made smartphones popular across the world.

India now has hundreds of millions of smartphone users, more than any country except China. Cheap phones and low-cost mobile data brought many families online for the first time, for video calls, online classes and payments. A device that was rare when your parents were children is now, for many people, the main way they reach the internet.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You should let your phone battery run down to zero before charging it.

Truth: That advice comes from older kinds of batteries. Modern lithium-ion batteries don't need to be fully emptied, and often last longer if kept away from very low and very high charge.

Try this

With an adult's phone, open the compass app and slowly turn around. Then bring a fridge magnet near the top of the phone and watch the needle swing. Phones contain a tiny magnetometer that senses Earth's magnetic field, and a magnet confuses it. Move the magnet away afterwards.
