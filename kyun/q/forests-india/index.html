What kinds of forests grow in India?

India has rainforests with trees as tall as 15-storey buildings, thorny scrub in the desert, and pine forests high in the snowy Himalaya.

The short answer

India's forests are many different kinds, shaped mostly by two things: how much rain falls and how high the land is. Wet slopes grow tall evergreen rainforests. Drier plains grow forests that drop their leaves for part of the year. Deserts grow thorny scrub. Mountains grow pine, oak and fir, and salty coasts grow mangroves.

According to the India State of Forest Report 2023, about 21.8% of India's land is covered by forest. Add trees outside forests, in farms, villages and cities, and the figure rises to about 25.2%. Madhya Pradesh has the most forest area of any state.

Surprising but true

The English word "jungle" comes from a Sanskrit word, jangala, which meant dry, open land, not thick forest.

Rainforests on wet slopes

Where the monsoon dumps huge amounts of rain, tropical evergreen forests grow. You'll find them along the Western Ghats, in the Northeast and on the Andaman Islands. Some trees here grow 45 metres or taller, about as high as a 15-storey building, and the leaves stay green all year.

Light hardly reaches the forest floor. Climbing plants, orchids and ferns cling to trunks to get closer to the sun. These forests hold many plants and animals found nowhere else, such as the lion-tailed macaque of the Western Ghats.

Forests that rest in summer

Most of India's forest is tropical deciduous. "Deciduous" means the trees drop their leaves for part of the year. In central India, the hot, dry months before the monsoon turn whole hillsides brown and bare.

These forests are home to valuable trees like teak and sal, and to much of India's wildlife, including many of its tigers. Kanha, Pench and Bandhavgarh in Madhya Pradesh are deciduous forests. They are also full of bamboo, which is really a giant grass.

Thorns, pines and salty roots

In Rajasthan and Gujarat, rain is scarce, and only tough trees survive. Thorn forests of babul, khejri and ber have small leaves and deep roots that reach for water far underground.

Climb into the Himalaya and the forests change with height. Lower slopes grow chir pine and oak. Higher up come deodar, fir and spruce, then rhododendron, which blooms red in spring. Above about 3,500 metres, trees give way to alpine meadows.

On muddy coasts, mangroves grow with their roots in salty tidal water. The Sundarbans, shared by India and Bangladesh, is one of the largest mangrove forests on Earth, and home to tigers that swim between islands.

Growing or shrinking?

Every two years, the Forest Survey of India uses satellite images to measure the country's tree cover. Recent reports show India's forest and tree cover slowly rising. That sounds like good news, and in many places it is.

Some scientists and conservation groups urge caution, though. Satellites count any patch thick with trees, so tea gardens, fruit orchards and fast-growing plantations of a single tree can count as forest. A plantation of eucalyptus is very different from an old natural forest full of birds, insects and hundreds of plant species. Meanwhile, some old forests are still being cleared for mines, dams and roads. So the experts ask two questions: how much forest is there, and what kind?

Forests and people

Millions of people live in or near forests and depend on them for firewood, fruit, honey, medicine and grazing. Many Adivasi communities have looked after forests for generations, and some villages protect sacred groves where no tree may be cut.

In the 1970s, village women in the Himalaya hugged trees to stop loggers, in what became the Chipko movement. Forest officers today divide India's forests into many types, following a system published by Harry Champion and S. K. Seth in 1968. Its 16 main groups, from wet evergreen to alpine scrub, are still used to plan how forests are protected.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The word "jungle" has always meant a thick, wet forest.

Truth: It comes from the Sanskrit jangala, meaning dry, open, wild land. British writers took the Hindi word "jangal" into English, and over time it came to mean dense tropical forest.

Try this

Collect fallen leaves from five different trees near your home or school. Sort them by thickness, size and whether they feel waxy or papery. Thick, waxy leaves usually belong to trees that keep their leaves all year, while thin ones often belong to trees that drop them in the dry season.
