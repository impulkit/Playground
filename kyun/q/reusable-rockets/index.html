How do rockets land themselves?

A rocket booster taller than a 12-storey building can fall back from the edge of space and land upright on a ship's deck about the size of a football field.

The short answer

A reusable rocket brings its biggest part, the first stage or booster, back to Earth instead of letting it crash into the sea. After pushing the upper stage towards space, the booster separates, turns around and fires its engines again to fly back. Near the ground it fires once more to slow from hundreds of kilometres per hour to almost zero, then lowers four legs and touches down.

The first orbital-class booster to land like this was a SpaceX Falcon 9 in Florida, on 21 December 2015. Since then, landings have become routine. The point is cost: building a rocket and then throwing it away after one flight is like scrapping an aeroplane after every trip.

Surprising but true

Some Falcon 9 boosters have flown to space and landed more than 20 times each (2025).

Turning around at the edge of space

About two and a half minutes after launch, the Falcon 9 booster is roughly 70 km up and moving fast. It separates from the upper stage. Small gas thrusters flip it around, and a few engines fire to slow it and push it back towards land. This is the boostback burn.

The booster then falls, engines first. Four metal grid fins pop out near the top, like waffle-shaped flaps, to steer through the air. As it hits the thicker atmosphere, the engines fire again in the entry burn to stop it from overheating.

The final seconds

Gravity pulls the booster down at enormous speed. In the last half-minute, one engine lights for the landing burn. Computers measure height and speed many times a second and swivel the engine to keep the tall, thin rocket upright, like balancing a pencil on your palm.

The engine can't throttle low enough to hover, so the timing has to be exact. The booster reaches zero speed at the very moment it touches the pad. When there isn't enough fuel to fly back to land, the booster lands instead on a robot ship waiting hundreds of kilometres out at sea.

How does a parachute slow you down?A skydiver falling belly-first tops out at about 200 km/h. Open a parachute, and seconds later they drift down slower than a person jogging.

Other ways to come home

Parachutes are used for capsules carrying astronauts, and some companies have tried them for boosters. But a booster is heavy and fragile when empty, and splashing into salty seawater damages its engines. Landing on legs on a dry pad means it can be checked, refuelled and flown again within weeks.

The Space Shuttle, which flew from 1981 to 2011, was partly reusable. It landed like a glider on a runway, and its side boosters parachuted into the sea to be fished out. But each flight needed months of repairs, so it never became as cheap as hoped.

SpaceX's giant Starship goes further. In October 2024, its booster flew back to the launch tower in Texas and was caught in mid-air by two huge mechanical arms. Blue Origin, another American company, landed its small New Shepard rocket a month before the first Falcon 9 landing, though New Shepard only makes short hops to space and straight back down.

India's reusable plans

ISRO is testing a winged spaceplane called the RLV, nicknamed Pushpak, after a flying chariot in the Ramayana. In 2023 and 2024, it was dropped from a helicopter at Challakere in Karnataka and glided down to land on a runway by itself. ISRO hopes a full-size version will one day launch satellites and fly back like a plane, ready for another trip to the edge of space.

Myth or fact?

Myth: SpaceX was the first to reuse a rocket.

Truth: NASA's Space Shuttle was reused from 1981. Its orbiter and its two side boosters flew again and again, though they needed months of costly repairs. Falcon 9 was the first orbital-class booster to land upright and be flown again regularly.

Try this

Hold a pencil upright on your palm and try to keep it balanced by moving your hand. That's the problem a landing rocket solves, except the rocket balances by swivelling its engine instead of moving a hand. Notice how you have to make lots of tiny corrections every second.
