What is sushi?

Sushi began as a way to keep fish from rotting, by burying it in rice for months, and the rice was thrown away.

The short answer

Sushi is a Japanese dish made with short-grain rice seasoned with vinegar, a little sugar and salt. That rice is the heart of it. It is then pressed into small bite-sized blocks with a slice of fish on top, rolled up in sheets of seaweed, or packed into little cones.

Many people think sushi means raw fish, but it does not. Raw fish without rice is called sashimi. Sushi can be made with cooked prawns, grilled eel, sweet omelette, cucumber or pickled radish. In Japan, it is often a special-occasion meal, eaten with soy sauce, a dab of wasabi and slices of pink pickled ginger. Real wasabi is a rare, costly root, so the green paste served outside Japan is usually horseradish and mustard, dyed green.

Surprising but true

Most of the green "wasabi" served with sushi outside Japan is horseradish and mustard, coloured green. Real wasabi root is rare and costly.

Fish buried in rice

Sushi began as a way to preserve food. More than a thousand years ago, in parts of South-East Asia, China and then Japan, people packed cleaned fish in cooked rice and left it for months. The rice fermented and turned sour, and the acid stopped the fish from spoiling. When it was time to eat, they threw the rice away and ate the fish.

In Japan, one ancient form, called narezushi, is still made today near Lake Biwa. Over the centuries, people began eating the rice too, and fermenting it for shorter times.

Fast food in old Tokyo

By the 1600s, cooks had discovered a shortcut: add vinegar to fresh rice and you get the sour taste at once, with no waiting. Then, in the early 1800s, street stalls in Edo, the city now called Tokyo, began selling nigiri sushi. A cook pressed a little rice into a block by hand and laid a slice of fresh fish from Tokyo Bay on top.

It was quick, cheap food for busy workers, eaten standing up. A chef named Hanaya Yohei is often given credit for popularising it. Today the same style can cost a fortune in top restaurants.

Seaweed sheets and rolls

Rolled sushi, called maki, is wrapped in nori. Nori is made from a red seaweed that grows on nets in shallow coastal water. It is chopped, spread thin on frames like paper, and dried into dark green sheets.

A sushi chef lays nori on a bamboo mat, spreads rice over it, adds a line of filling and rolls it into a tight log before slicing. Inside-out rolls, with rice on the outside, became popular in the United States. The California roll, with crab and avocado, was made for American diners who were nervous about seaweed and raw fish. Several chefs in Los Angeles and Vancouver claim to have invented it.

Is raw fish safe

Fish meant to be eaten raw has to be very fresh and handled with great care. Many countries require or advise that it be frozen solid for a set time first, which kills tiny parasites. Good sushi restaurants buy fish from trusted suppliers and keep it cold all the way.

Young children, pregnant women and people who are unwell are often told to stick to cooked or vegetarian sushi. It is a sensible question to ask an adult before trying it.

Sushi in India

Sushi has become popular in Indian cities over the past twenty years or so, first in big hotels and then in malls and delivery apps. Indian chefs quickly made it their own, with rolls of paneer, mango, avocado or spicy tempura vegetables for vegetarian diners. Some even add a hint of chilli to the mayonnaise, which a sushi chef in Tokyo might find very surprising.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Sushi always means raw fish.

Truth: Sushi means vinegared rice, and many kinds have no raw fish at all. Cucumber rolls, egg sushi and cooked eel are all common in Japan.

Try this

Make a pretend sushi roll with an adult, no fish needed. Spread cooked rice or poha thinly on a chapati, lay a line of cucumber and carrot sticks along one edge, then roll it up tightly and cut it into rounds with a butter knife held by an adult. Look at the spiral and count the layers.
