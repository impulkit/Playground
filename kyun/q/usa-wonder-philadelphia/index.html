What happened in Philadelphia in 1776?

In the summer of 1776, men in a stuffy brick hall in Philadelphia voted to break away from the most powerful empire on Earth.

The short answer

In 1776, Philadelphia was the largest city in Britain's American colonies and the meeting place of the Continental Congress, a gathering of leaders from the 13 colonies. On 2 July, meeting in the Pennsylvania State House, they voted to declare independence from Britain. On 4 July, they approved the Declaration of Independence explaining why.

That building is now called Independence Hall. In 1787, delegates met in the same room to write the US Constitution. So, in the space of 11 years, one brick hall saw the birth of a new country and the rules it still runs on.

Surprising but true

Most signers did not sign the Declaration of Independence on 4 July. The neat handwritten copy was mostly signed on 2 August 1776.

A city on the edge of war

By 1776, fighting between the colonies and Britain had already begun, over taxes, trade and the right to govern themselves. Philadelphia, with around 30,000 people, sat in the middle of the colonies and was a busy port.

Delegates rode in from as far as New Hampshire and Georgia. They met in the State House through a hot, humid summer, with windows often shut to keep conversations secret. Flies from a nearby stable were said to bother them so much that they sped up debates.

Writing the Declaration

In June, Congress chose five men to draft a declaration. They gave the writing to Thomas Jefferson, a 33-year-old from Virginia. He worked in two rented rooms in the house of a bricklayer, Jacob Graff, writing on a small portable desk he had designed himself.

Benjamin Franklin and John Adams suggested changes. Congress then cut and edited it further, removing about a quarter of the words. One passage blaming the British king for the slave trade was taken out.

Three important days

On 2 July, Congress voted for independence. Adams wrote to his wife Abigail that the day would be celebrated with parades, games, bells and fireworks for ever after. He was right about the parties, wrong about the date.

On 4 July, Congress approved the final wording. On 8 July, it was read aloud to a crowd in the yard behind the State House. Most delegates signed a carefully handwritten copy on 2 August. John Hancock, Congress's president, signed so big that "John Hancock" became American slang for a signature.

The Liberty Bell

The bell in the State House tower was cast in London in 1752 and cracked the first time it was rung in Philadelphia. Two local metalworkers, Pass and Stow, melted it down and recast it.

It was not called the Liberty Bell in 1776. Campaigners against slavery gave it that name in the 1830s, because of words cast on it from the Bible: "Proclaim Liberty throughout all the Land". The famous crack appeared in the 1840s. Today the bell sits in its own glass building, across from Independence Hall.

Big words, unfinished promises

The Declaration says that "all men are created equal" and have rights to "life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness". Yet many of its signers, including Jefferson, enslaved people. Women could not vote, and Native Americans were left out. Those promises were fought over for two more centuries, as the story of slavery and freedom shows.

Echoes in India

The ideas of 1776 travelled. When India's Constituent Assembly wrote the Indian constitution in the 1940s, it studied the US Constitution, among others. Ideas like fundamental rights and courts that can strike down unfair laws found a place in it. B. R. Ambedkar, who led its drafting, had studied at Columbia University in New York.

In 2026 the USA marked 250 years since 4 July 1776, with celebrations across the country, including in Philadelphia. Every country marks its birthday differently, as you can see in independence days around the world.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Liberty Bell rang out on 4 July 1776 to announce independence.

Truth: There is no evidence it did. The story comes from a made-up tale published in 1847. The bell may have rung with others on 8 July, when the Declaration was first read aloud, but nobody knows for sure.
