Were pterosaurs flying dinosaurs?

Some were as wide as a small plane. But were they dinosaurs?

The short answer

Pterosaurs were flying reptiles that lived alongside the dinosaurs. They were close cousins of dinosaurs, but they were not dinosaurs. They were the first animals with backbones to fly by flapping, in the Late Triassic, more than 215 million years ago.

Their wing was skin and muscle held out by one enormously long fourth finger, stretching back to the leg. Birds use feathers; bats use several fingers. The biggest, Quetzalcoatlus, had wings about 10 metres across, close to the wingspan of a small plane.

Surprising but true

Some pterosaurs were as tall as a giraffe when standing on the ground.

Cousins, not dinosaurs

Scientists sort animals by their family tree, not just by looks. Dinosaurs share certain features of the hip and legs, which hold their legs straight under the body. Pterosaurs don't have them.

The two groups split from a shared ancestor before either appeared, more than 240 million years ago. Think of them as cousins who grew up in the same neighbourhood. The flying animals that really are dinosaurs are birds, which evolved feathered wings separately, much later.

How the wing worked

A pterosaur's arm ended in three small clawed fingers and one huge fourth finger. In big species, that single finger could be longer than the rest of the animal's body. A flap of skin, strengthened with thin fibres, ran from it to the hind leg.

The skin could tighten and loosen, a bit like a sail. Unlike a bird's feathers, it was alive, with muscles and blood vessels. Their bones were hollow and thin-walled, so even giants were light for their size. That is the same trick that keeps aeroplanes light: strong tubes rather than solid bars.

Giants and tiny ones

Pterosaurs came in a huge range of sizes. The smallest had a wingspan shorter than a school ruler. Quetzalcoatlus, found in Texas, stood about as tall as a giraffe on the ground, with a head about as long as a grown-up is tall.

How did something so big take off? Many scientists think giants launched like a pole-vaulter, pushing off with their strong arms and leaping into the air. Bats use a similar push-off today.

Walking on all fours

On the ground, pterosaurs walked on four limbs. Fossil footprints from France, the western USA and elsewhere show small hand prints beside longer foot prints. The long wing finger folded back out of the way, and the animal walked on its knuckles and feet.

Those tracks settled an old argument. Some scientists had once pictured pterosaurs crawling clumsily, or hanging from cliffs like bats.

Fishers, filterers and furry fliers

Many pterosaurs lived by the sea and caught fish, perhaps skimming the surface. Pterodaustro, from Argentina, had hundreds of thin, bristle-like teeth, probably used to strain tiny food from water, much like flamingos do today.

Many pterosaurs had a fuzzy coat of fine fibres called pycnofibres. Some had bony head crests, which may have helped them show off.

The first discoveries

The first pterosaur fossil was described in 1784 from limestone in Germany. At first nobody knew what it was: some thought it was a swimming animal. In 1828, Mary Anning found the first pterosaur skeleton in Britain, on the cliffs at Lyme Regis.

Pterosaurs survived for about 150 million years. They vanished 66 million years ago with the non-bird dinosaurs, when the asteroid hit. Today the skies belong to birds and bats, two very different answers to the same problem.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pterodactyls were flying dinosaurs.

Truth: Pterosaurs, including Pterodactylus, were flying reptiles in their own group. They were close cousins of dinosaurs. The only flying dinosaurs are birds.

Try this

Make two paper wings of the same size. Tape a straw along the front edge of one, like a pterosaur's long finger bone. Leave the other plain. Hold each by one end and wave it gently. The stiff front edge keeps the "skin" from flopping, which is what that long finger did.
