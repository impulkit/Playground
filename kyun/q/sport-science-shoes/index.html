How do running shoes help athletes?

In 1960, Abebe Bikila won the Olympic marathon barefoot. In 2026, a runner in high-tech shoes finished a marathon in under two hours.

The short answer

Running shoes help in two big ways. They protect your feet from the ground, and the best racing shoes give back some of the energy you put into each step.

Every time a runner's foot lands, it pushes down with a force of about two to three times their body weight. Older shoes mostly soaked up that bump. Since 2017, a new kind of racing shoe, often called a "super shoe", has shaken up the sport. It uses thick, light, very bouncy foam and a stiff plate inside the sole. Together, they work a bit like a spring, returning energy with each stride. At the top level, that has helped knock minutes off marathon records.

Surprising but true

Nike's first famous running sole was made in 1971 by pouring rubber into a kitchen waffle iron.

Barefoot in Rome

In 1960, the Ethiopian runner Abebe Bikila lined up for the Olympic marathon in Rome. His shoes didn't fit well, so he ran barefoot over the city's stone streets. He won, and set a world best of 2:15:16.

Four years later, in Tokyo, he won again, this time wearing shoes. He was the first person to win the Olympic marathon twice in a row. His story shows that great runners come first. Shoes only help a body that is already trained.

From waffles to foam

In 1971, the American coach Bill Bowerman wanted a sole that gripped well but stayed light. At breakfast, he looked at his wife's waffle iron and poured rubber into it. The bumpy "waffle sole" became one of the first famous products of Nike, the company he co-founded.

For decades, most running shoes used a foam called EVA, the same family of material found in yoga mats and flip-flops. It cushions well, but it gets squashed flat over time and returns only part of the energy pressed into it.

The super shoe

In 2017, Nike launched a racing shoe with a new foam, made from a plastic called PEBA. It is lighter and much bouncier. Inside the sole sat a curved plate of carbon fibre, the same strong material used in racing bikes.

A lab study tested the prototype on top runners. On average, they used about 4% less energy at the same speed. That sounds small. For a marathon runner, though, 4% can mean several minutes.

Other companies soon made their own versions. Track spikes got the same treatment, and records in races from 5,000 m to the marathon began to fall.

How the spring works

When your foot lands, the foam squashes, like pressing on a spring. Good foam springs back fast and pushes your foot up and forward. The stiff plate stops your toes bending too much and helps the foot roll forward like a rocking chair.

The rubber underneath matters too. Its pattern creates friction with the road, so your push sends you forward instead of slipping.

Rules for shoes

By 2020, some people worried that shoes, not athletes, were winning races. World Athletics, which runs the sport, made new rules. Road racing shoes can have a sole no thicker than 40 mm. Spikes for track races have their own lower limits. Any shoe used in a big race must be available for anyone to buy.

Two hours, broken

In 2019, Kenya's Eliud Kipchoge ran a marathon in 1:59:40 in Vienna, but it didn't count as a record. He had rotating teams of pacers and a car showing the line to follow.

Then, at the London Marathon on 26 April 2026, Kenya's Sabastian Sawe ran 1:59:30 in a normal race. He became the first person to break two hours in a record-eligible marathon. Shoes played a part, but so did training, nutrition and smart pacing.

Shoes are only one piece of the speed puzzle. Starting blocks, arm swings and leg power matter as much to the fastest people alive, as the science of sprinting shows.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Super shoes do the running for you, so anyone wearing them becomes an elite athlete.

Truth: They save a few percent of energy, which matters a lot at the very top, where races are won by seconds. But they can't replace years of training. A beginner in super shoes is still a beginner.

Try this

Test bounce at home. Drop a tennis ball from the same height onto a hard floor, a folded towel, a pillow and a yoga mat. Mark how high it bounces back each time. The best running foam is like the surface that is soft but still sends the ball back high.
