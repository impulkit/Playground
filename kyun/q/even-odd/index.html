Why are some numbers even and some odd?

In 2016, Delhi let cars drive only on alternate days, depending on whether their number plate ended in an even or an odd digit.

The short answer

A whole number is even if you can split it into pairs with nothing left over, and odd if one is always left alone. Six socks make three pairs, so 6 is even. Seven socks leave one lonely sock, so 7 is odd. In maths language, even numbers can be divided by 2 exactly, and odd ones leave a remainder of 1.

Even and odd numbers take turns along the number line: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, odd, even, odd, even, odd. That simple pattern turns out to be useful in places you might not expect, from computer memory to traffic rules and the way houses are numbered on a street.

Surprising but true

Zero is an even number. It can be split into two equal groups of nothing, and it sits between two odd numbers, −1 and 1.

The last-digit trick

To tell if a big number is even, you only need to look at its last digit. If it ends in 0, 2, 4, 6 or 8, it is even. If it ends in 1, 3, 5, 7 or 9, it is odd. So 4,58,93,276 is even, however long it is.

This works because every 10 is even. Any big number is a lot of tens plus its last digit. The tens can always be paired up, so only the last digit can leave one over. The same idea means 2 is the only even prime number, since every other even number divides by 2.

Rules of the game

Even and odd numbers follow neat rules when you add them. Even plus even is even. Odd plus odd is even, because the two leftovers pair up with each other. Even plus odd is odd. Multiplying is simpler still: the answer is odd only if both numbers are odd.

These rules can save time. If someone adds 15 odd numbers and claims the total is 100, you know they made a mistake, because an odd count of odd numbers always gives an odd total.

Board games make use of them too. On an ordinary six-sided die, opposite faces add up to 7. So every odd face sits opposite an even one: 1 and 6, 2 and 5, 3 and 4.

Odd and even on the road

In January 2016, the city of Delhi tried an unusual experiment to fight air pollution. On odd dates, only private cars with number plates ending in an odd digit could drive. On even dates, only those ending in an even digit. There were exceptions for emergency vehicles, women driving alone and some others. The rule has been brought back several times since, usually in winter. Scientists still disagree about how much it cleaned the air. Cities such as Paris and Mexico City have tried similar schemes.

Many streets in India, the United States and Britain number houses with odd numbers on one side and even numbers on the other. That way, a postman knows which side to walk along.

Even and odd in daily life

In computers, a trick called a parity check uses even and odd to catch errors. When a computer sends a block of binary digits, it can add one extra bit to make the number of 1s even. If a block arrives with an odd number of 1s, the computer knows something got scrambled along the way.

Some customs care about odd numbers too. At many Indian weddings and festivals, people give gifts of money such as 101, 501 or 1,001 rupees. Many families say the extra one rupee is a blessing, a sign that the giving should continue and not end in a round, finished number.

Squares from odd numbers

Add the odd numbers in order and watch. 1 = 1, 1 + 3 = 4, 1 + 3 + 5 = 9, 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16. Every total is a square number. Draw it with dots and you will see why. Each new odd number wraps neatly around two sides of the square before, making it one row and one column bigger.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Zero is neither even nor odd.

Truth: Zero meets every test for an even number. It divides by 2 with no remainder, it ends in 0, and adding it to an even number gives an even number. Studies have found that even adults take a little longer to decide zero is even, which shows how unfamiliar the idea feels.

Try this

Grab a handful of buttons, beans or coins and pair them up. If one is left over, you had an odd number. Then try this. Lay out 1 coin, then 3 more around it to make a 2 by 2 square, then 5 more to make 3 by 3. Adding odd numbers in order always makes a square number.
