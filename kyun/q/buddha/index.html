Who was the Buddha?

A prince left his palace at 29 to find out why people suffer. What he taught now guides hundreds of millions of people.

The short answer

The Buddha was a teacher who lived in north India around 2,500 years ago. He was born Siddhartha Gautama, a prince of the Shakya people, at Lumbini in what is now Nepal.

At 29 he left his comfortable life to search for an end to suffering. After years of searching, he reached what Buddhists call enlightenment, a complete understanding of life, while meditating under a tree at Bodh Gaya in Bihar. From then on he was called the Buddha, meaning "the awakened one". He spent about 45 years walking across the Ganga plains, teaching anyone who would listen. His teachings became Buddhism, one of the world's major religions.

Surprising but true

The cheerful, round-bellied "laughing Buddha" statue isn't the Buddha at all. It shows a Chinese folk monk called Budai.

Four sights outside the palace

According to tradition, Siddhartha's father kept him inside the palace so he would never see anything sad. One day, out riding, the prince saw an old man, a sick man and a dead body. For the first time he understood that everyone grows old, falls ill and dies.

Then he saw a calm wandering monk. That sight gave him an idea. He left his wife, his baby son and his riches, and set out to find a way beyond suffering.

Historians can't check this story, and it may be a teaching tale more than a diary of events. But it captures something people across Asia have found powerful: a rich young man realising that money cannot protect anyone from growing old.

The middle way

For about six years, Siddhartha studied with teachers and tried extreme hardship, eating almost nothing. Stories say he grew so thin you could see his spine through his belly. It did not bring wisdom.

So he ate a meal and chose what he called the Middle Way, between luxury and harsh self-punishment. He sat under a pipal tree, a kind of fig like the banyan, and meditated. Buddhists believe he reached enlightenment there at about 35. A descendant of that tree still grows beside the Mahabodhi Temple.

What are the Jataka tales?Before he became the Buddha, Buddhists say, he lived hundreds of lives, as a monkey king, a deer, an elephant and a generous hare.

What he taught

At a deer park in Sarnath, near Varanasi, the Buddha gave his first teaching to five companions. He explained the Four Noble Truths: life includes suffering; suffering comes from craving; it can end; and there is a path to end it.

That path is the Eightfold Path. It includes right speech, right action, right effort and right mindfulness, among others. He taught kindness towards all living beings. He welcomed people of any caste into his community of monks, and later he accepted women as nuns too.

The last journey

The Buddha died at about 80 at Kushinagar, in today's Uttar Pradesh. His followers divided his ashes and built stupas over them.

About 200 years later, Emperor Ashoka visited Lumbini and set up a stone pillar there. Its inscription says, in effect, "Here the Buddha was born". It is one of the strongest pieces of evidence that the Buddha was a real person in a real place.

A teaching that travelled

Buddhism spread from India to Sri Lanka, Tibet, China, Korea, Japan and Southeast Asia. Monks carried texts along trade routes, and great monasteries like Nalanda drew students from across Asia.

Over the centuries, Buddhism faded in India, though it never vanished. In 1956, B.R. Ambedkar and hundreds of thousands of his followers became Buddhists in Nagpur, giving it new life. Today about 32 crore people call themselves Buddhists (a 2020 estimate), and many more follow some of his teachings alongside other traditions. Most of them live in East and Southeast Asia.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Buddha is a god whom Buddhists pray to for favours.

Truth: The Buddha taught that he was a human being who had woken up to the truth. Buddhists honour him as a teacher and follow his path, though customs vary a lot between countries.

Try this

Sit quietly for three minutes and simply count your breaths, from one to ten, then start again. Notice how often your mind wanders to something else. Many Buddhists practise this kind of quiet attention every day.
