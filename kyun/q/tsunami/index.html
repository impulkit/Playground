How does an earthquake make a giant wave?

In the middle of the ocean, a tsunami is almost invisible. Near the shore, it's a different story.

The short answer

A tsunami starts when an undersea earthquake suddenly lifts or drops the sea floor.

In deep water the wave is fast but low. In shallow water, it slows and grows tall.

After the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami, India built a warning centre in Hyderabad that watches the oceans day and night.

Surprising but true

In deep ocean, a tsunami travels about as fast as a jet plane.

Not an ordinary wave

Normal waves are made by wind. They only stir the top few metres of the sea, and the distance from one crest to the next is a few metres or tens of metres.

A tsunami is different. When the sea floor jumps, it shoves the entire column of water above it, from the bottom all the way to the surface, several kilometres deep. The wave that spreads out can be hundreds of kilometres long from crest to crest. That's why it carries so much energy, and why it doesn't break and fade like a beach wave.

The word "tsunami" is Japanese for "harbour wave". It isn't caused by tides, even though people sometimes call it a tidal wave. Big landslides, volcanic eruptions and, very rarely, asteroid impacts can also start tsunamis.

Slowing down and piling up

In the deep ocean, a tsunami can travel at up to about 800 km/h, as fast as a passenger jet. It could cross the Bay of Bengal in a couple of hours. As it reaches shallow water near the coast, its front slows down while the water behind keeps coming. The wave piles up higher and higher.

Sometimes the first thing people see isn't a wave at all. The sea pulls back suddenly, leaving fish flapping on the sand. That is a warning to head inland and uphill at once. In 2011, a tsunami in Japan reached heights of about 40 m in some places.

The tallest wave ever recorded was in Lituya Bay, Alaska, in 1958. An earthquake shook loose a huge rockslide into a narrow bay, and the splash tore away trees over 500 m up the opposite hillside.

The 2004 tsunami

On 26 December 2004, a magnitude 9.1 earthquake struck under the sea near Sumatra in Indonesia. Tsunami waves spread across the Indian Ocean, reaching Thailand, Sri Lanka, India, the Maldives and even East Africa. It was one of the deadliest natural disasters in modern history.

In India, the coasts of Tamil Nadu, especially around Nagapattinam, and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands were hit hardest. On the Indonesian island of Simeulue, very close to the earthquake, an old local story taught people to run to the hills when the sea pulled back. They called it "smong". Because people remembered, most of the island's residents survived.

Watching the oceans

At that time, there was no tsunami warning system for the Indian Ocean. Since 2007, the Indian Tsunami Early Warning Centre at INCOIS in Hyderabad has watched for undersea earthquakes around the clock. It uses seismometers, tide gauges and pressure sensors on the deep sea floor that can feel a tsunami passing overhead.

The Pacific has had a warning centre in Hawaii since 1949. Once a big undersea quake is detected, warnings can reach coasts within minutes.

If you're near the sea

If the ground shakes hard near the coast, or the sea suddenly drains away, don't wait for an official warning. Move to high ground or far inland straight away, and stay there. More waves may follow, and the first is often not the biggest.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A tsunami is one single giant wave.

Truth: A tsunami is a series of waves, which can arrive minutes to an hour apart. The first is often not the biggest. That's why people should stay on high ground until officials say it's safe, even if the sea seems calm again.

Try this

Pour a few centimetres of water into a long baking tray and prop a book under one end of the tray to make a "beach" of shallow water. At the deep end, push the water once with a flat ruler held upright. Watch the wave travel and pile up as it reaches the shallow end. Then compare it with small ripples made by blowing on the surface.
