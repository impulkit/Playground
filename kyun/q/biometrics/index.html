What is biometrics, and how does your body become a password?

Your fingertips, your eyes and even the way you walk carry patterns that belong to nobody else, and machines have learned to read them.

The short answer

Biometrics means measuring the body to recognise a person. The word joins two Greek pieces: bio, life, and metric, measure. A biometric system picks a feature that differs from person to person, such as a fingerprint or the shape of a face. Then it turns that feature into numbers.

Next time that person comes along, the machine measures them again and compares the new numbers with the stored ones. If they are close enough, the door opens, the phone switches on or the payment goes through. Nothing has to be remembered or carried, because the key is part of you.

Surprising but true

Identical twins share the same DNA, but they do not have the same fingerprints.

Patterns only you have

Fingerprints are the oldest example. The ridges on your fingertips form before you are born and stay the same pattern for life, even after a cut heals. Experts sort them into loops, whorls and arches, then look at where ridges split or stop.

The iris, the coloured ring around the pupil, has a pattern of folds and flecks that is even more detailed. Faces are trickier, because they change with age, glasses, beards and light. Some systems also use voices, the veins inside a palm, or the rhythm of a person's walk.

A good biometric feature has to pass a few tests. It should differ between people, stay steady over the years, and be quick to measure. Fingerprints and irises score well on all of these. Voices change when you have a cold, and faces change as you grow, so those systems need regular updates.

A story from Bengal

In the 1890s, police in British-ruled Bengal needed a way to sort criminal records. Two Indian officers, Azizul Haque and Hem Chandra Bose, worked out much of a filing system for fingerprints under their boss, Edward Henry. Calcutta opened one of the world's first fingerprint bureaus in 1897.

The method spread to Britain and then across the world. For decades it was called the Henry system, and the two Indian officers who did much of the maths were only properly credited much later.

Biometrics for a billion people

India now runs one of the largest biometric systems ever built. When someone signs up for Aadhaar, the 12-digit ID number, they give all ten fingerprints, scans of both irises and a face photo. More than 130 crore Aadhaar numbers had been issued by 2024.

People use it to collect rations, open bank accounts and get a SIM card. In the US, airports and passport offices also collect fingerprints and face photos from many travellers. A machine that can match a face in a second makes long queues move faster. Many Indian airports now let passengers walk through gates that check their face instead of a boarding pass.

The catch

If someone steals your password, you can make a new one. You cannot grow new fingerprints. That's why careful systems never store a plain picture of your finger. They keep a coded summary instead, often locked inside a secure part of the chip.

Machines also make mistakes. Worn fingertips, common among farm workers and labourers, can be hard to read. Some face systems have been less accurate for certain groups of people, a problem explored in face recognition. And many people ask who gets to keep these records and for how long, a question at the heart of privacy laws in both India and the US.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Identical twins have identical fingerprints.

Truth: Twins share their DNA, but fingerprints form in the womb as the skin presses and folds against its surroundings. Those tiny differences give each twin a different set of ridges.

Try this

Rub a soft pencil on a scrap of paper until it's dark, press your fingertip on it, then press the finger onto a strip of clear tape and stick it on white paper. Do the same for a friend or family member. Look for loops, whorls and arches, and see how many differences you can find.
