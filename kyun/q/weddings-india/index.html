Why are Indian weddings so big?

Some Indian weddings last five days, feed a thousand guests and need a brass band, a horse and a priest who watches the clock.

The short answer

An Indian wedding is usually seen as two whole families joining, as well as two people. So aunts, uncles, cousins, neighbours, old school friends and colleagues of both families may all be invited. A guest list of several hundred is common, and some run past a thousand.

There are also many events. A wedding can begin days earlier with henna parties, music nights and prayers, then the wedding itself, then a reception. Each event brings its own clothes, food and guests.

Not every wedding is huge. Plenty of couples choose a small ceremony or a simple court wedding. But the big wedding is a tradition that many families save up for over years.

Surprising but true

In many Hindu families the wedding time is chosen by studying the positions of the Sun, Moon and planets, so some ceremonies begin in the middle of the night.

Two families, not two people

In much of India, people grow up close to large families. Cousins can feel like brothers and sisters, and an elderly great-aunt expects to be asked. Leaving someone out can cause hurt that lasts for years.

The wedding is also a way of welcoming a new person into a whole network of relatives. Many rituals are about the families as much as the couple. In some Hindu weddings the bride's parents give her hand to the groom in a ceremony called kanyadaan. In a Sikh wedding, the Anand Karaj, the couple walks four times around the Guru Granth Sahib while hymns are sung.

Many days, many rituals

India has many religions and regions, and each has its own wedding customs. A Muslim wedding centres on the nikah, a contract agreed in front of witnesses. Christian couples in Kerala or Goa may marry in a church, then hold a big lunch.

Many North Indian weddings follow a pattern. At the mehendi, women's hands are painted with henna. At the haldi, turmeric paste is rubbed on the couple's skin. The sangeet is a night of music and dance, often to film songs. Then comes the baraat, where the groom arrives on a horse or in a car, with relatives dancing in the street to a brass band.

How do Indian calendars work?India has an official national calendar, yet most families plan festivals using a different one, and which one depends on where they live.

Food for a crowd

Feeding guests well is a matter of pride. A wedding may serve several meals a day, from a breakfast of puris to a dinner of dozens of dishes and a whole table of sweets. In parts of South India, the meal is served on banana leaves, with servers walking down long rows of guests.

Big weddings employ many people: cooks, tailors, florists, priests, drummers, photographers, decorators and tent makers. In wedding season, some streets fill with marigold sellers and lights.

Crowded calendars

Many Hindu families pick a date that astrologers call auspicious, or lucky. These dates come from traditional Indian calendars, so thousands of weddings can land on the same few nights. Banquet halls are booked a year ahead, and traffic jams form around wedding venues.

In the USA, a wedding is often a single day, with roughly a hundred guests. Indian American families sometimes blend both styles, with a mehendi on Friday and a church or courthouse wedding on Saturday.

The other side

Big weddings are not always happy stories. Some families borrow heavily to pay for them, and feel pushed by what neighbours expect. Dowry, money or gifts demanded from the bride's family, has been illegal in India since 1961, but it still happens in some places. That is why more couples now choose smaller weddings and spend the money on a home or their studies instead.

Myth or fact?

Myth: All Indian marriages are arranged by parents, and the couple has no say.

Truth: Many marriages are arranged by families, but today most families ask the couple, and love marriages are growing. Under Indian law, a marriage needs the free consent of both people.

Ask a grandparent or older relative about a wedding they remember. How many days did it last, what food was served and how many people came? Draw a simple family tree of the guests they mention and count how many were cousins.
