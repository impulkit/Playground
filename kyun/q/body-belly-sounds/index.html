Why do we blush?

Charles Darwin called blushing "the most peculiar and the most human of all expressions", and scientists still argue about why we do it.

The short answer

You blush when your body releases a burst of adrenaline, the same chemical behind the "fight or flight" feeling. Adrenaline makes the tiny blood vessels in your cheeks, ears and neck widen. More blood flows near the surface of the skin, so it turns pinker and feels warm.

Blushing usually comes with emotions like embarrassment, shyness or shame. You can't switch it off by trying. In fact, worrying about blushing often makes it worse.

The bigger mystery is why humans blush at all. No other animal seems to. Scientists think it might be a signal to other people that we know we've slipped up.

Surprising but true

Being told "you're blushing" can make you blush even more, even when you weren't embarrassed to begin with.

What happens in your face

Your face is packed with small blood vessels, many of them close to the surface. The skin on your cheeks is also thin. That makes changes in blood flow easy to see there.

When you feel embarrassed, your nervous system acts within seconds. Adrenaline is released, and the veins and small vessels in your face open wider. Blood rushes in. Your cheeks can warm by a degree or more, which heat cameras can pick up.

Most blushes fade within a few minutes. Some people also blush when they get praise or a surprise compliment, as well as when something goes wrong. Even good attention can set it off. Exercise, hot weather or spicy food can also redden your face, but that is a different kind of flushing, linked to heat rather than feelings.

Darwin's puzzle

In 1872, Charles Darwin wrote a book about how humans and animals show feelings. He called blushing "the most peculiar and the most human of all expressions." He noticed that people everywhere blushed, and that blind people blushed too, so it wasn't learned by watching.

About 25 years later, the American writer Mark Twain joked: "Man is the only animal that blushes. Or needs to."

Children seem to start blushing around the age of two to four, about the same time they become aware of how others see them. That suggests blushing is tied to thinking about what other people think of us.

A built-in apology

Here's one leading idea. If you break a rule or make a mistake, a blush shows others that you know and you feel bad. That might make them more likely to forgive you.

Experiments support this. Psychologists in the Netherlands showed people pictures and stories of someone who made a mistake. When the person blushed, viewers trusted them more and judged them more kindly. A blush seemed to work like a silent "sorry".

Blushing could be part of how humans live in groups and get along. It connects with empathy, our ability to feel what others feel. Not everyone agrees on this explanation, and research continues. Some scientists think blushing may also calm others down after an awkward moment, stopping a small argument from growing into a big one.

When blushing is a worry

Almost everyone blushes sometimes. For some people, though, it happens a lot and feels very upsetting. A strong fear of blushing has a name: erythrophobia. If blushing makes you anxious or stops you doing things, it is worth talking to a trusted adult or doctor, who can help.

Blushing looks different on different skin colours. On darker skin, the redness can be harder to see, but the warming and the feeling are just the same.

Trying hard not to blush usually backfires. Some experts suggest simply letting it happen and carrying on. And the strangest part? People often like someone better after seeing them blush, and doing small acts of kindness seems to win people over in much the same way.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Only people with fair skin blush.

Truth: Everyone blushes in the same way, because the blood vessels in the face widen. On darker skin it can be harder to see, but the cheeks still warm up and can be measured with heat cameras.

Try this

Ask a family member to remember an embarrassing moment out loud while you watch their cheeks and ears. Then swap. Notice whether the colour shows up, where it appears first and how quickly it fades.
