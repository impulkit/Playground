How old is Earth?

4,500 million years. Too big to picture, so let's squeeze it into one day.

The short answer

Earth is about 4.5 billion years old: 4,500 million years.

Scientists found this using atom clocks in the oldest rocks on Earth, in Moon rocks, and in meteorites.

If Earth's whole history were one day, humans would appear in the last few seconds.

Surprising but true

All of human history fits in the last few seconds before midnight.

Guesses before science

For centuries, people guessed Earth's age from stories or from how long it might take the sea to become salty. Their numbers were far too small. In 1650, an Irish archbishop named James Ussher added up the generations listed in the Bible and arrived at 4004 BCE. In the 1860s, the British physicist Lord Kelvin worked out how long a red-hot Earth would take to cool, and got tens of millions of years. He didn't know about the heat made by radioactive atoms deep inside.

Ancient Hindu texts describe time very differently, in vast repeating cycles. One day of Brahma, called a kalpa, lasts 4.32 billion years. It is a religious idea, not a measurement, but its scale is close to the real age.

Atom clocks

In the 20th century, scientists learned that some atoms change at a steady rate. Measuring them in rocks and meteorites gave Earth's real age. In 1913, the British geologist Arthur Holmes used uranium in rocks to show that Earth must be well over a billion years old, far older than Kelvin had said. Many scientists found that hard to accept at first.

The American scientist Clair Patterson made the key measurement in 1956. He studied lead in a meteorite from Canyon Diablo in Arizona and got about 4.55 billion years, very close to today's figure. Along the way, he found that lead from car petrol had polluted almost everything, even dust in his lab. He spent years campaigning against leaded petrol, which was later banned in many countries, helping to reduce air pollution.

Older than any rock

Earth's surface is restless. Plates move, mountains wear down, and rock sinks back into the hot interior to be melted and remade. So Earth's very first rocks are long gone. The oldest we have, from northern Canada, are about 4 billion years old.

Tiny crystals called zircons are tougher. A few from the Jack Hills in Western Australia are about 4.4 billion years old, which means solid ground and maybe even oceans existed very early. For the full age, scientists turn to meteorites, leftover pieces from the time the planets formed. The oldest are about 4.57 billion years old. Rocks brought back from the Moon tell a similar story, and help explain how the Moon formed.

What are meteorites made of?Some of the rocks that fall from the sky are older than Earth itself, and a few are pieces of Mars and the Moon.

Deep time

Earth's history is so long that the dinosaurs ruled for 165 million years, yet that's under 4% of it. Zoom in on the dinosaurs.

Squeeze it all into one day, starting at midnight. Simple life appears in the oceans before dawn. Animals with shells and eyes don't show up until about nine at night. Dinosaurs arrive at around a quarter to eleven and vanish at 23:39. Modern humans, who appeared about 300,000 years ago, show up just six seconds before midnight. Every king, every war, every invention you've read about happened in the last fraction of a second.

Geologists call this enormous span "deep time". Learning to think in it is one of the big ideas of science, like realising that Earth goes around the Sun.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The oldest rocks on Earth are as old as Earth itself.

Truth: Earth's crust is constantly being worn away, melted and remade. The oldest known rocks, in northern Canada, are about 4 billion years old, and only a few tiny zircon crystals are older. Earth's full age comes from meteorites and Moon rocks.

Try this

Make a deep-time ribbon. Cut a piece of string or ribbon 4.5 metres long, so each millimetre stands for one million years. Mark the first dinosaurs about 23 cm from one end, and the asteroid that ended them at 6.6 cm. All of human history fits in the last third of a millimetre.
