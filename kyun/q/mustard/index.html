How is mustard used across India?

A mustard seed has no sharp taste at all until you crack it and add water. Then a hidden chemical reaction makes it bite.

The short answer

In India, mustard is a seed, an oil, a vegetable and a sauce. Tiny black or brown seeds crackle in hot oil at the start of countless dals and curries. Mustard oil is the main cooking oil in Bengal, Bihar, Odisha, Assam, Punjab and much of Uttar Pradesh. The leaves become sarson ka saag in Punjab. In Bengal, ground mustard turns into a sharp paste for fish.

India is one of the world's biggest growers of mustard. In winter, fields across the north turn bright yellow with its flowers.

Surprising but true

Mustard oil, used in kitchens all over north and east India, is sold in the USA labelled "for external use only" because of a fatty acid that US rules limit in food.

A winter crop of yellow

Mustard is a rabi crop, sown after the monsoon in October and November and harvested in February and March. It needs little water, so it suits dry places. Rajasthan grows nearly half of India's mustard, followed by states such as Madhya Pradesh, Haryana and Uttar Pradesh.

When the plants flower, whole districts turn yellow. Bees buzz between the blooms, and farmers often keep beehives at the edges of fields, because pollinators help the seed pods fill. One of the most famous scenes in Hindi cinema, from the 1995 film Dilwale Dulhania Le Jayenge, is a song set among flowering mustard fields in Punjab.

Seeds that pop

In south and west India, mustard seeds (called rai, kadugu or mohari) go into almost every tadka, or tempering. They are dropped into hot oil until they pop and crackle, often with curry leaves, cumin and dried chillies. Then the whole spoonful is poured over dal, sambar, poha or a vegetable.

Frying in oil makes the seeds nutty instead of hot. That's because the sharp taste only forms when crushed seeds meet water, which produces a sharp chemical called allyl isothiocyanate. Heat destroys the enzyme that starts that reaction.

The sting of Bengal

In Bengal and Odisha, mustard is ground with water into a paste that can make your eyes water. Bengali cooks use it in shorshe ilish, hilsa fish steamed or cooked in mustard sauce, one of the region's most loved dishes. Kasundi, a fermented Bengali mustard sauce, is eaten with fried snacks and is now sold in jars around the world.

Raw mustard oil is strong and pungent too. Cooks often heat it until it smokes slightly to mellow it before adding spices. Many families in the north also use mustard oil for pickles. Mango, lemon and chilli pickles sit in jars of it for months, because the oil and spices help keep the vegetables from spoiling.

Greens and massage

In Punjab, the leaves are as important as the seeds. Sarson ka saag, slow-cooked mustard greens, is eaten in winter with makki di roti, flatbread made of maize, and a lump of white butter.

Mustard oil is also used outside the kitchen. In many north Indian homes, babies get a warm mustard oil massage, and the oil is rubbed into hair. In winter, some people put a drop in their nostrils, an old home custom.

Mustard around the world

In the USA, mustard usually means a bright yellow sauce squeezed onto hot dogs. It is made from milder white mustard seeds, vinegar and turmeric, which gives the colour. France is famous for Dijon mustard, made with brown seeds and wine or vinegar. Germany and England have their own versions.

American rules limit a fatty acid called erucic acid in food, and traditional mustard oil contains a lot of it. So in the USA, mustard oil is often sold labelled "for external use only". Scientists still debate how much it matters for people's health. Meanwhile, Indian scientists have bred new mustard varieties with less erucic acid. Canola, popular in North America, came from breeding the same family of plants.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Mustard gas, the poison used in the First World War, was made from mustard.

Truth: Mustard gas has nothing to do with the mustard plant. It got its name because it is yellowish-brown and some people thought it smelled a little like mustard or garlic.

Try this

With a grown-up, crush a few mustard seeds with the back of a spoon and taste a tiny bit dry. Then mix a pinch of crushed seed with a few drops of cold water, wait ten minutes and taste again (only a tiny bit). Notice how the sharpness grows.
