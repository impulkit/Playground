Is emoji a language?

In 2015, the Oxford dictionaries picked a "word" of the year that was not a word at all. It was the crying-with-laughter face.

The short answer

Most linguists, the scientists who study language, say emoji are not a language on their own. A language needs grammar: rules for putting signs together to say new, exact things, like "the dog the cat chased yesterday ran away". Emoji can't do that reliably.

But emoji are not meaningless either. Many researchers say they work like the gestures and facial expressions we use when we speak. A smile, a shrug or a thumbs-up adds tone to words. Emoji do the same job in text, where nobody can see your face.

Surprising but true

The first big set of emoji, 176 tiny pictures drawn for Japanese phones in 1999, is now part of the collection of the Museum of Modern Art in New York.

From 176 pictures to nearly 4,000

In 1999, a Japanese designer named Shigetaka Kurita drew 176 small pictures for a mobile phone service. Each was just 12 by 12 dots. There were weather symbols, hearts, faces and a few objects. The word emoji is Japanese for "picture" plus "character". It has nothing to do with the English word "emotion", though the match is a happy accident.

In 2010, emoji were added to Unicode, the standard that lets every computer and phone agree on letters and symbols. That made them spread worldwide. By 2025, Unicode listed nearly 4,000 emoji, counting different skin tones. New ones arrive every year after a long application process, and anyone can propose one. Proposals must show that lots of people would actually use the new emoji, and most are turned down.

What makes a real language

Spoken languages and sign languages can say anything. You can talk about the past, the future, what might have happened and what never will. You can make up a sentence nobody has ever said, and others still understand it.

Emoji struggle with that. Try writing "I would have come if my aunt had not called" in emoji. There is no emoji for "would have" or "if". Studies of real messages show most people use emoji alongside words, usually one or two at a time, to add feeling. Long chains of emoji with no words are rare, and often turn into a guessing game.

More like a wink than a word

Writer and linguist Gretchen McCulloch compares emoji to gestures. When you say "great" with a sigh and rolled eyes, your face changes the meaning. Text can't roll its eyes, so we add a face. A single emoji can turn "fine" from cheerful into annoyed.

Just like gestures, emoji change from place to place. The thumbs-up is friendly in India and the USA but rude in a few other countries. Younger people sometimes use the skull emoji to mean "I'm laughing so hard I died", while older people may read it as a threat.

Pictures that became letters

History shows how pictures can grow into writing. Egyptian hieroglyphs and early Chinese characters both started as pictures of things. Over time, many signs began to stand for sounds or ideas, until people could write anything they could say.

Could emoji travel the same road? Some experts think a few emoji already act like words, such as the fire emoji meaning "excellent". Others say there is no sign of a real grammar forming. One brave fan even "translated" the whole novel Moby-Dick into emoji, with the help of hundreds of online helpers. Readers agree it is hard to follow without the original beside it.

Still, people have been adding pictures to messages since long before phones. Thousands of years ago, the first signs scratched on clay were little drawings too. The story of how humans got from those pictures to alphabets is the story of how writing began. Meanwhile, deaf communities have built sign languages with grammar as rich as Hindi or English, made entirely of hand shapes, faces and movement.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Emoji mean the same thing to everyone everywhere.

Truth: Meanings shift between countries, age groups and even phone brands. A folded-hands emoji can mean "please", "thank you", "praying" or a high five, depending on who sends it.
