Where did guavas and papayas come from?

Guavas and papayas taste so Indian that many people call them desi fruits. Both actually came from the Americas, on ships, about 400 to 500 years ago.

The short answer

Guavas and papayas first grew in the warm forests of southern Mexico, Central America and northern South America. People there were eating them long before Europeans arrived.

After Columbus crossed the Atlantic in 1492, Spanish and Portuguese sailors carried American plants around the world. Guavas and papayas travelled across oceans along with chillies, tomatoes, potatoes and corn. They probably reached India in the 1500s and 1600s, and loved the warm climate so much that they grew almost everywhere. Today India grows more guavas and more papayas than any other country.

Surprising but true

Weight for weight, a guava has about four times as much vitamin C as an orange.

A great swap of plants

Historians call the swap of plants and animals between the Americas and the rest of the world the Columbian Exchange. Europeans took horses, wheat and sugarcane to the Americas. Ships brought back maize, potatoes, tomatoes, cocoa, chillies, pineapples and peanuts.

Portuguese ships sailed regularly between Brazil, Africa and Goa, so many American plants reached India through Portuguese ports. Some were grown first in Goa and Kerala before spreading inland. Within a couple of centuries, Indians were cooking with chillies and snacking on guavas as if they had always been here.

The guava

Guava trees are small and tough. They grow in poor soil, survive dry spells and fruit twice a year. Their fruit can be white or pink inside, packed with small hard seeds, and smells strongly sweet when ripe.

In India, guava is called amrood or peru in many places. Street sellers slice it and sprinkle it with salt and red chilli powder, which makes the sweet fruit taste even sweeter. Prayagraj, formerly Allahabad, is famous for a variety called Allahabad Safeda.

Weight for weight, guava has about four times as much vitamin C as an orange. Your body needs vitamin C to heal cuts and keep gums healthy, and it can't store much, so you need some every day.

The papaya

A papaya plant looks like a palm tree, but it isn't a tree at all. It is a giant herb with a soft, hollow stem and a crown of big leaves at the top. It can grow from a seed to a fruiting plant in less than a year, which is very fast for a plant that size.

Papaya fruit hangs in clusters right under the leaves. Inside, the flesh is orange and soft, with a hollow full of shiny black seeds that taste peppery. Green papaya is cooked as a vegetable or grated into salads in many parts of India and Southeast Asia.

Unripe papaya is full of an enzyme called papain, which breaks down proteins. Cooks in India have long added raw papaya paste to meat to make it tender, and papain is used in some meat tenderiser powders.

Saving Hawaii's papayas

In the 1990s, a virus called papaya ringspot spread through papaya farms in Hawaii. It made the plants weak and the fruit spotty, and the industry was close to collapse. Scientists at the University of Hawaii and Cornell University developed a papaya that resists the virus by giving it a small piece of the virus's own genes, a little like a vaccine.

Farmers began planting this "Rainbow" papaya in 1998, and Hawaiian papaya farming recovered. It is one of the best-known examples of a genetically modified crop helping farmers, though people still debate whether such crops should be grown.

At home far from home

Both fruits now grow wild in many tropical places they weren't native to. Guava trees spread so well that they have become a weed in some islands, crowding out local plants. In India, though, they mostly stay in gardens, orchards and street carts. It's strange to think that a fruit sold from a handcart outside an Indian school first ripened in a forest on the other side of the world.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Guavas and papayas are native Indian fruits.

Truth: Both first grew in tropical America. They spread to India within the last 500 years and grew so well here that India now grows more of them than any other country.
