How does a letter find its way?

India Post runs about 1.65 lakh post offices, including one that floats on a lake in Srinagar.

The short answer

When you drop a letter in a post box, it starts a relay. A postal worker collects it and takes it to a sorting office. There it is grouped with other letters going the same way, packed into bags and sent by van, train or plane. Near the end, a local post office sorts it once more, and a postman or postwoman carries it to your door.

The secret to all this sorting is the code at the bottom of the address. In India it is the six-digit PIN code. In the USA it is the five-digit ZIP code. Each number narrows the destination down step by step, the way a country, then a state, then a town would.

Surprising but true

Before 1840, the person who received a letter usually paid for it. Postage stamps flipped that, so the sender paid in advance.

Reading the code

India introduced the Postal Index Number, or PIN, on 15 August 1972. The first digit names one of eight large regions. Delhi, for example, starts with 1, and Kerala with 6. The second digit picks a smaller area, and the third a sorting district. The last three digits identify the exact post office that will deliver the letter.

The USA started ZIP codes in 1963. ZIP stands for Zone Improvement Plan. Its first digit also marks a big region, from 0 in New England to 9 on the West Coast. Without these codes, a sorter would have to know thousands of town names by heart.

Sorting at speed

In big sorting centres, machines now do much of the work. A camera photographs each envelope, and software reads the address, even many handwritten ones. The machine then prints a pattern of tiny lines on the envelope, a kind of barcode, so later machines can sort it in a blink.

Letters that the computer can't read go on screen to a human, who types in the code. In India, many letters are still sorted by hand into rows of pigeonholes. For more than a century, mail travelled in special carriages on India's railways, where clerks sorted it while the train rolled through the night.

At the end of the line, a postman or postwoman walks or rides a fixed route called a beat. Many know every house on it, which helps when an address is incomplete. Letters that can't be delivered or returned go to a special office, where staff open them to look for clues.

Stamps change everything

Until 1840, posting a letter in Britain could be expensive, and the person receiving it often had to pay. Then the Penny Black appeared, the first sticky postage stamp. Senders paid one penny in advance for a letter anywhere in the country. Other countries soon copied the idea. India's first stamps for the whole country came out in 1854.

In 1874, countries set up the Universal Postal Union so that mail could cross borders smoothly. A stamp from India pays for a letter's whole journey, even when another country's postman delivers it.

Post offices in surprising places

India Post has about 1.65 lakh post offices (2025), and about nine in every ten are in villages. Many are tiny, run from one room. Srinagar has a post office on a houseboat on Dal Lake. At Hikkim in Himachal Pradesh, about 4,400 m up, one of the world's highest post offices sends postcards from the snowy Spiti valley.

Post offices also do much more than mail. Many Indian families keep savings accounts there, and pensions and parcels pass across the same counter. In 2025 India Post introduced DIGIPIN, a code for any small square of land in the country. It could help find homes that have no street name at all, a problem maps and grids have long tried to solve.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The US Postal Service's official motto is "Neither snow nor rain nor heat nor gloom of night..."

Truth: The Postal Service has no official motto. Those words are carved on a big post office building in New York City and come from an ancient Greek writer, Herodotus, describing Persian messengers.

Try this

Write a letter or postcard to a relative or friend in another town and post it. Note the date you posted it and ask them to note the day it arrives. Look at the envelope when it comes back to you, or ask them to describe the stamps and postmarks it collected on the way.
