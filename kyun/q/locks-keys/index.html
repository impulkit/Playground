How does a lock know the right key?

A lock doesn't recognise its key. It has a row of tiny pins, and only the right key lifts each one perfectly.

The short answer

Most door locks are pin tumbler locks. Inside, there is a metal cylinder called the plug, which has to turn for the door to open. Running down into it are five or six tiny pin pairs, each pushed down by a spring. Each pair is split into two pieces, a lower pin and an upper pin, and the pins are all different lengths.

When no key is in the lock, the upper pins poke down into the plug and jam it. Slide in the right key, and its hills and valleys lift each pin pair by just the right amount. Now every split lines up with the edge of the plug, a line called the shear line. Nothing crosses it, so the plug turns and the bolt slides back. The wrong key leaves at least one pin sticking across, and the plug stays stuck.

Surprising but true

Your house key is probably not one of a kind. With only a few pin heights to choose from, other keys out there almost certainly have the same pattern.

Wooden keys of the ancient world

The pin idea is very old. About 4,000 years ago, people in ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia used large wooden locks. A wooden bar held the door shut, and loose wooden pins dropped into holes in the bar to hold it in place. The key was a wooden stick with pegs on it. Pushed in and lifted, its pegs raised the pins and the bar could slide.

Some of these keys were as long as an adult's arm and were carried over the shoulder. The Romans later made smaller metal locks and keys, and some Romans wore little keys as rings on their fingers.

The Yales of America

In the 1840s, the American locksmith Linus Yale Sr. built a lock using pins. His son, Linus Yale Jr., shrank the idea into a small cylinder and patented it in the 1860s. He also designed the flat key with a wavy edge that almost everyone still carries.

The design was cheap to make and hard to open without the key. It spread around the world, and the Yale name still appears on many locks today. Most keys in your home, your school and your local bank work the same way.

Aligarh, the city of locks

In India, the city of Aligarh in Uttar Pradesh has made locks for well over a century. Thousands of small workshops cast, file and assemble padlocks of every size, from tiny brass ones for suitcases to heavy iron ones for shop shutters. Many families have passed the skills down through generations, and Aligarh locks are sold all over India and beyond.

A padlock works on the same principle as a door lock. Some use pins, while others use flat metal plates called levers that must each be lifted to the right height.

Locks without keys

A combination lock has spinning discs instead of pins. Each disc has a notch, and the right numbers line up all the notches so a bar can drop through. Hotel key cards carry a code on a magnetic strip or a tiny chip. A reader in the door checks the code and releases the bolt with a small motor.

Phones go further still. They read your fingerprint or the shape of your face and compare it with a pattern stored inside. In a way, a password is a key too. It is a pattern only you are meant to know, and it only works when every character is in the right place.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Every key in the world is completely unique.

Truth: A typical lock has about five or six pins, each cut to one of a handful of depths. That gives tens of thousands of patterns at most, and fewer that work well in practice. With millions of locks around, many share the same pattern. They stay safe mostly because nobody knows which doors match.

Try this

Ask an adult for an old padlock with two different keys, or two old house keys. Lay them side by side on paper and trace around their edges with a pencil. Compare the hills and valleys in each outline. Every dip matches the height of one pin pair inside the lock.
