How does technology help people with disabilities?

Blind people can use a touchscreen phone that reads every button aloud, and many speed the voice up so much that nobody else can follow it.

The short answer

Assistive technology is any tool that helps a disabled person do something that would otherwise be hard or impossible. Some tools are simple, like a ramp or a large-print book. Others are high-tech: phones that read the screen aloud, glasses that describe what they see, and computers controlled with the eyes.

A good example is the screen reader. It turns everything on a screen into speech or braille. On a touchscreen, a blind person slides a finger around and the phone says what's underneath. A double tap selects it. With practice, many users set the voice to talk so fast that it sounds like a blur to everyone else.

Surprising but true

Many things everyone now uses, like subtitles, audiobooks and voice typing, were first developed or improved for disabled people.

Seeing with sound and touch

The story goes back to 1824, when a 15-year-old French boy named Louis Braille invented a system of raised dots that blind people could read with their fingertips. Braille is still used today, on lift buttons, medicine boxes and Indian banknotes, which have raised marks to help blind people tell them apart.

In 1976 the American inventor Ray Kurzweil built a reading machine that could scan a printed page and read it aloud in a computer voice. It was as big as a washing machine. Today, a free phone app can do the same with the camera, reading menus, signs and even handwriting.

Hearing with your eyes

For deaf and hard-of-hearing people, captions turn speech into text on a screen. Phones can now caption almost any video or call live, using speech recognition. Video calling has made sign language much easier to use at a distance, since two signers can now see each other's hands from anywhere.

Flashing doorbells, vibrating alarm clocks and phones that buzz in patterns help people who can't hear sounds. Hearing aids and cochlear implants help others hear better.

Control without hands

People who can't move their hands can control computers in other ways. Eye-tracking cameras watch where someone looks and move a pointer there. Switches can be pressed with the head, a foot or a puff of breath, stepping through options one by one. Voice assistants let people turn on lights, make calls and write messages by speaking.

Some people with conditions that affect speech use communication devices that speak for them. Researchers are even testing brain implants that let paralysed people move a cursor just by thinking.

Laws that open doors

Technology only helps if buildings, websites and services are designed for everyone. The Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 requires public places and many businesses in the United States to be accessible. India's Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act of 2016 does something similar, and the Accessible India Campaign, launched in 2015, works on making government buildings, transport and websites easier to use.

Websites can be built so that screen readers understand them, with labels on buttons and descriptions of pictures. Many still aren't, which shuts people out of shopping, banking and schoolwork. Making cities and schools work for everyone is the idea behind inclusion.

The curb-cut effect

In the United States, campaigners in the 1970s pushed cities to cut slopes into the kerbs at street corners, so wheelchair users could cross. Soon parents with prams, travellers with suitcases and delivery workers with trolleys were using them too. People now call this the curb-cut effect.

The same thing happens with technology. Captions help people learning a language and anyone watching on a noisy train. Voice typing helps busy cooks. Audiobooks, first recorded for blind readers in the 1930s, are now enjoyed by millions of commuters. Designing for disability often means designing better for all.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Accessibility features only help a small number of people.

Truth: About one in six people worldwide has a significant disability, and almost everyone will need some help as they age. Features designed for disabled people, such as ramps, captions and voice control, end up helping parents with prams, travellers, and anyone in a noisy room.

Try this

With an adult's help, turn on the screen reader on a phone or tablet (TalkBack on Android, VoiceOver on iPhone). Close your eyes and try to open a single app. Then turn on live captions while watching a video with the sound off, and notice how much you understand.
