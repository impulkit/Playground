What were the princely states?

In 1947, more than 560 kings, nawabs and rajas still ruled their own little countries inside India. Within about two years, almost all of them had joined the new nation.

The short answer

Princely states were kingdoms inside British India that were ruled by Indian princes, not by British officials. In 1947 there were about 565 of them. Together they covered around two-fifths of the land of undivided India.

The princes accepted Britain as the supreme power. The British controlled their defence and their dealings with the outside world, and kept an official called a Resident at many courts. Inside their states, the rulers collected taxes, made laws and lived in grand palaces. When the British left in 1947, the princes had to decide whether to join India or Pakistan. Almost all joined one or the other within two years.

Surprising but true

The ruler of Baroda paid for a young B.R. Ambedkar to study at Columbia University in New York.

Kings large and small

The states came in every size. Hyderabad, ruled by the Nizam, was larger than England and Scotland together. Jammu and Kashmir was even bigger in area. Mysore, Baroda, Travancore and Gwalior were also large and rich.

At the other end were tiny states, especially in Gujarat's Kathiawar region, that were little more than a few villages. The British ranked rulers by gun salutes. The most important received 21 guns when they visited a British official. Smaller rulers got fewer, and some got none, which they took as an insult.

Some states led the way

A few princely states were well ahead of British India in some ways. Mysore built one of Asia's early hydroelectric power stations at Shivanasamudra in 1902, and the Krishna Raja Sagara dam on the Kaveri.

In Baroda, Maharaja Sayajirao Gaekwad III made primary education free and compulsory in the early 1900s. He also gave a scholarship to a brilliant young student named B.R. Ambedkar, who went to Columbia University in New York and later led the drafting of India's Constitution. In 1936 Travancore's ruler opened its temples to people of all castes, a major step against untouchability.

Bringing them in

When independence came, the British said the princes could join India or Pakistan, or in theory stay independent. A patchwork of hundreds of independent kingdoms would have made a united India very hard to run.

Sardar Patel, India's Home Minister, and his secretary V.P. Menon set out to persuade the rulers. They offered an Instrument of Accession: the princes would hand over defence, foreign affairs and communications, and keep their titles, palaces and a yearly allowance called a privy purse. Through meetings, appeals to patriotism and some firm pressure, most rulers signed before 15 August 1947.

The hard cases

A few states held out. The Nawab of Junagadh, in Gujarat, joined Pakistan, though most of his people were Hindu and the state was surrounded by India. Indian forces moved in, and a vote in February 1948 showed most people wanted to join India.

The Nizam of Hyderabad wanted independence. After long talks failed and violence grew inside the state, the Indian Army entered in September 1948 in an operation lasting a few days, and Hyderabad joined India. In Jammu and Kashmir, Maharaja Hari Singh joined India in October 1947 after an invasion by tribal fighters from Pakistan. The region's status has been disputed between India and Pakistan ever since.

The end of the princes

Over the next few years, the old states were merged into new Indian states, such as Rajasthan, which joined together more than twenty princely states. Many rulers became governors, ministers or members of Parliament. Their old forts, like the great hill forts of Rajasthan, passed into the care of the new government or stayed with royal families.

In 1971, a constitutional amendment ended the privy purses and royal titles. Many palaces became hotels, museums or family homes. In Jaipur, Udaipur and Mysuru, visitors can now walk through rooms where kings once held court.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The British ruled every part of India directly.

Truth: About two-fifths of the land was ruled by Indian princes under British protection. The British controlled their defence and foreign affairs, but the princes ran most local matters.
