Can numbers lie?

During the Second World War, planes came back full of bullet holes. A mathematician said to protect the parts that had no holes at all.

The short answer

Numbers themselves can't lie. A count is a count. But people choose which numbers to show, how to show them and what to leave out. That can make true numbers tell a false story.

Some tricks are on purpose, used by advertisers or people trying to win an argument. Many more are honest mistakes. The good news is that most of them fall into a few patterns, and once you know them, you start spotting them everywhere.

In 1954, an American writer named Darrell Huff wrote a short, funny book called How to Lie with Statistics. It was meant as a warning, not a guide, and it became one of the best-selling statistics books ever written.

Surprising but true

The saying "lies, damned lies and statistics" was made famous by Mark Twain, who said a British prime minister invented it. Nobody has ever found the prime minister saying it.

The planes with holes

During the Second World War, American military planners studied bombers coming back from missions. The planes were peppered with bullet holes on the wings and body, but had far fewer near the engines. The obvious plan was to add armour where the holes were.

A mathematician named Abraham Wald, working with a team of statisticians in New York, saw the problem. The planners were only looking at planes that had come back. Planes hit in the engines mostly never returned. The places with no holes were the most dangerous places to be hit.

This mistake is called survivorship bias. It happens whenever you only look at the winners. If you only study famous people who dropped out of college, you might think dropping out leads to success, because you never hear about the many who struggled.

Together is not the same as because

Ice cream sales and sunburn both go up in summer. That doesn't mean ice cream burns your skin. Hot, sunny weather drives both.

When two things change together, statisticians say they are correlated. That is different from one causing the other. A famous joke chart shows that, in one country, the number of storks and the number of babies born rose together for years. Storks don't bring babies. Both were linked to other changes in the country.

How does news reach us?In 1850, Paul Julius Reuter used carrier pigeons to fly stock prices between two cities. His news agency, Reuters, still runs today.

Big words for small changes

Suppose a headline says eating a certain snack "doubles your risk" of an illness. That sounds scary. But if the risk goes from 1 in 10,000 people to 2 in 10,000, the change is tiny for any one person.

Percentages can do this in both directions. A rise "from 1 to 2" is a 100% rise. A rise from 50 to 60 is only 20%. Always ask: per cent of what?

Small groups cause trouble too. If an advert says "4 out of 5 people preferred our toothpaste", you'd want to know whether that meant 5 people or 5,000.

Squashed and stretched charts

The quickest way to change a story is to change the chart. If a bar chart's axis starts at 95 instead of 0, a score of 96 can look twice as tall as 95. A line graph that is stretched tall makes a gentle rise look like a cliff.

Averages can mislead as well. A company might say its "average" salary is high, using the mean, when a few big bosses earn most of the money. The median tells a truer story.

Lies, damned lies

The American writer Mark Twain popularised the saying "There are three kinds of lies: lies, damned lies and statistics." He credited it to Benjamin Disraeli, a British prime minister. Researchers have searched Disraeli's writings and speeches and never found it there, so its true origin is unknown.

Statistics are still one of the best tools we have for understanding the world, from vaccines to news reports. The trick is to ask good questions before believing the answer.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If two things happen together, one must be causing the other.

Truth: Many things rise and fall together by coincidence, or because a hidden third thing affects both. To show that one thing really causes another, scientists usually need careful experiments.

Try this

Look through a newspaper, magazine or advert for a chart or a number like "9 out of 10". Ask three questions about it. How many people were asked? Does the chart's axis start at zero? Who made it, and do they want you to buy something?
