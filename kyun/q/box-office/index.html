How does a film make money at the box office?

In November 2026, one Indian film sold more than 1,200 crore rupees of tickets around the world in just ten days, trade trackers estimate. Yet only a part of that money ever reaches the people who made it.

The short answer

The box office is all the money people pay for cinema tickets. When news says a film "made 1,000 crore", it usually means tickets worth that much were sold.

But that money is shared. The government takes tax. The cinema keeps a big slice. What is left goes to the distributor and the producer, who must first earn back what the film cost before they make any profit.

Surprising but true

A cinema can earn about as much from popcorn, samosas and cold drinks as it pays the film's makers. At PVR INOX, India's biggest cinema chain, food and drinks bring in about a third of all its money.

How it works

Think of a mango seller at a village mela. The seller sells a mango for 100 rupees. Part goes to the panchayat as a fee. Part goes to the owner of the ground who rents out the stall. Only the rest stays with the seller and the farmer who grew the mango. A film ticket works in the same way.

Here is what happens to a 300 rupee ticket:

  • 1. Tax comes out first. Cinema tickets carry GST. Tickets above 100 rupees are taxed at 18%, a percentage set by the government, so about 46 rupees of the 300 goes to the government.
  • 2. Gross and net. The full 300 rupees is called the gross. The 254 rupees left after tax is called the net. News reports use both, so always check which one you are reading.
  • 3. The cinema takes its share. The cinema, called the exhibitor, keeps a share of the net, often around half. The film's share is usually biggest in its first week and smaller in later weeks.
  • 4. The film's side. The rest, about 125 rupees, goes to the distributor, the company that delivers the film to cinemas, and then to the producer, who paid for the film.

Box office is only one way films earn. Makers also sell the rights to show the film on streaming apps and TV channels, sell the songs, and sell dubbed versions. Cinemas earn extra from popcorn and drinks, which they keep for themselves.

Where it came from

The phrase "box office" comes from theatres. A small booth, or office, sold tickets for the boxes, the private seats, and later for every seat. English speakers were using the phrase by the late 1700s.

Films came later. In 1895, the Lumière brothers showed the first paid film show in Paris. In 1913, Dadasaheb Phalke released Raja Harishchandra, India's first feature film.

For much of the 20th century, success was counted in weeks. A film that ran 25 weeks in a cinema celebrated a silver jubilee, and 50 weeks a golden jubilee. Sholay, released in 1975, ran for five years at Mumbai's Minerva cinema, one of the classic films everyone still quotes.

In 1997, India's first multiplex, PVR Anupam, opened in Saket, Delhi, with several screens under one roof. Big films could now open on thousands of screens at once. That changed everything: a film's money now arrives in a rush in its first few days, so opening weekend numbers became the new way to keep score.

India's story

India makes more than 1,000 films a year in many languages, more than almost any country. Bollywood in Mumbai is the best known, but Telugu, Tamil, Kannada and Malayalam films now earn huge sums too. That is why South Indian films have grown so big. Many films are now dubbed into other languages so one film can sell tickets across India and abroad. Tickets sold abroad are paid in other currencies, such as dollars, and counted in rupees for the news.

India's biggest hits now earn more than 1,000 crore rupees around the world. Dangal, released in 2016, is often listed as the biggest of all, at about 2,000 crore rupees, much of it from China. Baahubali 2 (2017), Pushpa 2 (2024) and Dhurandhar 2 (2026) each earned more than 1,600 crore, by trade estimates.

Big films cost big money. Making one means paying actors, building sets, adding special effects and advertising across the country. Its makers have said Ramayana's two parts together cost about 4,000 crore rupees, including advertising and release. If so, it is the costliest Indian film project yet. A film retelling the Ramayana, India's much-loved epic, also had a ready audience across languages and countries.

The numbers that matter

  • - More than 1,200 crore rupees: what Ramayana: Part 1 made worldwide in its first ten days, as estimated by trade trackers such as Sacnilk. In India, its gross was about 820 crore and its net about 686 crore. The gap of about 134 crore is mostly GST.
  • - About 13,395 crore rupees: everything Indians spent on cinema tickets in 2025, a record, according to Ormax Media. That is about 90 rupees for every person in the country, a small but busy corner of India's economy.
  • - About 83 crore tickets: sold in India in 2025, down 6% from 2024. Fewer people went, but each ticket cost more, about 161 rupees on average against 134 rupees a year earlier.
  • - About 10,000 screens: in all of India in 2025. That is only about 7 screens for every 10 lakh people, far fewer than in countries such as the USA, so many towns have no cinema at all.

What people argue about

Are the numbers real? India has no official body that counts every ticket. Figures come from trade trackers and from the films' own makers, and they often differ. Some people say makers boost their numbers to look successful. Others say the trackers are careful and that the big totals are broadly right.

Are tickets too expensive? Many families say a cinema trip, with snacks, costs too much. Cinema owners reply that rent, electricity and staff are costly, and that cheap seats are still sold in single-screen halls. In 2025, the government cut GST on tickets up to 100 rupees from 12% to 5%, hoping to help smaller cinemas.

Cinema or phone? Films now reach streaming apps a few weeks after release. Some say this keeps people at home. Others say a big film on a big screen is still an event no phone can replace.

Myths and mix-ups

"Gross and net are the same thing." No. Gross includes tax. Net is after GST. A headline can make a film look bigger just by quoting the gross. Learn to spot the difference using percentages.

"Box office is the film's profit." It is not even the film's income. After tax and the cinema's share, the makers get well under half, and then they must pay back the cost.

"A hit in India is a hit everywhere." Each place counts separately. Some Indian films earn more abroad than at home, and some flop overseas while doing well in India.

What it means for you

Every time your family buys a cinema ticket, your money is split many ways: the government, the cinema, the distributor and the people who made the film. The popcorn you buy helps keep the cinema running.

Whenever you see a giant number in a headline, ask three questions. Is it gross or net? Is it India only, or worldwide? Who counted it? Big numbers in lakh and crore are easy to quote and hard to check. Box office also follows supply and demand: when everyone wants to see the same film on the same weekend, cinemas add shows and prices rise. And like a company's market value, a film's box office tells you what people were willing to pay, not how much money anyone actually kept. Myth or fact?

Myth: A film that earns 1,000 crore rupees at the box office makes 1,000 crore rupees of profit for its makers.

Truth: Box office is all the ticket money, before tax and before the cinema takes its share. The makers get well under half of it, and they must first pay back what the film cost to make and advertise.

Try this

Ask everyone in your family how many films they saw in a cinema this year, and roughly what each ticket cost. Add it all up. Now take out about 15% for GST (the 18% tax is already inside the price) and split what is left in half. How much did your family give to film makers, and how much to cinemas?
