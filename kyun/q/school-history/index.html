When did children start going to school?

About 4,000 years ago, schoolboys in Mesopotamia copied lessons onto clay tablets. Some of their homework, mistakes included, survives today.

The short answer

Children have learned from adults for as long as there have been people, by watching, listening and helping. But schools, special places where a teacher instructs a group of students, began about 4,000 to 5,000 years ago, soon after writing was invented.

For thousands of years, only a small number of children went to school, mostly boys from rich or priestly families. The idea that every child should go to school, paid for by the government, is surprisingly recent. It spread in the 1700s and 1800s, and in many countries, including India, it became law only in the last hundred years.

Surprising but true

In 1848, Savitribai Phule and Jyotirao Phule opened one of the first schools for girls in India, in Pune. Savitribai faced people throwing mud at her on the way to teach.

Clay tablets and scribes

After writing was invented in Mesopotamia, scribes were needed to keep records. In schools that the Sumerians called edubba, or "tablet houses", boys learned to press wedge-shaped signs into wet clay. They copied lists of words, solved maths problems and practised writing contracts. Archaeologists have found their practice tablets, complete with mistakes and corrections.

Ancient Egypt, China and Greece had schools too, mostly for training scribes, officials and priests.

Gurukuls and great universities

In ancient India, many students lived with a teacher, or guru, at a gurukul, learning texts by heart, along with grammar, astronomy and other subjects. Later came large centres of learning. Nalanda, in present-day Bihar, flourished from about the 5th century CE and drew students from China, Korea and Central Asia. It had libraries, lecture halls and living quarters for thousands of monks and students.

What was Nalanda University?Students walked for months across mountains to reach a monastery in Bihar, and some failed the entrance test at the gate.

Elsewhere, monasteries in Europe and madrasas in the Islamic world taught reading, religion and science. The University of al-Qarawiyyin in Fez, Morocco, founded in 859 by a woman named Fatima al-Fihri, is often called the oldest university still operating.

School for everyone

In 1763, the king of Prussia, in what is now Germany, ordered that children should attend school. In the United States, Massachusetts passed the first state law making school attendance compulsory in 1852. By 1918, every US state had such a law.

In India, schools for girls and for children of oppressed castes were rare until reformers pushed for them. In 1848, Savitribai and Jyotirao Phule opened a girls' school in Pune. Savitribai became one of India's first women teachers, despite fierce opposition.

India catches up

When India became independent in 1947, most Indians could not read. At the 1951 census, only about 18% were literate. Governments built schools across the country, and the mid-day meal scheme encouraged poor families to send children to class. In 2009, the Right to Education Act made free schooling a right for every child aged 6 to 14, as part of the wider rights of children. By 2011, literacy had reached about 74%.

Still unfinished

Classrooms changed too. For centuries, students wrote on slates with chalk, because paper was expensive. Indian children practised letters on wooden boards called takhtis, or traced them in sand. Ballpoint pens became common only after the Second World War, and computers reached most classrooms only in recent decades.

Around the world, about 27 crore children and young people were out of school in 2025, according to UNESCO. War, poverty, distance and the need to work keep many at home, and girls are often the first to be kept back. Ideas about what school should look like keep changing too, as you can see in schools around the world.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The long summer holiday exists because children used to be needed on farms.

Truth: Farm work was busiest at planting and harvest time, in spring and autumn. Historians trace the American summer break mainly to city school reformers in the 1800s, who standardised calendars and avoided teaching in the summer heat.
