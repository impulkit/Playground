Why is Diwali celebrated in so many countries?

On Diwali night, diyas glow not just in Ayodhya and Amritsar but in Port Louis, Suva, Leicester and New York.

The short answer

Diwali travelled with people. Over the last 200 years, Indians have moved to almost every corner of the world, as workers, traders, students and families. Wherever they settled, they kept lighting diyas.

Today Diwali is a public holiday in more than ten countries, and big cities from London to Sydney hold Diwali fairs. Many neighbours who are not Indian join in too.

Surprising but true

In Trinidad and Tobago, Guyana, Suriname, Mauritius and Fiji, Diwali is a public holiday, even though these countries are thousands of kilometres from India.

Ships, sugar and diyas

In 1834, Britain ended slavery in its colonies. Plantation owners needed new workers, so from 1838 to 1917 more than a million Indians were taken across the oceans as indentured labourers. They signed contracts to work for years on sugar and rubber farms.

They went to Mauritius, Fiji, Trinidad and Tobago, Guyana, Suriname, South Africa and more. They took very little with them, but they carried their stories, songs and festivals. Their descendants still celebrate Diwali, which is why it is a public holiday in many of those countries.

Where in the world do Indians live?About 3.5 crore people of Indian origin live outside India, from Dubai and New Jersey to Fiji and the Caribbean.

A festival for new neighbours

In the 20th century, more Indians moved to Britain, the USA, Canada, the Gulf, Singapore and Australia. Leicester, in England, now holds one of the largest Diwali celebrations outside India, with thousands of lights along one road. In 2023, New York City decided to make Diwali a school holiday.

In Nepal, the festival is called Tihar, and it even honours crows, dogs and cows on different days. Singapore's Little India and Kuala Lumpur's streets glow with lights. The diaspora has turned a home festival into a world festival.

Same night, many stories

Diwali falls on amavasya, the new moon night, the darkest night of the month of Kartik. That is why its date on the ordinary calendar moves each year, guided by Indian calendars that follow the Moon.

For many Hindus, Diwali remembers Rama returning home to Ayodhya in the Ramayana, when people lit lamps to welcome him. Sikhs remember Guru Hargobind's release from prison, and Jains remember Lord Mahavira. Different stories, one festival of lights, shared by families across the world with sweets, fireworks and new clothes.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Diwali is only a Hindu festival.

Truth: Hindus celebrate Diwali, but so do Sikhs, who mark Bandi Chhor Divas on the same day, and Jains, who remember Lord Mahavira. Many Buddhists in Nepal and people of other faiths join in too.
