Which giant animals lived in the Ice Age?

Woolly mammoths were still alive on a remote Arctic island when the Great Pyramid of Egypt was already hundreds of years old.

The short answer

During the last ice age, which peaked about 20,000 years ago, the world was home to giant mammals. Woolly mammoths and woolly rhinos roamed the cold grasslands of Europe, Asia and North America. Sabre-toothed cats hunted in the Americas. Ground sloths as big as elephants browsed on trees, and armadillo relatives the size of small cars trundled across South America.

Scientists call these animals megafauna, meaning "big animals". Most had died out by about 10,000 years ago. Early humans lived alongside them, hunted some of them and painted them on cave walls, which is one reason we know so much about how they looked.

Surprising but true

In the Narmada valley of central India lived a straight-tusked elephant, Palaeoloxodon namadicus, that some scientists think may have been one of the heaviest land mammals that ever lived.

A colder, drier world

The ice ages were not one long freeze. Earth swung between cold spells and warmer gaps many times. At the last cold peak, ice sheets several kilometres thick covered Canada, much of the northern USA and northern Europe. So much water was locked up as ice that sea levels fell about 120 m. Britain was joined to Europe, and Sri Lanka to India.

South of the ice lay a huge, dry grassland that stretched from Spain across Siberia to Alaska. Scientists call it the mammoth steppe. Grazing animals thrived on its grasses and herbs.

A gallery of giants

Woolly mammoths stood about 3 m tall at the shoulder, about the height of a single-decker bus. They had small ears and short tails to save heat, and a coat of long hair over a layer of fat.

The giant deer, sometimes called the Irish elk, carried antlers up to about 3.6 m across, wider than a car. Cave bears were bigger than most grizzlies. In South America, Megatherium, a ground sloth, weighed as much as an elephant and could rear up on its back legs to pull down high branches.

In Los Angeles, tar seeping from the ground trapped animals for tens of thousands of years. The La Brea Tar Pits have produced bones of mammoths, giant wolves and more than 2,000 sabre-toothed cats. The cat Smilodon, with its long upper fangs, is now California's state fossil.

Frozen in time

In the frozen ground of Siberia, some Ice Age animals survive with skin and hair. In 2007, a reindeer herder in Russia's Yamal Peninsula found a baby mammoth about one month old. Scientists named her Lyuba. She had died around 42,000 years ago, and her body was so well preserved that milk from her mother could still be detected in her stomach.

Who made the very first paintings?Long before paper, pencils or paintbrushes, people were painting on rock. Some of their pictures are more than 50,000 years old.

Giants of India

India never had great ice sheets except in the high Himalaya, but its climate swung between wetter and drier times. Riverbanks along the Narmada have given up bones of an enormous straight-tusked elephant, Palaeoloxodon namadicus. Some estimates suggest it may have outweighed any elephant alive today by a wide margin, though the evidence comes from a few bones and is still debated.

Hippos, wild cattle and an elephant relative called Stegodon also lived in India's river valleys. Their fossils lie in the same layers as the stone tools of early people.

Where did they go?

By about 10,000 years ago, most megafauna had vanished from the Americas, Europe, northern Asia and Australia. Two causes get most of the blame. One is climate. As the ice melted, the mammoth steppe turned into forest and wetland, shrinking their food supply. The other is people. In many places, the giants disappeared not long after skilled human hunters arrived.

Most scientists now think both played a part, in different amounts in different places. A few big animals, like elephants in Africa and India, survived. The last mammoths survived on Wrangel Island until about 4,000 years ago, when the Egyptian pyramids were already standing.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Cave people lived at the same time as dinosaurs.

Truth: The big dinosaurs died out about 66 million years ago, long before any humans existed. The giant animals early people actually met were Ice Age mammals such as mammoths, giant deer and cave bears.

Try this

Take two ice cubes from the freezer and wrap one in a woolly sock or scarf. Leave the other bare. Check every 15 minutes to see which melts first. Thick hair and fat kept mammoths and woolly rhinos warm the same way, by trapping a layer of still air.
