Can ice be hot?

In 2019, scientists blasted a thin layer of water with a giant laser and made ice that stayed solid at thousands of degrees.

The short answer

Yes, ice can be hot, though not anywhere on Earth's surface. The ice in your freezer is just one kind. Scientists know of about 20 different kinds of solid water ice. Each one has its molecules packed in a different pattern.

Most of them need huge pressure. If you squeeze water hard enough, its molecules are pushed into tight crystals that stay solid even when they are very hot. One type, ice VII, can be solid at hundreds of degrees. Another, superionic ice, was made in a lab in 2019 at thousands of degrees, hotter than the surface of a lava flow. It may fill the insides of giant planets.

Surprising but true

Tiny bits of a hot, high-pressure ice called ice VII have been found trapped inside diamonds that came up from deep in the Earth.

Why ordinary ice is the odd one

The ice we know, called ice Ih by scientists, is strange. Its molecules hold hands in open six-sided rings with lots of empty space. That makes it lighter than liquid water, which is why ice floats. Most solids sink in their own liquid.

That open pattern is easy to crush. Press harder and the rings collapse into denser shapes. Scientists have named the different ices with Roman numerals, from ice I up to ice XIX and beyond, in the order they were found. Some of these ices exist only in labs. Others probably form naturally deep inside big icy moons such as Jupiter's Ganymede, where the weight of ice and water above squeezes the lower layers into denser kinds.

Pressure changes everything

The temperature at which water boils or freezes depends on pressure. A pressure cooker traps steam, raising the pressure so water stays liquid above 100 °C. That's why dal cooks faster in it.

Freezing works the same way in reverse. At pressures thousands of times greater than our air, the freezing point climbs above room temperature. At even higher pressures, ice VII can stay solid at several hundred degrees, hotter than a pizza oven.

How does a pressure cooker cook faster?A pressure cooker's whistle is a tiny safety valve, letting out just enough steam to keep water boiling at about 120°C instead of 100°C.

Hot ice inside diamonds

Ice VII is not just a lab trick. In 2018, scientists studying diamonds from deep in the Earth found tiny pockets of ice VII trapped inside them. The diamonds had formed hundreds of kilometres down, where water was squeezed into this dense ice.

The diamond acted like a pressure-proof safe. It held the ice tight even after the stone reached the surface. This was the first time ice VII had been found in nature, and it showed that there is water deep in Earth's mantle.

Ice that conducts electricity

Superionic ice is stranger still. In 2019, scientists from the Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory in California fired a powerful laser in New York at a thin layer of water. For a few billionths of a second, it reached pressures millions of times higher than our air and temperatures of thousands of degrees.

In that state, the oxygen atoms lock into a solid frame, but the hydrogen bits flow through it like a liquid. It is part solid, part flowing, and black rather than clear. Because charged hydrogen moves through it, it carries electricity.

Planets made of hot ice?

Uranus and Neptune are sometimes called "ice giants". Deep inside, scientists think, water may be squeezed into superionic ice. That could explain their wonky magnetic fields, which tilt away from their spin in odd directions.

Some planets around other stars may be mostly water too. Gliese 436 b, a planet about 30 light years away, is hot enough to boil water at our pressures, yet some scientists suggested it might hold hot, squeezed ice deep inside. And the strangest part? The closest planet to the Sun, Mercury, has ordinary cold ice hidden in the shadows of its craters.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ice is always cold, because water freezes at 0 °C.

Truth: Water freezes at 0 °C only at the air pressure we live in. Change the pressure and the freezing point changes too. Under extreme pressure, water can be a solid at hundreds or even thousands of degrees.

Try this

Put an ice cube on a plate and press a coin on it firmly for a minute while a friend holds a second cube without pressing. Look at where the coin was. Pressure changes how ice behaves, which is the start of how hot ice is possible.
