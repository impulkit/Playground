How does an airbag inflate so fast?

In a crash, a car's airbag fills with gas and catches you in less time than it takes to blink.

The short answer

An airbag is a folded nylon bag hidden in the steering wheel, the dashboard, the seats or the side of the roof. It inflates only in a crash.

Sensors in the car feel for a very sudden slowdown, much sharper than any normal braking. When that happens, a small computer sends an electric signal to a device called an inflator. The signal sets off a quick chemical reaction that produces a large puff of nitrogen gas. The gas rushes into the bag and fills it in about 30 thousandths of a second, faster than a blink.

The bag then starts to empty at once through small holes, so it cushions you instead of bouncing you back.

Surprising but true

An airbag isn't blown up with air. It is filled by a tiny, fast chemical reaction that makes a burst of harmless nitrogen gas.

Sensing a crash

In a crash, the car stops almost instantly, but the people inside keep moving forward. The airbag has to be in place before they reach the steering wheel or dashboard. Human reflexes are far too slow to help: by the time your brain notices a crash, it is already over. The car has to think for you.

Modern cars use electronic sensors called accelerometers, small chips that measure how fast speed is changing. Many smartphones contain the same kind of chip to know which way up they are. The car's computer compares the readings with patterns of real crashes. A bump in the road or a hard stop shouldn't trigger the bag. A crash into a wall at speed should.

Gas from a pellet

Blowing up a big bag with a pump would take far too long. Instead, the inflator contains a small amount of solid chemical. When the electric signal reaches it, the chemical reacts in a flash, producing a lot of hot gas, mostly nitrogen, the same gas that makes up most of the air you breathe.

That gas pours through a filter into the bag. Within a fraction of a second, a bag the size of a beach ball is ready. You might see a puff of powder afterwards. That's usually just cornflour or talc used to stop the folded bag sticking to itself.

Catching you gently

Stopping suddenly is what hurts in a crash. An airbag spreads that stop over a slightly longer time and a larger area of your body. Instead of your head hitting a hard wheel, it sinks into a soft, emptying cushion.

But an airbag opens with great force. It is designed for an adult wearing a seatbelt, which holds the body in the right place. That's why safety agencies in the USA and elsewhere say children under 13 should ride in the back seat. A baby's rear-facing car seat must never go in front of an active airbag.

How airbags spread

Inventors in the USA and Germany patented early airbag ideas in the early 1950s, but the technology for fast sensors and safe inflators took decades. Some luxury cars offered airbags in the 1970s and 1980s.

In the USA, front airbags became compulsory for both front seats in all new cars from 1998. In India, the government made front airbags compulsory in new cars in the early 2020s. Many cars now have six or more airbags, including curtains that drop down beside the windows. Safety rules took years of argument, testing and crash data, and road safety experts still say the cheapest protection of all is the belt.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If a car has airbags, you don't need to wear a seatbelt.

Truth: Airbags are designed to work together with seatbelts. Without a belt, a person can slide forward and hit the bag too early or in the wrong place, which can cause serious injuries. Belt first, airbag second.

Try this

Feel how a soft landing helps. Drop a raw potato or a ball onto a hard floor, then onto a thick cushion. Notice the cushion lets it stop more slowly, over a longer distance. An airbag does the same for your head, turning a sudden hard stop into a slower, gentler one.
