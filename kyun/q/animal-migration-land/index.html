Which land animals migrate?

Every year, more than a million wildebeest walk in a giant loop across Tanzania and Kenya, chasing the rain.

The short answer

Lots of land animals migrate, which means they travel between different places at different times of year. Wildebeest, zebras and gazelles in East Africa do it. So do caribou in the Arctic, elephants in India, bison in North America and red crabs on a small island in the Indian Ocean.

Animals usually migrate for one of three reasons: to find food, to find water, or to reach a safe place to have their young. Unlike birds, land animals can't fly over mountains, rivers and roads. Their journeys are slow, hard and sometimes dangerous, and they depend on open land to move through.

Surprising but true

Some caribou in Alaska and Canada travel more than 1,000 km a year, among the longest known walks of any land animal.

The great loop of the Serengeti

The most famous land migration on Earth happens in the Serengeti in Tanzania and the Masai Mara in Kenya. An aerial survey in 2024 counted about 6 million antelope moving across South Sudan, which may be even bigger. More than a million wildebeest, along with hundreds of thousands of zebras and gazelles, travel in a vast loop that follows the rains and the fresh grass.

In the early months of the year, the wildebeest gather on the southern plains, where hundreds of thousands of calves are born within a few weeks. As the grass dries, the herds head north and west. Crossing the Mara River, they face strong currents and waiting crocodiles. Then, as rain returns to the south, they turn back again.

Walking the Arctic

In Alaska and northern Canada, caribou make some of the longest land journeys known. The Porcupine caribou herd, about 200,000 strong in recent counts, travels each spring to the coastal plain near the Arctic Ocean. There, the females give birth among fresh spring plants, with fewer wolves and bears about than in the forests.

Satellite collars show some caribou cover more than 1,000 km in a year. They swim rivers, cross mountains and dig through snow for lichen to eat. In winter, they move back south into forests where snow is softer.

Some migrations were hiding in plain sight. In Wyoming in the United States, researchers who put GPS collars on mule deer reported in 2014 that some walked about 240 km each way between desert and mountains. Local people had seen the deer for generations, but nobody had known how far they went.

Moving with the seasons in India

India's Asian elephants are wanderers. A herd may walk long distances between forests as the seasons change, searching for water and food. Many routes now pass through farms, villages and railway lines. In 2023, the Government of India listed 150 elephant corridors, narrow strips of land that link forests and that conservationists are working to keep open.

Some people migrate with their animals too. Gaddi shepherds in Himachal Pradesh take flocks of sheep and goats up to high mountain meadows in summer and down to the valleys in winter. This kind of seasonal journey, called transhumance, has gone on for centuries. The arrival of the monsoon shapes the timing of many such movements.

A march to the sea

On Christmas Island, an Australian territory in the Indian Ocean, red crabs live in the forest for most of the year. When the wet season begins, tens of millions of them march to the coast to breed, timed with the phase of the Moon.

Roads on the island close, and people build little tunnels and bridges so the crabs can cross safely. Migrations everywhere face the same problem: fences, roads, towns and farms can block old routes. Animals that have walked the same paths for thousands of years need open land to keep moving.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Animals migrate only to escape the cold.

Truth: Many migrate to follow rain and fresh food, to find water in the dry season, or to reach safe places to give birth. Wildebeest live in warm East Africa, yet they make one of the biggest journeys on Earth.
