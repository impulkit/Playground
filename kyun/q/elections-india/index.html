How does India hold the world's biggest election?

In 2024, nearly 98 crore Indians could vote, and officials set up a polling booth deep in Gujarat's Gir forest for just one voter.

The short answer

Every five years, India elects the 543 members of the Lok Sabha, the lower house of Parliament. The party or group that wins most seats forms the government. In 2024, about 98 crore people were registered to vote, more than the entire populations of the USA and the European Union put together.

The independent Election Commission runs the whole thing. In 2024 it set up about 10.5 lakh polling stations. Voting took place in seven phases spread across six weeks, from 19 April to 1 June. All the votes were counted on a single day, 4 June. About 64.6 crore people voted.

Surprising but true

The rule in India is that no voter should have to travel more than about 2 km to reach a polling booth, even in forests, deserts and mountains.

Why it takes six weeks

Holding everything on one day would need too many police and officials at once. So the country votes in phases, region by region, and security forces move from place to place between them.

The summer timing is a problem. Temperatures in parts of north India reached well over 45 °C during the 2024 polls. Booths were told to provide shade, water and first aid. Voters over 85 and people with disabilities could, for the first time in a general election, choose to vote from home.

A booth for every village

The Commission tries to keep every voter within about 2 km of a booth. That leads to some extraordinary journeys. Officials have walked for days into the hills of Arunachal Pradesh, crossed rivers by boat in Assam and ridden in helicopters to islands. In Gir forest in Gujarat, a booth is set up for a single voter, a priest who lives near a temple among the lions.

At Tashigang in Himachal Pradesh, about 4,650 m above sea level, sits one of the highest polling stations in the world. The air there is so thin that the polling team has to watch for altitude sickness.

Machines, paper and ink

India first tried electronic voting machines in 1982 and has used them in every booth across the country since 2004. A machine is a small box with a button beside each candidate's name and symbol. Symbols matter because millions of voters cannot read well. A lotus, a hand, a bicycle or an elephant can stand for a whole party.

Since 2019, every machine has a printer attached. When you press a button, a slip with your candidate's name and symbol appears behind glass for seven seconds, then drops into a sealed box. Some critics have argued for a full return to paper ballots. In April 2024, India's Supreme Court rejected that demand.

Before leaving, each voter gets a line of purple ink painted on the left forefinger. It cannot be washed off for days, so nobody can vote twice. The ink has come from the same company in Mysuru since 1962.

Counting day

On 4 June 2024, counting began at 8 in the morning in hundreds of halls across the country. Each machine was opened, read and checked in front of agents from the parties. By evening, television screens were full of maps changing colour seat by seat, and most results were clear by night.

India's first general election, in 1951–52, took about four months and used a separate ballot box for every candidate. Many people then were voting for the first time in their lives. The scale today is far bigger, but the idea is the one written into the Constitution: every adult citizen, rich or poor, gets one vote of equal weight.

In the USA, elections work differently. Each state sets many of its own rules, and no single national body runs the voting the way India's Election Commission does.

Myth or fact?

Myth: India's voting machines are connected to the internet, so results arrive the moment people vote.

Truth: Each machine runs on its own battery and has no internet or wireless link. Votes stay stored inside until counting day, when the machines are opened in front of candidates' agents and the totals are read out one by one.

Try this

Hold a class vote with paper slips on a simple question, like the best school lunch. Have two people count the slips separately and see if they get the same total. Then work out the turnout, the share of the class who actually voted.
