What is the Kuiper Belt?

Beyond Neptune lies a vast ring of frozen leftovers from the birth of the solar system, and a spacecraft has visited one shaped like a squashed snowman.

The short answer

The Kuiper Belt (say "KY-per") is a huge, doughnut-shaped region of icy objects beyond the orbit of Neptune. It stretches from about 30 to 50 times Earth's distance from the Sun.

It is filled with frozen leftovers from when the planets formed, about 4.6 billion years ago. Most are small chunks of ice and rock. A few are big enough to be dwarf planets, including Pluto, the largest known.

It is like the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter, but much wider and much more massive, and made mostly of ice instead of rock. Many comets that swing past the Sun every few decades or centuries probably started out here.

Surprising but true

Arrokoth, visited by New Horizons on New Year's Day 2019, is the most distant object any spacecraft has ever flown past.

Guessing it was there

After Pluto was discovered in 1930, some astronomers wondered whether it had company. In the 1940s and 1950s, Kenneth Edgeworth in Ireland and Gerard Kuiper in the USA both wrote about small bodies beyond Neptune. Kuiper actually thought any such belt would have been cleared away long ago. The name stuck to him anyway, and some astronomers say "Edgeworth–Kuiper Belt" to share the credit.

For decades, nobody could see anything there. The objects are small, dark and very far away, so they reflect only a sliver of sunlight.

The search that paid off

In 1987, two astronomers, David Jewitt and his student Jane Luu, began searching using telescopes on Mauna Kea, a tall volcano in Hawaii. They took pictures of the same patch of sky over time and looked for faint dots that moved.

After five years, in 1992, they found one. It was the first Kuiper Belt object known after Pluto and its moon Charon. Since then, astronomers have found thousands more. NASA estimates there may be hundreds of thousands of icy bodies larger than 100 km across, and perhaps a trillion or more comets. A trillion is a million million, more than a hundred comets for every person on Earth.

A snowman at the edge

NASA's New Horizons spacecraft flew past Pluto in 2015. Then engineers pointed it at a much smaller target, found by the Hubble Space Telescope only in 2014.

On 1 January 2019, New Horizons flew past it, about 6.6 billion km from Earth. It is the most distant object any spacecraft has visited. The pictures showed two flattened, reddish lumps gently stuck together, about 36 km long in total. It looked a bit like a squashed snowman.

The object was named Arrokoth, meaning "sky" in the Powhatan language of the Native people of the Chesapeake Bay region. Its two lobes probably drifted together very slowly, early in the solar system's history, and have barely changed since.

A frozen time capsule

Out there, the Sun looks like a very bright star, and temperatures are around −230 °C. Nothing melts, and few things crash. So Kuiper Belt objects may be the best-preserved leftovers of the solar system's birth.

Some may have been thrown around by the giant planets long ago. Neptune's largest moon, Triton, orbits backwards, which suggests it was once a Kuiper Belt object captured by Neptune's gravity. Sunlight, the fastest thing there is, takes four to seven hours to reach the Kuiper Belt. A car driving nonstop at 100 km/h would need more than 5,000 years just to reach its inner edge.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Kuiper Belt is a crowded field of rocks, like in science-fiction films.

Truth: It holds a huge number of objects, but they are spread across an enormous region. A spacecraft can cross it without coming anywhere near anything, unless it aims for a target on purpose.

Make a scale model of the solar system on a long corridor or playground with a teacher. If Earth is 1 step from the Sun, Neptune is 30 steps and the Kuiper Belt runs from about 30 to 50 steps. Place a pebble for each planet and scatter grains of rice for the Kuiper Belt.
