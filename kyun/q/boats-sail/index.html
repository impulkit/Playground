How can a sailboat sail into the wind?

A sailboat can't sail straight into the wind, but it can zigzag towards it, because its sail works like an aeroplane wing turned on its side.

The short answer

When the wind blows from behind, a sail simply catches it like a parachute and the boat is pushed along. That part is easy. The clever part is going against the wind.

When a sail is set at an angle to the wind, it curves like an aeroplane wing standing on its edge. Air flowing around the curved sail is bent and speeds up on one side, and the sail is pushed sideways and slightly forwards. This push is called lift. Underneath the boat, a fin called a keel grips the water and stops the boat sliding sideways. With sideways movement blocked, the push turns into forward motion.

No boat can sail straight into the wind. But most can sail at about 45 degrees to it, so they zigzag their way upwind.

Surprising but true

The fastest racing sailboats can travel at two or three times the speed of the wind that pushes them.

A wing on its side

An aeroplane wing is shaped and tilted so that air moving past it is bent downwards. Pushing the air down pushes the wing up. A sail does the same thing sideways. It bends the wind, and the wind pushes back on the sail.

On its own, that sideways push would just shove the boat across the water like a leaf. The keel stops it. Think of squeezing a slippery orange seed between your fingers: you press from the sides, and the seed shoots forward. The sail presses from one side, the keel resists from the other, and the boat squirts forward between them.

Tacking

If a sailor points the boat too close to the wind, the sail flaps uselessly. Sailors call this area the no-go zone. To reach a place directly upwind, they sail at an angle, first to the left and then to the right, turning the front of the boat through the wind each time.

Each turn is called a tack. A boat beating upwind traces a zigzag across the water, like someone climbing a steep hill by a winding path. It travels further, but it gets there.

Triangles on the Indian Ocean

For centuries, sailors in the Arabian Sea and the Indian Ocean used a triangular sail called a lateen sail on wooden boats known as dhows. A lateen sail can be set closer to the wind than a square sail, so dhows could sail across and partly against the wind.

Traders also learned to use the monsoon. Winds blowing from the south-west in summer carried ships from Arabia and East Africa towards India, and north-east winds in winter took them back. These seasonal trade routes carried spices, cotton, horses and ideas for well over a thousand years. Wooden dhows are still built by hand in places like Mandvi in Gujarat.

Faster than the wind

Modern racing boats push the idea to extremes. The America's Cup, a sailing race first held off England in 1851, now uses boats that rise out of the water on thin underwater wings called foils. With less water dragging on the hull, they can race at two or three times the wind's speed.

This sounds impossible, but it isn't. When a boat sails across the wind, its own speed creates extra wind over the sail, the way you feel a breeze on a bicycle even on a still day. Ice yachts and land yachts, which roll or skate with little friction, can go even faster compared with the wind. A kite climbs into a breeze using a similar mix of lift and a tight string.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A sailboat can only go where the wind blows it.

Truth: A sail works like a wing, producing a sideways push that the keel turns into forward motion. Sailboats can travel across the wind and even upwind, by zigzagging. Only the zone straight into the wind is off-limits.

Try this

Float a lid or a flat piece of foam in a tub of water, with a paper sail stuck on a straw. Blow gently from behind, then from the side. Then glue a strip of card under the boat as a keel and blow from the side again. See how the keel helps the boat go forward instead of sliding away.
