Why did woolly mammoths disappear?

The last mammoths were still alive when the Great Pyramid already stood in Egypt.

The short answer

Woolly mammoths were hairy elephants of the Ice Age. They lived on cold, dry grasslands across Europe, Asia and North America. When the Ice Age ended, about 10,000 years ago, those grasslands shrank as forests and wetter ground spread.

At the same time, people were hunting them. Most scientists think the two together, a warming climate and hunting, pushed mammoths to extinction. Which mattered more is still argued over.

Surprising but true

The last woolly mammoths lived on Wrangel Island, off Siberia, until about 4,000 years ago, about 500 years after the Great Pyramid was built.

Built for the cold

A woolly mammoth stood about 3 metres tall at the shoulder, roughly as tall as a single-decker bus. It had a coat of long, shaggy hair over a thick, woolly underlayer, and a layer of fat under its skin.

Its ears were small and its tail was short. Both helped, because less skin sticking out means less heat lost. Its long curved tusks may have been used to sweep snow off plants, as well as for fighting and showing off. Some tusks grew more than 4 metres long.

A shrinking home

Mammoths ate grasses, herbs and low shrubs. During the cold, dry Ice Age, a huge grassland called the mammoth steppe stretched from Spain to Canada. As the planet warmed, more rain and snow fell, and much of that grassland turned into forest, bog and wet tundra. There was simply less good food.

Climate alone may not explain it, though. Mammoths had survived earlier warm spells. This time, there was something new: people.

Hunters on the steppe

Stone Age people hunted mammoths and used every part. In what is now Ukraine, people built round huts from hundreds of mammoth bones about 15,000 years ago. In North America, stone spear points have been found among mammoth bones.

Mammoths had only one calf every few years, so even a little hunting could hurt a herd that was already short of food. Many scientists think the pressure from Stone Age hunters, added to a changing climate, was too much.

The last mammoths

A small group survived on Wrangel Island, off the coast of Siberia, until about 4,000 years ago. Rising seas had cut the island off, and no people lived there yet. By then, the Great Pyramid of Egypt had already been standing for about 500 years.

The island group was small, and studies of their DNA suggest they had become inbred, with many faults in their genes. Nobody knows exactly what finished them. It might have been a bad winter, a drought or a disease.

How do we know so much?

Mammoths have been found frozen in the ground in Siberia, sometimes with hair, skin and even food in their stomachs. In 2007, a reindeer herder found a baby mammoth in Russia's Yamal Peninsula, so well kept that scientists could study her trunk and eyelashes. She was named Lyuba.

Tusks hold clues too. A tusk grew in thin layers, a little each day, like the rings of a tree. In 2021, scientists studied the chemistry of those layers in one Alaskan mammoth's tusk. It showed that during its life, the mammoth walked a distance that would nearly circle the Earth twice.

Scientists have also read mammoth DNA. It shows they were most closely related to Asian elephants, like the ones in India's forests today.

Could they come back?

Some scientists want to use DNA to make elephants with mammoth features, such as thick hair and extra fat. Others ask whether it is wise, or kind to the elephants involved. For now, the mammoth is gone, and its relatives in India and Africa face troubles of their own.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Mammoths were giant animals, much bigger than any elephant.

Truth: Woolly mammoths were about the size of today's African elephants, around 3 m tall at the shoulder. Some other kinds, like the steppe mammoth, were larger.

Try this

Test how fur keeps out the cold. Fill two small cups with the same amount of cold water from the fridge. Wrap one in an old woolly sock or scarf and leave the other bare. After an hour, feel which one is still colder. Mammoths used the same trick in reverse, keeping their body heat in.
