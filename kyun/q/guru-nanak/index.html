Who was Guru Nanak?

Sikh tradition says that more than 500 years ago, a young man in Punjab came out of a river after three days and said, "There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim." It became the start of Sikhism.

The short answer

Guru Nanak was a spiritual teacher who lived in Punjab from 1469 to 1539. He is the founder of Sikhism and the first of the ten Sikh Gurus. His name is often written Guru Nanak Dev Ji, as a mark of respect.

He taught that there is one God, present in everyone and everything, and that people come closer to God through honest work, remembering God's name and sharing what they have. He spoke out against empty rituals and against treating people as high or low because of their caste.

His hymns, written in Punjabi and related languages, are part of the Guru Granth Sahib, the Sikh holy book. Today there are around 2.5 crore Sikhs around the world.

Surprising but true

Guru Nanak travelled for many years, mostly on foot, with his Muslim friend and musician Bhai Mardana. Mardana played the rabab while Nanak sang his hymns.

A curious child

Nanak was born in the village of Talwandi, near Lahore, now called Nankana Sahib in Pakistan. His father, Mehta Kalu, was a village accountant. His older sister, Bebe Nanaki, is remembered as the first person to recognise how special he was.

Stories tell of a thoughtful boy who asked his teachers difficult questions. In one well-known story, his father gave him 20 rupees to make a good business deal. Nanak spent it all feeding hungry holy men, and called it the truest bargain, sacha sauda.

As a young man he worked as a storekeeper in the town of Sultanpur Lodhi.

The river and the message

According to Sikh tradition, when Nanak was about 30, he went to bathe in the Kali Bein river and did not come out. Friends feared he had drowned. After three days he returned and said, "There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim."

Many understand this to mean that God doesn't see people by religious labels, only by how they live. His teaching opens with the words Ik Onkar, "there is one God".

Journeys on foot

Guru Nanak then set out on long journeys, called udasis, over more than 20 years. His companion was Bhai Mardana, a Muslim musician, who played the rabab, a stringed instrument, while Nanak sang.

The Janamsakhis, life stories written later, say they travelled across India and as far as Sri Lanka, Tibet, Mecca and Baghdad. Historians can't confirm every place, but they agree he travelled very widely and talked with Hindu and Muslim holy people. Like Kabir before him, he sang in the language of ordinary people.

A village of farmers and singers

In his later years, Guru Nanak settled at Kartarpur, on the Ravi river, and worked as a farmer. His followers, called Sikhs, meaning learners, lived and worked there too.

They began each day with prayer and sang hymns together in the evening. Everyone ate in a shared kitchen called the langar. Rich and poor, men and women, people of every caste sat on the floor together. The langar continues today in gurdwaras around the world. The one at the Golden Temple in Amritsar feeds tens of thousands of people a day.

Before he died in 1539, Guru Nanak chose his follower Lehna, renamed Angad, as the next Guru, rather than either of his sons. His hymns were later gathered by the fifth Guru, Guru Arjan, into the scripture completed in 1604.

Remembered today

Guru Nanak's birthday, Gurpurab, is celebrated on the full moon of the month of Kartik, in October or November. Gurdwaras are lit up, hymns are sung through the night, and processions fill the streets.

Kartarpur is now in Pakistan, just a few kilometres from the border with India. In 2019, for the 550th anniversary of his birth, the two countries opened the Kartarpur Corridor so pilgrims from India could visit without a visa.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Sikhism is just a mix of Hindu and Muslim ideas.

Truth: Guru Nanak grew up among both traditions and spoke with followers of each. But Sikhs see his teaching as a new and distinct revelation, with its own scripture, practices and community. Scholars discuss its influences, but it is a religion in its own right.

Try this

Plan a "langar lunch" with your family or class. Everyone helps with one job, such as washing vegetables, rolling rotis, serving water or cleaning up, and then everyone sits together in rows to eat the same food. Afterwards, talk about which job felt most important.
