Were carrots always orange?

The first farmed carrots were purple and yellow. Orange ones only took over in the 1600s, after Dutch growers chose them.

The short answer

For most of history, carrots were not orange. The wild carrot is a thin, white, bitter root that still grows along roadsides in Europe and Asia. When people in Central Asia, around what is now Afghanistan and Iran, began farming carrots about 1,100 years ago, the roots were mostly purple and yellow.

Carrots spread west to Europe and east to China and India. Orange carrots became common in the Netherlands in the 1600s. Dutch growers produced sweet, reliable orange varieties, and these spread around the world.

Today orange carrots are so normal that a purple one looks like a trick. In fact it is closer to the original. Purple, yellow, white and red carrots are all still grown, and you can sometimes find bags of mixed "rainbow carrots" in supermarkets in India and the US.

Surprising but true

The chemical that makes carrots orange, carotene, is named after the carrot. It was first pulled out of carrots in 1831.

A colour from the sun

Orange carrots get their colour from beta-carotene, a pigment that also colours mangoes, pumpkins and sweet potatoes. The word carotene comes from the carrot itself. A German chemist, Heinrich Wackenroder, first pulled it out of carrot juice in 1831.

Your body turns beta-carotene into vitamin A, one of the vitamins your eyes and skin need. Eat a really huge pile of carrots every day and your palms can turn slightly orange. It is called carotenaemia, it is harmless, and it fades when you eat fewer.

Purple carrots get their colour from anthocyanins, the same pigments found in red cabbage and blackberries. Yellow carrots have other carotene-like pigments in smaller amounts.

The Dutch and the orange story

There is a popular story that Dutch farmers bred orange carrots to honour William of Orange, the prince who led their fight for independence. It's a lovely tale, but historians have found no clear evidence for it. Orange carrots may simply have been sweeter, grown better in the Dutch climate and kept well.

What we do know comes partly from art. Paintings of markets and kitchens from the Netherlands in the 1600s show piles of orange carrots. Before then, European pictures more often show purple or yellowish roots.

In 2016 scientists read the carrot's full set of genes. They found a gene change linked to the build-up of orange colour, and confirmed that carrots were first farmed in Central Asia.

India's red and black carrots

Walk through a North Indian market in winter and you will see carrots that are deep red, not orange. These desi carrots are juicy and sweet, and they are the classic choice for gajar ka halwa. They belong to an Asian group of carrots, separate from the orange European kind.

Some parts of North India also grow black carrots, which are really a very dark purple. They are used to make kanji, a tangy fermented drink that turns a brilliant purple-red. It is often made around Holi, when the weather starts to warm up.

The carrot that fooled a war

During the Second World War, Britain told the public that its pilots could shoot down enemy planes at night because they ate plenty of carrots. Historians think the story helped hide the real secret: a new radar system. It also encouraged people to eat carrots, which were easy to grow while other foods were scarce.

The myth stuck around long after the war. Carrots are good for your eyes in the sense that they help prevent vitamin A shortage, a real cause of night blindness in some parts of the world. But eating more than you need won't sharpen your sight.

And the strangest part? Tiny cells at the back of your eye sort the colours of a carrot from those of a sunset, and you can find out how in how your eyes see.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Eating lots of carrots lets you see in the dark.

Truth: Carrots give you vitamin A, which healthy eyes need, but extra carrots won't give you night vision. The story was spread in Britain during the Second World War, partly to hide the fact that pilots were using radar.

Try this

Grate a little carrot and press it onto a white paper towel. Do the same with beetroot or a purple vegetable if you have one. Compare the stains. The orange one comes from carotene, while purple colours come from a different family of chemicals.
