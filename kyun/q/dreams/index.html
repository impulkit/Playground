Why do we dream?

You probably dream for about two hours every night, and forget almost all of it within minutes of waking up.

The short answer

Nobody knows for certain, and scientists still argue about it. What they do know is when dreams happen. Most vivid dreams come during REM sleep, short for "rapid eye movement", when the brain is almost as active as when you're awake.

Several ideas try to explain why. Dreams may help the brain sort and store the day's memories. They may help us handle strong feelings, such as worry or sadness, in a safer setting. Or they may be a way of rehearsing tricky situations. Some scientists think dreams are partly a side effect of a busy brain making stories out of random signals.

It's quite possible that more than one of these ideas is right.

Surprising but true

During your most vivid dreams, your brain switches off most of your muscles, so you don't act out what you are dreaming.

The boy with wires on his head

In 1953, a young researcher named Eugene Aserinsky was studying sleep at the University of Chicago with his professor, Nathaniel Kleitman. One of his test sleepers was his own eight-year-old son, Armond, with wires taped near his eyes.

Aserinsky noticed that, several times a night, the sleepers' eyes darted rapidly under their lids. When people were woken during these periods, they usually described vivid dreams. This was the discovery of REM sleep, and it turned dreams into something scientists could measure.

Your body on pause

During REM sleep, your brain sends out signals that block most of your muscles. You can still breathe, and your eyes can move, but your arms and legs go limp. This keeps you from leaping out of bed to chase a dream football.

Sometimes, just as you wake up, the pause lasts a few seconds too long and you can't move. It feels scary but is harmless, and it passes quickly.

Formulas, songs and stories

Some people say their best ideas came in dreams. The Indian mathematician Srinivasa Ramanujan told friends that the goddess Namagiri showed him mathematical formulas while he slept. Paul McCartney of the Beatles said the tune of his song "Yesterday" came to him in a dream, and he worried at first that he'd copied it from someone.

People have puzzled over dreams for thousands of years. The ancient Indian text called the Mandukya Upanishad describes waking, dreaming and dreamless sleep as different states of mind. In 1899, the Austrian doctor Sigmund Freud argued that dreams show hidden wishes. Many scientists today doubt his ideas, though they agree dreams are often linked to feelings.

Do animals dream?

Many mammals and birds have REM sleep too. If you've watched a sleeping dog twitch its paws and give soft little barks, it may have been dreaming. In experiments, rats that learned a maze replayed similar patterns of brain activity while they slept. We can't ask a rat what it saw, of course.

Nightmares and lucid dreams

Bad dreams are common, especially in childhood, and having them doesn't mean anything is wrong. Talking about a nightmare with a trusted adult, or drawing it with a new and better ending, often helps it lose its power.

A few people sometimes realise they're dreaming while still asleep. This is called lucid dreaming. In sleep labs, some lucid dreamers have even signalled to scientists by moving their eyes left and right in a pattern agreed beforehand.

Why dreams vanish

Dreams fade fast because the brain's memory-making chemistry is turned down during sleep. Unless you think about a dream soon after waking, it slips away. One study found that older people who grew up with black-and-white television reported more black-and-white dreams than younger people. Even our dreams, it seems, borrow from our memories.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Some people never dream.

Truth: Nearly everyone dreams several times a night. People who say they never dream usually just don't remember their dreams. Woken up in a sleep lab during REM sleep, they almost always report one.

Try this

Keep a notebook and pencil next to your bed for a week. As soon as you wake, before you get up or talk to anyone, write down anything you remember from your dreams, even a single picture or feeling. Many people find they remember more dreams as the week goes on.
