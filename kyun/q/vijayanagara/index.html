What was the Vijayanagara Empire?

A Portuguese visitor in the 1520s said this south Indian capital was as big as Rome, and had markets heaped with rubies and diamonds.

The short answer

The Vijayanagara Empire was a great south Indian kingdom that lasted from 1336 to the 1600s. At its height it covered most of the land south of the Krishna river, across today's Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Tamil Nadu.

Its capital, Vijayanagara, meaning "city of victory", stood on the Tungabhadra river. Its ruins are now known as Hampi. The empire grew rich from farming, temple economies and trade in cotton, spices and gems, and it bought war horses from overseas. Its kings supported temples, poets and festivals in Kannada, Telugu, Tamil and Sanskrit. After a heavy defeat at the Battle of Talikota in 1565, the capital was abandoned.

Surprising but true

Tenali Ramakrishna, the joker hero of the Tenali Raman stories, was a real poet at the court of Vijayanagara's greatest king.

Two brothers and a city of victory

The usual story says two brothers, Harihara and Bukka of the Sangama family, founded the kingdom in 1336. They had served local rulers who were swept aside when armies of the Delhi Sultanate raided the south.

Legend adds that a sage named Vidyaranya guided them, and that they once saw a hare turn and chase hunting dogs at the spot where they built their city. Historians treat that tale with caution. The brothers' early careers, and whether they spoke Kannada or Telugu, are still argued about.

The poet king

The empire reached its peak under Krishnadevaraya, who ruled from 1509 to 1529. He won wars against the Deccan sultanates and the Gajapati kings of Odisha, and he built and repaired many temples.

He also wrote poetry. His Telugu poem Amuktamalyada tells the story of Andal, a woman saint of Tamil Nadu. His court had eight famous poets, called the Ashtadiggajas. One of them was Tenali Ramakrishna, whose witty adventures became the Tenali Raman stories that children still love.

A city as big as Rome

Visitors were stunned. The Persian envoy Abdur Razzaq came in 1443 and described seven rings of walls. A Portuguese trader named Domingo Paes, who visited around 1520, wrote that the city was as large as Rome and very beautiful.

Paes described markets piled with rice, fruit, cloth and spices, and stalls selling pearls, rubies and diamonds. He also watched the Mahanavami festival, when the king sat on a great stone platform to watch wrestling, dancing and parades of elephants and horses. That platform still stands at Hampi.

Horses from across the sea

Cavalry won wars in medieval India, but good war horses did not thrive in the south. So Vijayanagara imported thousands every year from Arabia and Persia, landing them at west coast ports.

After the Portuguese took Goa in 1510, they became important horse traders. Krishnadevaraya kept friendly terms with them so the horses would keep coming. Trade kept the empire strong as much as conquest did. Portuguese writers such as Fernão Nunes, who lived in the city in the 1530s, left detailed accounts of its kings, armies and customs, which historians still rely on.

The fall of the capital

In 1565, an alliance of four Deccan sultanates defeated Vijayanagara's army near Talikota, far to the north of the capital. The regent, Aliya Rama Raya, was killed.

With its army gone, the city had no defence. It was looted and burned over several months, and its people left. An Italian traveller who passed through soon afterwards found it empty. The dynasty carried on from smaller capitals, and the old city's granite temples and walls were left to the monkeys, the boulders and, much later, the archaeologists.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Vijayanagara Empire ended the day its capital was looted in 1565.

Truth: The capital never recovered, but the dynasty carried on from new capitals such as Penukonda and Chandragiri for almost another century.
