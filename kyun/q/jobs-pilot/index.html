How do you become a pilot?

In India you can start flying lessons at 16 and hold a commercial pilot's licence at 18, but an airline will want years of training and hundreds of hours in the air first.

The short answer

To become an airline pilot, you need to learn to fly, pass written exams, pass a strict medical check, and build up hundreds of hours in the air. In India, the Directorate General of Civil Aviation (DGCA) gives out the Commercial Pilot Licence. You must be at least 18, have studied physics and maths up to Class 12, and have flown about 200 hours.

That licence lets you be paid to fly, but it is only the start. Airlines then train new pilots on the exact plane they will fly, mostly in simulators, before they ever carry passengers.

Surprising but true

In 1936, Sarla Thakral earned her pilot's licence at 21, already a mother, and flew a small Gipsy Moth plane over Lahore while wearing a sari.

Learning to fly

Most pilots begin in a small plane with one propeller and two seats, with an instructor beside them. The first lessons are about the basics: taking off, turning, climbing and, above all, landing. After about 10 to 20 hours of lessons, many students fly their first solo, alone in the plane for a few minutes. Pilots often remember that day for the rest of their lives. By tradition in some flying schools, a student's shirt tail is cut off after the first solo and pinned to the wall with the date.

Students also study weather, navigation, radio calls, engines and the science of how planes fly. They take exams in each subject. In India, a student pilot can start training at 16.

Hours in the sky

Experience is measured in flying hours. In the USA, the Federal Aviation Administration says a pilot usually needs at least 1,500 hours before flying for an airline as a first officer, the co-pilot who sits on the right. Many young pilots build those hours by teaching other students.

Training is expensive. A commercial licence can cost tens of lakhs of rupees in India and tens of thousands of dollars in the USA. Some airlines run cadet schemes that pay for training in return for a promise to work for them. Another path is through the Indian Air Force or the US military, where the armed forces train their own pilots.

A day at work

An airliner has two pilots: a captain on the left and a first officer on the right. They take turns flying and keep checking each other. Before every flight they study the weather, decide how much fuel to carry and walk around the plane looking for problems.

Much of the cruising is done by the autopilot, but pilots are always watching, talking to air traffic controllers and ready to take over. Takeoff and landing are usually flown by hand, or closely watched if the plane lands itself in fog. Long-haul pilots may cross several time zones in a day and have to manage tiredness carefully.

Staying fit to fly

Pilots must pass a medical check every year, and more often as they get older. Doctors test their eyes, hearing, heart and blood pressure. In most countries, airline pilots must retire at 65. Every six months or so, airline pilots go back to the simulator to practise emergencies and pass a test again. Pilots also have strict limits on how many hours they can fly in a day, a week and a month, so that a tired crew is never at the controls.

Women in the cockpit

In 1936, Sarla Thakral became the first Indian woman to earn a pilot's licence. Today India has a higher share of women airline pilots than most countries: more than one in ten pilots in India are women, compared with about one in twenty worldwide (2020s figures).

The first licence in India went to J.R.D. Tata in 1929. In 1932 he flew a small plane carrying mail from Karachi to Bombay, the first flight of the airline that later became Air India. Every pilot who takes off from an Indian airport today follows that route into the sky.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You need perfect eyesight to become a pilot.

Truth: Many airline pilots wear glasses or contact lenses. What matters is that your vision, with correction, meets the medical standard, and that you can see colours properly.
