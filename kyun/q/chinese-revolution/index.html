How did modern China begin?

In 1912 a six-year-old emperor gave up a throne that had lasted over 2,000 years. It took nearly four more decades of war before modern China took shape.

The short answer

For more than 2,000 years, China was ruled by emperors. The last dynasty, the Qing, grew weak in the 1800s after losing wars to foreign powers. In 1911 a revolt broke out, and in early 1912 the boy emperor Puyi gave up the throne. China became a republic.

The republic struggled. Generals fought over territory, Japan invaded, and two parties, the Nationalists and the Communists, battled for control. In 1949 the Communists under Mao Zedong won, and founded the People's Republic of China. Much of what China is today grew out of those decades of upheaval.

Surprising but true

Puyi, China's last emperor, ended his life working as a gardener and an archive assistant in Beijing under the communist government.

The end of the emperors

By 1900 many Chinese people blamed the Qing for letting foreign countries push China around, ever since the Opium Wars. A doctor named Sun Yat-sen spent years abroad raising money for a revolution, including from Chinese workers in America.

On 10 October 1911, soldiers in the city of Wuchang rebelled. Province after province joined them. Sun became the first president of the new Republic of China on 1 January 1912, and Puyi, aged six, gave up his throne a few weeks later. He was allowed to keep living in the Forbidden City for a while, an emperor with no empire.

Sun Yat-sen's own time in charge was short, but his ideas lasted. Today he is honoured both in mainland China and in Taiwan, two governments that agree on very little else. His portrait hangs in Tiananmen Square every year on National Day.

Generals, Nationalists and Communists

Sun soon lost power, and for years China was split between warlords, generals with private armies. Sun's Nationalist Party, later led by Chiang Kai-shek, tried to reunite the country. The Chinese Communist Party, founded in 1921, at first worked with the Nationalists, then became their bitter enemy.

In 1934 the Communists, close to defeat, set out on the Long March, a retreat of thousands of kilometres across mountains and rivers. Most who started did not finish. But the march made Mao Zedong the party's leader.

War with Japan, then civil war

Japan invaded northern China in 1931 and launched a full war in 1937. Millions died. The Nationalists and Communists fought Japan, sometimes side by side, while still distrusting each other. When Japan was defeated in 1945, their civil war began again.

The Communists won the support of many poor farmers and gained ground. On 1 October 1949 Mao announced the People's Republic from the Tiananmen gate in Beijing. Chiang's government retreated to the island of Taiwan, where the Republic of China continued. As the Cold War set in, the United States backed Chiang's government and the Soviet Union backed Mao's. How Taiwan relates to mainland China is still disputed today.

Hard years and big changes

Mao's rule brought land reform, more schools and better basic health care. It also brought disasters. The Great Leap Forward, a rushed plan to industrialise from 1958, led to a famine in which tens of millions of people died. The Cultural Revolution, from 1966, closed many schools and turned students against teachers and parents.

After Mao died in 1976, a new leader, Deng Xiaoping, changed course. From 1978 China opened to foreign trade and let people run businesses. The country grew into one of the world's largest economies, though the Communist Party still holds firm control. Today China makes a large share of the world's phones, toys and clothes.

Myth or fact?

Myth: China became communist the moment the emperor fell.

Truth: The emperor fell in 1912, but China was then a republic, often torn apart by rival generals. The Communist Party was not even founded until 1921, and it took power only in 1949.
