Why does cornflour slime act like a solid and a liquid?

Punch a bowl of cornflour and water and it feels like hitting clay. Dip your finger in slowly and it slides in like syrup.

The short answer

Mix cornflour with water and you get a strange goo. It isn't quite a liquid or a solid. Its behaviour depends on how hard and fast you push it.

The mixture is made of countless tiny grains of starch floating in water. When you move slowly, the water acts like a lubricant, and the grains slide easily past each other. The goo pours like thick cream. But when you hit it, squeeze it or stir it fast, the grains are forced together faster than the water can get out of the way. They jam into a tight, solid clump, and the mixture suddenly feels hard.

Scientists call this a shear-thickening liquid. Many children know it as oobleck.

Surprising but true

People have run across a swimming pool filled with cornflour and water without sinking, as long as they kept moving fast. When they stopped, they sank.

Newton's simple rule

In the late 1600s, the English scientist Isaac Newton described how ordinary liquids flow. Water, oil and honey each have a fixed thickness, or viscosity, at a given temperature. Stir water gently or wildly and it stays just as runny. Honey gets runnier when you warm it, but stirring it faster doesn't change how thick it is.

Liquids that follow this rule are called Newtonian. Liquids that break it are called non-Newtonian. Their thickness changes depending on the force on them. Cornflour slime gets thicker when you push it hard. Others do the opposite.

A name from a children's book

The nickname oobleck comes from a 1949 picture book by Dr. Seuss, Bartholomew and the Oobleck. In the story, a bored king wants something new to fall from the sky, and his magicians produce a sticky green goo that gums up the whole kingdom.

Science teachers borrowed the name for cornflour and water, which is gloopy and a bit magical too. It's a favourite classroom experiment in India and the USA, partly because cornflour is cheap, safe and washes off. Families sometimes add a drop of food colouring to make it look more like the book.

Running on custard

Because oobleck turns solid under sudden force, people have tried running across pools of it, often for science shows on television. Each footstep slams down hard, so the surface firms up and holds them for a moment. As long as they keep running, they stay on top.

If they stop, the grains relax, the mixture turns liquid again, and their feet slowly sink. Pulling a foot out quickly is hard, because the fast tug makes the goo grip again. The only way out is to move gently. Engineers have tested similar liquids for flexible body armour that stiffens on impact.

Thinning and quicksand

Some liquids do the opposite and get runnier when pushed. Ketchup, made from tomatoes, is one. It sits stubbornly in the bottle until you shake or tap it hard, then suddenly rushes out. Paint is another. It spreads smoothly under a brush, but stays put on the wall without dripping.

Quicksand is a non-Newtonian mixture too, of sand, clay and water. It looks solid until you step on it, then it turns soft and swallows your feet. In 2005, scientists in Amsterdam showed in tests that a person can't sink completely, because the body is less dense than the sand. Films love scenes of people disappearing into quicksand, but in real life you'd float about waist-deep, stuck but safe. Some ordinary-looking sand, it turns out, has a hidden liquid side.

Quick facts

Recipe
about 2 parts cornflour to 1 part water
Nickname
oobleck, from a 1949 Dr. Seuss book
Type
shear-thickening, it gets thicker when pushed hard
Opposite type
shear-thinning, like ketchup and paint
Named after
Isaac Newton, whose simple rule these liquids break

Myth or fact?

Myth: Quicksand can suck you under and drown you.

Truth: People float in quicksand because the human body is less dense than the wet sand. You might sink to your waist and get stuck, but not go under. Getting out means moving slowly to let water back in around your legs.

Try this

Mix about two cups of cornflour with one cup of water in a bowl, adding the water slowly and stirring with your fingers. Tap it quickly, then press slowly. Squeeze a handful into a ball, then open your hand and watch it melt. Wash up with plenty of water, and don't pour it down the sink.
