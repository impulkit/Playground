How is a plane built?

A Boeing 747 is put together from about six million parts, made in factories across several countries and flown, shipped or trucked to one giant hangar.

The short answer

An airliner is built in big pieces called sections: the front, middle and back of the fuselage (the long tube), the wings, the tail and the engines. Different factories, often in different countries, make each one. The sections travel to a final assembly hangar, where workers join them together, add wiring, seats, kitchens and toilets, paint the plane and fit the engines.

Then come months of tests, on the ground and in the air, before the first passengers ever climb aboard. A big airliner such as a Boeing 747 has about six million parts.

Surprising but true

Engineers bend new airliner wings upwards on purpose until they are close to breaking, to prove they can survive far worse than any storm.

Pieces from around the world

Few planes are made in one place. For Airbus, wings are built in Broughton in Wales, while other sections come from France, Germany and Spain. They are carried to final assembly lines in cities including Toulouse in France and Hamburg in Germany, and also in China and the USA.

Some sections travel inside special cargo planes with huge, bulging bodies. Airbus uses the Beluga, named after the white whale it resembles. Boeing used the Dreamlifter, a puffed-up 747, to carry 787 wings and fuselage sections to its factories.

Metal, then plastic-like carbon

Most planes since the 1930s have been built mainly from aluminium, a metal light enough to fly and strong enough to hold the pressure of a cabin at high altitude. Thousands of rivets hold the skin to the frame, and each one must be fitted perfectly, because a cabin is pumped up and let down like a balloon on every flight.

Newer planes use carbon-fibre composites, made of thin carbon threads set in hard resin. They are lighter than aluminium and do not rust. About half the Boeing 787 by weight is composite. Its fuselage sections are baked in a giant oven, the way you might bake a clay pot, but at far bigger scale.

The giant hangar

Final assembly is like a slow car factory. On some lines, planes inch forward on a moving floor, a few centimetres a minute. Workers climb ladders and scaffolds to fit miles of wiring, hydraulic pipes, windows and doors.

Boeing's factory at Everett, near Seattle, is the largest building in the world by volume. It covers about 40 hectares, and Disneyland's main park could fit inside it. It was first built in the 1960s for the 747, and each of its biggest doors is about as wide as an American football field is long.

Breaking it on purpose

Before a new type of plane flies passengers, test copies are tortured. One airframe is bent, twisted and shaken by machines for years, copying many lifetimes of take-offs and landings.

Wings are pulled upwards with huge weights until they flex several metres, to prove they can carry one and a half times the heaviest load expected in real flight. Engines face bird strike tests. Test pilots fly through freezing clouds, extreme heat and strong crosswinds. Only then do regulators give the plane a certificate. For a brand-new airliner, the whole process from first drawing to first passenger often takes around ten years.

Planes made in India

In October 2024, Tata Advanced Systems and Airbus opened a final assembly line in Vadodara, Gujarat, for the C295, a military transport plane. It is India's first private-sector final assembly line for a military plane. In Bengaluru, Hindustan Aeronautics builds the Tejas fighter for the Indian Air Force.

Indian factories also make parts for Boeing and Airbus airliners. And all of it goes back to 1903, when the Wright brothers built their first plane from spruce wood, muslin cloth and bicycle chain in a small workshop. It flew 37 metres, less than the wingspan of a 747.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Plane wings are rigid and should never bend.

Truth: Wings are designed to flex. On a big airliner the wingtips rise noticeably in flight and in rough air. In ground tests, wings are bent far further than they ever will be with passengers aboard.

Try this

Hold a strip of paper about 20 cm long just under your bottom lip so it droops down. Blow steadily across the top of it. The paper rises, because the moving air above it has lower pressure. Plane wings are shaped to make this kind of lift.
