What is the coldest place where people live?

In the Siberian village of Oymyakon, the air once dropped to about minus 68 °C, cold enough that boiling water flung into the air turns into a cloud of ice crystals.

The short answer

The coldest places where people live all year round are two villages in Siberia, Russia: Oymyakon and Verkhoyansk. Both have recorded temperatures of about minus 68 °C, and both claim the title.

These are proper settlements, with homes, schools and shops, not research stations. In January, the average temperature in Oymyakon is around minus 50 °C. Far colder temperatures have been measured in Antarctica, but nobody lives there permanently. Places like Yakutsk, Siberia's big city, and towns in Alaska, northern Canada and Greenland also get bitterly cold, and people manage there too.

Surprising but true

In Yakutsk, a Siberian city of over 3 lakh people, many buildings stand on stilts so their warmth doesn't melt the frozen ground and make them sink.

The Pole of Cold

Oymyakon and Verkhoyansk sit in deep valleys in the Sakha Republic, also called Yakutia, in eastern Russia. In winter, cold heavy air slides down from the mountains and pools in the valleys, like water collecting at the bottom of a bowl. With almost no wind and long nights, the cold just builds.

Verkhoyansk recorded about minus 67.8 °C in 1892. Oymyakon recorded about minus 67.7 °C in 1933, though some reports suggest even lower figures. A large sign in Oymyakon calls it the "Pole of Cold". Oymyakon is home to about 500 people.

Living in the deep freeze

Daily life takes planning. People wear layers of fur and thick felt boots. Cars may be left running or kept in heated garages, because engines can freeze solid. Fish sold at outdoor markets freeze stiff and stand up in buckets like sticks.

Most of the ground in Yakutia is permafrost, frozen solid all year. Pipes are often built above ground, and many buildings stand on stilts so their heat doesn't thaw the soil. Schools close for the youngest children only on the most bitterly cold days. Bare skin can get frostbite in minutes, so people cover their faces with scarves that quickly frost white.

Even colder, but nobody lives there

The coldest air temperature ever measured at a weather station was minus 89.2 °C, at Vostok Station in Antarctica on 21 July 1983. Satellites have since detected surface temperatures below minus 90 °C in hollows on the high Antarctic ice.

At those temperatures, breathing outside without covering your mouth would hurt your lungs. Scientists at Vostok come and go in shifts. Nobody makes a permanent home there.

America's coldest corners

The lowest temperature ever recorded in the United States was about minus 62 °C (minus 80 °F) at Prospect Creek, in Alaska, in January 1971. Towns in Alaska, such as Fairbanks, often see minus 40 in winter.

In the lower 48 states, a few small towns compete for the nickname "Icebox of the Nation", including International Falls, Minnesota, and Fraser, Colorado.

India's coldest towns

India's coldest inhabited places are high in the Himalayas. Dras, in Ladakh, lies about 3,300 m above sea level, and its winter nights are often well below minus 20 °C. Snow can cut it off from the rest of India for months.

Leh, Kargil and villages in Spiti, in Himachal Pradesh, also face long, freezing winters. People there use thick mud-brick walls, wood and dung stoves, and warm woollen clothes to stay comfortable. Your body has its own tricks for cold, such as shivering and the goosebumps left over from furrier ancestors.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Dras in Ladakh is the second-coldest inhabited place in the world.

Truth: Dras is one of the coldest places in India, with winter nights often far below freezing. But towns in Siberia, northern Canada and Alaska regularly get much colder, so it is nowhere near second.
