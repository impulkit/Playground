Who are some women who shaped India's history?

A sultan who rode into battle on an elephant, a queen who rebuilt temples across India, and a teacher who was pelted with mud on her way to class.

The short answer

Women have shaped India's story for thousands of years, as rulers, warriors, thinkers, poets, teachers, doctors and freedom fighters. Many are famous, like Rani Lakshmibai of Jhansi. Others are less well known outside their own regions.

Here are a few of them. You will meet a sultan in Delhi, a queen in central India, a teacher in Pune, a doctor who studied in America, and the women who helped write India's constitution. Each one did something people around her said a woman could not do.

Surprising but true

In 1886 Anandibai Joshi became one of the first Indian women to earn a degree in Western medicine, studying in Philadelphia in the USA.

A philosopher and a sultan

The Brihadaranyaka Upanishad is one of India's ancient texts. In it, a woman scholar named Gargi asks the sage Yajnavalkya question after question about the nature of the universe, in front of a king's court. She is remembered as a sharp and fearless thinker.

Centuries later, in 1236, Razia became ruler of the Delhi Sultanate. Her father, Iltutmish, chose her over her brothers. Razia dressed like a male ruler, held court in public and rode an elephant into battle. Some nobles disliked being ruled by a woman, and she was overthrown and killed in 1240. She was the only woman to rule from the throne of Delhi in the sultanate era.

The philosopher queen of Malwa

Ahilyabai Holkar became ruler of the Maratha state of Malwa, in today's Madhya Pradesh, in 1767, after the deaths of her husband and son. She governed for nearly 30 years from Maheshwar, on the Narmada river.

She was known for fair justice and careful use of money. She built and repaired temples, wells, ghats and rest houses across India, from the Himalayas to the far south. In 1780 she rebuilt the Kashi Vishwanath temple in Varanasi, the version that still stands today.

The teacher who would not quit

In 1848 Savitribai Phule and her husband, Jyotirao Phule, opened a school for girls in Pune. At that time, very few girls in India went to school, and education for girls from lower castes was fiercely opposed. Savitribai became one of India's first women teachers.

She went on to open more schools, care for widows and orphans, and write poetry. In 1897, while nursing people during a plague outbreak, she caught the disease herself and died. Her birthday, 3 January, is marked in Maharashtra as a day to honour women's education.

A doctor from Kalyan

Anandibai Joshi, born near Kalyan in Maharashtra, lost a baby son when she was 14, partly because good medical care was not available. She decided to become a doctor. In 1883 she sailed to the United States and studied at the Woman's Medical College of Pennsylvania. She graduated in 1886, one of the first Indian women to earn a degree in Western medicine.

She returned to India to lead the women's ward of a hospital in Kolhapur, but she had fallen ill with tuberculosis. She died in 1887, aged just 21.

Writing the constitution

When India's Constituent Assembly met to write the constitution, 15 of its members were women. They included Sarojini Naidu, poet and freedom fighter; Hansa Mehta, who later helped write the Universal Declaration of Human Rights; and Dakshayani Velayudhan, the only Dalit woman in the Assembly. Thanks partly to them, the constitution gave every adult woman the vote from the very start in 1950, something many older democracies had taken decades to do.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Women played little part in India's history until modern times.

Truth: Women ruled kingdoms, led armies, wrote poetry, debated philosophy and ran trade for many centuries. Their stories were often written down less, so historians have to look harder to find them.
