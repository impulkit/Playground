Why is it hard to breathe on high mountains?

At the top of Mount Everest, every breath you take holds only about a third of the oxygen it would at the beach.

The short answer

Air is a mixture of gases, about 21% oxygen. That share stays the same whether you are on Marine Drive in Mumbai or on top of Everest. What changes is how tightly the air is packed. Near the sea, the weight of all the air above squeezes it together. Climb higher and there is less air above you, so the air is thinner.

Thin air means each breath holds fewer molecules of oxygen. Your lungs have to work harder, your heart beats faster, and you may get a headache. On the summit of Everest, at 8,849 metres, the air pressure is only about a third of what it is at sea level.

Surprising but true

Many Tibetans carry a gene variant that helps them cope with thin air, and scientists think it came from the Denisovans, ancient cousins of humans who died out tens of thousands of years ago.

What your body notices first

Oxygen gets into your body through tiny air sacs in your lungs, called alveoli. From there it jumps into red blood cells, where a protein called haemoglobin carries it around. When the air is thin, less oxygen crosses over with each breath.

Your body reacts within minutes. You breathe faster and deeper. Your heart speeds up to push blood around more quickly. Stairs feel like a hill. Leh, in Ladakh, sits about 3,500 metres up. Its airport is higher than any peak in the Western Ghats or the Appalachians. Tourists who fly in from Delhi are told to rest for a whole day before doing anything, and hotels often keep oxygen cylinders handy.

Altitude sickness

Going up too fast can make people ill. Acute mountain sickness can bring a headache, sickness, tiredness and trouble sleeping. It often starts above about 2,500 metres. Rarely, fluid can build up in the lungs or brain, which is a medical emergency.

The best treatment is simple: go back down. Climbers follow a rule of "climb high, sleep low", and try not to raise their sleeping height by more than about 300–500 metres a day once they are above 3,000 metres. Anyone feeling unwell in the mountains should tell an adult straight away.

Getting used to it

Over days and weeks, the body adjusts. This is called acclimatisation. The kidneys release a hormone that tells bone marrow to make more red blood cells, so the blood can carry more oxygen. Breathing settles into a new, faster pattern.

That is why athletes sometimes train at height, in places such as Ooty or the mountains of Colorado. Denver, in the USA, calls itself the Mile High City because it sits about 1,600 metres (one mile) up, and visiting sports teams often complain of puffing harder than usual.

People born to the heights

Some people have lived high up for thousands of years. Tibetans, Sherpas in Nepal, and people in the Andes of South America all cope with thin air better than most lowlanders. Their bodies have adapted in different ways. Many Tibetans have a version of a gene called EPAS1 that stops their blood from becoming too thick. Scientists traced it to the Denisovans, an extinct group of ancient humans.

Studies in the Andes found that children who grow up in the high Andes, around La Paz in Bolivia, tend to develop bigger chests and lungs than children raised by the sea.

Animals manage too. The bar-headed goose flies over the Himalaya on migration, and yaks have huge lungs and hearts.

The death zone

Above about 8,000 metres, the air is so thin that the body slowly runs down, however well it has adjusted. Climbers call it the death zone. Most people who climb Everest breathe bottled oxygen there. Tenzing Norgay and Edmund Hillary used it on the first ascent in 1953. In 1978, Reinhold Messner and Peter Habeler reached the summit without it, something many doctors had thought impossible.

Myth or fact?

Myth: There is less oxygen in mountain air because oxygen is heavy and sinks.

Truth: The mix of gases is almost the same at sea level and on a mountain top. What changes is the air pressure. Thinner air means fewer oxygen molecules in each breath, even though the percentage stays about 21%.

Try this

On a family trip up a hill road, or on a flight, ask an adult to bring a sealed packet of chips. Look at it at the bottom and again at the top. The packet puffs up because the air outside is pressing on it less hard than the air sealed inside.
