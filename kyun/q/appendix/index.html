What is the appendix for?

Charles Darwin thought the appendix was a useless leftover. Some scientists now think it may be a hideout for helpful gut bacteria.

The short answer

The appendix is a thin pouch, about the length of your little finger, attached to the beginning of your large intestine on the lower right side of your belly. For a long time, scientists thought it did nothing at all.

Today, many researchers think it has small but real jobs. It is packed with immune tissue, which helps the body learn about the microbes in the gut. Some scientists also suggest it acts as a safe store of helpful gut bacteria, ready to repopulate the intestine after an illness clears them out. Still, people who have their appendix removed live normal, healthy lives.

Surprising but true

Among mammals, an appendix seems to have evolved separately at least 30 times, which suggests it may give some advantage.

Darwin's leftover

In his 1871 book The Descent of Man, Charles Darwin argued that the human appendix was vestigial, meaning a shrunken leftover from an ancestor. He noticed that many plant-eating animals, such as rabbits, have a large pouch at the same spot, used to digest tough leaves. Perhaps, he thought, our ancestors needed that pouch and we no longer do.

The idea of leftover body parts became a famous piece of evidence for evolution. Your tailbone, a few small fused bones at the bottom of your spine, is often given as a human example: it is what remains of an ancestor's tail. Whales, for their part, carry tiny hip bones from ancestors that walked on land.

A safe house for bacteria?

In 2007, researchers at Duke University in the USA suggested a new idea. The appendix is narrow and tucked out of the main flow of food. If a bad stomach infection, like cholera, washes most bacteria out of the gut, the appendix might keep a store of helpful ones safe. Afterwards, they could spread out again and refill the intestine.

Supporters point to studies showing that the appendix holds a thick layer of bacteria. Other scientists say the evidence is still thin. The debate continues.

Another clue comes from comparing animals. A 2013 study found that an appendix has appeared separately in different groups of mammals at least 30 times, and has rarely disappeared once it evolved. Something that keeps evolving again and again may well be useful.

When the appendix gets sick

Appendicitis happens when the appendix becomes blocked, swollen and infected. It is most common in children and young adults. The usual signs are pain that starts near the belly button and moves to the lower right side, along with fever, sickness or not wanting to eat.

If someone has strong belly pain like this, it is important to tell a parent or trusted adult straight away, so they can get medical help. An infected appendix can burst, which is dangerous. In hospitals today, doctors usually remove it with a short operation, often through a few tiny cuts. In some mild cases, they treat it with antibiotics instead.

The first operations

In 1735, a surgeon in London named Claudius Amyand removed an inflamed appendix from an 11-year-old boy. It is often described as the first recorded successful appendix operation. For the next century and a half, though, appendicitis was poorly understood and often fatal.

In 1886, an American doctor, Reginald Fitz, described appendicitis clearly and gave it its name. He urged doctors to operate early. Thanks to good hospitals, anaesthesia and antibiotics, it is now usually a quick fix, and most patients go home within a few days.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Swallowing seeds, like guava or watermelon seeds, gives you appendicitis.

Truth: Seeds very rarely have anything to do with it. Appendicitis usually starts when the opening of the appendix gets blocked, often by a small lump of hardened stool or swollen tissue, and then becomes infected.
