Why does India want a digital ID for every farmer?

To get crop insurance or a farm loan, many farmers once had to carry the same land papers to office after office. A single digital ID is meant to do that job in seconds.

The short answer

A Farmer ID is a unique digital identity for each farmer in India. It connects the farmer's Aadhaar number to records of the land they hold and, over time, the crops they grow.

The idea is simple. When a farmer needs a loan, crop insurance or a government payment, officials can check the ID instead of asking for a pile of papers. By late 2026, India had created 11 crore of these IDs.

Surprising but true

India has about 14.6 crore farm holdings, and the average one is only about the size of one and a half football fields. That is why tracking who farms what is so hard.

How it works

The Farmer ID is one part of a bigger digital system called AgriStack. Think of it as three connected notebooks.

The first notebook is the farmer registry. It lists each farmer, their Aadhaar-linked identity and the plots of land in their name.

The second is a set of village maps. Each plot of land is drawn on a digital map with its exact location, so officials know where every field is.

The third is the crop record. Each season, surveyors visit fields, often with a phone app, and note what is growing.

Put the three together, and you know who is farming what, and where. It is a bit like a school register that shows not just each student's name, but their seat, their subjects and their marks, all in one place.

Banks, insurance companies and government offices can then use the ID, with the farmer's permission, to check details quickly. It can work alongside a biometric check, like a fingerprint, to confirm the farmer's identity.

What is Aadhaar?The first Aadhaar number went to a woman in a small village in Maharashtra in 2010. Today well over 130 crore people have one.

Where it came from

Keeping records of who farms which land is very old in India. In the 1500s, Emperor Akbar's finance minister, Raja Todar Mal, had fields measured to fix fair land taxes. Later, village record-keepers called patwaris kept handwritten books of land and crops.

Those books were hard to search and easy to get wrong. From the 1980s, states began typing land records into computers. In 2016, India launched digital payments like UPI, and the idea of building shared digital systems for everyone grew.

In September 2024, the government approved the Digital Agriculture Mission, with about ₹2,817 crore, to build AgriStack. The goal was 11 crore Farmer IDs by March 2027.

India's story

Farming feeds 140 crore people and employs nearly half of India's workers, from small family farms to large estates. But most Indian farms are small. According to the last full farm census, about 86% of holdings were smaller than 2 hectares.

Small farmers often depend on the monsoon and on government help. Schemes like PM-KISAN, which sends ₹6,000 a year to farming families, crop insurance and cheap loans through the Kisan Credit Card all need to know exactly who the farmer is.

In the past, a farmer might fill in the same details again and again for each scheme. With a Farmer ID, the government says help can reach the right person faster, with fewer middlemen. Many states held camps in villages so that farmers could sign up with help from officials.

The data can also help the whole country. Knowing which crops are sown, and where, makes it easier to plan buying at fixed prices and to spot shortages early.

The numbers that matter

  • - 11 crore: Farmer IDs created by late October 2026, five months before the deadline.
  • - More than 6 crore: farmers who the government says have already received benefits using the ID.
  • - About 14.6 crore: farm holdings counted in India, in the 2015-16 Agriculture Census. Holdings and farmers are not quite the same, because one farmer can hold more than one plot.
  • - About 86%: share of holdings smaller than 2 hectares, about the size of three football fields.
  • - About 14%: share of holdings run by women in the same census, even though women do a great deal of farm work.

What people argue about

Supporters say the ID cuts paperwork, reduces cheating and helps money reach farmers directly. It could also help with ideas like giving fertiliser subsidies based on the size of a farm.

Critics worry about who gets left out. Land records in many places are old or disputed. Tenant farmers, sharecroppers and many women farm land that is not in their name. If the system starts from ownership, they could miss out on help they need.

Others raise privacy. A single database that links identity, land and crops holds a lot of personal information. They say farmers must clearly agree to how it is used, and that strong rules must protect it. India's data protection law of 2023 sets some of these rules.

Myths and mix-ups

"The Farmer ID is the same as Aadhaar." No. Aadhaar proves who you are. The Farmer ID connects that identity to farming details like land and crops.

"Every farmer is now on the system." Not yet. The 11 crore figure is large, but tenant farmers and others without land records are still being added.

"It is only useful for the government." Farmers can also use it to get loans, insurance and quicker payments, if their details are correct.

What it means for you

Even if your family does not farm, farmers grow the rice, wheat, dal and vegetables you eat. When help reaches them on time, especially in a bad season of drought or flood, food supplies stay steadier.

If your family does farm, it is worth checking that your Farmer ID details, especially land and crop records, are correct. A mistake could delay a payment or an insurance claim.

And the idea behind it may spread. Today it is farmers. Tomorrow similar digital records could help fishers, weavers and other workers who have long found it hard to prove who they are and what they do.

India's farms have changed before. The Green Revolution of the 1960s brought new seeds, irrigation and bigger harvests. Today's change is quieter, made of data and phones, but it is part of the same story of technology reaching the fields. Whether it helps every farmer, including the smallest, is the real test.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A Farmer ID gives the government control of a farmer's land.

Truth: The ID does not change who owns the land. It is a record that connects a farmer to land records and to the crops they grow, so schemes and payments can reach the right person faster.

Try this

Draw a map of your street or a nearby park. Divide it into "plots", give each one a number, and write who looks after each. Now imagine some people swap or rent plots. How would you keep your map up to date? That is the problem land records try to solve.
