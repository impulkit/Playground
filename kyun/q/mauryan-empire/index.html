How did the Mauryan Empire unite India?

About 2,300 years ago, one family ruled from Afghanistan to Bengal, and a Greek ambassador wrote home about its capital.

The short answer

Around 321 BCE, a young man named Chandragupta Maurya overthrew the powerful Nanda kings of Magadha, in today's Bihar. According to tradition, he was guided by a sharp teacher called Chanakya.

Chandragupta then pushed west, taking land left behind by Alexander the Great's army. His son Bindusara and grandson Ashoka went further. At its biggest, the empire stretched from Afghanistan to Bengal and deep into the south. It held together through a strong army, a large staff of officials, spies, taxes and good roads. For the first time, most of the subcontinent answered to one ruler.

Surprising but true

When Chandragupta Maurya made peace with the Greek general Seleucus, he paid him 500 war elephants.

A deal with the Greeks

After Alexander died in 323 BCE, his generals split his empire. One of them, Seleucus, tried to win back land in the Indus region around 305 BCE. Instead, he made a treaty with Chandragupta.

Seleucus handed over large areas in the northwest. In return, Chandragupta sent him 500 war elephants, which Seleucus later used to win a major battle at home. The two sides also exchanged ambassadors. The Greek envoy Megasthenes lived at the Mauryan court and wrote a book called Indica. The book is lost, but other Greek and Roman writers quoted parts of it.

A capital of timber

Pataliputra stood where Patna is today, beside the Ganga. Megasthenes said it was enormous, with a wooden wall full of towers and gates, and a wide moat around it.

That sounds like a traveller's tall tale, yet excavations at Patna have uncovered lines of heavy wooden beams in the ground. At Kumhrar, archaeologists also found the remains of a great hall with polished stone pillars. Wood rots, so most of the city is gone. The fact that any of it survived in damp earth for over 2,000 years is lucky.

Running a giant empire

Ruling so much land took organisation. The empire was divided into provinces, often run by princes. Officials measured fields, collected taxes, managed mines and forests, and kept roads in repair. A great royal road linked the northwest to Pataliputra, along much the same line as the later Grand Trunk Road.

A Sanskrit book called the Arthashastra, linked by tradition to Chanakya, explains how a king should govern. It covers spies, prices, punishments, famine relief and war. Historians use it with care, because parts of it were probably written later.

A changed king and a lasting emblem

Chandragupta did not stay on the throne to old age. Jain tradition says he gave it up, became a monk and went south to Shravanabelagola in Karnataka.

The most famous Mauryan, Ashoka, turned away from war after a bloody conquest and spread messages carved on rocks and pillars. One of his pillars at Sarnath was topped by four lions sitting back to back. In 1950, India made that Lion Capital its national emblem. You can see it on every rupee note.

How it ended

After Ashoka died around 232 BCE, the empire shrank under weaker rulers. Around 185 BCE, the last Mauryan king, Brihadratha, was killed by his own general, Pushyamitra Shunga, who started a new dynasty.

The empire broke into smaller kingdoms again. But the idea that one government could hold together this huge and varied land had been tested, and it would return under the Guptas, the Mughals and modern India.

Myth or fact?

Myth: India was always one country, and the Mauryas simply ruled it.

Truth: Before the Mauryas, the subcontinent was split into many kingdoms and republics. The Mauryas were the first to bring most of it under one ruler, and even they never ruled the far south.
