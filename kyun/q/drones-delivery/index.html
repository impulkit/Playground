Will drones deliver our parcels?

In Rwanda, drones have been parachuting packets of blood to hospitals since 2016, faster than any ambulance on a winding mountain road.

The short answer

Some already do. Drones carry blood, medicines and vaccines to hard-to-reach places in Africa and Asia, and in parts of the United States they drop off takeaway food and small shopping orders. But they are still rare, and most parcels will keep coming by van, bike or post for some time.

The trouble is that drones can only carry a few kilograms before their batteries run flat. They also need good weather, clear rules about where they can fly, and neighbours who don't mind the buzzing. Drones are best where roads are slow and the cargo is light and urgent.

Surprising but true

Some delivery drones never land. They hover high above a garden or hospital and lower the parcel on a string, or drop it under a small paper parachute.

Blood from the sky

In 2016 a company called Zipline began flying drones in Rwanda, a hilly country in East Africa where roads twist through mountains. Hospitals running short of blood for an operation can order it by text message. A fixed-wing drone, shaped like a small plane, is launched from a catapult and flies to the hospital.

It doesn't land. It swoops low and drops a box under a paper parachute, then turns back for home. Since then, Zipline has expanded to Ghana, Nigeria, the United States and other countries.

Medicine over hills in India

India changed its drone rules in 2021 to make flying easier. In the same year, the Telangana government tested "Medicine from the Sky", flying vaccines and medicines to remote health centres. The Indian Council of Medical Research used drones to carry vaccines across hilly parts of Manipur and Nagaland.

Every drone flight in India is checked against the Digital Sky map. Green zones are open, yellow zones need permission, and red zones, such as near airports and borders, are off-limits without special approval. This is a small part of the country's air traffic system, which must keep drones away from planes and helicopters.

How a delivery drone finds you

Most delivery drones fly themselves. They use GPS to know where they are, plus cameras and other sensors to judge height and spot obstacles. A human operator may watch many drones at once on a screen, stepping in only if something goes wrong.

Some drones are quadcopters, with four spinning rotors, a bit like small helicopters. They can hover over a garden and lower a parcel on a cable. Fixed-wing drones fly further and faster on the same battery, but they can't hover, so they drop parcels on the move.

The weight problem

A drone must hold up its own weight and its cargo every second it is in the air. Heavier cargo drains the battery faster. That's why most delivery drones carry a payload of only about 1 to 3 kg, roughly a big bottle of water and a packet of biscuits.

Wind and rain make things worse. A strong monsoon gust can push a small drone off course, and water can damage its electronics. So many services pause in bad weather.

Will they fill the skies?

Companies in the United States, including Wing, owned by Google's parent company, and Amazon, have run home-delivery trials in towns in Texas, Virginia and California. Some neighbours like the speed. Others complain about noise or worry about privacy and cameras overhead.

Most experts think drones will grow fastest for urgent, light deliveries: a missing medicine, a lab sample, a spare part for a machine. A drone dropping off a whole week's groceries, flying past the windows of a crowded Mumbai or New York street, is still a long way off.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Drones can fly anywhere they like, as long as someone is controlling them.

Truth: In India, the USA and most countries, drones must follow strict rules. India's Digital Sky map marks red zones, such as near airports and borders, where flying is banned without special permission. Delivery companies need approvals for every route.

Try this

Make a mini parachute from a square of a plastic bag, four pieces of thread and a paper clip as the "parcel". Drop it from a safe height, such as standing on the floor with your arm raised, and time how long it takes to land. Try a bigger or smaller square and compare.
