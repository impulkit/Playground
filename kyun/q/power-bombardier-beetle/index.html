How does the bombardier beetle make a hot spray?

A beetle no longer than your fingernail can fire a spray of boiling-hot chemicals at an attacker, popping like a tiny machine gun hundreds of times a second.

The short answer

The bombardier beetle keeps two chemicals in a sac at the tip of its abdomen. They are hydroquinones and hydrogen peroxide, the same hydrogen peroxide used in some first-aid kits. On their own, they don't do much.

When an ant, frog or bird attacks, the beetle squeezes some of this mix into a second, armoured chamber full of enzymes. The enzymes set off a violent reaction. In an instant, the liquid heats up to around 100 °C, the boiling point of water, and blasts out of the beetle's rear with a pop. The spray is hot, smelly and stinging, and most attackers quickly let go.

Surprising but true

In a 2018 experiment in Japan, toads that swallowed bombardier beetles often threw them back up, and the beetles walked away alive, some after more than an hour inside.

A chemical gun

The beetle's weapon works like a tiny engine. In one species, scientists found it doesn't fire one big blast but about 500 small ones every second. Each little explosion pushes open a valve, lets out a puff of spray, and then the valve closes again, ready for the next.

That pulsing helps protect the beetle. Firing in short bursts gives the chamber walls a moment to cool between pops. In 2015, researchers used powerful X-ray cameras to watch the reaction happen inside living beetles.

The beetle can aim, too. Some can swivel the tip of the abdomen and spray forward, sideways or even over their own backs. Thomas Eisner, a famous American insect scientist, spent years filming beetles to show how well they aim.

The toad that throws up

The most surprising experiment came in 2018 in Japan. Scientists fed bombardier beetles to toads. The toads swallowed them whole, the way they eat most insects.

Inside, the beetles fired. Many toads threw them back up, sometimes after more than an hour. Most of the thrown-up beetles walked away alive, a little slimy but fine. It is one of very few known cases of a prey animal escaping by making its predator vomit.

Hot chemistry

The heat comes from the reaction. When the enzymes break down hydrogen peroxide, they release oxygen and lots of heat. The hydroquinones turn into benzoquinones, irritating chemicals that sting and smell. The fast release of gas pushes it all out.

Engineers have studied the beetle to design better fuel injectors and fire extinguishers, because it sprays an exact amount at high speed without blowing itself up. Some rocket fuels also use hydrogen peroxide, which is broken down quickly to make hot gas, a cousin of rocket fuel chemistry.

Beetles everywhere

There are hundreds of kinds of bombardier beetles, and they live on every continent except Antarctica. In India, some are brightly marked in yellow and black, a warning colour that tells predators to stay away. They usually hide under stones and leaves by day and hunt small insects at night.

Many hunters, from spiders to birds, have learned to leave them alone. Frogs and toads still try, which keeps the experiments going. The beetle's trick makes it one of the best defended small insects in the world.

A favourite puzzle

The bombardier beetle has long been a favourite example in debates about evolution, because its system seems so complex. Biologists have shown that simpler versions exist in related beetles, each step useful on its own, so a hot spray could have built up gradually. Its watery cousin in the sea, the pistol shrimp, also uses heat as a weapon, in a bubble that flashes for less than a blink.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The bombardier beetle's spray is so hot it could badly burn a person.

Truth: The spray is hot and stings, and it can stain skin brown, but the amount is tiny. It is a powerful weapon for ants, frogs and toads, not a serious danger to people. It is still best not to pick one up.

Try this

Fill a clean squeeze bottle with water. Try squirting it at a target in one long stream, then in many quick, short squeezes. Which goes farther and which is easier to aim? Bombardier beetles fire in rapid pulses rather than one long blast.
