What are some of India's folk music traditions?

In Maharashtra, volunteers have collected about one lakh songs that women sang while grinding grain by hand at dawn.

The short answer

Folk music is the music of everyday life: songs for sowing and harvest, weddings and births, festivals, work and worship. In India, almost every region, language and community has its own. Most of it was never written down. It passed from parent to child, and from village to village, by ear.

Well-known examples include the Baul songs of Bengal, the Bihu songs of Assam and Lavani from Maharashtra. There are also the desert ballads of Rajasthan's Manganiyar and Langa musicians, and the boat songs of Bengal's rivers. Each has its own instruments, often made from local materials such as gourds, bamboo, horn and goatskin. Many classical ragas are thought to have grown from folk tunes.

Surprising but true

Some Manganiyar musicians of Rajasthan can sing family histories going back many generations for the families who are their patrons.

Songs of the desert

In western Rajasthan, the Manganiyar and Langa communities have been professional musicians for centuries. Traditionally, each family of musicians served particular patron families. They sang at their births, weddings and festivals, and kept their family histories alive in song.

Their music is played on the kamaicha, a bowed instrument with a round body covered in goatskin. The dholak drum keeps time, along with the khartal, pairs of wooden blocks clicked in one hand like castanets. Some Manganiyar musicians now tour the world, playing in concert halls far from the sand dunes near Jaisalmer.

Songs of work

Many folk songs helped people through hard, repetitive work. In Maharashtra, women grinding grain on heavy stone hand mills before dawn sang ovi, short verses about family, gods, joy and sorrow. Researchers and volunteers spent decades recording them, and the People's Archive of Rural India is publishing a collection of more than one lakh of these grindmill songs.

In Bengal and Bangladesh, boatmen drifting on wide rivers sang bhatiali, slow, long-breathed songs that seem to follow the current. Farmers across India have planting songs, so that a whole row of workers moves together.

Festival songs

Rongali Bihu, Assam's spring festival, comes alive with Bihu songs and dances. Young people dance to the dhol drum and the pepa, a pipe made from a buffalo horn with a loud, wailing sound. The songs talk about spring, love and the new year.

Lavani, from Maharashtra, is a fast, powerful song-and-dance form with a quick beat on the dholki drum. Punjab has the tumbi, a one-string instrument at the heart of bhangra music. In the south, villages sing devotional songs and ballads at temple festivals that can go on all night.

Songs of the spirit

Some folk music is a path to the divine. The Bauls of Bengal wander from village to village singing about the god within each person, playing the one-string ektara. In Chhattisgarh, Pandavani singers act out stories from the Mahabharata, holding a decorated tambura and changing voices for each character. The singer Teejan Bai made this form famous around the world.

In Rajasthan, Bhopa singers perform the epic of the folk hero Pabuji at night, in front of a long painted scroll called a phad, lit by a lamp. They point to each scene as the story unfolds.

Folk music and the wider world

Folk traditions travel and mix. American music has its own folk roots, from the work songs that grew into the blues to the ballads behind country music. In India, film composers have borrowed folk tunes for decades. Many folk musicians worry that young people in their own villages are drifting towards film and pop songs. Recordings, festivals and new audiences abroad are helping some of these traditions find fresh listeners.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Folk music is simple music that anyone can play without training.

Truth: Many folk musicians train for years, often from early childhood, learning from parents and grandparents. Manganiyar and Langa singers, for example, master long songs, complex rhythms and difficult instruments.

Try this

Ask an older relative or neighbour to sing you a song they learned as a child, such as a lullaby, a wedding song or a festival song. Record it on a phone with their permission, and ask where and when it was sung. You will be doing what folk song collectors do.
