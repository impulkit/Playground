What is Narnia?

It began with a picture that popped into a 16-year-old's head, of a faun carrying an umbrella and parcels through a snowy wood.

The short answer

Narnia is a magical land in a series of seven children's books by C. S. Lewis, called The Chronicles of Narnia, published between 1950 and 1956. It is a world of talking animals, fauns, giants, dwarfs and witches, ruled over by a great lion named Aslan, whose name is simply the Turkish word for lion.

In each book, children from our world find their way into Narnia, most famously through the back of a wardrobe. They have adventures, fight evil and grow braver, then return home, often finding that almost no time has passed. The books have sold more than 100 million copies and been turned into radio plays, TV series and films. For many readers, Narnia is the first fantasy world they ever fall in love with.

Surprising but true

Real child evacuees from London stayed at C. S. Lewis's house near Oxford during the Second World War, and he made the four Pevensie children wartime evacuees too.

A faun in the snow

Clive Staples Lewis was born in 1898 in Belfast, in what is now Northern Ireland, part of the UK. His family called him Jack. As a boy he and his brother invented an imaginary country of talking animals, which they wrote about and drew.

Lewis later wrote that Narnia started with a picture he had carried in his head since he was about 16: a faun carrying an umbrella and parcels through a snowy wood. Many years later, when he was around fifty, he decided to write a story around it. That faun became Mr Tumnus, the first creature Lucy meets in Narnia.

Evacuees and a wardrobe

During the Second World War, the British government moved many children out of London to protect them from bombing. Some evacuees came to stay at The Kilns, Lewis's house near Oxford.

In The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe, published in 1950, the four Pevensie children, Peter, Susan, Edmund and Lucy, are sent away from London for the same reason. Exploring the big old house, Lucy steps into a wardrobe full of fur coats and comes out in a snowy forest lit by a lamp-post.

Myths from everywhere

Narnia mixes creatures from many traditions. Fauns, centaurs and the god Bacchus come from Greek and Roman myths. Dwarfs and giants come from northern European tales. Father Christmas appears in the first book.

Lewis's friend J.R.R. Tolkien did not like this mix. He thought a made-up world should hang together more carefully. Lewis didn't mind.

Lewis was a Christian, and many readers see Aslan as a picture of Jesus, especially when he gives his life for Edmund and then returns. Lewis said he did not intend a simple allegory, where each character stands for something else. He described it as imagining what Christ might be like in a world like Narnia. Some later writers, such as Philip Pullman, have criticised the books for their religious message and for how they treat Susan at the end of the series.

Which book first?

The books were published in one order and happen in another. The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe came out first, but The Magician's Nephew, published in 1955, tells how Narnia was created, so it comes first in Narnia's own history.

Many modern editions number the books in story order. Others argue that new readers should meet Narnia through the wardrobe, as the first readers did. In a 1957 letter to a young American fan, Lewis said he thought story order was probably fine, but that it didn't much matter. Readers have been arguing about it ever since.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In the Narnia books, the wardrobe is the only way into Narnia.

Truth: The wardrobe is used in one book. In others, children get in through magic rings, a painting of a ship, a railway station platform, and a door in a school wall.
