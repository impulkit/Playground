What is the Booker Prize?

Every autumn, writers across the world wait for one envelope to be opened in London. Winning can turn a quiet novel into a worldwide bestseller.

The short answer

The Booker Prize is one of the most famous awards for a novel written in English. It began in 1969, and the winner now gets £50,000. Each year a panel of five judges reads more than a hundred and fifty novels. They pick a longlist of about 13, cut it to a shortlist of six, and name one winner in the autumn.

For a writer, the money is the small part. A Booker winner gets reviews, translations and huge sales. Some winning books end up on school reading lists around the world.

The judges are usually a mix of novelists, critics, academics and sometimes an actor or musician. They meet many times over the summer. Each judge must read every book submitted, which can mean three or four novels a week for months.

Surprising but true

In 2019 the judges broke the prize's own rules and gave it to two writers at once, Margaret Atwood and Bernardine Evaristo.

A prize named after a food company

The name comes from Booker McConnell, a British company that traded in sugar and food. It paid for the prize at the start. From 2002 to 2019 an investment firm called the Man Group sponsored it, so it was called the Man Booker. Now it is simply the Booker again.

For many years only writers from the UK, Ireland and the Commonwealth could enter. In 2014 the rules opened up to any novel in English published in Britain or Ireland. Two years later, Paul Beatty became the first American to win, for The Sellout. Some British writers worried that big American publishers would crowd out everyone else. Others said a prize for English writing should welcome English from anywhere.

India and the Booker

Indian writers have done well. Salman Rushdie won in 1981 for Midnight's Children, a story about children born at the stroke of India's independence. It was later voted the best of all Booker winners, in 1993 and again in 2008. Arundhati Roy won in 1997 for The God of Small Things, set in Kerala. Kiran Desai won in 2006, and Aravind Adiga in 2008 with The White Tiger.

Kiran's mother, Anita Desai, was shortlisted three times without winning. Many readers think she is one of the best Indian writers in English never to take the prize.

The International Booker

A sister award, the International Booker Prize, is given to a book translated into English. The prize money is shared equally between the author and the translator, which shows how much skill translation takes.

In 2022 Geetanjali Shree's Hindi novel Ret Samadhi won as Tomb of Sand, translated by the American Daisy Rockwell. In 2025 the Kannada writer Banu Mushtaq won for Heart Lamp, a collection of short stories translated by Deepa Bhasthi. It was the first story collection to win.

Arguments every year

Book prizes always cause arguments, and the Booker enjoys them. Readers grumble that a favourite was left out. In 2019 the judges refused to choose between two books and split the prize between Margaret Atwood and Bernardine Evaristo, even though the rules said they should not.

A few writers have won twice, including Hilary Mantel, J. M. Coetzee and Peter Carey. Nobody has won three times yet. Mantel's two winners were both about Thomas Cromwell, an adviser to King Henry VIII, which made her the first to win with a book and its sequel. The judges change every year, so nobody can be sure what the next panel will love. That uncertainty is part of the fun of watching from London or from Lucknow.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Booker Prize is only for British writers.

Truth: It started as a prize for writers from the UK, Ireland and the Commonwealth, which included India. Since 2014 any novel written in English can win, if it is published in the UK or Ireland.

Try this

Hold a Booker in your class. Each person suggests one favourite book, then the group makes a "longlist" of six and a "shortlist" of three. Everyone reads a page from each finalist aloud, and you vote by secret ballot.
