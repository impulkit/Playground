What are tangrams?

Seven flat pieces cut from one square can make a cat, a running man, a boat and thousands of other shapes.

The short answer

A tangram is a puzzle made from a square cut into seven pieces, called tans. There are two large triangles, one medium triangle, two small triangles, one square and one parallelogram, a slanted rectangle.

The game is to use all seven pieces, laid flat and not overlapping, to make a shape. Usually you are shown only the outline, a solid silhouette of a cat, a teapot or a dancing person, and you have to work out where each piece goes. The puzzle comes from China, where it is called qiqiaoban, "seven boards of skill". It spread around the world about 200 years ago and is still used in classrooms to teach shapes.

Surprising but true

In 1942 two mathematicians in China proved that the seven tangram pieces can make exactly 13 convex shapes, shapes with no dents in their outline.

A square in seven pieces

The pieces fit together in a neat way. Take the small triangle as one unit. The square, the parallelogram and the medium triangle each cover two units. Each large triangle covers four. All seven together make 16.

The angles fit too. Every corner on every piece is 45, 90 or 135 degrees. That is why the pieces slide together so neatly, and why so many shapes can be made. People have published thousands of tangram figures, and you can always invent new ones.

From China to the world

The earliest books of tangram puzzles we know of were printed in China around 1813. The game probably appeared there not long before, in the late 1700s.

Sailors and traders soon carried it abroad. Around 1817, tangrams became a craze in Europe and the United States. Books of puzzles sold quickly, and people played at parties. A story says that Napoleon, sent into exile on the island of St Helena, passed the time with a tangram set. Historians aren't sure it's true, but sets from that period do survive.

The puzzle may have grown out of older Chinese ideas, such as sets of banquet tables that could be pushed together in different patterns. In China itself, tangram books kept being printed all through the 1800s.

The great Tan hoax

In 1903 Sam Loyd, a famous American puzzle maker, published a book about tangrams. In it he claimed the puzzle was 4,000 years old, invented by a god named Tan, and that ancient books described seven sets of a thousand puzzles each.

None of it was true. Loyd was known for playful tricks, and he probably expected readers to see the joke. Many didn't. His story was repeated in books and newspapers for decades. Even now, you may read that tangrams are thousands of years old. The real story is younger, though no less interesting.

Tangram puzzles that seem impossible

Some tangram pictures look like magic. The English puzzle writer Henry Dudeney showed two figures of a man that look almost exactly the same. Both use all seven pieces. Yet one man has a foot and the other doesn't.

The trick is that the bodies are slightly different shapes. The area of the missing foot has been spread around the rest of the figure, so thinly you hardly notice. This kind of puzzle is a visual paradox, and it teaches you to check the area instead of trusting your eyes.

Thirteen shapes and no more

A convex shape is one with no dents. A rubber band stretched around it would touch every edge. In 1942, two mathematicians from China, Wang Fu Traing and Hsiung Chuan-Chih, asked how many convex shapes the seven tans can make. They proved the answer is exactly 13. These include a square, a rectangle, a triangle and several kinds of five- and six-sided shapes.

Tangrams are now used in schools from Delhi to Detroit. They help children learn about area, angles and fractions with their hands. And for people of any age, they offer a quiet challenge: seven pieces, one outline, no peeking at the answer.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Tangrams are 4,000 years old and were invented by a Chinese man named Tan.

Truth: This story was made up as a joke by the American puzzle writer Sam Loyd in 1903. The earliest known tangram books are from China around 1813, and the puzzle is probably not much older than the late 1700s.

Try this

Make your own tangram from a square of paper. Fold it corner to corner and tear along the fold, then keep folding and tearing to get the seven pieces (look up a tangram diagram to guide you). Try to make a cat, a house and a boat. Then put the square back together, which is harder than it sounds.
