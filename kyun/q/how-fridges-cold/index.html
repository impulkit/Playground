How does a fridge stay cold?

A fridge doesn't make cold. It pumps heat out of its box and dumps it into your kitchen, which is why its back feels warm.

The short answer

A fridge works by moving heat, not by making cold. Inside its pipes is a special liquid called a refrigerant, which boils at very low temperatures. As it boils inside the fridge, it soaks up heat from your food and the air around it.

A pump called a compressor then squeezes the refrigerant gas, making it hot. The hot gas flows through coils on the back or bottom of the fridge, where it gives its heat to the kitchen and turns back into a liquid. Then it flows back inside to boil again.

Round and round it goes, carrying heat out of the box all day and night. That is why the back of a fridge feels warm.

Surprising but true

Albert Einstein and his former student Leo Szilard patented a fridge design in 1930 that had no moving parts.

The cooling trick you already know

When you climb out of a swimming pool on a windy day, you shiver. As water on your skin evaporates, it carries heat away with it. Sweat cools you the same way. Turning a liquid into a gas always needs heat, and it takes that heat from whatever is nearby.

A clay matka uses this idea to keep drinking water cool. Water seeps through tiny pores and evaporates from the outside, chilling the rest, as explained in how a clay pot cools water.

A fridge does this in a closed loop. Its refrigerant evaporates in pipes inside the fridge, pulling heat from the food. Then the compressor squashes it back into a liquid outside, where the heat escapes. No liquid is lost, so it can keep going for years.

The four main parts

Follow the refrigerant on one trip around:

  • 1. The evaporator is a set of cold pipes inside the fridge. Here the refrigerant boils and soaks up heat.
  • 2. The compressor, the humming motor near the bottom, squeezes the warm gas so it gets hotter still.
  • 3. The condenser coils on the back release that heat into the kitchen. The gas cools and becomes a liquid again.
  • 4. The expansion valve is a narrow gap. The liquid sprays through, its pressure drops suddenly, and it becomes very cold, ready to start again.

A thermostat switches the compressor on and off to keep the inside at about 4°C. The freezer is kept near –18°C.

Before fridges

For thousands of years, people kept food cool with cellars, clay pots, snow and salt. In ancient Persia, people built tall domed ice houses called yakhchals, which kept ice into the hot summer.

In the 1800s, ice became a business. In 1833, a ship from Boston carrying ice cut from frozen New England lakes reached Calcutta, now Kolkata, after a voyage of about four months. Much of it melted on the way, but enough survived to sell. Ice houses were soon built in Indian port cities.

Jacob Perkins, an American living in Britain, patented a machine in 1834 that used the compression idea fridges still use. Home fridges spread across the US in the 1920s and 1930s, and across India much later.

How is food frozen?Modern frozen food began when an American naturalist watched Inuit fishers in icy Labrador pull fish from the water and saw them freeze solid almost at once.

Einstein's fridge and the ozone hole

In 1930, Albert Einstein and the physicist Leo Szilard patented a fridge with no moving parts. They had read about a family harmed by gas leaking from a faulty fridge seal. Their design was never widely used, but it shows how many clever minds have worked on cooling.

Many early fridges used dangerous gases. In the 1930s, chemicals called CFCs replaced them because they were safe to breathe. Decades later, scientists found that CFCs drifting into the sky were destroying the ozone layer, which shields us from harmful sunlight.

In 1987, countries agreed to the Montreal Protocol, phasing out CFCs. Every member of the United Nations has since joined it. Today many fridges use other refrigerants, including isobutane.

India has fridges that need no electricity at all. In Gujarat, a potter named Mansukhbhai Prajapati designed the Mitticool, a clay fridge cooled by evaporating water. And the strangest part? An air conditioner is basically a fridge turned inside out, cooling your room instead of a box, as you can find in how air conditioning works.

Myth or fact?

Myth: An open fridge can cool down a hot room.

Truth: A fridge only moves heat from inside to the coils outside. With the door open, it moves heat around the same room while its motor adds extra heat. The room gets warmer, not cooler.

Try this

Wet one wrist and leave the other dry, then gently blow on both. The wet one feels colder because evaporating water carries heat away. A fridge uses the same trick with a special liquid, over and over again in a loop.
