Why is India so good at badminton?

India's Satwiksairaj Rankireddy once smashed a shuttlecock at 565 km/h, faster than a racing car's top speed, and Guinness World Records measured it.

The short answer

India has become one of the strongest badminton countries in the world in about 25 years. P.V. Sindhu has won two Olympic medals and a world title. Saina Nehwal reached world number one. In 2022, India's men won the Thomas Cup, the world team championship, for the first time.

There is no single reason. Most people point to a few things working together. Champions inspired children to pick up racquets. Strong academies, especially in Hyderabad, trained them. And badminton was already a game Indians loved, played on rooftops, in lanes and at family weddings long before anyone dreamed of medals.

Surprising but true

Some of the earliest written rules of modern badminton were drawn up in Pune in the 1870s, and for a while British players in India called the game "Poona".

An old Indian connection

Games of hitting a shuttle back and forth are ancient, in India, China, Greece and elsewhere. The modern game took shape in British India in the 1800s. Army officers in Pune, then called Poona, played it and wrote down rules in the 1870s.

The game took its name from Badminton House, an estate in England where it became a fashionable pastime. Through the 20th century, India produced good players, but world titles stayed with China, Indonesia, Denmark and Malaysia.

The breakthrough came in 1980. Prakash Padukone, from Bengaluru, won the All England Championships, then the most famous tournament in the sport. Twenty-one years later, his countryman Pullela Gopichand won it too.

The Gopichand effect

After he retired, Gopichand wanted Indian players to have world-class training at home. In 2008 he opened an academy in Hyderabad. Reports say he mortgaged his family home to help pay for it.

The academy produced champions quickly. Saina Nehwal won bronze at the 2012 London Olympics, India's first Olympic medal in badminton. Four years later, Sindhu won silver in Rio. In 2019 she became world champion, beating Nozomi Okuhara of Japan in the final, and she added an Olympic bronze in Tokyo in 2021.

Their success did something money alone can't. Millions of children watched someone who looked like them beat the best in the world.

Who is PV Sindhu?As a girl she travelled about 56 kilometres a day to reach badminton practice. She became the first Indian to be crowned world badminton champion.

A game anyone can play

Badminton needs very little: two racquets, a shuttle and some space. A string tied between two poles works as a net. That makes it one of the easiest sports to start in India's crowded cities, where few children have access to a cricket ground or tennis court.

A shuttlecock is a small marvel. It has 16 goose or duck feathers stuck into a cork base and weighs about 5 g. Its shape makes it fly fast off the racquet, then slow down sharply and flip to fall cork first. Players must judge that sudden slowing every single time.

At the top level, badminton is brutal on the body. A long match can have players lunging, jumping and sprinting short bursts for over an hour. Indian coaches now work with fitness trainers, physiotherapists and video analysts, the way cricket teams do. That backroom support, paid for partly by government schemes and private sponsors since the 2010s, is another reason India's players can now last through a tough tournament week.

New stars and a home Worlds

India's next wave came in doubles and men's singles. Satwiksairaj Rankireddy and Chirag Shetty won Asian Games gold in 2023 and reached world number one. They won bronze at the World Championships in 2022 and again in 2025. Lakshya Sen finished fourth at the 2024 Paris Olympics, just missing a medal.

In August 2026, New Delhi hosted the BWF World Championships, the first time India had held the event since 2009. Indian badminton is no longer about one hero. Strong players come from Uttarakhand, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Assam and beyond, and children copy their smashes in lanes across the country.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Badminton is a gentle garden game, slower than tennis.

Truth: In top matches the shuttle leaves the racquet faster than any tennis ball, and players change direction hundreds of times a match. Its feathers make it slow down quickly, which is why it looks gentle on TV.

Try this

Drop a shuttlecock and a small ball from the same height at the same time. Watch which lands first and how the shuttle always turns to fall cork-first. Then try throwing the shuttle hard and see how quickly it slows down in the air.
