Why do names have meanings?

Almost every name was once an ordinary word. Aditya means sun, Smith meant a metal worker, and Johnson meant "son of John".

The short answer

Names have meanings because almost all of them began as ordinary words. Parents chose words for qualities they hoped their child would have, for gods and heroes, for things in nature, or for the day the child was born. Over centuries, many names travelled far from their original language, and people forgot what they meant.

In India, many first names come from Sanskrit, Persian, Arabic or Tamil words. Aditya means sun, Priya means beloved and Noor means light. English names often hide old meanings too. Peter comes from a Greek word for rock, and Sophia means wisdom.

Surprising but true

In Iceland, most people have no family surname. A child's last name usually comes from a parent's first name.

Surnames tell old stories

Family names, or surnames, came later than first names in many places. In England, they became fixed in families from about the 1300s, as villages grew and there were too many Johns to tell apart. People were named by their job, like Smith (a metal worker), Baker or Taylor, by a place, like Hill or Brook, or by their father, like Johnson or Wilson.

In India, surnames can come from a village, a family trade, a title, a clan or a community. Many Marathi and Konkani surnames end in -kar, which links a family to a place, so the Mangeshkar family traces its roots to Mangeshi in Goa. Some Indians use no surname at all.

Fathers, mothers and initials

Some cultures build names from parents' first names. In Iceland, a man named Jón whose father is Einar becomes Jón Einarsson, "Einar's son", and his sister might be Guðrún Einarsdóttir, "Einar's daughter". The Icelandic phone book lists people by first name.

In Tamil Nadu, many people put their father's first name as an initial before their own name, as in R. Lakshmi. The mathematician Srinivasa Ramanujan carried his father's name, Srinivasa, in front of his own. In Russia, a middle name comes from your father, so Ivan's son Pyotr is Pyotr Ivanovich.

Do families look the same everywhere?Among the Khasi people of Meghalaya, children take their mother's family name, and the youngest daughter traditionally looks after the family home.

Choosing a name

Many families mark a child's naming with a ceremony. In Hindu families, the namakarana is often held about 11 or 12 days after birth, and some families choose a first letter linked to the child's birth star. In many Muslim families, a baby's name is announced at the aqiqah, often on the seventh day. Some Sikh families open the Guru Granth Sahib at random and choose a name beginning with the first letter of the hymn on that page.

In the USA, the government keeps records of baby names from birth registrations, so trends are easy to track. Liam and Olivia have topped the lists in recent years. Names that were popular around 1950, like Linda and Gary, are now rare for babies, though plenty of grandparents still carry them.

Names that change

Names change as they travel. The Hebrew name Yohanan, "God is gracious", became John in English, Juan in Spanish, Ivan in Russian, Jean in French and Yahya in Arabic. One name, five languages, and most people who carry it never realise they share it. Some Indian names have travelled the other way: Maya, for example, is now a popular name for babies in the USA.

Places get renamed as well. In 1995, Bombay officially became Mumbai, after the goddess Mumbadevi, and in 2014 Bangalore officially became Bengaluru. Every name, of a city or a child, carries a small piece of history that people repeat every day without thinking about it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Everyone in the world has a first name and a family surname.

Truth: Naming customs vary hugely. Many Icelanders use a parent's name instead of a surname, many people in Tamil Nadu use an initial from their father's name, and some people in India and Indonesia use only one name.
