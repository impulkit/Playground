Where are lentils eaten around the world?

The country that grows the most lentils is Canada, and much of that harvest is shipped across the world to Indian kitchens.

The short answer

Lentils are eaten almost everywhere, but they matter most in South Asia, the Middle East, North Africa and southern Europe. They are small, lens-shaped seeds from a bushy plant in the pea family. In fact, the glass lens got its name from the lentil, because early lenses had the same shape.

India eats more lentils than any other country. Red masoor dal simmers in kitchens from Kolkata to Lahore. In Lebanon, people eat lentils with rice and crispy onions. In Ethiopia, red lentils become a spicy stew. In France and Italy, small green lentils go into soups and salads. And Canada, which grows the most, eats only a small part of its harvest.

Surprising but true

In Italy, many families eat lentils on New Year's Eve, because the little round seeds look like coins and are said to bring money in the year ahead.

One of the first crops

Lentils were among the very first plants people farmed. Seeds found at early farming villages in the Middle East show that lentils were being grown around 9,000 to 10,000 years ago, alongside wheat, barley, peas and chickpeas. The crop spread to Egypt, Greece, India and beyond.

Lentils show up in old stories too. In the Bible, Esau sells his birthright to his brother Jacob for a bowl of red lentil stew. Lentils have also been found in ancient Egyptian tombs, buried with food for the afterlife.

The Canada surprise

Today the biggest lentil grower is Canada, mostly on the wide, flat prairies of Saskatchewan. Canadian farmers only began growing lentils seriously in the 1970s. The crop handles cold winters and dry summers well, and it needs little fertiliser.

India grows a lot of lentils too, mostly in Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh. But it eats even more, so it buys large amounts from Canada and Australia. When India changes its import rules, lentil farmers in Saskatchewan pay close attention. In the United States, lentils grow in the Palouse region of Washington and Idaho, where the town of Pullman holds a yearly lentil festival.

How did farming begin?The ancestor of corn was a skinny wild grass with a few rock-hard seeds. It took thousands of years of farmers' choices to turn it into a cob.

A plant that feeds the soil

Lentils belong to the legume family, like peas and beans. Their roots carry tiny lumps filled with bacteria that can take nitrogen from the air and turn it into a form plants can use. When the plants die back, some of that nitrogen stays behind, helping the next crop.

That is why farmers often grow lentils in turns with wheat. It makes the soil richer without as much fertiliser.

Lentils on the world's plates

In India and Pakistan, masoor dal is cooked with turmeric, then finished with a tadka of hot ghee or oil, cumin, garlic and chilli. In the Middle East, mujaddara mixes lentils, rice and deeply browned onions. Egypt's famous street food koshari piles lentils, rice, pasta and tomato sauce into one bowl.

In Ethiopia, misir wot is a red lentil stew flavoured with a spice blend called berbere, eaten with spongy injera bread. France grows small green lentils around Le Puy, a variety protected by law, a bit like Darjeeling tea. In Italy, lentils with sausage are a New Year's Eve tradition, eaten for luck and wealth.

Small seed, big nutrition

About a quarter of a dry lentil's weight is protein, which is why lentils are so important in vegetarian diets. They also have fibre, iron and folate. Dal and rice together give a good mix of the building blocks the body needs.

Unlike kidney beans or chickpeas, lentils don't need soaking and cook in about 20 to 30 minutes. The UN named 2016 the International Year of Pulses to encourage people to eat more of them. They are cheap, they keep for months in a jar, and they don't need much water to grow.

Myth or fact?

Myth: All dal is made from lentils.

Truth: In India, dal means split pulses of many kinds, including pigeon peas (toor), mung beans (moong), black gram (urad) and chickpeas (chana). Only masoor dal comes from the lentil plant.

Try this

Put a few whole lentils or moong on damp cotton wool in a saucer and keep it near a window. Add a few drops of water each day. Within two or three days, tiny roots should poke out. Watch how fast they grow over a week, and draw them each day.
