How did the circus begin?

The modern circus was started in London in 1768 by a former soldier who discovered he could stand on a galloping horse if it ran in a circle.

The short answer

The modern circus began in London in 1768. Philip Astley, a former cavalry soldier, performed riding tricks in a ring in a field near the River Thames. He soon added acrobats, jugglers, tightrope walkers and a clown to fill the gaps between horse acts.

The idea spread across Europe, to America and to India. Travelling circuses put up big tents, called big tops, in town after town. For more than a century, the circus was one of the most popular shows on Earth.

Surprising but true

The tight one-piece outfit called a leotard is named after Jules Léotard, a French acrobat who performed the first flying trapeze act in 1859.

A soldier and a ring

Astley was a skilled horseman. He noticed that if a horse galloped in a circle of the right size, it leaned inwards and the rider could stand up on its back without falling. The best size turned out to be about 13 metres (42 feet) across. Rings around the world are still close to that today.

A ring also had another advantage. Unlike a stage, everyone sitting around it could see the act clearly. Astley built a wooden building called Astley's Amphitheatre, and audiences came in thousands. He later took his show to Paris and other European cities, and copycat circuses sprang up wherever he went.

Flying through the air

In 1859, the French acrobat Jules Léotard swung from one trapeze bar to another in Paris, high above the audience. It was the first flying trapeze act. He wore a tight, stretchy costume so it would not catch on the bars, and the outfit is still called a leotard.

Trapeze artists use the physics of a swing. At the top of each swing they slow down, and that's when they let go. Then gravity pulls them along a curved path towards their partner's hands. A net or safety line is usually waiting below.

The American big top

In the United States, circuses travelled by train, carrying tents, animals and hundreds of workers. The showman P. T. Barnum and the Ringling brothers created some of the largest. In 1919 they merged into Ringling Bros. and Barnum & Bailey, known as "The Greatest Show on Earth".

These circuses featured elephants, lions, tigers and horses. Over time, many people became worried about how the animals were trained and kept. Ringling Bros. stopped using elephants in 2016 and closed in 2017. It returned in 2023 with human performers only.

Circus in India

India's modern circus history goes back to the 1880s. Vishnupant Chatre, a horse rider, founded the Great Indian Circus in 1880. In Kerala, a gymnastics teacher named Keeleri Kunhikannan began training circus performers in Thalassery around 1901. So many stars came from there that Thalassery is called the cradle of Indian circus.

Indian circuses such as Gemini and Jumbo toured the country for decades. In 1998 the Government of India banned the training and display of lions, tigers, bears, monkeys and panthers as performing animals. Many zoos and rescue centres took in former circus animals.

Circus without animals

In 1984, a group of street performers in Quebec, Canada, started Cirque du Soleil. It had no animals at all. Instead, it used acrobats, music, costumes and lighting to tell a story, more like theatre.

Today many circuses work this way. Circus schools teach juggling, aerial silks, balancing and clowning, and some children learn circus skills simply for fun. The old ring is still there, about 13 metres across, just as Astley measured it.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The word "circus" comes from the ancient Roman circus, so circuses have been around since Roman times.

Truth: The Roman Circus Maximus was a stadium for chariot races, not acrobats and clowns. The modern circus began in the 1700s and borrowed the old Latin word, which means circle.
