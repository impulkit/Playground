How is a car built?

A big car factory can finish a new car about every minute, built from roughly 30,000 parts that arrive from hundreds of suppliers.

The short answer

A car begins as giant rolls of steel, some weighing as much as several elephants. Machines press the steel into doors, roofs and floors. Robots weld these panels into a shell, which is painted, and then workers fit the engine, wheels, seats, wires and glass. At the end, every car is driven, tested and sprayed with water to check for leaks.

The whole job happens on an assembly line, where the car moves past stations and each team does one task. A large plant can finish a new car roughly every minute, all day.

Surprising but true

All the paint on a car, layer after layer, usually adds up to about a tenth of a millimetre, roughly the thickness of a sheet of paper.

Squashing steel into shapes

The first stop is the press shop. Steel unrolls from coils, is cut into flat sheets and fed into presses as tall as a two-storey house. A press slams down with a force of hundreds or even thousands of tonnes and stamps a sheet into the curved shape of a bonnet or door in a second or two.

It is loud. Workers wear ear protection, and the floor shakes with each stroke.

Robots and sparks

In the body shop, the pressed panels meet. Hundreds of robot arms grip, turn and weld them, with sparks flying in showers. A single car body is held together by several thousand spot welds, small points where metal is melted together by an electric current.

The result is a bare metal shell with no paint, engine or seats. Car makers call it the "body in white", probably because early car bodies were primed in a pale colour.

A bath, then paint

Dust ruins paint, so the paint shop is one of the cleanest places in the factory. First, the whole body is dipped into a tank of liquid and charged with electricity, so a protective coat sticks to every corner, inside and out. This stops rust.

Robot arms then spray primer, colour and a clear top coat. All those layers together are usually about a tenth of a millimetre thick, roughly the thickness of a sheet of paper. The body bakes in ovens between coats, like a cake.

What was the Industrial Revolution?About 250 years ago, machines began doing work that hands had done for thousands of years, and the way people lived changed faster than ever before.

Putting it all together

Final assembly is where people take over. The painted body moves slowly along the line while workers fit wiring harnesses, the bundles of wire that work like a car's nervous system. Then come the dashboard, seats, glass, lights and wheels.

The big moment is called "the marriage". The body is lowered on to the engine and axles from below, and bolted together. For an electric car, the marriage joins the body to a flat battery pack under the floor.

Parts arrive from hundreds of suppliers, sometimes only hours before they are needed. If one lorry of seats is late, the whole line can stop.

The line that changed the world

In 1913, Henry Ford's factory near Detroit in the USA set up a moving assembly line for the Model T. Instead of workers walking around a car, the car came to them on a chain. Building a chassis dropped from about 12 hours to about an hour and a half. Cars became cheap enough for ordinary families, and Ford made about 15 million Model Ts.

Before that, the first cars were mostly built by hand, one at a time, as you can see in the story of early cars.

India's car factories

India sold a record of about 45 lakh passenger vehicles in 2025, making it one of the biggest car markets on Earth. Factories in Gurugram, Pune, Chennai and Sanand build cars for India and for ships to Africa, the Middle East and beyond. Chennai is sometimes called the Detroit of India.

Every new design is also crashed on purpose. Test cars smash into barriers carrying dummies packed with sensors, which record forces no person could safely feel. Yet the hardest-working part of most cars stays hidden under the bonnet, where a petrol engine sets off thousands of tiny explosions every minute.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Cars today are built entirely by robots, with no people in the factory.

Truth: Robots do most of the welding and painting, which are heavy, hot or messy jobs. People still do much of the final assembly, fitting wires, seats and dashboards, because those tasks need careful hands.
