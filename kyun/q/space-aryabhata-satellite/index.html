What was India's first satellite?

India's first satellite was built in sheds in Bengaluru, launched from the Soviet Union in 1975, and later printed on the two-rupee note.

The short answer

India's first satellite was called Aryabhata. It went into orbit on 19 April 1975, carried by a Soviet rocket from Kapustin Yar, a launch site in what is now Russia. It was built by Indian scientists and engineers at a small new facility in Peenya, on the edge of Bengaluru.

It weighed 360 kg, about as much as four grown men, and had 26 sides, most of them covered in solar cells. Its job was to study X-rays from space, the Sun and the upper atmosphere. Its three experiments came from Indian research labs, so every reading it sent down was new science done by Indians.

It was named after Aryabhata, the great Indian mathematician and astronomer who lived about 1,500 years ago.

Surprising but true

Aryabhata's data was first received in a building in Bengaluru where a toilet had been converted into part of the control room.

Built in sheds

In the early 1970s, India's space programme was tiny. Scientists had assembled small rockets in a church in Kerala, but nobody in India had ever built a satellite. Then, in 1972, the Soviet Union agreed to launch one, in return for letting Soviet ships use Indian ports.

A team led by the physicist U. R. Rao had about three years to build it. They worked in tin-roofed industrial sheds in Peenya. Many parts had to be designed from scratch, because they could not be bought. The budget was set at about 3 crore rupees.

The satellite's data reception centre in Bengaluru was squeezed into whatever space could be found. One part of it was a converted toilet.

Who was Aryabhata?Around 1,500 years ago, a 23-year-old astronomer in India wrote that the stars only seem to move because the Earth itself is spinning.

A ball with 26 sides

Aryabhata was a polyhedron, a solid shape with many flat faces, about 1.4 metres across. Picture something a bit taller than a school desk and a little rounder.

The satellite spun gently but did not point itself at the Sun. So the designers covered 24 of its 26 faces with solar cells. Whichever way it turned, some panels caught the light. That's the same idea behind a 3D shape such as a football, which looks much the same from every angle.

Five days of science

After launch, signals poured in. But on about the fifth day in orbit, a fault in the power system cut off its science instruments. The experiments stopped. Aryabhata kept sending some signals for a while longer, which still let engineers learn how their satellite behaved in space.

On launch day, the news went out on All India Radio, and newspapers splashed it across their front pages. India had become one of only about a dozen countries with a satellite of its own. It stayed in orbit for almost 17 years, circling Earth silently, before falling back and burning up in the atmosphere in February 1992.

So was it a failure? Not really. Building and running Aryabhata taught India's engineers how to design satellites, track them and talk to them. Within a few years, India was building satellites for TV, weather and phones. The same team went on to build Bhaskara, an Earth-watching satellite, in 1979. ISRO still runs its satellite centre in Bengaluru, now named after U. R. Rao.

Money in your pocket

In 1976, the Reserve Bank of India printed a picture of Aryabhata on the back of the two-rupee note. It stayed there until 1997. For two decades, millions of Indians carried India's first satellite in their pockets, often without knowing what the strange shape was.

Five years after Aryabhata, India launched a satellite on its own rocket for the first time, from Sriharikota in 1980. Today ISRO has sent missions to the Moon, Mars and the Sun. And it all began even more humbly than a toilet control room. Parts of India's very first rocket were carried to the launch pad on a bicycle.

Myth or fact?

Myth: India launched its first satellite on its own rocket.

Truth: Aryabhata was built in India but launched on a Soviet rocket. India's first satellite launched on an Indian rocket was Rohini, which flew on the SLV-3 in 1980.

Try this

Make a simple many-sided shape. Cut out several equal paper squares and triangles and tape them into a closed ball shape. Shine a torch at it from different sides and count how many faces catch the light each time. That was Aryabhata's trick for staying powered.
