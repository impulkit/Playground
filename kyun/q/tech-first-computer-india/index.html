What was India's first computer?

India's first home-built computer filled a whole room, used 2,700 glowing glass tubes, and was named by Jawaharlal Nehru himself.

The short answer

It depends on what "first" means. The first computer installed in India was the HEC-2M, a British-made machine set up at the Indian Statistical Institute in Kolkata in 1956. It was brought in by the statistician P. C. Mahalanobis to crunch survey data.

The first computer designed and built in India was TIFRAC, short for TIFR Automatic Calculator. A team at the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research in Mumbai built it in the 1950s, and it was formally commissioned in 1960. Jawaharlal Nehru gave it its name. It filled a room, used about 2,700 vacuum tubes and could store just 2,048 numbers.

Surprising but true

TIFRAC's whole memory held 2,048 numbers. A cheap phone today can store millions of times more.

Kolkata gets a computer

In the 1950s, India was planning its economy and needed numbers: how much grain was grown, how many people were poor, how factories were doing. Mahalanobis had started a huge survey to collect such facts, a cousin of the census. Working them out by hand took armies of clerks.

So in 1956 his institute brought in the HEC-2M from Britain. In 1959 a second machine, the URAL, arrived from the Soviet Union. The HEC-2M read its instructions from punched cards, stiff paper cards in which the pattern of holes stood for numbers and commands.

Building TIFRAC

In Mumbai, the physicist Homi Bhabha had a bolder idea. Instead of buying a computer, why not build one? A young team led by R. Narasimhan began work in 1955. They first made a small trial machine, which ran in 1956. Then they built the full computer, based on a famous design from Princeton in the USA.

India had almost no electronics industry then, so many parts had to be imported. The team then wired thousands of them together by hand. TIFRAC used about 2,700 vacuum tubes, 1,700 germanium diodes and some 12,500 resistors. Its memory was made of tiny rings of magnetic material called ferrite cores, then a new idea.

What it could do

Each vacuum tube worked as a switch, on or off, standing for 1 or 0 in binary code. TIFRAC's memory held 2,048 "words", each 40 binary digits long. That adds up to roughly 10 kilobytes, less than one tiny photo on a phone.

Yet scientists queued to use it. TIFRAC solved problems in physics, engineering and statistics that would have taken people weeks of calculation. It stayed in use until around 1965, when newer imported machines took over.

Transistors and teaching

Vacuum tubes glow like small light bulbs and get hot. A room full of them needed powerful cooling, and a single burnt-out tube could stop the whole machine until engineers tracked it down. Smaller, cooler and far more reliable transistors replaced them. In 1966, the Indian Statistical Institute and Jadavpur University in Kolkata finished ISIJU, an early Indian-built computer that used transistors instead of tubes.

Universities joined in. IIT Kanpur got an IBM 1620 in 1963 and began teaching students how to program it. Many of India's first computer scientists learned on that machine. Students wrote programs on paper, punched them onto cards, and sometimes waited hours for a result.

The supercomputer nobody would sell

In the late 1980s, India wanted a supercomputer to forecast the monsoon. The USA, worried about how such machines might be used, would not sell its most powerful model. So India decided to build its own.

A new centre called C-DAC was set up in Pune in 1988, led by the scientist Vijay Bhatkar. In 1991 it unveiled PARAM 8000, which linked many processors to work together. It was India's first supercomputer. Today the descendants of those machines help plan ISRO missions, forecast cyclones and design medicines.

Myth or fact?

Myth: India only started using computers in the 1980s or 1990s.

Truth: Indian scientists were using and building computers in the 1950s. Kolkata had an imported machine in 1956, and Mumbai's TIFRAC, built in India, was running by 1960, only about 15 years after the first electronic computers appeared.

Try this

Make a human computer. Give five friends a card each saying 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16. Each friend stands for on and sits for off. Call out a number up to 31 and let the group work out who must stand to make it. That is how binary switches store numbers.
