How can atoms work like a clock?

Some atoms change into other atoms. Slowly. Predictably. Like the ticking of a clock.

The short answer

Some atoms are unstable. Over time they change into a different kind of atom.

Nobody can say when one single atom will change. But in a big crowd, almost exactly half change in a fixed time, called a half-life.

Count the old atoms and the new ones, and you can work out how much time has passed.

Surprising but true

Tiny crystals can keep time for billions of years without a battery.

A crowd of coin tosses

Think of a huge crowd of coins. Toss them all, and remove every one that lands heads. You can't say which coin will go next, but you can be sure about half will go each round. Toss after toss, the pile halves again and again: 1,000, then 500, 250, 125.

Unstable atoms behave in a similar way. Each one has a fixed chance of changing in any period of time. With billions of atoms, those chances add up to a smooth, predictable curve. That's the clock.

Different clocks for different times

Carbon-14 works for things that were once alive, up to about 50,000 years old, like wood, bone or cloth. Potassium, uranium and other atoms date rocks millions or billions of years old.

Uranium-238 has a half-life of about 4.5 billion years, nearly the age of Earth itself. So in rocks as old as our planet, about half the original uranium has turned into lead. Measuring tiny crystals of zircon, which trap uranium when they form, has revealed the ages of the oldest pieces of Earth and the age of Earth itself.

How carbon dating works

Every living thing takes in carbon, from the air or from food. A tiny share of it is radioactive carbon-14. While a plant or animal is alive, it keeps topping up. When it dies, the topping up stops, and the carbon-14 slowly fades away. After 5,730 years, half is gone.

The American chemist Willard Libby worked this out in the late 1940s and won a Nobel Prize for it. Carbon dating changed archaeology overnight. It has been used on Ötzi the Iceman, found in the Alps, who lived about 5,300 years ago. Charcoal and grain from the Indus cities have been dated the same way, helping archaeologists build a timeline of when they grew and declined.

Where it all began

In 1896, the French scientist Henri Becquerel noticed that uranium salts left marks on photographic plates kept in a dark drawer. Something invisible was coming out of them. Marie Curie studied this further, discovered new elements such as radium and polonium, and named the effect radioactivity. In 1903, Becquerel and Marie and Pierre Curie shared the Nobel Prize in Physics for this work.

In the early 1900s, the physicist Ernest Rutherford realised that this steady change could measure time. In 1907, the American chemist Bertram Boltwood measured uranium and lead in rocks to estimate their ages. The first rocks had been dated, and they turned out to be far older than most people had believed.

Why it's reliable

Heat, pressure and weather barely change the decay rate. That's why it's such a trusted clock, used to date the asteroid layer and ancient art.

Scientists also cross-check. If two different atom clocks in the same rock, such as uranium and potassium, give the same age, they can be confident it's right. Different labs, different methods and different countries keep arriving at the same answers.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Radioactive things glow green.

Truth: Most radioactive materials don't glow at all. The idea comes partly from cartoons and partly from old clock dials, which were painted with radium mixed with another substance that glows when radiation hits it.

Try this

Put 50 coins in a box, close it and shake. Tip them out and remove every coin that lands heads up. Count what's left and write it down. Repeat until no coins remain. Each shake is one "half-life". Draw a graph of your results and see the curve that scientists use to read atom clocks.
