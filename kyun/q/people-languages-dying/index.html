What happens when the last speaker of a language dies?

When Boa Sr died in the Andaman Islands in 2010, a language that may have been spoken for tens of thousands of years died with her.

The short answer

When the last fluent speaker of a language dies, the language stops being used for everyday talk. Linguists call it extinct or dormant. If nobody recorded it, the words, grammar, songs and stories may be lost for ever. If people did make recordings and dictionaries, it might one day be woken up again.

There are about 7,000 languages in the world, but most are spoken by small groups. The language guide Ethnologue estimates that roughly 40 percent are endangered, usually because children are growing up speaking a bigger language instead. Linguists disagree about how fast languages are disappearing, but they agree it is happening across every continent.

Surprising but true

Manx, the old language of the Isle of Man, lost its last native speaker in 1974, yet today children there go to a primary school taught entirely in Manx.

The last voice of Bo

Boa Sr lived on the Andaman Islands and was the last speaker of Bo, one of the Great Andamanese languages. Some linguists believe the Andamanese languages descend from some of the oldest languages in Asia. For her last years she had nobody to speak Bo with. She talked to birds, she told researchers, because they understood.

She survived the 2004 tsunami and died in 2010, aged about 85. Researchers had recorded some of her speech and songs. Those recordings are now among the only traces of the language.

Why languages fade

Languages rarely vanish because people suddenly stop speaking. They fade over generations. Parents may feel their children will get better jobs if they speak a major language, such as English, Hindi or Spanish. Schools may teach only in the big language. Sometimes, as with many Native American languages in the USA, children were punished at boarding schools for speaking their own.

India is home to hundreds of languages. UNESCO's atlas lists nearly 200 Indian languages as endangered, more than any other country. Many belong to small tribal communities whose children now study in a regional language and English.

What gets lost

Every language carries a particular way of seeing the world. Some languages have no words for left and right, using north, south, east and west instead, even for things on a table. Others have dozens of words for kinds of rain, fish or forest plants, and some of those names record which plants heal and which ones poison.

When a language dies, that knowledge does not always move into the new language. A word can carry a whole story inside it, and a story can hold the memory of a flood, a migration or a star that guided sailors.

Waking a language up

Sometimes a language comes back. The most famous case is Hebrew. In the late 1800s, people in what is now Israel began speaking it at home again, and today millions use it every day.

In the USA, a Wampanoag woman named Jessie Little Doe Baird studied old documents, including a Bible translated into her people's language in the 1600s. She helped bring Wampanoag back, and her daughter became one of its first native speakers in about a century. In the Isle of Man, Manx lost its last native speaker in 1974, but a Manx-language primary school now teaches children in it.

Can technology help?

Linguists record elders, write grammars and build dictionaries, sometimes in a race against time. Apps, keyboards and online videos help young people use small languages on their phones. Some researchers are working on translation apps for languages with only a few thousand speakers.

None of that works without speakers who care. The strongest protection for a language is children who use it with their friends. Even languages that need no voice at all can be in danger, as you can see with sign languages around the world.

Myth or fact?

Myth: When a language disappears, nothing important is lost, because people can say the same things in another language.

Truth: Every language carries knowledge that may not translate easily, such as names for plants and medicines, ways of counting, songs, stories and jokes. When it vanishes without records, much of that knowledge can vanish too.

Try this

Ask a grandparent or an older relative to teach you five words or a short song in a language or dialect they spoke as a child. Write them down with their meanings, and record them saying the words on a phone if they agree. You have just made a tiny language archive.
