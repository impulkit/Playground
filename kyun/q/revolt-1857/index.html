What happened in 1857?

Early in 1857, chapatis were secretly passed from village to village across north India. Nobody, then or now, knows exactly why.

The short answer

In 1857, Indian soldiers in the army of the East India Company rose up against their British officers. The revolt began at Meerut on 10 May and spread quickly across north and central India.

Rebel soldiers marched to Delhi and declared the elderly Mughal emperor, Bahadur Shah Zafar, their leader. Kings, queens, landlords, farmers and townspeople joined in many places. Leaders included Rani Lakshmibai of Jhansi, Begum Hazrat Mahal of Lucknow, Nana Sahib of Kanpur, Tatya Tope and Kunwar Singh of Bihar. The fighting was fierce, with terrible killings on both sides. By mid-1858 the British had crushed the revolt. The Company lost control of India, and the British Crown took over.

Surprising but true

The revolt brought about the end of two powers at once, the East India Company and the Mughal dynasty.

A mystery of chapatis

In early 1857, British officials noticed something odd. Village watchmen were receiving chapatis from a nearby village, with instructions to make more and pass them on. Thousands of chapatis travelled hundreds of kilometres this way.

Nobody could explain it. The British feared it was a secret signal for rebellion. Some villagers seem to have thought it was a charm against disease. Historians still don't know what it meant, but it shows how tense and uneasy north India was.

The cartridge and the spark

The army issued a new rifle, the Enfield, whose paper cartridges had to be bitten open. Rumours spread that they were greased with fat from cows and pigs, which would offend both Hindu and Muslim soldiers.

In March 1857, a sepoy named Mangal Pandey attacked British officers at Barrackpore, near Calcutta, and was hanged. In late April, 85 soldiers at Meerut refused the cartridges, and on 9 May they were put in chains in front of their regiments. The next evening, 10 May, their comrades broke them out, killed British officers and rode through the night to Delhi.

Deeper anger

The cartridges lit a fire that had long been ready to burn. The Company had taken over kingdoms such as Awadh in 1856, and Jhansi and others under a rule called the Doctrine of Lapse. Rulers, nobles and their soldiers lost status.

Farmers faced high land taxes. Many Indians feared British laws and missionaries were attacking their religion and customs. Sepoys were paid much less than British soldiers and could rarely be promoted. So when the revolt began, many groups had reasons of their own to join.

Delhi, Kanpur and Lucknow

Delhi was held by the rebels for four months. In September 1857, British forces stormed the city after a long siege. Bahadur Shah Zafar was captured, and two of his sons and a grandson were shot.

At Kanpur, British men, women and children were killed after surrendering. British troops took brutal revenge in many towns, hanging villagers and blowing rebels from cannons. At Lucknow, British families held out in the Residency for months. The ruined Residency still stands, its walls marked by cannonballs.

What changed

In 1858, Parliament ended the Company's rule, and Queen Victoria became ruler of India. Bahadur Shah Zafar was exiled to Rangoon, where he died in 1862, ending the Mughal line.

The British reorganised the army and became more careful about interfering with religion. Indians remembered 1857 in many ways. In 1909, Vinayak Damodar Savarkar called it the First War of Indian Independence, and the name spread. In 2007, India marked 150 years since the revolt with events across the country.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The revolt of 1857 was only about greased cartridges.

Truth: The cartridges were the spark. Underneath were years of anger about kingdoms taken over by the British, heavy land taxes, low pay for soldiers and fears that the British wanted to change Indians' religions and customs.
