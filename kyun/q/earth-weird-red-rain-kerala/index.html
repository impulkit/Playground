Why did red rain fall in Kerala?

In the summer of 2001, rain the colour of blood fell on parts of Kerala for about two months, staining clothes pink and starting rumours of aliens.

The short answer

From late July 2001, people in parts of Kerala, especially Kottayam and Idukki districts, saw red rain falling. It came in short showers, on and off, for about two months. Clothes hung out to dry turned pink. Some people also reported yellow, green and black rain.

The Centre for Earth Science Studies in Thiruvananthapuram and the Tropical Botanic Garden and Research Institute investigated. They found the red colour came from tiny spores of an alga called Trentepohlia, which grows on trees, rocks and walls across Kerala. Why so many spores got into the rain at once is still not fully understood. But the cause of the colour has been identified.

Surprising but true

The red particles turned out to be spores of a tiny alga called Trentepohlia, which grows on tree bark, rocks and even lamp posts in Kerala.

Rain the colour of blood

The first red rain fell on 25 July 2001. Showers were often local, falling over a few square kilometres and lasting only minutes. Many people collected samples in bottles. Under a microscope, the red water was full of tiny round particles, each about a hundredth of a millimetre across.

People were worried. Some thought it was blood, or a sign. Others feared chemical pollution. Newspapers carried rumours. The government asked scientists to investigate quickly. Samples were collected from dozens of places, and some were kept in fridges and studied for years afterwards.

The first idea: a meteor

At first, some scientists suggested a meteor had exploded high over Kerala, leaving red dust in the air. People in one area reported a loud boom before the first red rain.

But when researchers looked at the particles closely, they didn't look like dust. They looked like living cells, with thick walls. Dust from a meteor wouldn't look like that.

The algae answer

Scientists at the Tropical Botanic Garden grew the particles in the lab. They became an alga, Trentepohlia annulata, which lives on land. It is a relative of green seaweeds, but it has an orange-red colour, from the same family of pigments that makes carrots orange.

In Kerala's wet monsoon, Trentepohlia grows thickly on tree trunks, rocks and even lamp posts in the area where red rain fell. When it releases spores, they can be carried into the air. Scientists think heavy rain washed huge numbers of spores out of the sky at once. Some researchers have suggested that unusual winds or weather in 2001 helped lift many spores into the clouds. That part is still debated.

A space claim

In 2006, two physicists from Mahatma Gandhi University in Kottayam, Godfrey Louis and A. Santhosh Kumar, published a paper suggesting the red cells could have come from space, perhaps from a comet. They said the cells did not contain DNA, which would make them very strange.

Other scientists were not convinced. The alga had already been grown from the samples in a lab, which pointed firmly to a living thing from Earth. Most researchers accept the algae explanation. The space idea remains a reminder that big claims need big evidence.

Has it happened again?

Red rain has been reported in Kerala again, including in 2012, and in other places around the world, such as Sri Lanka in 2012. Coloured rain can also be caused by dust. Red rain sometimes falls in Europe when strong winds carry Sahara desert dust north. In India, dust storms can tint rain in the north.

So coloured rain isn't magic. It is rain that has picked up something on the way down: dust, spores or pollution. For stranger rain still, look beyond Earth. On some distant planets, scientists think it may rain glass or even metal, sideways, at thousands of kilometres an hour.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The red rain in Kerala contained alien life from a meteor.

Truth: A couple of researchers suggested that, but the government's study found the red cells were spores of a known alga. Later work grew the alga from samples. No evidence has shown that the cells came from space.

Try this

Look for green algae near your home with an adult. Check shady walls, tree bark, damp steps or old flowerpots for green or orange patches. Look closely with a magnifying glass and draw what you see. Some kinds of Trentepohlia, the alga in the red rain, look like orange velvet on tree trunks.
