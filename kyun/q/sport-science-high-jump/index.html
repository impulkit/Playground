How did the Fosbury Flop change the high jump?

In 1968 a lanky American student went over the high-jump bar backwards, head first, and won Olympic gold with a style people laughed at.

The short answer

Before 1968, top high jumpers went over the bar facing it, rolling their body over the top. Dick Fosbury, a student from Oregon in the USA, did something odd. He ran in on a curve, turned his back to the bar, arched over it head first and landed on his shoulders.

At the 1968 Olympics in Mexico City, he won gold with a jump of 2.24 metres, an Olympic record. Within about ten years, most of the world's best jumpers had copied him. Today, almost every high jumper on Earth uses the Fosbury Flop.

Surprising but true

A good Fosbury Flop lets a jumper clear the bar while the centre of their body actually passes underneath it.

A teenager who couldn't roll

Fosbury wasn't good at the popular style of the time, called the straddle, in which you roll over the bar belly down. So in high school he went back to an older, simpler scissors style and kept fiddling with it. Each time the bar went up, he leaned back a little more. Eventually he was going over flat on his back.

In 1964 his local newspaper in Medford, Oregon, ran the headline "Fosbury Flops Over Bar". The name stuck.

Why soft mats mattered

For most of the sport's history, jumpers landed in pits of sand or sawdust. Coming down head first would have been dangerous. In the 1960s, many schools in the USA switched to thick foam landing mats. Fosbury could now land on his back safely, and his style became possible.

That's a lesson that runs through sports science: sometimes the equipment changes first, and the technique follows.

The trick of the bend

Every object has a balance point called its centre of mass. For a person standing up, it's somewhere around the belly button. To get over a bar, the jumper's whole body must clear it, but their centre of mass doesn't have to.

When a flopper arches like a rainbow over the bar, their head and arms drop down on one side while their legs hang on the other. At that moment, the centre of mass can sit a few centimetres below the bar. The jumper doesn't need to lift their body's balance point as high as the bar itself, so the same leg muscles can clear a higher bar.

The curved run-up helps too. Running in an arc makes the jumper lean in, like a cyclist on a bend, and the lean turns into a twist that rotates them back-first over the bar.

The styles that came before

Before the flop, jumpers tried many styles. In the scissors, you swing one leg over the bar and then the other, staying almost upright. In the Western roll, popular in the early 1900s, you go over sideways. The straddle, rolling over face down, was used to set world records in the 1960s and 1970s. Each new style brought the body's middle closer to the bar, and the flop took that idea furthest of all.

How high people can go

The men's world record is 2.45 metres, set by Javier Sotomayor of Cuba in 1993, as of 2026. A standard doorway is only about 2 metres high. He was about 1.95 m tall, so he cleared half a metre above his own head. The women's world record is 2.10 metres, set by Yaroslava Mahuchikh of Ukraine in Paris in 2024. It broke a record that had stood for 37 years.

At the Tokyo Olympics in 2021, Mutaz Essa Barshim of Qatar and Gianmarco Tamberi of Italy were tied after hours of jumping. Barshim asked an official whether they could share gold. They could, and they did, with a hug the world remembered.

And the strangest part? Another field event had the opposite problem. Throwers got so good that officials had to change the javelin itself to stop it flying out of stadiums.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Fosbury Flop was worked out by scientists in a laboratory.

Truth: Fosbury found it by trial and error as a teenager, because he couldn't master the older styles. Scientists explained why it works only later.

Try this

Lie on a bed and arch your back so your tummy points up and your head and heels drop down. Ask someone to hold a ruler level across the highest point of your arch. Notice how your middle can be above a line while much of your weight hangs below it. That's the flop's secret.
