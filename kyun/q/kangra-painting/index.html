What is Kangra painting?

In Kangra paintings, Krishna and Radha wander through green hills, rain clouds and flowering trees that look a lot like the real Kangra valley.

The short answer

Kangra painting is a style of small, delicate pictures made in the hill kingdoms of the western Himalaya, mainly in the late 1700s and early 1800s. It is named after Kangra, a kingdom in what is now Himachal Pradesh, where it reached its peak under Raja Sansar Chand. It belongs to the wider family of Pahari, or "hill", painting, which is part of Rajput painting.

Kangra paintings are known for soft colours, flowing lines and graceful figures with gentle faces. Most tell love stories of Radha and Krishna, set in green countryside with rivers, banana trees and distant hills. They feel calm and poetic, quite unlike the bold, bright pictures made in many Rajasthani courts.

Surprising but true

Much of the famous Kangra style was created by one family of painters from the tiny hill state of Guler, passed down from father to sons and nephews.

One family of painters

Much of the Kangra style grew out of one family from Guler, a small state close to Kangra. The painter Pandit Seu had two famous sons, Manaku and Nainsukh. Nainsukh worked for a prince in Jasrota and painted lively scenes from court life, including the prince listening to music, smoking a hookah and watching dancers.

The next generation, sons and nephews of these brothers, carried the style further. Working for rulers including Sansar Chand, they painted great sets of pictures for the Gita Govinda and the Bhagavata Purana. The art historian B. N. Goswamy spent years tracing this family through old records, including notes left by pilgrims at Haridwar.

Radha, Krishna and poetry

Kangra painters often worked from poems. The Gita Govinda, written by the poet Jayadeva in the 12th century, describes Radha and Krishna's love, their quarrels and their meeting again. Painters turned each verse into a scene.

They also painted the Baramasa, poems about the twelve months, showing how each season feels to lovers apart or together. Women in Kangra paintings are drawn with a fine profile, long dark hair and a slight lean, an ideal of beauty people still call "the Kangra face".

The look of the hills

Kangra painters looked closely at the land around them. You can spot the curving river Beas, the layered hills of the Dhauladhar range, mango and banana trees and village huts with sloping roofs. Night scenes glow with moonlight, and storm scenes have thin lines of real gold for lightning.

Colours were made from minerals and plants, ground by hand and mixed with gum. Fine brushes used a few hairs from a squirrel's tail. Artists built up layers so thin that the paper underneath seems to glow through, and polished the finished painting with a smooth stone. Hill painters learned a lot from Mughal miniatures, especially the fine shading and the careful, natural-looking faces, and then made the style their own.

A court that rose and fell

Raja Sansar Chand ruled Kangra from 1775 to 1823 and loved art, music and poetry. He kept many painters at his court in Tira Sujanpur and Nadaun. For a time, his kingdom was the strongest in the hills.

Then troubles came. Gurkha armies from Nepal attacked, and in 1809 the Sikh ruler Ranjit Singh took Kangra Fort. Later the British took control of the area. Without rich patrons, the painters scattered or turned to cheaper work.

Kangra today

In 1905, a powerful earthquake shook Kangra and destroyed much of the town and its old fort. Many paintings had already left the region, and today the best collections are in museums in Chandigarh, New Delhi, Chamba, London and Boston.

Some artists in Himachal Pradesh still paint in the Kangra style, using natural colours and fine brushes, and teach young painters to carry it on. Their pictures sell to visitors in Dharamshala and Kangra, a short drive from the hills the old masters painted.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Miniature paintings were painted by one artist working alone.

Truth: Many Kangra paintings were made by workshops. A master might draw the outline, while family members prepared paper, ground colours, painted backgrounds or added gold. Skills passed down through families for generations.
