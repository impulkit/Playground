How do hummingbirds hover?

A hummingbird can hang perfectly still in front of a flower, then zip backwards out of it like a tiny helicopter.

The short answer

A hummingbird hovers by beating its wings very fast, often 50 or more times a second, in a flat figure-of-eight pattern. Most birds get almost all their lift on the downstroke. A hummingbird twists its wings over on the way back, so the upstroke pushes air down too.

That gives it a steady push upwards on both halves of each beat, and lets it hang in front of a flower as if pinned in the air. By tilting the stroke, it can also dart sideways, fly backwards, or rise straight up like a tiny helicopter.

Surprising but true

Hummingbirds live only in the Americas today, but fossils show their ancestors once lived in Europe.

Wings like a swimmer's arms

Watch a swimmer treading water. Their hands sweep forwards and backwards, tilted so both strokes push water down. A hummingbird's wings do something similar in air.

Its shoulder joint can swivel much more than ours. On the forward stroke the wing moves with its top surface up. On the backstroke the wing flips almost upside down. Scientists filmed hummingbirds in a wind tunnel full of tiny particles to watch the air move. They found the downstroke gives about three-quarters of the lift, and the upstroke about a quarter.

That makes hummingbird flight a bit like insect flight. The wings beat so fast that they blur, and the buzzing sound they make gave the birds their name.

Tiny and hungry

The smallest bird in the world is the bee hummingbird of Cuba. It is about 5 to 6 cm long and weighs about 2 g, less than a one-rupee coin. Its eggs are about the size of a pea.

Hovering burns huge amounts of energy. A hummingbird's heart can beat more than 1,000 times a minute while it feeds. To keep going, it visits hundreds of flowers a day, sipping sweet nectar with a long tongue that can flick in and out many times a second. It also snaps up tiny insects and spiders for protein.

Switching off at night

A bird this small can't eat while it sleeps, so it risks running out of fuel overnight. Many hummingbirds solve this by going into torpor, a deep, cold sleep. Their body temperature drops sharply and their heartbeat slows right down. In the morning they warm up again and head straight for breakfast.

Long-distance flyers

For their size, some hummingbirds make astonishing journeys. The ruby-throated hummingbird spends summer in the eastern USA and Canada and winters in Central America. Many cross the Gulf of Mexico, about 800 km of open water, in a single nonstop flight. Before leaving, they fatten up and can nearly double their weight.

The rufous hummingbird travels even farther, from Alaska and Canada to Mexico. Measured in body lengths, that is one of the longest migrations of any bird.

Only in the Americas

Hummingbirds live only in the Americas, from Alaska to the tip of South America. But fossils about 30 million years old, found in Germany, show that their relatives once lived in Europe too.

India has no hummingbirds. It does have sunbirds, small, shiny birds with long curved beaks that also drink nectar. They usually perch to feed, although they can hover for a moment. Scientists see the two as a good example of different animals finding the same solution to the same problem: how to get at the sugar hidden deep inside a flower.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Small hummingbirds hitch a ride on the backs of geese when they migrate.

Truth: This is an old folk tale. Hummingbirds fly every kilometre themselves. Ruby-throated hummingbirds even cross the Gulf of Mexico, about 800 km, in one nonstop flight.

Try this

Flap your arms up and down as fast as you can for 10 seconds while a friend counts. Most people manage about 20 to 40 flaps. A hovering hummingbird can beat its wings more than 500 times in the same 10 seconds.
