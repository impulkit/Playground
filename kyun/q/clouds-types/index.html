How do you tell clouds apart?

A fluffy cloud can weigh as much as a hundred elephants, and its shape tells you what weather is coming.

The short answer

You can tell clouds apart by their shape and their height. There are three basic shapes. Heaps and puffs are called cumulus. Flat, grey layers are stratus. Thin, wispy streaks high up are cirrus. Add nimbus, meaning rain, and you have the building blocks of most cloud names.

Weather scientists group clouds into 10 main types, depending on these shapes and on whether they float low, in the middle or high in the sky. Each type forms in a different kind of air, so knowing your clouds can help you guess what the weather will do next.

Surprising but true

In 2017, scientists officially recognised a new kind of cloud, asperitas, after cloud-spotters around the world sent in photos of its strange wavy underside.

The man who named the clouds

Before 1802, clouds had no widely used scientific names. Then a young English chemist named Luke Howard gave a talk in London. He suggested using Latin words for their shapes: cumulus for heap, stratus for layer, cirrus for a curl of hair, and nimbus for rain cloud. You could combine them, so a rain-bringing layer became nimbostratus.

His idea caught on fast. The German writer Goethe even wrote poems in Howard's honour. Today the World Meteorological Organization keeps an official guide, the International Cloud Atlas, which still uses his names.

Low, middle and high

Low clouds sit below about 2 km. Stratus is a grey blanket that can hide hilltops. Fog is simply a stratus cloud touching the ground. Fluffy cumulus clouds, the kind children draw, usually form on sunny days as warm air bubbles up from the ground.

Middle clouds float between about 2 and 7 km up. Altocumulus looks like rows of small puffs, sometimes called a mackerel sky.

High clouds, above about 6 km, are made of ice crystals. Cirrus looks like brushstrokes or horses' tails. A thin, milky sheet of cirrostratus can make a ring, or halo, around the Sun or Moon. Halos often show up a day or so before rain, as a weather system approaches.

The king of clouds

Cumulonimbus is the biggest cloud of all. It starts as a cumulus and keeps growing, sometimes to more than 10 km tall, higher than Mount Everest. At the top, it hits a layer of the air it can't rise through and spreads out flat, like the anvil of a blacksmith.

Inside, strong winds rush up and down. These clouds bring heavy rain, hail, thunder and lightning. During India's monsoon, towering cumulonimbus clouds build up over the Western Ghats and the plains, and burst into downpours.

How heavy is a cloud?

Clouds look weightless, but they hold a lot of water. Scientists have estimated that a small fluffy cumulus cloud contains around 500 tonnes of water, about the weight of a hundred elephants.

It doesn't fall because that water is split into billions of droplets, each far smaller than a grain of sand. They are so tiny that rising air holds them up. When the droplets bump together and grow too heavy, they fall as rain.

A new cloud

New kinds of clouds don't come along often. In 2017, the International Cloud Atlas added asperitas, a dark, rippled cloud base that looks like a rough sea seen from underneath. It was recognised partly thanks to photos sent in by members of the Cloud Appreciation Society from many countries.

Some clouds are made by people. The white lines behind high-flying jets are contrails, clouds of ice crystals formed from water in the engine exhaust. On a cold, damp day they can spread out and become cirrus clouds. The same sky science feeds the computers behind weather forecasts.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Clouds are made of water vapour.

Truth: Water vapour is an invisible gas. Clouds are made of tiny drops of liquid water, or ice crystals, so small and light that rising air keeps them afloat. That's why you can see them.

Try this

Keep a cloud diary for a week. Look at the sky at the same time each day, sketch the clouds, and try to name them using the three basic shapes, heaps, layers and wisps. Then write down what the weather did over the next few hours. Did the clouds give you a warning?
