Who are some great women mathematicians?

Before John Glenn flew around the Earth in 1962, he asked for one person to check the computer's flight numbers by hand. Her name was Katherine Johnson.

The short answer

Women have done important mathematics for at least 1,600 years, often while being told they shouldn't. Many were blocked from universities, paid less or had their work credited to men. Some taught themselves from borrowed books.

A few of the best known are Hypatia in ancient Alexandria, Ada Lovelace and Sophie Germain in the 1800s, and Emmy Noether in the early 1900s. Closer to our time come Katherine Johnson in the United States, Shakuntala Devi in India and Maryam Mirzakhani from Iran. Their work runs through modern physics, computing and space travel.

Surprising but true

Sophie Germain taught herself maths from her father's books in Paris, and for years wrote to famous mathematicians using a man's name so they would take her seriously.

Hypatia of Alexandria

Hypatia lived in Alexandria, in Egypt, around 1,600 years ago. Her father, Theon, was a mathematician, and she became a famous teacher of maths, astronomy and philosophy. Students travelled long distances to hear her.

She worked on editions of older Greek maths books, including works on astronomy and geometry, and helped preserve them. She was killed in 415 CE during a period of violent political and religious conflict in the city. She is often remembered as the first woman mathematician whose life is well recorded.

Ada Lovelace and Sophie Germain

In the 1840s, the English mathematician Ada Lovelace studied a machine designed by her friend Charles Babbage, called the Analytical Engine. It was never fully built. In 1843 she published long notes on it, including a step-by-step method for the machine to calculate a series of special numbers. Many people call this the first published computer program. She also guessed that such machines might one day handle music and pictures as well as numbers.

In France, Sophie Germain wasn't allowed to study at university. She got lecture notes from friends and sent her work to the great mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss under the name "Monsieur LeBlanc". When Gauss found out, he wrote to praise her. Her work on how surfaces vibrate helped engineers understand how structures bend.

Emmy Noether

Emmy Noether was a German mathematician who, for years, was not allowed to be paid as a university teacher because she was a woman. She lectured under a male colleague's name.

In 1918 she proved what is now called Noether's theorem. It links patterns of symmetry to rules in physics, such as why energy is never lost. Physicists still use it every day. After she died in 1935, Albert Einstein wrote in a letter to The New York Times that she was the most significant creative mathematical genius produced since women began higher education.

Katherine Johnson

Katherine Johnson worked at NASA in Virginia as one of its "computers", people who did complicated calculations by hand. As an African American woman in the segregated South, she faced unfair rules at work, at a time when the struggle for equal rights was still going on.

In 1962, before John Glenn became the first American to orbit the Earth, he asked that she check the electronic computer's numbers for his flight. She did, and he flew. Later she helped calculate paths for the Apollo Moon missions. In 2015 she received the Presidential Medal of Freedom.

Shakuntala Devi and Maryam Mirzakhani

Shakuntala Devi, born in Bengaluru in 1929, became famous as a "human computer". In 1980, at Imperial College London, she multiplied two 13-digit numbers in her head in 28 seconds, a feat recorded by Guinness World Records. She toured the world showing that mental maths could be fun.

Maryam Mirzakhani grew up in Tehran and became a professor at Stanford University. She studied the geometry of curved surfaces. In 2014 she became the first woman to win the Fields Medal, often called the highest prize in mathematics. She died in 2017, aged 40.

Today, many women continue the work. In India, the algebraist Raman Parimala has won major awards for her research. Girls in maths olympiads around the world are adding new names to the list, much as Ramanujan once did from a small town in Tamil Nadu.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Girls are naturally worse at maths than boys.

Truth: Large studies in many countries find little or no average difference in maths ability between girls and boys. Where gaps exist, they vary a lot from country to country and have shrunk over time, which points to opportunity and encouragement, not ability.
