How does waterproof cloth work?

The best rain jackets have billions of holes in every few square centimetres, and those holes are exactly what keeps you dry.

The short answer

Ordinary cloth is made of threads with tiny gaps between them. Water soaks into the threads and seeps through the gaps. To make cloth waterproof, you either block the gaps completely, with a coating of rubber, plastic or wax, or you make the gaps so small that liquid water can't get through.

The cleverest rain jackets do something more. They have a thin inner layer full of microscopic pores. Raindrops are far too big to pass through them, but water vapour from your sweat is small enough to escape. That is what makers mean by "breathable".

Surprising but true

In Britain, people still call a raincoat a "mac", after Charles Macintosh, a Scottish chemist who patented rubber-coated cloth in 1823.

Rubber from trees

Long before Europe had raincoats, peoples in Central and South America collected the milky sap of rubber trees. They used it to make balls, shoes and waterproof cloth. European explorers brought rubber back as a curiosity.

In 1823, a Scottish chemist named Charles Macintosh found a way to dissolve rubber in a liquid made from coal tar. He spread it between two layers of cloth and pressed them together. The rubber in the middle stopped water completely. Coats made from this "Macintosh" cloth became popular in rainy Britain, even though they smelled, went stiff in the cold and turned sticky in the heat.

In 1839, the American inventor Charles Goodyear found that heating rubber with sulphur made it much tougher and less sensitive to temperature. This process, called vulcanisation, made rubber raincoats, boots and tyres far better.

Wax and oil

Sailors and fishermen had another trick. They rubbed linseed oil or wax into heavy cotton or linen. The oil filled the gaps between the threads and made water bead up and roll off. Their coats, called oilskins, were often yellow so that a sailor who fell overboard was easier to spot.

Waxed cotton jackets are still made today. They can be re-waxed when they start to let water in.

Why does water roll off a lotus leaf?The lotus grows in muddy ponds, yet its leaves never stay dirty. Scientists have copied the trick to make self-cleaning paint.

Plastic coats

In the 1900s, plastics such as PVC replaced rubber in cheap raincoats. A plastic raincoat is simply a thin, flexible sheet with no holes at all. It is light and cheap, which is why so many children in India pull one on over their school uniform in the monsoon.

The problem is sweat. Your body gives off water all the time, even when you are not running. In a plastic coat, it has nowhere to go. It condenses on the inside, and after a long walk you may feel as wet as if you had no coat at all.

The breathable membrane

In 1969, an American engineer named Bob Gore was working with a slippery plastic called PTFE, the material used on non-stick pans. He found that stretching it quickly turned it into a thin sheet full of tiny holes. The material, called Gore-Tex, went into rain jackets in the 1970s.

Its pores are thousands of times smaller than a raindrop but hundreds of times bigger than a molecule of water vapour. Rain stays out, and the vapour from your skin slowly escapes. Many outdoor jackets now use membranes like this, sandwiched between an outer fabric and a soft lining.

A slippery outer coat

Most rain jackets also have a water-repellent finish on the outside, which makes drops bead up the way they do on a lotus leaf. Some of these finishes use chemicals that last a very long time in nature, so many companies are switching to newer, cleaner ones.

People in rainy places have always found their own answers. Farmers in Assam wear the japi, a wide hat woven from bamboo and palm leaves. In Kerala, some farmers working in paddy fields still use a hat-like umbrella made of palm leaf that leaves both hands free.

Myth or fact?

Myth: If a raincoat is waterproof, you can never get wet inside it.

Truth: Plain plastic or rubber coats trap the sweat your body makes, so you can end up damp from the inside. That is why breathable fabrics were invented.

Try this

Collect small scraps of different fabrics, such as cotton, wool, a plastic bag and an old raincoat or umbrella. Lay each one over the mouth of a cup and hold it with a rubber band. Put one spoonful of water on top of each and wait two minutes. Which cups stayed dry, and which fabrics let water through?
