What causes tooth decay?

Cavities aren't caused by sugar itself. They're caused by germs in your mouth that eat sugar and leave acid behind.

The short answer

Your mouth is home to hundreds of kinds of bacteria. Most are harmless, but a few, such as one called Streptococcus mutans, love sugar. When you eat sweets, biscuits or even white bread, these bacteria feast on the sugars and give off acid as waste.

That acid slowly dissolves the hard outer coat of your teeth, called enamel. One attack does little harm, and your saliva helps repair it. But if sugar keeps arriving all day, the acid wins, and a weak spot becomes a hole. That hole is a cavity, also called tooth decay or dental caries.

Surprising but true

The sticky film on your teeth

If you run your tongue over your teeth before brushing in the morning, they may feel fuzzy. That's plaque, a sticky film of bacteria. It clings to teeth, especially in the grooves of back teeth, between teeth and along the gums.

Bacteria in plaque are protected from your saliva, so the acid they make stays pressed against the enamel. Brushing twice a day breaks up the film. Cleaning between teeth with floss or a small brush reaches places a toothbrush misses. If plaque is left for days, it hardens into tartar, which only a dentist can remove.

How a cavity grows

Enamel is the hardest material in your body, harder than bone. But it is made of minerals, and acid can dissolve minerals. Once the acid in your mouth gets strong enough, minerals start leaving the enamel.

At first, a chalky white spot appears. At this stage the damage can still reverse, if acid attacks become less frequent and fluoride helps minerals return. If the attacks continue, the spot turns brown and finally breaks into a hole. Under the enamel lies softer dentine, which decays faster. When decay reaches the nerve in the centre of the tooth, it can cause a sharp toothache.

It's how often, not just how much

The surprising part is that how often you eat sugar matters more than how much. After each sugary snack or drink, the mouth turns acidic for roughly half an hour. Then saliva washes the acid away and helps rebuild the enamel.

So a child who has one sweet with lunch gives their teeth one short attack. A child who sips juice or a fizzy drink all afternoon, or nibbles biscuits every hour, keeps the acid going for hours. Sticky foods like toffees and dried fruit cling to teeth and stretch out the attack too. Sugar hides in many foods, including ketchup, flavoured yoghurt and some breakfast cereals.

Fluoride, the tooth helper

Fluoride is a mineral that makes enamel tougher and helps it repair itself. In the 1930s and 1940s, American scientists noticed that people in towns with natural fluoride in their water had fewer cavities. In 1945, Grand Rapids in Michigan became the first city to add a small amount of fluoride to its tap water on purpose.

Today most toothpastes contain fluoride. Dentists advise using a small amount, about the size of a pea for children, and spitting it out without rinsing too much afterwards, so a little stays on the teeth.

The tooth worm and other old ideas

For thousands of years, many people believed toothache came from worms boring into teeth. The idea appears in old Babylonian writings and in folk beliefs from India, China and Europe. In the 1700s, the French dentist Pierre Fauchard argued against it. Like many old ideas that doctors later dropped, it gave way to the real cause: bacteria and acid.

Tooth decay is still very common. The World Health Organization estimated in 2022 that about 2 billion people have decay in their adult teeth. Yet it is also one of the most preventable diseases. Brushing with fluoride toothpaste, cutting down on sugary snacks between meals, and seeing a dentist regularly keep most cavities away.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Baby teeth don't need much care because they fall out anyway.

Truth: Baby teeth help children chew, speak clearly and keep space for the adult teeth growing underneath. Decay in baby teeth can hurt, cause infections and affect the teeth that come next, so dentists say to look after them carefully.

Try this

After eating a biscuit, look at your back teeth in a mirror and notice where the crumbs stick, often in the grooves and between teeth. Rinse your mouth with water and look again. Then brush and check once more. Those sticky spots are where decay most often starts.
