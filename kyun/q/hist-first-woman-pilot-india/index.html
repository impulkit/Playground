Who was India's first woman pilot?

In 1936, a 21-year-old mother named Sarla Thakral climbed into an open cockpit in Lahore, wearing a sari, and flew solo into the sky.

The short answer

Sarla Thakral is the name most people give. In 1936, at the age of 21, she earned her pilot's licence at the Lahore Flying Club, in what was then British India. She flew a small two-seat biplane, a de Havilland Gipsy Moth, alone, and went on to complete 1,000 hours of flying.

But she wasn't the only early flyer. Urmila Parikh earned a licence in 1932, and some members of the Tata family had flown even earlier. Sarla became the best known, and her story was celebrated with a Google Doodle on her birthday in 2021.

Surprising but true

Today about 1 in 7 pilots in India is a woman, a far higher share than the world average of around 1 in 20.

A family of flyers

Sarla was born in Delhi on 8 August 1914. She married young, at 16, and had a daughter by the time she learned to fly. Her husband, P. D. Sharma, was a pilot himself and came from a family with nine pilots. He was the first Indian to earn an airmail pilot's licence, flying between Karachi and Lahore.

He and his father encouraged Sarla to learn. In the 1930s, a married woman climbing into an open cockpit, in a sari, was a bold sight. People came to the airfield to watch.

Up in a Gipsy Moth

The Gipsy Moth was a light biplane, with two sets of wings stacked one above the other, made of wood and fabric. It had an open cockpit, no radio and a top speed of about 160 km/h. Pilots wore goggles and a leather helmet. On cold days the wind cut straight through.

To earn her "A" licence, Sarla flew solo and built up her hours slowly. She dreamed of the next step, a commercial licence that would let her fly passengers for pay.

Flying in the 1930s was risky. There were no weather radar systems and few proper runways, and pilots found their way using maps, rivers, railway lines and their own eyes. Planes could stall in strong winds, and engines failed more often than they do now. Every flight Sarla made needed careful planning and a cool head.

Dreams change course

Then life turned hard. In 1939, her husband died in a plane crash. That same year, World War II began, and civil flying training in India was suspended. Sarla couldn't continue.

She went back to Lahore and studied art at the Mayo School of Art. After Partition in 1947, she moved to Delhi with her two daughters. She remarried, built a career as a painter and designed clothes and jewellery. She died in 2008, at 93.

Other women in the sky

Urmila Parikh of Bombay is recorded as the first woman of Indian nationality to earn a licence, in 1932. Sylla Petit, from the Tata family, got one in India in 1929, but she held British nationality. Each could be called a "first", depending on the rules you use.

After independence, more women took off. Prem Mathur became one of India's first women commercial pilots in the late 1940s. Durba Banerjee later became the first woman pilot at Indian Airlines, the government's domestic airline.

India leads the way

Today India has one of the highest shares of women pilots in the world. About 15 percent of its licensed pilots are women (2020s), compared with roughly 5 percent worldwide. Some fly giant passenger jets, others fly fighter jets for the Indian Air Force, which began training women fighter pilots in 2016.

And women from India have gone even higher. Kalpana Chawla flew to space in 1997, and more Indian women are training for missions to come. Across the world, early pilots like Amelia Earhart faced the same doubts Sarla did, and one of them vanished over the Pacific in a mystery still unsolved.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Women in India only started flying planes recently.

Truth: Indian women were earning flying licences in the 1930s. Some of them flew before many countries allowed women to vote, and India now has one of the highest shares of women pilots in the world.

Try this

Fold three paper planes with different wing shapes, narrow, wide and with the tips bent up. Fly each one five times from the same spot and measure the distance. Write down which flew furthest and straightest. Pilots like Sarla learned how small changes to wings change a flight.
