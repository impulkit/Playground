What is the Pythagorean theorem?

An American congressman, who later became president, found his own proof of this 2,500-year-old rule in 1876.

The short answer

The Pythagorean theorem is a rule about right-angled triangles, the kind with one square corner. If you build a square on each of the three sides, the two smaller squares together have exactly the same area as the biggest one. In symbols, a² + b² = c², where c is the longest side, called the hypotenuse.

The classic example is a triangle with sides 3, 4 and 5. 3 × 3 is 9, and 4 × 4 is 16. Together that makes 25, which is 5 × 5. The rule works for every right-angled triangle, large or small, and it lets you find a missing side whenever you know the other two.

Surprising but true

People have published hundreds of different proofs of the Pythagorean theorem. One book, in its 1940 edition, collected 367 of them.

Who was Pythagoras?

Pythagoras was a Greek thinker born on the island of Samos around 570 BCE. He later settled in southern Italy, where he led a group of followers who studied numbers, music and the stars. They believed numbers were the key to understanding the universe.

We know surprisingly little about him for certain. He wrote nothing that survives, and many stories about him were written centuries after he died. His followers often credited their own discoveries to their famous teacher. So the theorem carries his name, but nobody can be sure he proved it himself.

Much older than Pythagoras

The rule is far older than its name. A clay tablet from Babylon, now known as Plimpton 322 and kept at Columbia University in New York, dates from about 1800 BCE. It records numbers from triangles that fit the rule, such as one with sides 119, 120 and 169.

In India, the Sulba Sutras, written to guide the building of fire altars, state the rule clearly. The oldest, by Baudhayana, is usually dated somewhere between about 800 and 500 BCE. Chinese mathematicians also knew it and called it the gougu rule. The Greeks, especially Euclid around 300 BCE, became famous for writing down careful proofs.

A president's proof

Because the theorem is so simple and so useful, people have kept finding new ways to prove it. An American teacher named Elisha Loomis collected them in a book. Its first edition came out in 1927, and the 1940 edition contained 367 proofs.

One of them came from James Garfield. In 1876, while he was a member of the US Congress, Garfield worked out a proof using a trapezium made of three triangles. It was published in a journal for teachers. The journal's note said he found it during friendly maths discussions with other members of Congress. After more than 2,000 years, the theorem was still a puzzle worth playing with. Five years later, he became the 20th President of the United States.

A triangle in your pocket

The theorem turns up everywhere. Builders use the 3-4-5 rule to check that the corners of a room are square. Firefighters use it to work out how long a ladder must be to reach a window. A ladder leaning against a wall, the ground and the wall make a right-angled triangle.

Screens are measured using it too. The size of a TV or a smartphone screen is the length of its diagonal. Computers use the theorem constantly to find the distance between two points. A video game uses it to tell how far your character is from a monster. A delivery app uses it to estimate how far a rider is from your door. A rule from clay tablets now runs inside almost every device you own.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pythagoras discovered the Pythagorean theorem.

Truth: The rule was known in Babylon more than a thousand years before Pythagoras, and in India around his time. Later Greek writers gave him credit, but he left no writings, and we cannot be sure what he himself proved.

Try this

Make a perfect right angle with string. Tie 12 knots evenly spaced along a loop of string, or mark 12 equal spaces. Ask two friends to help you pull it into a triangle with sides of 3, 4 and 5 spaces. The corner between the 3 and the 4 will be a right angle. Check it against the corner of a book.
