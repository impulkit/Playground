Why does your stomach growl?

That loud rumble in a quiet classroom isn't your stomach complaining. It's your gut doing its regular cleaning round.

The short answer

Your stomach and intestines are long, muscly tubes. Their walls squeeze in waves to push food, liquid and gas along, a bit like squeezing toothpaste along a tube. When gas and liquid get pushed through narrow spaces, they gurgle and rumble.

Those noises happen all the time, but you usually don't notice. They get loudest a couple of hours after a meal, when your stomach is nearly empty. Then your gut sends a strong wave through to sweep out leftovers. With hardly any food inside to soak up the sound, the rumble echoes. Doctors call it borborygmi, a Greek word that sounds a bit like the noise itself.

Surprising but true

Your stomach and intestines make gurgling noises all the time. You hear them most when the stomach is empty, because there's no food to muffle the sound.

A gut on the move

The walls of your gut have layers of muscle that tighten and relax without you thinking about it. This squeezing is called peristalsis. It moves food from your throat down to the stomach, and from there into about 6 m of small intestine.

At the same time, your gut is never just food. You swallow air when you eat and talk. Bacteria in your large intestine make gas as they break down fibre. All this gas and liquid sloshes along with the food, and that's what makes the noise. Some escapes upwards as burps.

The cleaning crew

When there's food to work on, your stomach churns it with acid and enzymes into a thick soup. Then it slowly lets it into the intestine.

Once it's empty, a different routine starts. Every hour and a half to two hours, a strong wave of squeezing sweeps from the stomach all the way through the small intestine. Scientists call it the migrating motor complex. It works like a cleaning crew, pushing out leftover bits of food, mucus and bacteria. These are the waves that make the big, embarrassing rumbles.

As soon as you eat again, the cleaning waves stop and normal digestion takes over.

What do the bacteria in your gut do?Tens of trillions of bacteria live in your gut. They eat what you can't digest, make vitamins and help train your body to fight germs.

Hunger signals

Rumbling and hunger often come together, and that's no accident. When your stomach has been empty for a while, it makes a hormone called ghrelin. Ghrelin travels in your blood to your brain and makes you feel hungry. Another hormone, called motilin, helps switch on those cleaning waves.

Your body also learns your mealtimes. If you always eat lunch at one o'clock, your gut starts getting ready shortly before. Even the smell of food cooking can start your stomach making acid and moving. That's why a whiff of frying pakoras can set a stomach off.

Can you stop it?

Not really, and you shouldn't want to. A working gut makes noise. Eating small meals at regular times can mean fewer loud rumbles. Drinking water can help a little. Gulping fizzy drinks or eating very fast adds more gas.

Sound carries well through water and soft tissue, and your belly is full of both. In a silent exam hall, a rumble can seem as loud as a drum to you, while the person next to you barely notices.

When doctors listen

Doctors sometimes press a stethoscope to a patient's belly to listen. Normal gurgles are a good sign that the gut is moving. Very few sounds, or very loud ones together with pain, can give clues that something is wrong.

If someone has a lot of tummy pain, swelling or vomiting, that is a reason to tell a trusted adult and see a doctor. A rumble on its own, though, is just your gut getting on with its job.

Animals rumble too. Vets listen to a cow's left side, where its huge first stomach churns grass about once or twice a minute. If that stomach goes quiet, the vet knows the cow is unwell.

Quick facts

borborygmi
What makes the sound

Cleaning waves when empty
roughly every 1½ to 2 hours
Hunger hormone
ghrelin, made mainly in the stomach
Length of the small intestine
about 6 m in an adult

Myth or fact?

Myth: A growling stomach always means you need to eat right now.

Truth: Your gut rumbles whether it's empty or full. The noise is loudest when it's empty, and it can come along with feeling hungry, but it can also happen while you are digesting a big meal.

Try this

Ask a family member if you can listen to their tummy. Use an empty kitchen-roll tube, with one end gently on their belly and the other at your ear. Try just before a meal and again an hour after. Count how many gurgles you hear in a minute each time.
