How do solar panels turn sunlight into electricity?

The oldest human-made object still circling Earth is a little satellite from 1958, and it was the first to run on solar power.

The short answer

Most solar panels are made of thin slices of silicon, a material made from purified sand. Each panel holds dozens of these slices, called solar cells. Every cell has two layers of silicon, treated with tiny amounts of other elements so that one layer has spare electrons and the other has gaps where electrons are missing. Where the two layers meet, an invisible electric "slope" forms.

When sunlight hits the cell, its energy knocks electrons loose from silicon atoms. The slope pushes those free electrons one way, towards a metal contact on top. Connect a wire from the top to the bottom, and the electrons flow round it as an electric current. No moving parts, no fuel and no noise: just light going in and electricity coming out.

Surprising but true

Solar panels work better on a cold, bright day than on a scorching one. Heat makes them less efficient, even though they need strong light.

A teenager's discovery

In 1839, a 19-year-old French scientist named Edmond Becquerel was experimenting in his father's lab. He found that shining light on certain materials dipped in a liquid made a tiny electric current. This is now called the photovoltaic effect, from the Greek word for light and the name of Volta, the battery pioneer.

For over a century, solar cells stayed weak and expensive. Then, in 1954, three scientists at Bell Labs in the USA made a silicon cell that turned about 6% of sunlight into electricity, enough to power a small radio. Newspapers called it the start of a new age. Even so, the first panels cost so much that only governments and big companies could afford them. Prices have since fallen so far that, in many sunny countries, solar is now among the cheapest ways to make electricity.

How do satellites help us every day?Weather forecasts, maps on your phone, card payments and cricket on TV all depend on machines circling far above your head.

Power in space

The first big customers were satellites, which could not carry enough fuel for years in orbit. In 1958, the USA launched Vanguard 1, a satellite the size of a grapefruit, with small solar cells on its outside. Its radio fell silent in 1964, but the satellite is still up there. It is the oldest human-made object in orbit around Earth.

Today almost every satellite, the International Space Station and many of India's ISRO spacecraft run on sunlight caught by large solar wings.

Sun-rich India

India gets plenty of strong sunshine, and solar power has grown very fast there since the 2010s. The Bhadla Solar Park in the Thar Desert of Rajasthan is one of the largest in the world. Its rows of panels cover about 57 square kilometres, roughly the area of 8,000 football fields. In 2015, Cochin International Airport in Kerala became the first airport in the world to run entirely on solar power.

Rooftop panels now sit on homes, schools and railway stations across India and the USA. Because the panels make electricity right where it is used, less is wasted travelling long distances through wires.

Limits and fixes

Panels make no electricity at night and less on cloudy or dusty days. In Rajasthan's deserts, workers and robots clean dust off the panels regularly, because a layer of dust can block a lot of light. To keep the lights on after sunset, solar power is often paired with batteries or other power stations.

The best panels sold today turn just over a fifth of the sunlight hitting them into electricity. A leaf does far worse, storing only about 1% of sunlight through photosynthesis. The leaf, though, builds and repairs itself for free.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Solar panels don't work at all on cloudy days.

Truth: They still make electricity on cloudy days, just much less, because some light gets through clouds. They make none at night, which is why solar power is often paired with batteries or other power sources.
