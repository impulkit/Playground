Are crocodiles living fossils?

Crocodiles survived the asteroid. But they haven't stayed the same.

The short answer

Not really. Crocodile relatives once included fast land runners and sea-going hunters. Today's crocodiles are only a few survivors.

The crocodile family tree goes back more than 200 million years, to the same early group that gave rise to dinosaurs and, later, birds. That group is called the archosaurs, or "ruling reptiles". Crocodiles look a lot like their ancestors from the age of dinosaurs, so people call them living fossils. But they have kept changing, and the line has been through many shapes.

India has three crocodilians: the mugger, the saltwater crocodile and the gharial.

The gharial, with its long thin snout for catching fish, is critically endangered. The Chambal river is one of its last homes.

Surprising but true

Crocodiles and birds are each other's closest living relatives.

A family with many shapes

Around 200 million years ago, some crocodile relatives were slim, long-legged hunters that ran on land. Others moved into the sea, swapping legs for flippers and growing tails like a fish. A few had blunt teeth for chewing plants.

Some were giants. Sarcosuchus, nicknamed "SuperCroc", lived in Africa about 110 million years ago and grew perhaps 9 to 11 metres long, about the length of a city bus. In North America, Deinosuchus grew nearly as big and probably ate dinosaurs that came to drink. Today's crocodilians, the crocodiles, alligators, caimans and gharials, are only one branch of this once crowded tree.

How they survived the asteroid

About 66 million years ago, an asteroid struck Earth and wiped out the giant dinosaurs. Many crocodile relatives died too, but some made it through. Scientists think a few habits helped them. Crocodiles are cold-blooded, so they need far less food than a warm-blooded animal of the same size and can go months without eating. Living in rivers and lakes, they could feed on fish and dead animals, which were easier to find than fresh plants after the disaster.

India's three crocodilians

The mugger lives in rivers, lakes and reservoirs across India, and even in some city ponds. The saltwater crocodile, the largest reptile alive, lives in the Sundarbans, the mangroves of Bhitarkanika in Odisha and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands. Large males can grow longer than 6 metres.

The gharial is the odd one out. Its narrow snout is lined with small, sharp teeth, perfect for snapping up fish but no good for big prey. Adult males grow a round lump on the tip of the snout, shaped like a clay pot called a ghara, which gave the animal its name. They use it to make buzzing sounds and bubbles.

By the 1970s, gharials had almost vanished, with only around 200 thought to be left in the wild. India started a crocodile conservation project in 1975, collecting eggs from riverbanks and raising young gharials until they were big enough to release. Today the National Chambal Sanctuary, on a long stretch of the Chambal river, holds most of the world's wild gharials.

Caring parents

Crocodiles are careful parents. A mother mugger digs a nest in a sandy bank and guards it for about two months. Temperature inside the nest decides whether the babies hatch as males or females. When she hears the hatchlings squeak, she digs them out and carries them to the water in her jaws, the same jaws that can crush bone.

That kind of care is something crocodiles share with birds, their closest living relatives. Both build nests, both guard their young, and both probably inherited these habits from ancestors they shared with the dinosaurs.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Crocodiles are living fossils that haven't changed since the time of the dinosaurs.

Truth: Today's crocodiles look ancient, but they have kept evolving. Their extinct relatives included fast runners on land, sea-going hunters with flippers, and plant-eaters. Modern crocodiles are the few survivors of a once much more varied family.

Try this

Test how temperature can decide things. Crocodile eggs that are kept warmer or cooler hatch into males or females. You can't hatch eggs at home, but you can feel the difference a few degrees make. Put one hand in a bowl of cool tap water and the other in slightly warmer water for a minute. Then swap and notice how strongly your skin senses a small change.
