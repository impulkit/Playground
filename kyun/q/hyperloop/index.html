What is a hyperloop?

A hyperloop would shoot passenger pods through a tube with almost no air inside, faster than a plane, if engineers can make it work.

The short answer

A hyperloop is a planned kind of transport. Passengers would sit in a pod that races through a long, sealed tube. Pumps suck most of the air out of the tube, so the pod has almost nothing to push against.

Without air in the way, and with magnets lifting the pod so it barely touches anything, it could in theory reach about 1,000 km/h. That is about as fast as a jet airliner, and roughly three times faster than the quickest bullet trains.

So far, though, hyperloops exist only as test tracks a few hundred metres long. Nobody can yet buy a ticket for one.

Surprising but true

The idea of sending vehicles through tubes is more than 200 years old. A London inventor named George Medhurst wrote about sending goods through pipes using air pressure in 1812.

Why get rid of the air

When you cycle fast, you feel the wind pushing on you even on a still day. That push is called drag. The faster you go, the harder it pushes. For a train at 300 km/h, most of its energy goes into shoving air aside.

A hyperloop tube would hold air at about a thousandth of normal pressure, a bit like the thin air very high above the Earth. With so little air, drag almost disappears.

The other thing that slows vehicles down is friction with the ground. Hyperloop designs borrow from maglev trains and float the pod on magnetic fields, so there are no wheels rubbing on rails.

An old dream with a new name

Tube trains are not a new idea. In 1812 a London inventor named George Medhurst described pushing carriages through pipes with air. In the early 1900s, Robert Goddard, the American rocket pioneer, sketched a train in a vacuum tube.

In 2013 Elon Musk, the head of Tesla and SpaceX, put out a paper with a team of engineers called Hyperloop Alpha. It suggested a tube between Los Angeles and San Francisco and gave the idea its catchy name. Companies sprang up around the world to build one.

What has been built

In November 2020 a company then called Virgin Hyperloop gave the first passenger rides, in a 500 m test tube in the Nevada desert. Two employees reached 172 km/h. That was a real step, but a long way from 1,000.

In February 2025 IIT Madras showed off a 422 m test tube on its campus near Chennai, built with support from Indian Railways. Students there have worked on hyperloop pods since 2017. A test centre with a tube of about the same length opened in the Netherlands in 2024.

India came close to a much bigger project. In 2018 the Maharashtra government agreed to study a hyperloop between Mumbai and Pune, promising a trip of about 25 minutes instead of three hours by road. The plan stalled a couple of years later.

Meanwhile some of the big early companies ran out of money. Hyperloop One, the firm behind the Nevada rides, shut down at the end of 2023.

The hard parts

A real hyperloop would need hundreds of kilometres of tube, kept almost airless and dead straight. Steel tubes grow and shrink as the weather warms and cools, and even a small leak lets air rush in.

Then there is the question of people. Passengers would ride in a sealed capsule, probably with no windows, and turns at such speeds would have to be very gentle or riders would feel sick. Getting everyone out safely in an emergency, in a tube with no air, is a puzzle engineers are still working on.

Supporters say these are problems to solve, not reasons to stop. Critics say a fast ordinary train, such as India's Vande Bharat or Japan's Shinkansen, already does the job at a price countries can pay.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Elon Musk invented the idea of trains in tubes.

Truth: People have proposed vacuum or air-powered tube trains since the 1800s, including the rocket scientist Robert Goddard in the early 1900s. Musk's 2013 paper made the idea famous again and gave it the name hyperloop.

Try this

Hold a sheet of paper flat in front of you and walk quickly. Then crumple it into a ball and walk again. The flat sheet pushes against much more air. That push is drag, the thing a hyperloop tube is designed to get rid of.
