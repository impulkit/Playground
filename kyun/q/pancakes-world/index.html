Why is the pancake found all over the world?

A dosa in Chennai, a crêpe in Paris and injera in Addis Ababa are all answers to the same simple idea, runny batter on a hot surface.

The short answer

A pancake is one of the easiest foods a person can make. Mix ground grain with water or milk, pour it onto something hot, and in a minute you have food. You do not need an oven, yeast or a lot of skill.

Since almost every culture grows some kind of grain and has fire, almost every culture invented pancakes. What changes is the grain and the treatment. India uses rice, lentils and gram flour to make dosa, uttapam and cheela. Ethiopia uses teff. France uses wheat for thin crêpes, and Americans add baking powder to make thick, fluffy stacks. China has jianbing, Russia has blini, and Korea has pajeon, a pancake full of spring onions.

Surprising but true

In Ethiopia, a giant pancake called injera is often the plate and the spoon at the same time, and when the stew is gone, diners eat the plate.

A very old idea

Ancient Greek poets, writing about 2,500 years ago, mentioned tagenites, cakes of wheat flour cooked in olive oil and eaten with honey. The Romans had something similar. In south India, dosa is mentioned in Tamil writings from more than a thousand years ago.

Pancakes probably go back much further than any written record, because they need so little. Ground grain, water and a flat stone heated beside a fire are enough. A hot stone works perfectly well as a tawa.

Two ways to make bubbles

A heavy, flat pancake is fine, but people like them light. There are two main ways to get bubbles into batter.

The old way is fermentation. Dosa batter, made of soaked rice and urad dal ground together, is left overnight. Wild yeast and bacteria feed on it and give off gas, so the batter rises and turns slightly sour. Ethiopian injera and Indian appam use the same trick.

The newer way is chemistry. Baking soda, and baking powder, which became common in the 1800s, react with acid and moisture to release carbon dioxide in minutes. That is how American pancakes rise on the griddle, with tiny holes popping on top just before you flip.

Injera, the edible plate

In Ethiopia and Eritrea, a meal is often served on one enormous, soft, spongy pancake called injera. It is made from teff, a tiny grain grown in the Ethiopian highlands for thousands of years. The batter ferments for days, and the finished injera is full of holes and slightly sour.

Stews and vegetables are spooned on top. Diners tear off pieces of injera with their right hand and use them to scoop up food, a bit like eating with roti. When the stews are finished, the plate is eaten too.

Pancake races and festivals

In Britain, Shrove Tuesday is called Pancake Day. It falls just before Lent, a time when many Christians traditionally gave up rich foods. Pancakes used up the eggs, milk and butter in the house. In the town of Olney, women run a race each year flipping a pancake in a frying pan, a custom said to date back to the 1400s.

In Russia, people eat piles of blini during Maslenitsa, a festival marking the end of winter. The round, golden blini are often said to look like the returning sun.

Why they brown

When batter hits a hot pan, sugars and proteins in it react to make hundreds of new flavour and colour chemicals. Food scientists call this part of cooking science the Maillard reaction, and it is why a crisp dosa and a golden pancake smell so good. The same reaction browns toast, roasted peanuts and the crust of bread.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The first pancake fails because of bad luck.

Truth: It is physics, not luck. The pan's surface is still heating unevenly and the oil has not spread properly. Cooks in many countries throw away, or quietly eat, the first one for this reason.

Try this

Put a spoon of baking soda in a glass and add a spoon of lemon juice. Watch the fizz. Those bubbles are carbon dioxide, the same gas that baking soda or baking powder makes inside pancake batter to puff it up.
