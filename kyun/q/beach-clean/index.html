Why do beach clean-ups matter?

In 2015 a Mumbai lawyer and his neighbour started picking rubbish off Versova beach. Three years later, baby sea turtles hatched there again.

The short answer

Most rubbish on beaches doesn't come from boats. It comes from land: blown off streets, washed down drains and rivers, or left behind by visitors. Once it's in the sea, it is very hard to get out again.

Picking it up on the beach catches it at the last step, before waves drag it back in. That helps animals right away. Turtles, seabirds and fish swallow plastic or get tangled in it, and many die.

Beach clean-ups do something else too. Volunteers count what they find. Year after year, those counts tell scientists and governments what kinds of rubbish are reaching the sea and where it is coming from, so they can stop it at the source.

Surprising but true

In the 2025 International Coastal Cleanup, the item volunteers found most often worldwide was the plastic drinks bottle, more than 1.3 million of them.

The Versova story

In October 2015, Afroz Shah, a lawyer in Mumbai, moved to a flat overlooking Versova beach. The sand was buried under plastic, in places more than a metre deep. With an elderly neighbour, he started picking it up by hand at weekends.

Fishing families, students and film stars began to join in. Week after week, volunteers hauled away truckloads of waste. In 2016 the United Nations Environment Programme named Shah a Champion of the Earth.

Then, in 2018, Olive Ridley turtle hatchlings were seen scrambling towards the sea at Versova. People who had lived there for years couldn't remember seeing that before. A clean beach had given sea turtles a place to nest again.

Twenty million pairs of gloves

The world's biggest clean-up is the International Coastal Cleanup, started by the American group Ocean Conservancy in 1986. Every September, volunteers in more than 100 countries spend a morning on beaches, riverbanks and lake shores.

Since it began, about 20 million people have collected over 405 million pounds of rubbish, about 184 million kilograms. That's heavier than the steel in many big ships.

Every item is logged on a data card or phone app. For years, cigarette ends were the most common thing found. In 2025, plastic drinks bottles topped the list, with bottle caps close behind.

What the counts change

Those tallies have helped convince cities and countries to act. When clean-ups keep finding the same plastic bags or straws, it becomes easier to argue for banning them. India banned many single-use plastic items, such as plastic straws and cutlery, in July 2022. Several US states have bottle deposits that pay people to return empty drink bottles.

The data also shows what clean-ups can't fix. Tiny pieces of plastic, called microplastics, are too small to pick up and are now found from the deepest trenches to Arctic ice.

What happens to things we recycle?A drinks can you recycle today could be back on a shop shelf as a new can in about two months.

Doing it safely

Beach clean-ups are best done in a group with adults. Volunteers wear gloves, carry sacks, and leave anything sharp, like broken glass or needles, for a grown-up with tongs. They also leave shells, seaweed and driftwood where they are, because those belong to the beach. Many groups weigh their sacks at the end, so they can report exactly how much they removed. Some also photograph unusual finds, from toothbrushes to car tyres, to help trace where rubbish comes from.

Sorting what's collected matters too. Some bottles and cans can go to recycling. Old fishing nets can be turned into carpets and clothes.

Starting upstream

A clean beach stays clean only if less rubbish arrives. That's why many groups now work along rivers and in towns far from the coast, cleaning drains and creeks. A crisp packet dropped in Pune can travel down rivers and streams for weeks before it reaches the sea. Stop it there, and nobody has to pick it off the sand.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A plastic bottle in the sea breaks down and disappears in a few years.

Truth: Sunlight and waves break plastic into smaller and smaller pieces called microplastics, but it doesn't vanish. Scientists estimate a bottle may take hundreds of years to break down fully.

Try this

Make a "litter tally" for your school yard or a park with an adult. Wear gloves, pick up rubbish for 15 minutes, and tick each item into groups like wrappers, bottles and caps. Your top three items show where the rubbish starts, which is the first step to stopping it.
