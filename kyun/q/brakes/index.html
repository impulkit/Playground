How do brakes stop a car?

Brakes don't destroy a car's speed. They turn it into heat, and on a racing car the discs can glow orange.

The short answer

A moving car has a lot of energy because it's heavy and fast. To stop it, that energy has to go somewhere. Brakes turn it into heat, using friction.

When you press the brake pedal, you push a piston that squeezes a special oil called brake fluid in a set of pipes. The fluid carries the push to all four wheels. At each wheel, the push presses hard, rough pads against a metal disc or drum that spins with the wheel. The rubbing slows the wheel down and heats the brakes.

The car slows, the brakes warm up, and the energy that was speed has become heat that drifts away into the air.

Surprising but true

Many electric cars and trains brake by turning their motors into generators, so stopping actually recharges the battery or sends power back into the wires.

Discs and drums

Most cars have disc brakes at least on the front wheels. A disc is a flat steel plate, a bit like a thick dinner plate, that turns with the wheel. A clamp called a caliper sits over its edge. When you brake, pistons in the caliper squeeze two pads onto either side of the disc, like fingers pinching a spinning coin.

Drum brakes, often on the back wheels of smaller cars, work from the inside. Curved shoes push outwards against the inside of a metal drum. A bicycle's rim brakes are a simple version of the same idea, with rubber blocks squeezing the wheel rim.

The front brakes do most of the work. When a car slows, its weight tips forward onto the front wheels, so they can grip harder.

The power of fluid

How does a light press of your foot stop a two-tonne car? The answer is fluid. Liquids can't be squashed, so a push on the fluid at one end comes out at the other. If the pistons at the wheels are wider than the one at the pedal, the force gets multiplied. Most cars also have a booster that adds extra help.

On Formula One cars, the brakes are so powerful that the carbon discs can heat up to around 1,000 °C. At night races you can see them glowing orange.

Brakes for trains

Trains are far heavier than cars and harder to stop. Early trains relied on brakemen who ran along the roofs turning a wheel on each carriage. In 1869, the American inventor George Westinghouse patented an air brake. A pipe ran the length of the train, and air pressure worked the brakes on every carriage at once. A few years later he improved it, so that if the train split in two and the pipe broke, the brakes came on by themselves.

Air brakes are still used around the world, including on Indian Railways. A long goods train can still need more than a kilometre to stop.

Stopping without wasting energy

Electric cars and many electric trains have a trick. When the driver slows down, the motor is switched to work as a generator. The wheels turn the motor, which makes electricity, and that resistance slows the vehicle. This is called regenerative braking.

In an electric car, the electricity goes back into the battery. On electric trains, such as Mumbai's suburban trains and the Vande Bharat expresses, it can be fed back into the overhead wires for other trains to use. Ordinary friction brakes are still there for hard stops and for the last few metres, when the motor can't do the job alone.

A system called ABS, now in most cars and many motorbikes, rapidly pulses the brakes in an emergency, many times a second. That stops the wheels locking and skidding, so the driver can steer around danger.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Anti-lock brakes (ABS) are mainly there to make a car stop in a much shorter distance.

Truth: ABS's main job is to stop the wheels locking up and skidding, so the driver can still steer while braking hard. On some surfaces it shortens the stop, and on loose gravel or snow it can even lengthen it.

Try this

Rub your palms together hard and fast for ten seconds and feel them warm up. That's friction turning movement into heat, exactly what brake pads do. Then ride a bicycle slowly with an adult, squeeze the brakes, and carefully feel near the brake pads afterwards. Notice the slight warmth.
