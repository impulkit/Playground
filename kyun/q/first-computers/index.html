What were the first computers like?

The word "computer" once meant a person. Rooms full of people did sums by hand before machines took over the job.

The short answer

The first computers were enormous, noisy and slow by today's standards. Before them, "computers" were people, often women, who did long calculations by hand for astronomers, engineers and armies.

The first electronic computers were built in the 1940s. They filled whole rooms, used thousands of glass vacuum tubes that glowed and often burned out, and were programmed by plugging in cables and flipping switches. They could do in seconds what took human computers days.

Within 30 years, transistors and microchips shrank computers from the size of a room to the size of a desk, and later to something that fits in your pocket.

Surprising but true

ENIAC, an early American computer finished in 1945, weighed about 30 tonnes, as much as five elephants, and had around 18,000 glass vacuum tubes.

Gears and brass

People have built calculating machines for a very long time. Around 2,100 years ago, the ancient Greeks made the Antikythera mechanism, a box of bronze gears that predicted eclipses and the positions of the Sun and Moon. It was found in a shipwreck in 1901.

In the 1800s, the English mathematician Charles Babbage designed steam-age machines of brass gears that could calculate and even follow programs on punched cards. He never finished building them, but his ideas came very close to a modern computer.

Machines that went to war

The Second World War pushed computing ahead fast. In Britain, codebreakers at Bletchley Park built Colossus, finished in 1944. It helped read secret German messages. Its existence was kept secret for decades. The mathematician Alan Turing also worked at Bletchley Park, on a different codebreaking machine.

In the USA, the Harvard Mark I, finished in 1944, was a giant calculator about 15 metres long, longer than a city bus. Grace Hopper was one of its first programmers.

ENIAC, the room-sized brain

ENIAC, built at the University of Pennsylvania and finished in 1945, was one of the first electronic computers that could be programmed for many tasks. It weighed about 30 tonnes and filled a large room. Its roughly 18,000 vacuum tubes used so much electricity that it gave off heat like a furnace.

Six women, including Kay McNulty, Betty Jennings and Frances Bilas, programmed it. For years their work was barely mentioned. Today they are recognised as pioneers of programming.

The tiny switch that changed everything

Vacuum tubes were big, hot and fragile. In 1947, scientists at Bell Labs in New Jersey invented the transistor, a small switch made from solid material with no glass and no glowing filament. By the 1960s, many transistors could be built together onto one chip.

Computers shrank, got cheaper and became more reliable. They guided rockets and helped NASA reach the Moon. By the late 1970s, people could buy personal computers for their homes.

Smaller and smaller

The first microprocessor, a whole processor on a single chip, went on sale in 1971. It was about the size of a fingernail yet did a similar amount of work to ENIAC, which had filled a room. Every computer since, from laptops to the chip in a toy, builds on that idea.

India's first computers

In 1956, the Indian Statistical Institute in Kolkata installed one of the first computers in India, to help with the huge calculations needed for planning and surveys.

In Mumbai, scientists at the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research decided to build one themselves. Their computer, called TIFRAC, was completed in 1960 using thousands of vacuum tubes. It was India's first home-built computer, and it helped train a generation of Indian engineers. Today India's computing industry employs millions, and its roots go back to rooms full of humming tubes.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Computer bugs got their name from a moth found inside an early computer.

Truth: Engineers were already calling faults "bugs" in Thomas Edison's time, in the 1870s. In 1947, a team working on the Harvard Mark II computer found a real moth in it and taped it in their logbook as a joke, because the word already existed.

Try this

Become a human computer team. Give three friends a long list of additions, and split the work so one adds, one checks and one writes the answers neatly. Time how long it takes. Then do the same sums on a calculator and compare. This is how many calculations were done before electronic computers.
