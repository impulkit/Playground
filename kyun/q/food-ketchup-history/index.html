Was ketchup once a medicine?

In the 1830s, Americans bought tomato ketchup pills from chemists, promised to cure everything from indigestion to diarrhoea.

The short answer

Yes, for a short while. In 1834, an American doctor named John Cook Bennett claimed that tomatoes could treat diarrhoea, indigestion and liver trouble. He suggested eating them cooked or in ketchup. Soon, quick-thinking sellers were marketing tomato extract pills as medicines. The craze lasted about twenty years before people realised the pills didn't work, and that some contained no tomato at all.

By then, ketchup had already had a long and strange history. The word probably comes from kê-tsiap, a fermented fish sauce from southern China. Early English ketchups were made from mushrooms and walnuts. The thick, sweet red sauce you squeeze on chips arrived only in the late 1800s.

Surprising but true

The first ketchups had no tomatoes at all. They were salty sauces made from fish, mushrooms or walnuts.

Fish sauce on a sailing ship

In southern China and Southeast Asia, people have long made sauces by fermenting fish with salt. In the Hokkien language, one of these was called something like kê-tsiap. British traders in the 1600s tasted it and liked how it kept for months without spoiling.

Back in England, cooks tried to copy it. They didn't have the right fish, so they used what was around them: mushrooms, walnuts, oysters and anchovies. A 1700s English ketchup was dark, thin and salty. Cookbooks of the time often had several ketchup recipes side by side. You can still buy mushroom ketchup in Britain today.

The tomato's bad reputation

For a long time, many Europeans and Americans were suspicious of tomatoes. The plant belongs to the nightshade family, which includes some poisonous plants. Some people believed tomatoes caused illness. Gardeners grew them for their bright colour as much as for food.

By the early 1800s, Americans were cooking with tomatoes more, and tomato ketchup recipes began to appear. One from 1812, by a scientist named James Mease, is often called the first published tomato ketchup recipe. It had no sugar.

Ketchup pills

Then came Dr Bennett. In lectures and newspaper articles in 1834, he praised the tomato as a cure for many problems. Newspapers across the country repeated his claims. Other sellers, including a man named Archibald Miles, rushed out "tomato pills" and "extract of tomato", with big promises and big adverts.

It was a time before medicine was carefully tested, and strange cures were everywhere. Competitors accused each other of fraud. When people tested some of the pills, they found no tomato in them at all. By the 1850s, the tomato pill boom had collapsed. Tomatoes stayed, as food.

Heinz and the glass bottle

In 1876, Henry J. Heinz, in Pittsburgh, began selling tomato ketchup. Many ketchups back then were badly made and used chemical preservatives to stop them rotting. In the early 1900s, Heinz switched to a thicker, sweeter recipe with more vinegar and ripe tomatoes, so it kept well without those chemicals. He sold it in clear glass bottles so shoppers could see it was clean.

Thick ketchup is slow to pour, which is why people have tapped the "57" on the neck of a Heinz bottle for generations. Ketchup is a non-Newtonian fluid, one that suddenly runs more easily once you shake or tap it. Too gentle and nothing comes out. Too hard and half the bottle lands on your plate.

Ketchup in India

In India, ketchup spread through cities in the late 1900s, eaten alongside samosas, pakoras and Maggi noodles. Many Indian brands make it spicier or tangier, and some sell versions without onion and garlic. Ketchup is a cousin of Indian tomato chutney, which also mixes tomato, sugar, vinegar or tamarind, and spices. Today, ketchup sits on tables from Texas to Thane, usually next to a pile of fries or a burger, and nobody expects it to cure anything.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Ketchup has always been a tomato sauce, invented in America.

Truth: The word and the idea came from a fermented fish sauce in southern China and Southeast Asia. British sailors and traders brought it home in the 1600s and 1700s, and cooks made it from whatever they had. Tomatoes came into it later, in America.

Try this

Do a ketchup label hunt. Look at a ketchup bottle at home or in a shop and read the list of ingredients. Ingredients are listed from most to least by weight, so find where sugar comes on the list. Compare two brands, or a ketchup and a homemade tomato chutney recipe.
