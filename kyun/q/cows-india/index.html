Why are cows so important in Indian villages?

India's humped cattle were tamed over 8,000 years ago, and their descendants now graze on ranches in Texas and Brazil.

The short answer

For thousands of years, cattle have been at the heart of village life in India. Cows give milk, which becomes curd, ghee and paneer. Bullocks pull ploughs and carts. Dung is dried for cooking fuel, spread on fields as manure and used to plaster mud floors.

Cattle also matter for religious and cultural reasons. Many Hindus regard the cow as sacred, a gentle, giving animal linked to the god Krishna, who grew up as a cowherd. Other communities in India have their own traditions and views about cattle.

Today India has about 19 crore cattle, one of the biggest herds in the world, and together with buffaloes they make it the world's biggest milk producer.

Surprising but true

Brahman cattle, a famous American ranch breed, were bred in Texas from cattle brought over from India.

The humped cattle of India

Indian cattle look different from the cows in a European or American picture book. They are zebu, with a hump of muscle and fat over the shoulders, big drooping ears and a fold of loose skin under the neck called a dewlap.

Those features help them cope with heat. Loose skin gives off more warmth, and zebu sweat more than European cattle. They also put up better with ticks, flies and dry, poor grass.

Zebu were tamed from wild cattle called aurochs, near the Indus valley, more than 8,000 years ago. A few thousand years later, seals from the Indus cities were carved with proud humped bulls.

Working partners

Before tractors, almost every farm in India depended on a pair of bullocks. They pulled ploughs through the soil, turned wheels that lifted water from wells, crushed sugarcane and carried crops to market in carts.

Dung was just as useful. Pressed into flat cakes and dried in the sun, it burns slowly in a village stove. Mixed with water, it makes a smooth, clean floor coating. In biogas plants, dung is left to rot in a closed tank, giving off a gas that can be used for cooking. What's left over is good fertiliser.

How did India become the world's top milk producer?India makes about a quarter of the world's milk, and most of it comes from small farmers with just two or three animals each.

Cows in belief and festivals

In Hindu tradition, Krishna is often called Gopala, the protector of cows. Shiva rides a bull named Nandi, whose statues sit facing many Shiva temples. Kamadhenu, a wish-granting cow, appears in old stories.

Festivals honour cattle too. During Pongal in Tamil Nadu, one day is Mattu Pongal, when cattle are washed, their horns painted and garlanded. In Maharashtra, farmers celebrate their bullocks at the festival of Pola. Many states have laws protecting cows, though these laws differ from state to state.

From Gujarat to Texas

Indian cattle have travelled the world. In the 1800s and early 1900s, ranchers in the USA brought zebu cattle from India, including Gir, Ongole and Kankrej. In Texas, they crossed them with other cattle to make the Brahman breed, which handles the hot, humid Gulf Coast well.

In Brazil, Ongole cattle from Andhra Pradesh became the Nelore, now the country's most common beef breed. Gir cows from Gujarat have become important dairy cattle there too.

Milk, machines and change

In the 1970s, India launched Operation Flood, which linked village dairy cooperatives to cities. It was led by Verghese Kurien and grew out of the Amul cooperative in Anand, Gujarat. By the late 1990s India had become the largest milk producer in the world, a story told in the White Revolution.

Village life has changed since then. Tractors have replaced many bullocks. Farmers often prefer crossbred cows that give more milk, so some native breeds have become rare. The government and breeders are now working to protect them, because those heat-hardy Indian cattle may matter even more in a warming world.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Cows have four stomachs.

Truth: A cow has one stomach divided into four parts. The biggest part is full of microbes that break down tough grass, and the cow chews its cud, bringing food back up to chew again, to help them.
