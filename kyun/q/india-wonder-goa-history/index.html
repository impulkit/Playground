Why is Goa so different from the rest of India?

Goa was ruled by Portugal for 451 years and only joined India in 1961, fourteen years after the rest of the country became free.

The short answer

Goa feels different because its history is different. While most of India was ruled by the British, Goa was ruled by Portugal, from 1510 until 1961. That is 451 years, longer than almost any other European rule in Asia.

Over those centuries, Portuguese ideas mixed with Konkani, Hindu and Muslim traditions. Whitewashed churches stand near ancient temples, and a local bread called pao is sold from bicycles every morning. The food, music, houses and festivals feel like nowhere else in India.

Goa is India's smallest state, about 3,700 square km. It joined India on 19 December 1961 and became a full state in 1987.

Surprising but true

Everyday Hindi words such as pav (bread), chabi (key), almari (cupboard) and balti (bucket) came into Indian languages from Portuguese.

How the Portuguese arrived

In 1498, the Portuguese sailor Vasco da Gama reached Kerala by sea, the first European to sail round Africa to India. Portugal wanted control of the spice trade. In 1510, the commander Afonso de Albuquerque captured Goa from the Sultan of Bijapur.

Goa became the capital of Portugal's empire in Asia. Old Goa grew into a grand city of churches, monasteries and markets, with ships sailing to Lisbon, Africa, China and Japan. The Basilica of Bom Jesus, built in 1605, still holds the remains of the missionary St Francis Xavier. The churches and convents of Old Goa are a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

A hard side of history

Portuguese rule was not gentle for everyone. Many Goans were converted to Christianity, some under pressure. From 1560, the Goa Inquisition, a church court, punished people for following Hindu, Muslim or Jewish practices, and many temples were destroyed. Some Hindu families moved their temples' idols to nearby lands outside Portuguese control.

Today Goa's people are mostly Hindu, with a large Christian minority and a Muslim minority. Festivals of all three are celebrated widely, from Ganesh Chaturthi to Christmas, and Carnival before Lent fills the streets with floats and music.

Food from across the oceans

Portuguese ships carried plants from the Americas, and many reached India through Goa. Chillies, potatoes, tomatoes, pineapples, papayas and cashews all came this way. Indian cooking would be hard to recognise without them.

Goan food blends both worlds. Vindaloo comes from a Portuguese meat dish made with wine and garlic, "vinha d'alhos", which Goan cooks changed with vinegar and chillies. Xacuti, sorpotel and bebinca, a layered coconut cake, are Goan favourites. Feni, a strong drink for adults, is made from cashew fruit.

Words, houses and laws

The Portuguese left words behind in Konkani, Marathi and Hindi too. Pav, the soft bread in Mumbai's vada pav, comes from Portuguese "pão". Chabi (key), almari (cupboard), balti (bucket) and girja (church) are others. Many Indians use them every day without knowing.

Goan houses also look different, with tiled roofs, wide verandas, coloured walls and windows once paned with thin oyster shells instead of glass. And Goa follows its own civil code, based on an old Portuguese law, for matters like marriage and property, applying the same rules to people of all religions.

Freedom, fourteen years late

When India became independent in 1947, Portugal refused to leave Goa, saying it was part of Portugal itself. Goan freedom fighters protested for years, and many were jailed. In December 1961, after talks failed, Indian troops entered Goa in an operation called Vijay. Portuguese forces surrendered within about two days.

Goa was a Union Territory until 1987, when it became India's 25th state and Konkani became its official language. Today millions of visitors come for the beaches, but the old churches, spice farms and village feasts tell the deeper story. And across the sea in Mumbai, every vada pav carries a little piece of Portugal, inside a word that crossed oceans.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Goa became part of India in 1947 along with the rest of the country.

Truth: In 1947, Goa was still ruled by Portugal, which refused to leave. Indian troops entered Goa in December 1961, and Portugal's rule ended after 451 years.
