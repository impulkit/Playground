What is the Tower of London?

For centuries the Tower of London kept lions, an elephant and even a polar bear, which was allowed to fish in the River Thames on a long chain.

The short answer

The Tower of London is a castle on the north bank of the River Thames in London. Despite its name, it is not one tower but a group of buildings inside two rings of thick stone walls, with a dry moat around the outside.

It was begun nearly 1,000 years ago by William the Conqueror, a French-speaking duke from Normandy who invaded England in 1066. Over the centuries it has been a royal palace, a fortress, a prison, a zoo, a place for making coins and a store for weapons. The Royal Mint, which makes Britain's coins, worked inside its walls for about 500 years, until it moved out in the early 1800s. Today it is a museum. Millions of visitors come each year to see the Crown Jewels, the old walls and the guards in red and black uniforms.

Surprising but true

Despite its grim fame, only about 20 people were ever executed inside the Tower's walls, and just seven of them before the 1900s. Most executions took place on Tower Hill, just outside.

Built to frighten

After William conquered England, he needed to show Londoners who was in charge. Around 1078 work began on a huge square stone keep, later called the White Tower. It was far taller than anything else in the city and looked down over the river.

Later kings added walls, towers, gates and a moat, making it one of the strongest castles in England. Its gate onto the river became known as Traitors' Gate, because some prisoners arrived by boat through it.

The Tower is still guarded by Yeoman Warders, nicknamed Beefeaters, in uniforms that date back to Tudor times. To join, they must have served in the armed forces for at least 22 years. They live inside the walls with their families and lead visitors on tours.

Prisoners and mysteries

The Tower held many famous prisoners. Anne Boleyn, a wife of King Henry VIII, was executed there in 1536. A young Princess Elizabeth, later Queen Elizabeth I, was held there for about two months. Two young princes, Edward and Richard, disappeared there in 1483, and historians still argue about what happened to them.

Its reputation for executions is bigger than the facts. Only a small number happened inside, while many more took place in public on nearby Tower Hill.

Lions, a bear and the ravens

For about 600 years, kings kept a royal menagerie at the Tower. In 1252, King Henry III was given a white bear, probably a polar bear, from Norway. It was led on a chain to fish in the Thames. An elephant, a gift from France, arrived a few years later. The animals were moved out in the 1830s, and many went to the new London Zoo, an early chapter in the story of zoos.

Today, the Tower is famous for ravens. Legend says that if they ever leave, the kingdom will fall. At least six live there, looked after by a Ravenmaster. Ravens are clever birds, as smart as crows, and some have escaped or been sent away for bad behaviour.

The Crown Jewels

The Crown Jewels are kept in a heavily guarded Jewel House. They include the crowns, sceptres and orbs used at coronations, including the one in 2023. One of the most famous stones is the Koh-i-Noor, a diamond from India, now set in a crown made in 1937.

The Koh-i-Noor came to Britain in 1849, after the British East India Company took over the Punjab from the young Sikh ruler Duleep Singh. India, Pakistan and others have asked for it to be returned. The British government says it was acquired legally. The argument continues, and the diamond stays behind glass.

Every night, Yeoman Warders lock the Tower's gates in a short ceremony called the Ceremony of the Keys, said to have been held for about 700 years.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Hundreds of people had their heads chopped off inside the Tower of London.

Truth: Before the 1900s only seven people were executed inside the walls, mostly important prisoners such as two of Henry VIII's queens. Many more were executed in public on Tower Hill, outside.
