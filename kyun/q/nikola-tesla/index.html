Who was Nikola Tesla?

The electricity flowing into your home right now travels as alternating current, a system Nikola Tesla helped design in the 1880s.

The short answer

Nikola Tesla was an inventor and engineer, born in what is now Croatia, who did most of his work in the United States. He is best known for designing motors and systems that run on alternating current, or AC. Most of the world's electricity grids still use AC today.

He also invented the Tesla coil, which makes sparks crackle through the air, and did early experiments with radio and remote control. He was brilliant and unusual, and he died poor. In recent years he has become something of a hero online, and his life story is now mixed up with a fair amount of legend.

Surprising but true

The unit used to measure the strength of a magnetic field is called the tesla, after him.

A boy who could see machines

Tesla was born in 1856 in the village of Smiljan, into a Serbian family. His father was a priest. As a student in Austria, he grew curious about a type of motor and wondered whether it could work without the sparking brushes that wore out.

Tesla said he could picture machines in his mind in great detail and test them there before building anything. In 1882, while walking in a park in Budapest, he later recalled, the idea for a motor driven by a rotating magnetic field came to him all at once.

Working for Edison

In 1884 Tesla sailed to New York with very little money. He worked for Thomas Edison's company for a few months. Edison's system used direct current, or DC, which flows one way. It was hard to send DC far, so power stations had to be close to homes.

Tesla believed alternating current, which switches direction many times a second, was better. AC can be raised to high voltage for long journeys and lowered again for safe use in homes. The two men disagreed, and Tesla left. Their rivalry, and the wider fight between the Edison and Westinghouse companies, became known as the war of the currents.

Lighting up America

In 1888 Tesla patented his AC motor. The businessman George Westinghouse bought the rights and hired him. In 1893, Westinghouse lit the Chicago World's Fair with AC, using thousands of bulbs.

The biggest test came at Niagara Falls. A power station there began working in 1895, and in 1896 it sent electricity about 40 kilometres to the city of Buffalo. It used AC generators built on Tesla's ideas. After that, AC spread across the United States and the world. In India, too, the electricity in homes is AC, switching direction 50 times a second.

Coils, radio and a tower

In 1891 Tesla invented the Tesla coil, which produces very high voltages and long, crackling sparks. He used it to explore wireless lighting and signals. In 1898 he steered a small boat by radio in front of a crowd in New York.

He dreamed of sending power and messages through the air with no wires at all. On Long Island he began building Wardenclyffe Tower, a giant mast. His money ran out, the project was abandoned and the tower was pulled down in 1917. Some of his ideas helped shape radio, though many people deserve credit for that.

A strange and lonely end

Tesla never married. In later life he lived in New York hotels, fed pigeons in the parks and announced inventions that were never built. He died alone in a hotel room in 1943.

His name lives on. The tesla is the scientific unit of magnetic field strength. An American company named its electric cars after him, and his face once appeared on a Yugoslav banknote. The Nikola Tesla Museum in Belgrade, Serbia, holds his papers and his ashes.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Tesla invented radio and Marconi stole it.

Truth: Many people worked out pieces of radio, including Tesla, Marconi, Jagadish Chandra Bose and Oliver Lodge. Marconi built the first practical long-distance system. In 1943 a US court cancelled some of Marconi's patents because earlier inventors, Tesla among them, had come up with key ideas first.

Try this

Put a compass flat on a table and slowly bring a fridge magnet towards it from different sides. Watch the needle swing. Now move the magnet in a circle around the compass. Tesla's motor used magnetic fields that rotate in a similar way to keep a shaft spinning.
