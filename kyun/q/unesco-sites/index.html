What is a World Heritage Site?

In the 1960s, the world cut two giant Egyptian temples into blocks and moved them uphill to save them from a flood.

The short answer

A World Heritage Site is a place so important that it is treated as belonging to all of humanity, as well as to the country it sits in. It might be a building, like the Taj Mahal, a ruined city, a forest or a whole mountain range.

The list is kept by UNESCO, the part of the United Nations that looks after education, science and culture. Countries suggest places, experts check them, and a committee decides each year which ones join.

There are now more than 1,200 sites in over 160 countries. India has more than 40 of them, and the United States has about 25.

Surprising but true

Being on the World Heritage list is not forever. A site can be removed if it is badly damaged, and a few, such as the Dresden Elbe Valley in Germany, have been.

The temples that moved

The idea grew out of a rescue. In the 1950s, Egypt decided to build the Aswan High Dam across the Nile. Its huge lake would flood ancient temples, including Abu Simbel, carved into a cliff more than 3,000 years ago.

UNESCO asked the world for help, and about 50 countries joined in. Between 1964 and 1968, engineers sliced Abu Simbel into large blocks, lifted them about 65 m up the hill, and fitted them back together. People realised that some places were too precious to leave to one country alone.

A promise made in 1972

In 1972, countries signed a treaty at UNESCO in Paris called the World Heritage Convention. They promised to protect special places at home and help protect them elsewhere.

The first 12 sites were listed in 1978. They included the Galápagos Islands and Yellowstone in the USA. India's first four came in 1983: the Taj Mahal, Agra Fort, and the cave temples of Ajanta and Ellora.

How was the Kailasa temple carved from one rock?The Kailasa temple at Ellora was not built up from blocks. It was carved down into a cliff, like a giant sculpture cut from a single rock.

Three kinds of treasure

Sites are cultural, natural or mixed. Cultural sites are made by people, such as the ruins of Hampi or Dholavira, one of the great Indus cities, added in 2021. Natural sites protect wild places, like Kaziranga, home of the one-horned rhino, or the Grand Canyon.

A few sites are both. India's only mixed site is Khangchendzonga National Park in Sikkim, which protects high mountains and also places sacred to the people who live there.

How a place gets on the list

Getting listed takes years. A country first puts a place on a "tentative list" of hopefuls. Then it writes a long report, called a nomination, explaining why the place has "outstanding universal value", meaning it matters to people everywhere.

Experts visit and write their own reports. For cultural sites, this is a group called ICOMOS. For natural sites, it is the IUCN, a world nature organisation. Finally, the World Heritage Committee, made up of 21 countries, meets each summer and votes.

The place must also have a plan for its care: who guards it, how visitors are managed and what building is allowed nearby. In 2023, the temples of the Hoysala kings in Karnataka and Santiniketan in West Bengal, where the poet Rabindranath Tagore built his school, were both added after this long process.

When a site is in trouble

Some sites are put on a "List of World Heritage in Danger" because of war, floods, pollution or too much building. That warning often brings help and money.

If things get worse, a site can be removed. Germany's Dresden Elbe Valley lost its place in 2009 after a large road bridge was built across it. In 2021, the waterfront of Liverpool in England was removed too. Being listed is an honour, and it comes with homework that never really ends.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Once a place becomes a World Heritage Site, UNESCO owns it and runs it.

Truth: The country where the site is keeps owning and looking after it. UNESCO keeps the list, gives advice and sometimes helps with money. If a country lets a site fall apart, UNESCO can warn it and, in the end, take the site off the list.

Try this

Make a heritage list for your own neighbourhood. Pick three places or things that are old or special, such as a tree, a temple, a shop or a well. For each one, write why it matters and what would be lost if it disappeared. Then ask an older neighbour what they would add.
