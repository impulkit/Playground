What was Nalanda University?

Students walked for months across mountains to reach a monastery in Bihar, and some failed the entrance test at the gate.

The short answer

Nalanda was a huge Buddhist monastery and centre of learning in Bihar, about 90 km southeast of Patna. It was founded in the 5th century CE, during the Gupta age, and flourished for around 700 years.

Monks and students lived there, studying Buddhist philosophy along with logic, grammar, medicine and astronomy. Scholars came from China, Korea, Tibet and Southeast Asia. Because it had teachers, students, lecture halls and a library, it is often called one of the world's first universities, long before Oxford or Bologna. Around 1200 CE it was attacked and destroyed. Its red-brick ruins are now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Surprising but true

According to the Chinese monk Xuanzang, gatekeepers quizzed visitors on hard questions, and most who tried were turned away.

A visitor from China

In the 7th century, a Chinese monk named Xuanzang set out to find the original Buddhist texts. He crossed deserts and mountains along the Silk Road and spent several years studying at Nalanda.

His account and his biography describe tall towers, pools with blue lotuses and teachers known across Asia. He says about 10,000 people lived there. Xuanzang took hundreds of texts back to China and spent the rest of his life translating them. Much of what we know about Nalanda comes from him and from another Chinese monk, Yijing, who came a few decades later.

Life inside the walls

Students lived in monasteries built around open courtyards, each with small stone cells for sleeping and studying. Days were filled with lessons, debates and prayers.

Debate mattered a lot. Students learned to argue a point and defend it against sharp questions. Nalanda was supported by kings, including Harsha in the 7th century and later the Pala rulers of Bengal. Grants of villages paid for food and supplies, so students did not have to pay fees.

How did libraries begin?One of the oldest libraries ever found was made of clay. When enemies burned it, the fire baked the tablets hard and helped them survive.

Books by the thousand

Tibetan tradition says Nalanda's library was spread over three buildings with poetic names: Sea of Jewels, Ocean of Jewels and Delighter of Jewels. Books were copied by hand on palm leaves and birch bark.

Some Nalanda manuscripts survived because monks carried them to Tibet and Nepal. Today a few are kept in museums far from Bihar. Each one was copied by a scribe working slowly, one letter at a time.

Paper was not yet in common use in India, so a single long text could fill a stack of leaves as thick as a brick. Losing a library like this meant losing books that might exist nowhere else. That is why scholars treasure every leaf that escaped.

How it ended

By the 12th century, Buddhism had weakened in India. Around 1200 CE, soldiers led by Bakhtiyar Khalji attacked monasteries in Bihar. A history written a few decades later describes a monastery being destroyed and its monks killed. Scholars still discuss whether that account refers to Nalanda or to a nearby monastery, but Nalanda was ruined around this time.

Over the centuries, the site disappeared under earth and grass. The Archaeological Survey of India began careful excavations in 1915 and uncovered monasteries, temples and stupas.

Nalanda reborn

In 2016, UNESCO listed the ruins as a World Heritage Site. You can walk among the thick brick walls and peer into the tiny rooms where students once slept.

Not far away, near Rajgir, a new Nalanda University opened in 2014, with help from several Asian countries. It moved into a new campus in 2024. The old name is back, along with its idea of students from many lands learning together.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Nalanda's library was so big it burned for six months.

Truth: This story is very popular, but no record from the time describes it. It first appears in much later writings. The destruction was real, but the six-month fire may be a legend.
