Who was Mahavira?

Some Jain monks sweep the ground before every step so they won't crush an ant. The idea goes back to a teacher who lived 2,500 years ago.

The short answer

Mahavira was a spiritual teacher who lived in north India around 2,500 years ago, about the same time as the Buddha. He was born Vardhamana, a prince, near Vaishali in Bihar.

At 30 he gave up his home and possessions to live as a wandering monk. After more than twelve years of hard discipline and meditation, Jains believe he reached perfect knowledge. He then taught for about 30 years. People called him Mahavira, "the great hero", because he had conquered his own desires. His followers are called Jains, and their most important teaching is ahimsa, not harming any living being.

Surprising but true

Jains celebrate Diwali too. For them, it marks the night Mahavira reached final liberation at Pavapuri in Bihar.

A prince who gave everything away

According to Jain tradition, Vardhamana grew up in comfort. One version of his story says he waited until his parents had died, then left home with his elder brother's permission.

Jains believe he walked from village to village for twelve and a half years, often fasting, speaking little and enduring heat, cold and insults. Jain traditions say that in time he gave up even his clothes. At 42, sitting under a sal tree on a riverbank, he reached kevala jnana, which Jains understand as complete, all-seeing knowledge.

Harm nothing

Mahavira taught that every living thing has a soul, from humans and animals down to insects, plants and even tiny beings in water and earth. Harming any of them weighs down your own soul.

That is why some Jain monks and nuns sweep the path in front of them with a soft brush and cover their mouths with cloth. Many Jains eat before sunset, so they don't swallow insects in the dark. They are strict vegetarians, and many avoid root vegetables. It is a very demanding way to live, and Jains take it seriously.

Why are so many Indians vegetarian?India has more vegetarians than any other country, but the reasons, and the numbers, change a great deal from state to state.

Five promises

Jain monks and nuns take five great vows: not to harm, not to lie, not to steal, to stay celibate, and not to own things. Ordinary Jains follow gentler forms of the same vows.

The last one, aparigraha, means not clinging to possessions. Jains believe that owning too much ties you down, just as harming others does. Many Jain families are known for giving generously to animal shelters, hospitals and schools. In Delhi, a Jain bird hospital near the Red Fort has treated injured pigeons, sparrows and other birds for decades.

Many sides of the truth

Mahavira also taught anekantavada, the idea that truth has many sides and no single view captures all of it.

Jains often explain it with the story of several blind men touching an elephant. One feels the trunk and says it's a snake. Another feels a leg and says it's a tree trunk. Each is partly right and none is wholly right. The story is told in Buddhist and Hindu traditions too, and it is a gentle reminder to listen before arguing.

Mahavira's legacy

Mahavira died at 72 at Pavapuri in Bihar. Jains celebrate his birth on Mahavir Jayanti, and his final liberation on Diwali.

Jainism has stayed fairly small, with about 45 lakh followers in India in 2011, but its influence is wide. At Shravanabelagola in Karnataka, a 17-metre statue of the Jain saint Bahubali, carved from a single rock more than 1,000 years ago, draws pilgrims from across India. Mahatma Gandhi grew up in Gujarat among Jain neighbours and friends, and his idea of fighting without violence owes something to Mahavira's ahimsa.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Mahavira founded Jainism.

Truth: Jains believe Mahavira was the last of 24 great teachers called Tirthankaras. He revived and organised a much older tradition. The 23rd, Parshvanatha, is thought by many historians to have been a real person too.
