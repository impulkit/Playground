Were dinosaurs warm-blooded?

Dinosaurs raised babies in northern Alaska, where the Sun disappears for weeks every winter.

The short answer

Probably many of them were, at least partly. Warm-blooded animals, like birds and you, burn food to make their own body heat. Cold-blooded animals, like lizards and crocodiles, mostly warm up by lying in the Sun.

For a long time, dinosaurs were pictured as giant, sluggish lizards. Today, the evidence points another way. Dinosaurs grew fast, many had feathers for keeping warm, and some lived near the North Pole. The open question is how warm, and whether every kind of dinosaur was the same.

Surprising but true

Chemistry inside fossil eggshells can work like a thermometer, showing how warm the mother's body was when she made the egg.

From lizard to bird

When dinosaurs were first named in the 1840s, scientists thought of them as huge reptiles. Paintings showed them lumbering and dragging their tails in swamps. Then, in 1964, the American scientist John Ostrom found Deinonychus in Montana, a slim hunter with a big claw that looked built for leaping and slashing.

Ostrom argued it must have been active and agile, more like a bird than a lizard. His student Robert Bakker went further and said dinosaurs were fully warm-blooded. Their ideas started what is often called the "dinosaur renaissance", a rethink that changed how dinosaurs look in every museum.

Footprints helped too. Dinosaur trackways almost never show marks of a dragging tail. Tails were held up off the ground, balancing the body over the hips like a seesaw. Their legs stood straight under the body rather than sprawling out to the sides like a lizard's.

Clues in the bones

Cut a thin slice of a lizard bone and you see slow, neat growth rings. Most dinosaur bones look different. They are full of channels where blood vessels ran, a type of bone that forms when an animal grows quickly. Fast growth needs plenty of energy, and that needs a fast-burning body.

There is a clever chemical test too. As an animal makes an eggshell, the heat of its body leaves a tiny signature in the way certain atoms are arranged. The shell works like a thermometer frozen in stone. In 2015, scientists tested fossil eggshells this way. A titanosaur from Argentina came out at about 38°C, close to a human. A smaller oviraptor-like dinosaur from Mongolia came out cooler, around 32°C.

Dinosaurs in the dark

Some of the best evidence comes from northern Alaska. Along the Colville River, scientists have found bones of many kinds of dinosaur, including babies that had only just hatched. Seventy million years ago this area was already far north. Winters had weeks of darkness and may have brought snow.

The world was warmer then, but these winters were still cold. A lizard-like animal could not have warmed up in the Sun for weeks on end. Raising young there suggests dinosaurs that could keep themselves warm from inside.

Somewhere in the middle?

Not everyone thinks dinosaurs were as warm as birds. In 2014, one team compared growth rates of hundreds of living and extinct animals. They suggested many dinosaurs were "in-between", making some of their own heat without keeping it perfectly steady. Tuna, some sharks and leatherback turtles work a bit like this today.

Big animals also hold heat simply because of their size. A giant sauropod lost heat slowly, the way a full pot of hot tea cools more slowly than a cup. It may have struggled more to keep cool than to keep warm. A small feathered hunter faced the opposite problem, which is one more reason the answer probably differed from one kind of dinosaur to another.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Dinosaurs were slow, cold-blooded lizards that dragged their tails.

Truth: Footprints show their tails were held up, and bones show they grew fast. Many dinosaurs were active animals that made at least some of their own body heat, and their closest living relatives, birds, are fully warm-blooded.

Try this

Fill two identical cups with warm water. Wrap one in a woolly sock or scarf and leave the other bare. After 20 minutes, dip a finger in each (ask an adult to check the water isn't too hot to start with). The wrapped one stays warmer. That is what a coat of feathers does.
