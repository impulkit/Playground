What happened to the Sphinx's nose?

Many people blame Napoleon's soldiers for shooting off the Great Sphinx's nose, but drawings made decades before they arrived already show it missing.

The short answer

The Great Sphinx of Giza, a lion with a human head carved from a hill of limestone, has guarded the desert near the pyramids for about 4,500 years. Its nose, about 1 m wide, is missing.

For a long time, people liked to say that Napoleon's soldiers knocked it off with cannon fire. They didn't. A Danish traveller, Frederic Louis Norden, drew it without a nose in 1737, long before Napoleon came. Marks on the face show the nose was deliberately pried off with rods or chisels. The Egyptian historian al-Maqrizi, writing in the 1400s, said a man named Muhammad Sa'im al-Dahr damaged the face in 1378.

Surprising but true

Pieces of the Sphinx's stone beard are kept in the British Museum in London, thousands of kilometres from the rest of its face.

A lion as long as a jumbo jet

The Sphinx is about 73 m long, about as long as a large passenger jet, and 20 m high, as tall as a six-storey building. Most experts think it was carved for the pharaoh Khafre, whose pyramid stands behind it. Its face may even be his portrait.

It wasn't built from blocks. Workers carved it out of the bedrock, cutting away the stone around it. The head is made of harder rock than the body, which is why the body has worn away more.

When new, the Sphinx was probably painted. Traces of red paint survive on its face. It wore a royal striped headcloth, and at some point gained a long, braided beard like the gods.

Who took the nose?

According to al-Maqrizi, people living near Giza were making offerings to the Sphinx, hoping it would bring good harvests. In 1378, a Sufi sheikh named Muhammad Sa'im al-Dahr was angered by what he saw as idol worship and damaged the face. Al-Maqrizi also wrote that sand blew over the area afterwards, which locals took as revenge.

This is the best-known explanation, but al-Maqrizi was writing decades after the event. Some historians think the face may have been damaged more than once over the centuries. What's clear from the tool marks is that people did it.

Why blame Napoleon?

In 1798, Napoleon invaded Egypt with an army and also more than 150 scientists, artists and engineers. They measured and drew monuments, and their soldiers found the Rosetta Stone, which later helped scholars read hieroglyphs.

Because French soldiers were there with cannons, the story of them using the Sphinx for target practice was easy to believe. It still appears in films and cartoons. But Norden's drawing and other older pictures prove the nose was gone before 1798.

Buried, found and rebuilt

For much of its life, the Sphinx was buried up to its shoulders in sand. Around 3,400 years ago, a prince who later became the pharaoh Thutmose IV is said to have dozed beside it. In a dream, the Sphinx promised him the throne if he cleared the sand. He did, and set up a stone slab, the Dream Stele, between its paws. It's still there.

Later diggers cleared the sand again in the 1800s and 1920s. Pieces of the fallen beard were found and taken to the British Museum and to Cairo. Today, conservators patch crumbling stone on the body and watch out for groundwater, which can damage the limestone from below. Repairing old statues is a delicate job, and the experts behind art restoration argue about how much to fix.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Napoleon's soldiers shot the Sphinx's nose off with a cannon.

Truth: A drawing published in 1755, from a visit in 1737, already shows the Sphinx without its nose. Napoleon arrived in Egypt in 1798, more than 60 years later.
