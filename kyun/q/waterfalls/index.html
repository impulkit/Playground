How do waterfalls form?

Waterfalls walk backwards. Niagara Falls has crept about 11 km upstream since it formed at the end of the last ice age.

The short answer

A waterfall forms where a river suddenly drops over a steep step. Most often, the river flows over a layer of hard rock lying on top of softer rock. The water wears the soft rock away faster, and a step appears. Water tumbling over the edge digs a deep hole at the bottom, called a plunge pool, and scoops out the soft rock behind the falls.

Eventually the hard ledge on top is left hanging with nothing underneath. It breaks off, and the waterfall moves a little upstream. Over thousands of years, this repeats again and again, leaving a deep, narrow gorge downstream. Glaciers, earthquakes and volcanoes can make waterfalls too.

Surprising but true

In 1969, engineers turned off the American side of Niagara Falls for several months. They built a temporary dam so geologists could study the bare rock face and see how fast it was crumbling.

Hard rock, soft rock

Rivers carry sand and stones that scrape at their beds, wearing them down. But different rocks wear away at different speeds. Hard rocks like sandstone, granite or limestone resist. Soft rocks like shale crumble easily.

When a river crosses from hard rock onto soft, the soft part erodes faster and drops lower. The water falls further each year, so it hits harder and digs deeper, speeding up the process. The spray splashes back against the cliff, and the soft rock behind the curtain of water wears away into a hollow. Some waterfalls have a gap behind them big enough to walk into, with a curtain of water roaring past your nose.

Niagara on the move

Niagara Falls, on the border of the USA and Canada, formed about 12,000 years ago, when the ice sheets of the last ice age melted. A thick layer of hard dolostone sits on top of soft shale. The river has been chewing through it ever since.

The falls have moved about 11 km upstream, leaving the Niagara Gorge behind. In the past they retreated around a metre a year. Today, much of the river is sent through tunnels to power stations before it reaches the edge, which has slowed the erosion to a fraction of that.

In 1969, engineers built a temporary dam to stop the water flowing over the smaller American Falls. For several months, visitors could walk on the dry riverbed and look at the bare cliff. Engineers decided against trying to tidy away the fallen boulders at its foot.

Other ways to make a waterfall

Glaciers are powerful diggers. A big glacier carves a deep valley, while smaller side glaciers carve shallower ones. When the ice melts, the small valleys are left hanging high above the main one, and their streams pour over the edge. Yosemite Falls in California and many waterfalls in Norway formed this way.

Earthquake faults can lift one block of land above another. Lava flows can harden into steps of rock. At the coast, streams sometimes tumble straight off cliffs into the sea.

The tallest waterfall on Earth, Angel Falls in Venezuela, drops about 979 m from a flat-topped mountain. The widest curtain of falling water belongs to Victoria Falls, on the Zambezi River in southern Africa, more than 1.7 km across.

Monsoon waterfalls of India

Many of India's best waterfalls roar during the monsoon and shrink to a trickle in the dry months. In the Western Ghats, Jog Falls in Karnataka drops about 253 m in four streams. Dudhsagar, on the Goa–Karnataka border, falls more than 300 m, and trains cross a bridge right in front of it. In Meghalaya, one of the rainiest places on Earth, Nohkalikai Falls plunges about 340 m into a turquoise pool.

Falling water also carries energy. In 1902, a power station at Shivanasamudra Falls in Karnataka began sending electricity to the Kolar gold mines, one of the earliest big hydroelectric plants in Asia.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Niagara Falls is the tallest waterfall in the world.

Truth: Niagara is famous for the huge amount of water it carries, but it's only about 51 m tall. Angel Falls in Venezuela drops nearly 20 times further, about 979 m.

Try this

Make a waterfall in a sink or tray. Stack a flat stone on top of a lump of soft clay or wet sand at the edge of a step, then pour water slowly over the stone. Watch the soft layer underneath wash away and the stone start to overhang, until it tips. That's how real waterfalls retreat.
