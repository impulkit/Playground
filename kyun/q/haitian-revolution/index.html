How did Haiti win its freedom?

In 1791, enslaved people on the richest colony in the Caribbean rose up. Thirteen years later they had beaten three empires and founded a new country.

The short answer

In the 1700s, the western part of the island of Hispaniola was a French colony called Saint-Domingue. It grew a huge share of the world's sugar and coffee, and it made French planters very rich. The work was done by about half a million enslaved Africans, who lived and died in brutal conditions.

In August 1791 they rose up. Over the next thirteen years they fought French, Spanish and British armies. On 1 January 1804 their leader, Jean-Jacques Dessalines, declared a new country. He gave it an old Taíno name for the island: Haiti. It was the second independent country in the Americas, after the United States, and the first where slavery was banned from the very start.

Surprising but true

Haiti was the first country in the world to be founded by people who had freed themselves from slavery by revolt.

A spark in the night

The uprising began in the northern plains in August 1791. Many accounts describe a secret religious ceremony at a place called Bois Caïman just before it, though historians argue about the details. Within weeks, thousands of people had left the plantations and many sugar mills were burning.

Across the ocean, the French Revolution had begun two years earlier, with its talk of liberty and equality. People in Saint-Domingue heard that talk too, and asked why it should stop at the colony's edge.

Toussaint Louverture

The best-known leader was Toussaint Louverture. He had been born into slavery, learned to read, and gained his freedom before the revolution. He turned out to be a brilliant general and a sharp politician. He switched sides between Spain and France when it helped his cause. His chosen surname, Louverture, means "the opening" in French. One popular story says it came from his knack for finding gaps in enemy lines.

France abolished slavery in its colonies in 1794, and Toussaint fought for France after that. By 1801 he governed the whole colony and wrote it a constitution that banned slavery for ever.

Napoleon's army and the mosquito

Napoleon had other plans. In 1802 he sent a large army to take back control, and many feared slavery would return. Toussaint was tricked into a meeting, arrested and shipped to France. He died in a cold prison in the Jura mountains in 1803.

The fight went on without him. Haitian soldiers knew the land, and the French troops had no protection against yellow fever, a disease spread by mosquitoes. Thousands of soldiers fell ill and died. In November 1803, at the Battle of Vertières, the French army was beaten for good.

Freedom at a price

Many powerful countries refused to treat Haiti as an equal. The United States, where slavery was still legal, did not officially recognise Haiti until 1862. In 1825 France sent warships and demanded a huge payment as the price of recognition. Haiti borrowed money to pay, and the debt dragged on for more than a century.

The revolution changed other places too. Losing his richest colony helped persuade Napoleon to sell the vast Louisiana territory to the United States in 1803, which opened the way for the journey of Lewis and Clark. Later, Haiti sheltered and supplied the South American leader Simón Bolívar, asking him in return to free enslaved people in the lands he won.

A fortress on a mountain

After independence, Haitians feared France would come back. On a mountaintop in the north they built the Citadelle Laferrière, one of the largest fortresses in the Americas. Its walls are taller than a six-storey building. The French never returned with an army, and today the fortress is a UNESCO World Heritage Site that looks out over the country it was built to protect.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Haiti's freedom was handed to it by France.

Truth: France did abolish slavery in 1794, but only after the uprising had already shaken the colony. When Napoleon later sent an army to take back control, Haitians defeated it in battle.
