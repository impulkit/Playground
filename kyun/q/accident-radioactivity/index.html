How was radioactivity discovered?

In 1896, a few cloudy days in Paris made a scientist put his experiment in a drawer, and a strange glow changed science forever.

The short answer

In February 1896, the French scientist Henri Becquerel was testing an idea. He thought uranium salts might soak up sunlight and then give off invisible rays. His plan was to put the salts on a photographic plate wrapped in thick black paper, leave it in the sun, and see whether the plate darkened.

Paris turned grey and cloudy, so he shoved everything into a drawer and waited. A few days later, for reasons he never fully explained, he developed the plate anyway. It was strongly fogged, with the shape of the salts clearly visible. The uranium had been giving off rays in the dark, all by itself. That property was later named radioactivity.

Surprising but true

Marie Curie's notebooks are still radioactive more than 120 years later, and are kept in lead-lined boxes in Paris.

The rays everyone was chasing

Becquerel wasn't working in a vacuum, so to speak. Just a few weeks earlier, at the end of 1895, the German scientist Wilhelm Röntgen had discovered X-rays, which could pass through flesh and show bones. Scientists all over Europe were excited and hunting for new kinds of rays.

Becquerel came from a family of physicists. His father had spent years studying materials that glow after light shines on them. So Becquerel guessed uranium worked the same way. The cloudy weather proved him wrong, which turned out to be far more interesting than being right.

Marie and Pierre take over

At first, few people cared about Becquerel's rays. X-rays seemed more exciting. Then a young Polish-born scientist in Paris, Marie Curie, picked uranium rays as the topic for her doctorate in 1897.

She found that the strength of the rays depended only on how much uranium was present, not on whether it was hot, cold, wet or powdered. That meant the rays were coming from inside the atoms themselves. It was a shocking idea at the time, when many people thought atoms could never change.

With her husband Pierre, she ground up and boiled tonnes of a black ore called pitchblende in a leaky shed. In 1898 they found two new elements. One they named polonium, after Marie's homeland, Poland. The other, radium, was about a million times more radioactive than uranium. They also coined the word "radioactivity".

Rays that kept going

In 1903, Becquerel and both Curies shared the Nobel Prize in Physics. In 1911 Marie won a second Nobel, in chemistry, making her the first person to win two.

Nobody yet understood the danger. Pierre carried radium in his pocket, and Becquerel once got a burn on his skin from a tube of it in his waistcoat. Marie's health was harmed by years of work with radioactive substances. Her lab notebooks, even her cookbook, are still radioactive today. Researchers who want to read them must wear protective clothing and sign a form.

From a drawer to the world

Radioactivity changed medicine, energy and our picture of the Earth. Doctors use it to treat cancer and to make images of the body. Nuclear power stations make electricity from atoms splitting apart. India's atomic research, started by Homi Bhabha in the 1940s, grew from this science.

There is also a slow, steady clock hidden inside radioactive atoms. Each kind breaks down at its own fixed pace, measured by its half-life. Measuring how much is left tells scientists the age of ancient bones, rocks and even the Earth itself, which turns out to be about 4.5 billion years old.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Radioactive things glow green, like in cartoons.

Truth: Most radioactive materials don't glow at all. Radium salts do give off a faint bluish glow, and old watch dials glowed because radium was mixed with a special paint. The green glow in films is mostly made up.

Try this

Look at a glow-in-the-dark toy or sticker. Hold it under a lamp for a minute, then switch off the lights and watch it fade. That glow needs light to charge it, which is what Becquerel expected from uranium. Radioactivity is different, because it never needs charging.
