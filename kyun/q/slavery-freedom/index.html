How did slavery end in the USA?

For nearly 250 years, millions of people in America were bought and sold. Ending it took escapes, books, protests and a civil war.

The short answer

Slavery in what became the United States began in the early 1600s, when enslaved Africans were brought to the colonies to work on farms and plantations. By 1860, nearly 40 lakh people were enslaved, mostly in the southern states.

It ended through a long struggle. Enslaved people resisted and escaped. Abolitionists, people who wanted to abolish slavery, wrote, spoke and protested. Northern states banned it one by one. Finally, the Civil War of 1861–1865 broke the power of the slave-holding South, and in December 1865 the 13th Amendment made slavery illegal everywhere in the country.

Surprising but true

Enslaved people in Galveston, Texas, only learned they were free on 19 June 1865, more than two years after the Emancipation Proclamation. That day is now the US holiday Juneteenth.

How slavery began

Over about 350 years, roughly 1.25 crore Africans were forced onto ships and carried across the Atlantic Ocean, mostly to the Caribbean and South America. Around 4 lakh were taken directly to North America. In 1619, about 20 Africans arrived in the English colony of Virginia, an early step in a system that grew for generations.

Enslaved people were treated as property. They could be sold, and families were often split apart. Children born to enslaved mothers were enslaved too. In the South, their forced labour grew cotton, tobacco and rice, making plantation owners rich.

Saying no

Enslaved people resisted in many ways: working slowly, keeping their music and faith, learning to read in secret, and running away. Some escaped north along routes known as the Underground Railroad, helped by a hidden network of people. It was not a real railway, but it used railway words: guides were "conductors" and safe houses were "stations". Harriet Tubman was its most famous conductor.

Frederick Douglass escaped slavery in Maryland in 1838 and became a powerful speaker and writer. His autobiography, published in 1845, showed thousands of readers what slavery was really like. Sojourner Truth, who had walked away from slavery in New York in 1826, travelled the country speaking against both slavery and the unfair treatment of women.

A country divided

Northern states ended slavery gradually after the Revolution, and importing enslaved people from abroad was banned in 1808. But the South depended on enslaved labour, and as the country grew west, the two sides fought over whether new states would allow it.

In 1852, Harriet Beecher Stowe's novel Uncle Tom's Cabin sold hundreds of thousands of copies and turned many northerners against slavery.

When Abraham Lincoln, who opposed the spread of slavery, was elected in 1860, southern states began leaving the Union. The Civil War followed.

War and freedom

In 1863, Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation declared enslaved people in rebel states free. As Union armies advanced, that freedom became real, town by town. Black soldiers fought in growing numbers.

The war ended in 1865, and the 13th Amendment was ratified that December. News travelled slowly. On 19 June 1865, a Union general arrived in Galveston, Texas, and announced that all enslaved people there were free. Celebrations of that day, called Juneteenth, began the next year.

The long road after

Freedom did not bring equality. The 14th and 15th Amendments promised citizenship and the vote to Black men, but southern states soon passed laws to keep Black Americans apart and away from the ballot box. Breaking those laws took another century and the civil rights movement.

Britain's empire ended most slavery in 1833, but India was left out of that law at first. Slavery was made illegal in British India ten years later, in 1843.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Civil War had nothing to do with slavery.

Truth: The southern states that broke away said themselves, in their official declarations, that protecting slavery was a main reason. Historians agree slavery was at the centre of the war.
