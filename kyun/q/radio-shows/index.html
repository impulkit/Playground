How did families listen to the radio?

Before TV, families sat in a circle and stared at a wooden box, listening to cricket, news and ghost stories that came out of thin air.

The short answer

From the 1920s to the 1960s, radio was the main way families heard news, music and stories. A radio set was often a big wooden cabinet that took pride of place in the living room. Everyone gathered around it at fixed times, the way people later gathered around a TV.

Radio worked by turning sound into invisible radio waves that travelled through the air from a tall transmitter. A receiver in each home turned the waves back into sound. Anyone in range could listen for free, at the same moment, whether they lived in a city flat or a farmhouse.

Surprising but true

For decades, owning a radio in India needed a government licence, renewed and paid for at the post office.

Wooden boxes and glowing valves

The first regular radio station in the USA, KDKA in Pittsburgh, went on air in 1920 by announcing election results. Within a few years, there were hundreds of stations. Early sets used glass valves, a bit like small light bulbs, that took time to warm up and glowed orange in the dark.

Radio brought live events into homes for the first time. Families heard boxing matches, concerts and, during the 1930s, President Franklin Roosevelt's "fireside chats", in which he spoke calmly to the nation about hard times.

Radio comes to India

In 1927, the Indian Broadcasting Company began broadcasting from Bombay and Calcutta. In 1936 the service became All India Radio, and in 1957 it took the name Akashvani, meaning "voice from the sky". The word had first been used in the 1930s for a small station in Mysore, and it fitted perfectly.

On the night of 14 August 1947, many Indians heard Jawaharlal Nehru give his "Tryst with Destiny" speech, broadcast as India became independent at midnight. Radio also carried cricket commentary, farm advice and classical music concerts to villages far from any stadium or concert hall.

Binaca Geetmala and the countdown

In 1952, Radio Ceylon, broadcasting from Colombo in Sri Lanka, began a weekly show of Hindi film songs called Binaca Geetmala. Its host, Ameen Sayani, greeted listeners with "Behno aur bhaiyon" (sisters and brothers), and counted down the most popular songs of the week.

It ran for decades and became one of the best-loved radio shows in Asia. Families tuned in every Wednesday night to find out which song was at number one. All India Radio answered with its own film music channel, Vividh Bharati, in 1957.

The transistor changes everything

In the 1950s, the transistor, a tiny electronic switch, replaced bulky valves. Radios became small, cheap and battery-powered. Teenagers could carry one to the beach, farmers could hang one from a tree in the field, and cricket fans could hold one to their ear at the ground.

In India, the small "transistor", as people called it, became a common wedding gift and a prized family possession. In many homes it was the first electronic device anyone owned.

Radio after TV

When TV arrived, many people expected radio to disappear. It did not. People kept listening in cars, kitchens and shops. FM radio, with clearer sound, spread across Indian cities in the 2000s, along with chatty radio jockeys.

Community radio stations now broadcast in local languages to villages, run by local groups and colleges. And the old habit of listening to a voice tell a story has found a new home in podcasts, which you can play whenever you like.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The 1938 War of the Worlds broadcast made millions of Americans panic.

Truth: The panic story was greatly exaggerated by newspapers at the time. Most people were not listening, and most who were understood it was a drama.

Try this

Make a radio play with your family. Write a two-minute scene, and make all the sound effects with things at home. Shaking a sheet of paper sounds like rain, and two coconut shell halves can sound like horses. Record it on a phone, close your eyes and listen back.
