How are matchsticks made?

The first friction match was sold in 1827 by an English chemist who discovered it by accident, scraping a stick that had dried with chemicals on its tip.

The short answer

A matchstick is a thin splint of wood or waxed cardboard with a blob of chemicals on one end. In a factory, a machine slices soft wood into thin sheets, then chops the sheets into tiny sticks. The sticks are soaked in a chemical so they don't keep glowing after they're blown out, then dried.

Next, a machine holds thousands of sticks in rows of holes on a moving belt. It dips their tips into melted wax, so the flame catches the wood, and then into a paste that forms the head. The heads dry, the sticks are pushed out into boxes, and the boxes get a strip on the side. A single big machine can make millions of matches a day.

Surprising but true

A safety match won't light on a wall or a shoe, because half of the chemistry is on the box, not on the stick.

A happy accident

Before matches, lighting a fire meant rubbing sticks, striking flint against steel or carrying hot coals around. Early chemical matches were dangerous: some had to be dipped into a little bottle of acid.

In 1826, an English chemist named John Walker was mixing chemicals with a stick. A blob dried on its tip. When he scraped it across the floor, it burst into flame. In 1827 he began selling "friction lights" in his shop, to be pulled through a fold of sandpaper. It was a classic accidental invention.

The poison in early matches

Later matches used white phosphorus, which lit easily on almost anything. But it was deadly. Factory workers, many of them young women and girls, breathed in the fumes and developed a terrible bone disease in the jaw called "phossy jaw".

In 1888, the matchgirls of a London factory went on strike over pay and conditions, and won better treatment. In 1906, many countries signed an agreement to ban white phosphorus in matches. Safer chemistry won.

The Swedish safety trick

In the 1840s, a Swedish chemist found a safer idea: split the chemicals in two. The head carries potassium chlorate, which gives off oxygen when heated, plus sulphur and glue. The box strip carries red phosphorus and powdered glass.

When you strike, friction heats a tiny bit of red phosphorus, which flares and lights the head. The potassium chlorate feeds oxygen to the sulphur, which catches, then the wax, then the wood. All of this happens in about a second. Swedish factories made safety matches famous around the world from the 1850s.

India's match town

Most of India's matches come from a small area of Tamil Nadu around Sivakasi, Kovilpatti and Sattur. The hot, dry climate is good for drying matches. The industry grew in the 1920s, when local businessmen learned the trade and set up factories.

Sivakasi is also famous for fireworks and printing. For many years, children worked in its match factories, filling boxes by hand. Laws, inspections and campaigns have greatly reduced this, and it is now illegal for children to work there. The wider problem is covered in the story of child labour.

Many match factories now use machines, though some smaller units still do steps like filling boxes by hand.

Tiny but powerful

A box of matches still costs very little in India. In 2021 the usual price rose from 1 rupee to 2 rupees, the first change in 14 years. Matches remain important for cooking stoves, lamps and festivals.

Lighters have replaced matches in many places, but a match never runs out of gas and needs no spare parts. They are tools, not toys, and only an adult should strike them, which is a key rule of kitchen safety.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The red tip of a safety match is made of red phosphorus.

Truth: The colour on the tip is usually just a dye. The red phosphorus is on the striking strip of the box. Keeping the two apart is what makes safety matches safer.

Try this

Rub your palms together fast for ten seconds, then hold them against your cheeks. Feel the warmth? That is friction turning movement into heat, the same start a match needs. Try it with dry hands and then with soapy wet hands and compare.
