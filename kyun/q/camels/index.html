How do camels survive the desert?

A thirsty camel can drink about 100 litres of water in a quarter of an hour, about five big buckets' worth.

The short answer

Camels survive the desert by being extremely careful with water. Their kidneys make very little urine, and their dung comes out so dry that people use it as fuel. Their noses catch moisture from each breath before it escapes.

They also let their bodies heat up. During the day, a camel's temperature can climb by about 6°C before it starts sweating, saving water. At night, it cools down again. A camel can lose about a quarter of its body weight in water and still be fine. A person who lost that much would be in great danger.

When it does find water, a thirsty camel can gulp about 100 litres in around a quarter of an hour.

Surprising but true

Camels first evolved in North America. They died out there thousands of years ago, after their relatives had crossed into Asia.

Built for sand and sun

Camels have two rows of long eyelashes and a thin third eyelid that wipes sand from their eyes like a windscreen wiper. Their ears are lined with hair, and their nostrils can close tight during a sandstorm.

Their feet are wide, with two toes joined by a tough, leathery pad that spreads out on soft sand. Thick pads on their knees and chest let them kneel on burning ground. Their lips are tough enough to munch thorny desert plants that other animals avoid.

Even their blood is special. Camel red blood cells are oval, not round. They keep flowing when the blood thickens from thirst, and they can swell when the camel drinks a lot without bursting.

Desert ships of India

Camels have carried people and goods across the Thar Desert for centuries. The Raika herders of Rajasthan have bred and cared for camels for generations. Every year, thousands of camels are traded at the Pushkar fair, and Rajasthan made the camel its state animal in 2014.

India's Border Security Force still patrols parts of the desert on camels, and its camel band marches in the Republic Day parade. In Kutch, Gujarat, Kharai camels do something even stranger: they swim out to islands of mangroves to graze.

But camel numbers in India are falling fast as roads and tractors replace them. The 2019 count found about 2.5 lakh, around a third fewer than in 2012. In Bikaner, a government research centre is working on camel milk, cheese and even ice cream, so that keeping camels pays.

Two humps for the cold

Two-humped Bactrian camels live in the cold deserts of Central Asia, where winters are freezing and summers are scorching. For centuries they carried silk, spices and tea along the Silk Road.

A few still live in the Nubra Valley of Ladakh, descendants of camels from the old caravans that crossed the mountains. Tourists now ride them over sand dunes surrounded by snowy peaks. In winter, they grow thick, shaggy coats that they shed in great clumps in spring.

Camels from America

Here is a surprise. The camel family began in North America, around 40 million years ago. Some early camels were no bigger than a goat. Over millions of years, some crossed a land bridge into Asia, and others moved into South America, where their relatives the llamas and alpacas still live. The camels left in North America died out thousands of years ago.

Camels came back for a short time. In the 1850s, the US Army brought dozens of camels to Texas to carry supplies across the dry Southwest. The plan was dropped after the American Civil War. In Australia, camels brought by cameleers from British India and Afghanistan in the 1800s were later set loose. Today hundreds of thousands roam wild across its outback.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Camels store water in their humps.

Truth: The hump is fat. Camels survive without drinking by losing very little water, and their bodies can cope with drying out far more than ours can.

Try this

Wet a handkerchief and lay it over one forearm, leaving the other bare. Fan both arms with a notebook. The wet arm feels cooler, because evaporating water carries heat away. That is how sweating cools you, but it costs water. Camels save water by sweating as little as they can.
