What happened to Gandhi in South Africa?

Gandhi went to South Africa for a one-year legal job. He stayed 21 years, and came home with the method that would shake the British Empire.

The short answer

In 1893 a 23-year-old lawyer named Mohandas Gandhi sailed to Durban to help an Indian trading firm with a court case. He meant to stay a year. Instead, he spent 21 years in South Africa, which was then made up of British colonies and settler states.

There, Indians were treated as second-class people by law. Gandhi set up organisations, ran a newspaper, and went to jail several times. Most important, he developed satyagraha, a way of resisting injustice without violence. When he returned to India in 1915, he was already famous, and ready to use the same method against British rule.

Surprising but true

Gandhi first tried out mass non-violent resistance in Johannesburg, not in India, in 1906, when he was 36.

The night at Pietermaritzburg

About two weeks after arriving, Gandhi took a train from Durban towards Pretoria with a first-class ticket. At Pietermaritzburg station, a white passenger objected to sharing the compartment with an Indian. Gandhi refused to move to the van compartment. He was pushed off the train and his luggage was thrown after him.

He sat all night in the freezing waiting room. Later he wrote that this was when he decided to stay and fight the "deep disease of colour prejudice". Today a plaque at the station marks the spot.

Why Indians were in South Africa

From 1860, the British brought thousands of Indians to Natal on contracts called indenture. They worked on sugar plantations for little pay, under harsh rules. Many stayed after their contracts ended. Indian traders followed, mostly from Gujarat. By Gandhi's time there were tens of thousands of Indians, part of a growing Indian diaspora around the world.

Laws kept them out of many places. Natal charged former workers who stayed on a heavy yearly tax of three pounds, several months' wages for many, and Indians in the Transvaal had to carry passes.

Where in the world do Indians live?About 3.5 crore people of Indian origin live outside India, from Dubai and New Jersey to Fiji and the Caribbean.

The birth of satyagraha

In 1906 the Transvaal government ordered every Indian to register and carry a pass with fingerprints. On 11 September, about 3,000 Indians packed a theatre in Johannesburg and swore they would not obey, whatever the punishment.

Gandhi called this new method satyagraha, from Sanskrit words meaning "holding on to truth". The idea was to refuse unfair laws openly, accept punishment calmly, and never hit back. Over the next years, thousands of Indians went to jail.

Farms, a newspaper and a long march

Gandhi started a newspaper, Indian Opinion, in 1903. He set up two communal farms, Phoenix Settlement near Durban and Tolstoy Farm near Johannesburg, where families grew food and shared work. Gandhi learned to bake bread and make sandals there.

In 1913, the movement reached its peak. Thousands of Indian mine workers went on strike and marched across the border from Natal into the Transvaal, breaking the law on purpose. Gandhi's wife, Kasturba, was jailed too. The next year the government agreed to scrap the three-pound tax and recognise Indian marriages.

A complicated record

Gandhi's struggle in South Africa was for Indians, not for the Black African majority, who suffered even worse treatment. His early writings include insulting remarks about Black Africans. Some people say his views widened later in life. Others say these remarks should not be brushed aside. In 2018, a statue of him was removed from a university campus in Ghana after protests.

Many Black South African leaders still drew on his ideas. Nelson Mandela called Gandhi an inspiration, and both South Africa and India claim him as part of their story.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Gandhi went to South Africa as a political leader to help Indians there.

Truth: He went as a young, little-known lawyer hired to help with one business case. He became a leader only after he saw how Indians were treated and decided to stay and organise.
