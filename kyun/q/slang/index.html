Where does slang come from?

OK began in 1839 as a jokey newspaper abbreviation. It is now one of the most widely understood words in the world.

The short answer

Slang is the informal, playful language of a group: teenagers, soldiers, musicians, gamers, a school or a city neighbourhood. It comes from the same urge that makes friends invent nicknames and secret handshakes. New words mark who belongs, add fun, and say things that ordinary words can't quite capture.

Slang words are made by shortening, mixing, borrowing and twisting older words. Most vanish within a few years. A few survive so long that they become ordinary language, and nobody remembers they were ever slang. "Mob", "bus" and "OK" all started out that way.

Surprising but true

OK started as a jokey abbreviation of "oll korrect", a deliberate misspelling of "all correct".

The joke that conquered the world

In the 1830s, Boston newspapers had a craze for jokey abbreviations of misspelled phrases. "O.W." stood for "oll wright" and "K.Y." for "know yuse". In March 1839, the Boston Morning Post printed "O.K." for "oll korrect", a silly spelling of "all correct".

Most of these jokes died quickly. OK survived, helped by the 1840 US presidential election. Supporters of President Martin Van Buren, nicknamed "Old Kinderhook" after his home town, formed "O.K. Clubs". The American scholar Allen Walker Read tracked down this history in the 1960s. Today people say "OK" in Hindi, Japanese, Swahili and dozens of other languages.

How slang is made

Slang has a toolkit. Shortening turns "fabulous" into "fab" and "information" into "info". Blending squashes words together. Changing meaning makes "sick" or "wicked" mean excellent. Borrowing pulls in words from other languages and other groups.

Some slang is meant to hide meaning from outsiders. In Cockney rhyming slang, from East London, you replace a word with a phrase that rhymes with it, and then often drop the rhyming part. "Apples and pears" means stairs. "Use your loaf" means use your head, because "loaf of bread" rhymes with head. Traders and street sellers could talk in front of strangers without being understood.

Soldiers have always been busy slang makers too. British soldiers who served in India took home the word "Blighty", meaning Britain. It came from the Hindustani vilayati, meaning foreign, and was used most widely during the First World War.

Music, streets and screens

Music has always been a slang factory. Jazz musicians in the USA in the 1930s and 1940s spread words like "cool", meaning calm and stylish, and "hip", meaning in the know. Hip-hop, born in New York in the 1970s, gave the world "diss", "bling" and many more.

India has its own slang hubs. Mumbai's street language, often called Bambaiyya, mixes Hindi, Marathi, Gujarati and English. Hindi films carried words like bindaas (carefree) and jhakaas (fantastic) across the country. College students everywhere invent their own words for exams, canteens and strict teachers.

From playground to dictionary

Social media now spreads slang faster than ever. A word can start in one online community, travel worldwide in weeks and sound old-fashioned within a year. Oxford Languages named "rizz", meaning charm or the ability to attract someone, its Word of the Year for 2023. The word grew from the end of "charisma" and spread through online videos.

Dictionaries wait to see whether a slang word lasts before adding it. Many don't. In 1710, the writer Jonathan Swift complained that "mob", short for the Latin mobile vulgus, was a lazy new word that was ruining English. Today nobody blinks at it, and some of the slang that annoys your parents may turn up in your grandchildren's textbooks.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Slang is just lazy or bad language.

Truth: Slang follows its own patterns and takes real creativity. Linguists study it closely, and many ordinary words, like "bus" and "mob", started life as slang that people once complained about.
