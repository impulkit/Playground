What does a journalist do?

India's first newspaper was so rude about the people in charge that its printing press was seized within two years.

The short answer

A journalist finds out what is happening and tells the public about it, clearly and truthfully. Some report on cricket matches or floods. Others spend months digging into secrets that powerful people would rather hide.

The job sounds simple, but the hard part is being sure. A reporter collects facts by watching, asking questions, reading documents and checking with several people. An editor then asks, "How do we know this?" Only when the answer is solid does the story go out in a newspaper, on television, on the radio or online.

Surprising but true

In 1889 the American reporter Nellie Bly raced around the world in 72 days, beating the 80 days of a famous novel, and sent reports home as she went.

Six simple questions

Every reporter learns six questions: who, what, where, when, why and how. A good news story answers most of them in the first few lines. That style is called the inverted pyramid, with the biggest facts at the wide top and smaller details below.

Journalists also try to keep facts and opinions apart. A news report says what happened. An opinion column, clearly marked, says what the writer thinks about it. Mixing the two quietly is one way readers get misled, along with tricks like numbers used carelessly.

A rude newspaper in Calcutta

India's first newspaper was Hicky's Bengal Gazette, started in January 1780 in Calcutta, now Kolkata. Its owner, James Augustus Hicky, was an Irishman who loved gossip and mocked the officials of the East India Company, including the Governor-General, Warren Hastings. In 1782 his press was seized and the paper shut down.

Within a century India had hundreds of newspapers in many languages. During the freedom struggle, leaders like Bal Gangadhar Tilak and Gandhi ran their own papers. Gandhi edited Young India and Harijan, and wrote much of them himself.

How did the printing press change the world?Before the printing press, a single book could take a scribe months to copy by hand. Within fifty years of Gutenberg, Europe had printed millions.

Digging deep

Some of the most famous journalism is investigative. In 1972 two young reporters at the Washington Post, Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein, began following a small story about a break-in at the Watergate building in Washington DC. It led to the US president's own team. In 1974 President Richard Nixon resigned.

In 1887 Nellie Bly pretended to be ill so she could be sent to a New York asylum for women. Her reports on the cruel conditions there led to changes. New York City put more money into caring for patients after her stories appeared. Two years later she went round the world in 72 days, beating the 80 days in Jules Verne's novel.

Rules of the job

In India, freedom of the press is protected as part of the right to free speech in the Constitution. In the USA it is written into the First Amendment. Both countries also have laws against spreading false, damaging claims about people.

Good journalists protect people who give them information in secret. They admit and correct their mistakes. In India, the Right to Information law lets anyone, including reporters, ask government offices for records.

News in your pocket

Today anyone with a phone can post a video of something happening. That helps reporters see events they missed. It also spreads rumours fast, which is why careful checking matters more than ever. Many newsrooms now have fact-checking teams that test viral posts and photos before anyone treats them as news.

Children can do journalism too. Many schools in India and the USA have student newspapers. Young reporters learn the same lesson as the professionals: ask, check, then write, and spot fake news before you pass it on.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Journalists can print anything they hear as long as they say "sources say".

Truth: Good newsrooms check facts with more than one source, ask the people involved for their side and correct mistakes publicly. Printing false and harmful claims about a person can break the law.

Try this

Be a reporter for one day. Choose something that happened at home or school and answer the six questions, who, what, where, when, why and how, in your notebook. Then ask a second person what they saw. Did their story match yours? Write a 100-word report using only facts both of you agree on.
