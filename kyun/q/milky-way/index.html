What is the Milky Way?

Every star you can see at night belongs to one giant spinning city of stars, and we live out in its suburbs.

The short answer

The Milky Way is our galaxy: a huge, flat, spinning disc of stars, gas and dust, held together by gravity. The Sun is one of somewhere between 100 billion and 400 billion stars in it.

On a dark night, away from city lights, you can see part of the disc as a faint, glowing band across the sky. That band is the light of countless distant stars, too far away to see one by one. Ancient Greeks thought it looked like spilled milk. In Sanskrit and Hindi it is called Akash Ganga, the river of the sky.

Surprising but true

Almost 80% of people in North America can no longer see the Milky Way from where they live, because of light from cities.

Our place in the disc

Seen from outside, the Milky Way would look like a giant pinwheel, with a bright bulge in the middle and curving arms of stars. A straight bar of stars crosses the centre, which is why astronomers call it a barred spiral.

The galaxy is about 100,000 light-years across. Light, the fastest thing there is, would take 100,000 years to cross it. If the whole Milky Way were shrunk to the width of India, the paths of all eight planets around the Sun would fit inside a bottle cap, about 3 cm wide.

We live in a quieter area about 26,000 light-years from the centre. When you look at the Milky Way's band in the sky, you are looking along the flat disc from the inside.

That makes mapping it hard, like trying to draw a map of a forest while standing among the trees. Thick dust hides much of the disc. Astronomers use radio telescopes and infrared light, which pass through dust, to trace the spiral arms. The European Space Agency's Gaia spacecraft, launched in 2013, measured the positions of more than a billion stars to build the best map yet.

A giant in the middle

At the very centre sits a supermassive black hole called Sagittarius A*. It has about 4 million times the mass of the Sun. Two teams, led by Reinhard Genzel in Germany and Andrea Ghez in the USA, tracked stars whipping around it for decades. Their work won the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics.

In 2022, the Event Horizon Telescope, a team using radio dishes across the world as one giant telescope, released the first picture of the glowing ring around it.

Seeing it for yourself

In 1610, Galileo pointed one of the first telescopes at the Milky Way. He discovered it was made of huge numbers of faint stars packed together. Before that, nobody knew what the glow was.

Today most people can't see it at all. Streetlights and buildings light up the sky so much that the faint band disappears. In 2022, India set up its first dark-sky reserve at Hanle in Ladakh, high in the mountains, where the Milky Way still stretches clearly from one horizon to the other.

Neighbours and the future

The Milky Way is not alone. Two small galaxies, the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds, can be seen from the southern half of the world. The nearest big spiral galaxy, Andromeda, is about 2.5 million light-years away and moving towards us.

Astronomers have long expected the two to collide in about 4 to 5 billion years. Newer studies suggest it is far from certain. Even if they do meet, the stars are so far apart that they would almost never hit each other.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Sun sits at the centre of the Milky Way.

Truth: Astronomers believed this until about a hundred years ago. In 1918, the American astronomer Harlow Shapley showed that we are far out in the disc, about halfway to the edge. The centre lies towards the constellation Sagittarius.

Try this

Make a model galaxy. Draw a fat dot in the middle of a dark sheet of paper, then sprinkle a pinch of salt or sugar in two curving arms around it. Mark the Sun about two-thirds of the way out from the centre. Now look at it edge-on, at eye level. You see a thin band, just as we see the Milky Way across the sky.
