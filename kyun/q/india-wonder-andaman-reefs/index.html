What lives in the Andaman reefs?

Off the Andaman Islands, a sea cow the size of a small car grazes on underwater grass, and India has only a few hundred of them left.

The short answer

The reefs around the Andaman Islands are among the richest in India. Hundreds of kinds of hard coral grow here, along with soft corals, sponges and sea fans. Among them live parrotfish, butterflyfish, clownfish in their anemones, moray eels, giant clams, octopuses, reef sharks and sea turtles.

In the seagrass beds nearby grazes the shy dugong, a sea mammal that is the official animal of the islands. The Andamans sit far out in the Bay of Bengal, about 1,200 km from Chennai, and much of the sea around them is still clear and less crowded than many mainland coasts. Divers come from all over the world to see them.

Surprising but true

The great tsunami of 2004 lifted parts of the northern Andaman Islands out of the sea, leaving some coral reefs high and dry.

Cities built by tiny animals

A reef is built by coral polyps, tiny animals with soft bodies and rings of tentacles. Each one makes a cup of limestone around itself. Millions of polyps side by side, over thousands of years, build a reef big enough to see from a plane.

Most corals live with even tinier algae inside them. The algae use sunlight to make food and share it with the coral, which is why reefs need clear, shallow, warm water. That is also why they are so colourful. The story of coral reefs everywhere starts with this partnership.

Who you might meet

Snorkel off Havelock Island, now called Swaraj Dweep, or in the marine park at Wandoor, and you might see parrotfish crunching coral with beak-like teeth. Their droppings come out as fine white sand. Much of the white sand on reef beaches has passed through a parrotfish.

Giant clams sit open on the reef, their wavy lips glowing blue and green. Lionfish drift with spines spread like feathers. Sea turtles visit to feed, and some islands are nesting beaches for green, hawksbill, olive ridley and huge leatherback turtles, especially in the Nicobar Islands to the south.

There was even a famous swimming elephant. Rajan, a retired logging elephant on Havelock, swam in the sea near the reefs for years and was photographed by divers before he died in 2016.

How do baby turtles find the sea?They hatch at night, alone, on a beach. Nobody shows them the way.

The sea cow

The dugong grows to about 3 m long and can weigh over 400 kg. It has a whale-like tail and a broad snout for pulling up seagrass. Old sailors' tales of mermaids may have been inspired by dugongs and their cousins, the manatees.

Dugongs breed slowly, a single calf every few years. Fishing nets, boats and damage to seagrass have made them rare. Only a few hundred are thought to survive in all Indian waters, in the Andamans, the Gulf of Mannar and the Gulf of Kutch.

Shaken, lifted and warmed

On 26 December 2004, a giant undersea earthquake set off the Indian Ocean tsunami. The quake lifted parts of the northern Andamans by a metre or more and dropped parts of the south. Some reefs ended up above water and died. Others were buried in mud. Many have slowly recovered.

A bigger long-term danger is heat. When the sea stays too warm for weeks, corals push out their algae and turn white, called coral bleaching. The Andaman reefs bleached badly in 1998, 2010 and 2016, and again in 2024. Some recovered, some did not.

Scientists now watch which corals survive heat waves, hoping they hold the key to the reefs' future. The clownfish, at least, has a head start, thanks to a slimy coat that stops its anemone from stinging it, the trick behind every clownfish home.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Corals are colourful rocks or plants.

Truth: Corals are animals, tiny relatives of jellyfish, that build stony skeletons. Most of their colour comes from microscopic algae living inside them, which make food from sunlight and share it with the coral.
