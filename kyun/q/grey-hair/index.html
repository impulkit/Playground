Why does hair turn grey?

A grey hair is not grey at all. Look closely and it is white or see-through, and the grey look comes from white hairs mixed with dark ones.

The short answer

Each hair on your head grows from a tiny pit in your skin called a follicle. Inside the follicle live pigment cells. As the hair grows, these cells pump colour, called melanin, into it. Lots of dark melanin makes black or brown hair, and a reddish kind gives red and golden shades.

As people get older, the pigment cells in some follicles slowly stop working. The hairs from those follicles grow out with no colour, so they look white or silvery. Mixed with the dark hairs still growing, the overall effect looks grey. Most people find their first grey hairs in their thirties, but some go grey in their twenties and others hardly at all before sixty.

Surprising but true

Hair that is already outside your head cannot change colour. A grey hair was grey before it ever poked out of your skin.

A colour factory in every root

Hair is made of a tough material called keratin, the same stuff as your nails. The part you can see is not alive. All the action happens down in the root, where new cells are made and pushed upwards, collecting melanin on the way.

That means a hair's colour is fixed when it is made. Hair on your head grows roughly 1 centimetre a month, so a hair growing out grey now will take months to show along its length. That is why grey usually appears first at the roots, and why stories of hair turning white overnight are probably exaggerated.

The stem cells that wander off

Pigment cells don't last forever. The follicle keeps a store of young "stem cells" that can turn into new pigment cells each time a hair starts to grow. In greying hair, this store runs low.

Research in mice has offered some clues. A 2023 study found that these stem cells normally move between two zones in the follicle, but as hairs age, many get stuck in one zone where they can't do their job. Other work suggests chemical wear and tear builds up in the follicle over time. Scientists are still piecing together how these ideas fit, and how well the mouse results match people.

It runs in families

The biggest clue to when you'll go grey is your family. If your parents and grandparents greyed early, you probably will too, because genes strongly shape the timing. Ancestry matters as well. On average, people of European descent tend to start greying a few years earlier than people of Asian or African descent, though there is a lot of overlap.

An old saying claimed that half of all people are half grey by age 50. A 2012 study of thousands of people across many countries found that figure was far too high. Most 50-year-olds in the study had much less grey than that.

Grey animals too

People aren't the only ones who go grey. Many dogs get white hairs around their muzzles as they age, and older horses can grey around the eyes. In mountain gorillas, adult males grow a patch of silver hair on their backs, which is why they're called silverbacks, though that is a sign of being grown up rather than old.

Grey hairs often feel different from dark ones. Many people find them coarser, wirier or curlier. Scientists think the follicles change in other ways as they age, so the hair they make may be shaped differently as well as lacking colour.

Can worry turn you grey?

People have long said that stress turns hair grey. In 2020, scientists at Harvard found that in mice, sudden stress made nerves in the skin release a chemical that used up the pigment stem cells for good. A 2021 study of people found that a few grey hairs regained some colour after a stressful period ended.

So stress may speed greying for some people, but it is far from the main cause. Going grey very young can sometimes be linked to low vitamin B12 or thyroid problems, so it is worth mentioning to a doctor. Many people simply enjoy their grey, while others colour it with dyes or plant pastes like henna. Either way, greying is a normal part of ageing.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Pull out one grey hair and two will grow back.

Truth: Each hair grows from its own root, called a follicle, and one follicle makes only one hair at a time. Plucking can't add hairs, but doing it again and again can damage the follicle.

Try this

Ask a grown-up with some grey hair if you can look at one of their grey hairs beside a dark one, using a magnifying glass under a lamp. Notice whether the grey hair is really grey, or white and almost clear. Check whether it is thicker, wirier or curlier than the dark one.
