How did the steam engine change the world?

The first useful steam engines were built to pump water out of flooded coal mines. Within a century they were pulling trains across India.

The short answer

A steam engine burns fuel, usually coal or wood, to boil water. When water turns into steam, it takes up well over a thousand times more space, so the steam pushes hard on anything in its way. In an engine, steam pushes a piston, a tight-fitting plug, back and forth inside a metal cylinder. Rods and wheels turn that movement into pumping or turning.

Before steam, almost all work was done by muscles, wind and flowing water. Steam engines could work anywhere, day and night, without getting tired. They pumped mines, drove factory machines, pulled trains and pushed ships, and they helped start the Industrial Revolution.

Surprising but true

Steam engines are still everywhere. Most coal and nuclear power stations make electricity by boiling water and using the steam to spin giant turbines.

A toy in ancient Egypt

About 2,000 years ago, Hero of Alexandria was a Greek engineer living in Egypt. He described a metal ball that spun when steam shot out of two bent pipes. It was a clever toy, but nobody used it for work.

The first steam engines with a real job came much later, in England. Coal mines there kept flooding as miners dug deeper. In 1712 Thomas Newcomen built an engine that used steam and the push of the air to work a giant beam, which pumped water up from the mine. It was slow and burned a lot of coal, but at a coal mine, coal was cheap. Some Newcomen engines kept working for decades, nodding their great wooden beams up and down day and night.

Watt makes it better

In the 1760s, a Scottish instrument maker named James Watt was asked to repair a model Newcomen engine. He noticed how much fuel it wasted. Each stroke, the cylinder was cooled to turn the steam back into water, then heated again.

Watt's answer was a separate condenser, a chamber where the steam could cool while the main cylinder stayed hot. He patented it in 1769. Later he and his business partner Matthew Boulton added ways to make the engine turn wheels smoothly. Now steam could drive cotton mills, flour mills and ironworks. The unit of power, the watt, is named after him.

How does a pressure cooker cook faster?A pressure cooker's whistle is a tiny safety valve, letting out just enough steam to keep water boiling at about 120°C instead of 100°C.

Steam on rails and rivers

Putting a steam engine on wheels created the railway. In 1829, George and Robert Stephenson's locomotive Rocket won a contest in England by pulling a train at what then seemed a shocking speed, close to 50 km/h. Railways soon spread around the globe.

On water, Robert Fulton's steamboat began carrying passengers up the Hudson River from New York in 1807. Ships no longer had to wait for the wind.

In India, the first passenger train ran on 16 April 1853 from Bori Bunder in Bombay to Thane, about 34 km, pulled by three steam locomotives. India's railways grew into one of the largest networks anywhere. The little steam engines of the Darjeeling Himalayan Railway, now a UNESCO World Heritage Site, still puff up into the hills.

Steam is still with us

Steam locomotives have mostly retired, but steam itself has not. In most coal, gas and nuclear power stations, heat boils water into high-pressure steam. The steam spins a turbine, a wheel of angled blades, connected to a generator. Much of the electricity in your home was probably made this way.

Burning coal for steam also released huge amounts of carbon dioxide, starting in the 1700s. That is one reason the steam age is part of the story of today's warming climate.

Myth or fact?

Myth: James Watt invented the steam engine.

Truth: Working steam engines existed for more than 50 years before Watt's improvement. Thomas Newcomen's engines were pumping water from mines from 1712. Watt made them far more efficient and better at turning wheels, which helped them spread into factories.

Try this

Watch steam push. With an adult in charge of the kitchen, notice how the lid of a pot of boiling water rattles and lifts, or how a pressure cooker whistle jumps. That push comes from water turning into steam, which takes up far more space than the water did. Steam engines harness that same push.
