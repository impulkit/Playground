How do dolphins talk to each other?

Every dolphin has its own name, and it's a whistle.

The short answer

Dolphins talk with sound, because sound travels far and fast underwater while light does not. They make two main kinds. Whistles help them keep in touch with their family and friends. Rapid clicks help them echolocate, which means finding things by listening to echoes.

The best-studied kind, the bottlenose dolphin, does something special with whistles. Each one invents its own signature whistle when it is a baby, and keeps it for life. Other dolphins learn it and copy it to call that dolphin, a bit like saying its name.

Nobody has found a dolphin grammar yet, so we can't call it a language. But it is far more than random noise.

Surprising but true

Dolphins seem to call each other by name.

Calling by name

In 2013, two scientists in Scotland, Stephanie King and Vincent Janik, tested wild bottlenose dolphins. They recorded each dolphin's signature whistle, then played copies back through an underwater speaker. Dolphins answered when they heard their own whistle, and mostly ignored others.

Another study found that dolphins remember the whistles of old tank mates even after 20 years apart. That is longer than any other animal's social memory measured so far, apart from ours.

In Sarasota Bay in Florida, scientists have followed the same wild dolphin families since 1970. They have found that mothers whistle their own signature much more often when they have a new calf, perhaps helping the baby learn whose call to follow.

Dolphins use sound to work together, too. In the town of Laguna in southern Brazil, wild dolphins herd fish towards people standing in the shallows. Then the dolphins make a sudden dive or head slap, and the fishers throw their nets. Scientists say this partnership has gone on for well over a century, passed down through dolphin and human families alike.

Seeing with sound

A dolphin's clicks come from its nose passages and are focused by the rounded, fatty bump on its forehead, called the melon. The clicks bounce off a fish, a rock or a net. The dolphin picks up the echoes through its lower jaw, which passes the sound to its ears.

From the echoes, it can work out how far away something is, how big it is and even what it is made of. Bats use the same trick in the air.

India's river dolphin

The Ganges river dolphin lives in the muddy waters of the Ganga and Brahmaputra and their branches, flowing down from the Himalayan rivers. In murky water, good eyes are useless, and its eyes have no lens at all. It can sense light, but it finds food and its way almost entirely by clicking.

People along the river call it susu, after the sound it makes when it surfaces to breathe. It often swims tilted on its side, sweeping its long beak through the mud for fish and shrimps.

India made it the National Aquatic Animal in 2009. The first count along the country's rivers, published in 2025, found about 6,300 river dolphins.

Noisy rivers and seas

Dolphins depend on hearing, so noise is a real problem for them. Ship engines, dams and dredging fill the water with sound that can drown out their calls. River dolphins also get tangled in fishing nets, and dams cut their rivers into pieces.

Some places are fighting back. The Vikramshila sanctuary in Bihar protects a stretch of the Ganga for river dolphins. At Chilika Lake in Odisha, boat tours watch another kind, the Irrawaddy dolphin. Like all dolphins, they are whales' close cousins, and their ancestors once walked on land.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Dolphins are a kind of fish.

Truth: Dolphins are mammals, like whales and people. They breathe air through a blowhole, are born live and drink their mother's milk.

Try this

Stand in a school playground with an adult, about 20 big steps from a tall wall, and clap once, sharply. Listen for the echo. Step closer and clap again. The echo comes back sooner, and that timing is what a dolphin uses to judge how far away a fish is.
