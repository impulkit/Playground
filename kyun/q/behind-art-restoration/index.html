How do experts restore old paintings?

For well over 100 years, people thought Rembrandt's most famous painting showed a night scene. Then restorers cleaned off the dirty varnish and found daylight.

The short answer

Old paintings get dirty and damaged. Smoke, dust and fingerprints settle on them. Varnish, the clear protective coat that artists brushed on top, slowly turns yellow or brown as air changes it, the same kind of slow chemical change that makes iron rust. Paint can crack, flake or fade.

Experts called conservators clean and repair paintings so that they last and look closer to how the artist meant. They work slowly, often with cotton swabs, mild liquids and microscopes. Before touching anything, they study the painting with X-rays, infrared cameras and tiny paint samples. And they follow one golden rule: anything they add should be removable by someone in the future.

Surprising but true

In 2012, an elderly amateur painter in Borja, Spain, tried to touch up a faded church fresco of Jesus. The result looked so odd that it went viral, and tourists now travel to the village to see it.

Detective work first

A conservator starts by looking very closely. Under a microscope, a crack can look like a canyon. Ultraviolet light makes old varnish glow greenish, while later touch-ups show up as dark patches.

X-rays pass through some paints but not others. That can reveal hidden changes. In Picasso's painting The Blue Room, scans found a portrait of a bearded man underneath. Infrared cameras can see the artist's first sketch lines below the paint, like seeing a rough draft under a finished essay.

Cleaning, one swab at a time

Removing old varnish is a delicate job. The conservator tests liquids on a tiny corner, about the size of a fingernail. The right one softens the varnish but leaves the paint alone.

Then comes cleaning, swab by swab. A large painting can take months or even years. As the brown layer comes off, colours that nobody alive had seen suddenly appear: bright blue skies, pink cheeks, gold threads.

The night that was really day

Rembrandt painted his huge group portrait of Amsterdam's city guards in 1642. Over time, layers of varnish and grime made it so dark that people began calling it The Night Watch. After cleaning in the 1940s, it turned out to be set in daylight, but the name stuck.

In 2019, the Rijksmuseum began Operation Night Watch. Conservators worked in a glass room inside the gallery, so visitors could watch. They took more than 8,000 super-detailed photos, joined into one giant image, and mapped every layer of paint.

Arguments over the Sistine Chapel

Between 1980 and 1994, restorers cleaned Michelangelo's ceiling and wall paintings in the Sistine Chapel in Rome. Centuries of candle smoke and old glue were removed. Underneath were bright oranges, greens and pinks.

Some art experts were thrilled. Others argued that the cleaning had removed Michelangelo's own final shadows along with the dirt. The debate shows that restoring art always involves hard choices.

Lessons from Ajanta

In India, the 1,500-year-old cave paintings at Ajanta have had a tricky history. In the 1920s, restorers coated parts of them with shellac varnish to protect them. Over time it turned dark and yellow, hiding the colours it was meant to save.

Later, conservators from the Archaeological Survey of India spent years slowly removing that varnish. Today they control light and humidity, and limit how many visitors enter some caves at once, because human breath carries moisture that can harm the paint.

When it goes wrong

In 2012, an elderly amateur painter in the Spanish town of Borja tried to fix a flaking church painting of Jesus. Her version looked more like a fuzzy monkey. Photos spread around the world. Since then, tourists have visited the church in large numbers, and the town has even sold souvenirs.

That is why museums trust trained conservators. The same scans and paint tests sometimes uncover a secret of a different kind: a painting that is not old at all, but a clever piece of art forgery.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Restorers repaint old paintings to make them look brand new.

Truth: Good conservators touch as little as they can. They remove dirt and failing varnish, and only fill tiny gaps where paint is lost, using paints that can be taken off again later. The aim is to keep the artist's work, not replace it.

Try this

Try a mini cleaning test. Draw a colourful picture with crayons, then rub a thin layer of brown shoe polish or cocoa paste over a corner. Let it dry, then gently clean a small square with a cotton bud dipped in a little water. Notice how slow and careful you have to be.
