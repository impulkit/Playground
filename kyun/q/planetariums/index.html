What happens inside a planetarium?

Inside a planetarium, you can see a sky full of thousands of stars at noon, in the middle of a crowded city.

The short answer

A planetarium is a theatre with a curved, dome-shaped ceiling that becomes a screen for the night sky. When the lights go down, a projector in the middle, or several around the edge, fills the dome with stars, planets and the Milky Way.

The audience sits in reclining seats and looks up. The projector can copy the sky from any place on Earth, on any date, past or future. It can speed up time so you watch a whole night pass in a minute, or show where the planets will be next year.

Modern planetariums can also fly you through space, past the rings of Saturn or out to distant galaxies, using digital projectors that cover the whole dome.

The machine that copies the sky

Before planetariums, people built mechanical models of the solar system, with balls on rods that turned with gears, called orreries. But they showed the planets from outside, not the sky as we see it.

In the early 1920s, engineers at the Zeiss optics company in Germany, led by Walther Bauersfeld, came up with a new idea. They built a projector shaped a bit like a giant dumbbell, packed with lenses. Bright lamps inside shone through tiny holes to throw sharp points of light onto a dome.

It was tested in 1923 on the factory roof in Jena. A permanent planetarium opened at the Deutsches Museum in Munich soon after. Crowds queued to see it, and other cities soon wanted planetariums of their own.

Planetariums spread

In 1930, the Adler Planetarium opened on the lakefront in Chicago, the first in the Americas. Many US cities followed, During the space race, NASA astronauts from the Mercury to the Apollo missions trained at the Morehead Planetarium in Chapel Hill, North Carolina. They learned to recognise stars they might need for steering if their instruments failed.

India's planetariums became famous too. The Birla Planetarium in Kolkata, opened in 1963, is one of the largest in Asia and is shaped a little like the Sanchi Stupa. The Nehru Planetarium in Mumbai opened in 1977, and many more now operate in cities and science centres across India.

Tricking your eyes

A planetarium works because your brain is easy to fool. The dome is only a few metres above you, but because it curves all around and you can't see its edges in the dark, it feels like an endless sky. That is the same kind of brain trick behind many optical illusions.

Many domes are made of thin metal sheets full of tiny holes. Speakers hidden behind the dome send sound through them, so music and voices seem to come from the sky. The room is kept very dark so your eyes can adjust and pick up faint stars, just as they would outdoors.

A dark sky indoors

Many children in big cities have never seen the Milky Way, because streetlights and buildings drown out the faint stars. On a really dark night in the countryside, you might see a few thousand stars at once. A planetarium can show all of them, plus the stars only visible from the other side of the world.

That is why planetariums are popular with schools. Students learn to spot constellations, see how the Moon's shape changes and find out why the stars seem to wheel around the North Star. Some then go outside to try stargazing for real.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A planetarium and an observatory are the same thing.

Truth: An observatory has real telescopes for looking at the sky. A planetarium shows a projected sky on a dome indoors, so it works in daytime, in cloudy weather and in cities full of light.

Try this

Make a mini planetarium. Ask an adult to help you poke small holes in the bottom of a paper cup in the shape of a constellation, like Orion. In a dark room, shine a phone torch into the cup and point it at the ceiling to project your stars.
