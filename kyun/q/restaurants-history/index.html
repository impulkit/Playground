When did the first restaurants open?

The word "restaurant" first meant a soup. In 1760s Paris, it was a bowl of rich broth sold to people who felt weak and needed "restoring".

The short answer

People have bought cooked food from shops, inns and stalls for thousands of years. But a restaurant in the modern sense, where you sit at your own table, choose from a menu and pay for what you order, is newer. Cities in Song dynasty China had places like this by about 900 years ago. In Europe, they took off in Paris in the 1760s.

The word itself is French. A restaurant was first a nourishing broth that "restored" your strength. Shops selling it began to call themselves restaurants, and the name stuck.

Surprising but true

Guinness World Records lists Sobrino de Botín in Madrid, open since 1725, as the oldest restaurant in the world still running.

Inns, stalls and taverns

For most of history, eating out meant eating whatever was cooking. Travellers stopped at inns, where the owner served one meal at a fixed time on a shared table. If you arrived late, you went hungry. In towns, stalls and taverns sold cheap food and drink. Roman towns had street counters for hot meals, and Indian cities had sweet shops and food sellers, as they still do.

What these places usually lacked was choice. There was no menu, no separate table for your family and no promise that the food would be ready whenever you came.

Song dynasty China

Around the year 1100, Kaifeng and later Hangzhou were among the largest cities on Earth. Writers of the time described restaurants serving dishes from different regions, with waiters who memorised long orders and shouted them to the kitchen. Some places specialised in noodles or vegetarian food, much like restaurants today.

Merchants and officials travelling from distant provinces wanted food from home. Restaurants offering the flavours of Sichuan or the south grew up to serve them, an early version of the regional food courts found in Indian malls.

What did ancient China invent?The oldest printed book with a date on it was made in China in 868 CE, almost 600 years before Gutenberg's printing press.

Paris and the soup shops

In the 1760s, a few Paris shops started selling bouillon, a rich meat broth, to customers who felt delicate. Unlike inns, they let people come at any time, sit at small separate tables and choose from a written list of dishes with prices. That list was an early menu.

The idea spread fast. After the French Revolution of 1789, some cooks who had worked for noble families opened restaurants of their own. By the early 1800s, Paris had hundreds of them, and the French word spread to languages all over the world.

Spain has an even older survivor. Sobrino de Botín in Madrid has served roast meats since 1725, and Guinness World Records lists it as the oldest restaurant still running. Its owners say the wood-fired oven has hardly gone out in three centuries.

Restaurants come to America

In New York, the Delmonico brothers, immigrants from Switzerland, opened a café in 1827 and later a grand restaurant. Delmonico's offered printed menus, tablecloths and a wide choice of dishes, which was a new experience for Americans used to taverns. Over the next century, diners, cafeterias and family restaurants spread across the USA, leading eventually to the fast food chains of the 1950s.

Udupi hotels and Irani cafés

In India, eating out has a long history at temples, festivals and food stalls. Modern restaurants grew in the late 1800s and early 1900s, often called "hotels" even when they offered no rooms. Cooks from Udupi in Karnataka opened vegetarian restaurants serving dosas and idlis in cities across India. The Mavalli Tiffin Room, or MTR, opened in Bengaluru in 1924 and still has queues on weekend mornings.

In Mumbai and Pune, Persian migrants from Iran started Irani cafés with bentwood chairs, marble tables and bun maska with chai. A few of these cafés, more than a century old, still serve the same breakfasts. Veeraswamy, opened in London in 1926, is often called the oldest surviving Indian restaurant in Britain.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Restaurants were invented when the French Revolution left royal chefs out of work.

Truth: Paris already had restaurants in the 1760s and 1770s, years before the Revolution of 1789. The Revolution did help, because some cooks who had worked for nobles opened their own places, but it did not start the idea.
