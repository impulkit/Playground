How do seatbelts save lives?

The engineer who designed the modern seatbelt let every car company use it for free, and it has saved more than a million lives.

The short answer

When a car crashes, it stops almost instantly. The people inside don't. They keep moving forward at the speed the car was going, until something stops them. Without a belt, that something might be the steering wheel, the windscreen or the seat in front.

A seatbelt stops you along with the car. It holds you across your hips and chest, which are protected by strong bones, and spreads the force over a wide area. The belt also stretches very slightly, so you slow down a fraction more gently.

That's why a seatbelt is one of the simplest, cheapest safety devices ever made, and one of the most effective. In the USA, safety experts estimate belts save around 15,000 lives a year.

Surprising but true

Volvo, the company that patented the three-point seatbelt in 1959, chose to let any carmaker in the world use the design without paying.

Why you keep moving

In 1687, the English scientist Isaac Newton described a rule now called his first law of motion. A moving object keeps moving at the same speed, in the same direction, unless a force stops it.

You've felt this on a bus. When the driver brakes hard, you lurch forward. You weren't pushed. Your body just kept going while the bus slowed down. In a crash, the slowing happens in a split second, so the lurch is violent. At 50 km/h, an unbelted person hits whatever is in front of them about as hard as if they'd fallen from the top of a three-storey building.

Who was Isaac Newton?Sent home from university by a deadly plague, a young man in a farmhouse worked out ideas about light, motion and gravity that still guide spacecraft.

A gift from Sweden

Early seatbelts were simple straps across the lap. They helped, but in a crash the body could fold forward over them, injuring the stomach and head. In 1959, an engineer named Nils Bohlin, working at the Swedish carmaker Volvo, designed a better belt. It had one strap over the shoulder and one across the hips, joining at a buckle beside the seat. This is the three-point belt that almost every car uses today.

Volvo patented it, then did something unusual. It let any carmaker use the design for free, because it was too important to keep for itself. Safety researchers estimate the three-point belt has saved more than a million lives worldwide.

The clever reel

A seatbelt has to let you move normally but lock instantly in a crash. Inside the reel by your shoulder is a spring that keeps the belt snug, and a locking mechanism. A small weight or a spinning catch reacts to a sudden jerk or a sharp stop, and jams the reel so no more belt can come out.

Many modern cars go further. Sensors trigger tiny charges called pretensioners that pull the belt tight in the first instant of a crash. Others have load limiters that let the belt give a little, so it doesn't press too hard on the chest. Belts are designed to work with airbags, not instead of them.

The back seat matters too

Many people buckle up in front and forget in the back. In India, the law has long required belts in all seats, but for years few people wore them in the back. In 2022, after a well-known businessman died in a crash while riding in the back seat without a belt, the issue became front-page news and police started enforcing the rule more often.

Back-seat passengers without belts are at serious risk themselves. They can also be thrown forward into the people in front. Children need car seats or booster seats until they're big enough for an adult belt to fit across their hips and shoulder, not their stomach and neck.

Myth or fact?

Myth: In a slow crash, you can brace yourself with your arms instead of a seatbelt.

Truth: Even at city speeds, the force of a sudden stop can be many times your body weight, far too much for arms to hold. A seatbelt spreads that force across the strongest parts of your body.

Try this

With an adult, sit in a parked car and buckle up. Pull the belt out slowly, then give it a quick, sharp tug, and feel it lock. Then put a toy on a skateboard or toy truck, roll it gently into a wall, and watch the toy fly off. Tie it on with a ribbon and try again.
