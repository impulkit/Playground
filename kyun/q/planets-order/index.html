How do you remember the order of the planets?

"My Very Educated Mother Just Served Us Noodles" holds all eight planets in order, from Mercury out to Neptune.

The short answer

There are eight planets, and their order going out from the Sun is Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune. The easiest way to remember them is a mnemonic, a silly sentence where each word starts with the same letter as a planet.

The most famous is "My Very Educated Mother Just Served Us Noodles". Some people say Nachos instead. If you prefer your own, you can make one up: "Many Volcanoes Erupt Mightily, Jumping Super Unusual Nights" works just as well. The sillier the sentence, the better your memory tends to hold on to it.

Surprising but true

Averaged over time, the planet closest to Earth is not Venus but Mercury, because Mercury never strays far from the Sun.

Before 2006, a ninth word

Older books had a sentence with nine words, like "My Very Excellent Mother Just Served Us Nine Pizzas". The P stood for Pluto. In 2006, the International Astronomical Union decided that Pluto did not meet its new definition of a planet, because it hasn't cleared its path of other objects. Pluto became a dwarf planet.

So the mother stopped serving pizzas. Many children were upset, and some scientists still argue about the decision. Out past Neptune, there are many more small icy worlds, and several are called dwarf planets too.

Rocky inside, giants outside

Another way to remember the order is to split it in two. The first four, Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars, are small rocky worlds. Then comes a gap filled with the asteroid belt. Then the four giants: Jupiter and Saturn, huge balls of gas, and Uranus and Neptune, colder worlds rich in ice.

There is a pattern in the sizes too. Jupiter is the largest of all, so big that more than 1,300 Earths could fit inside it. Saturn, with its rings, is next. Uranus and Neptune are a similar size, like twins. The rocky planets look tiny next to them.

Planets in Hindi and your week

In Hindi, the planets have names that many children already know from the days of the week: Budh (Mercury), Shukra (Venus), Mangal (Mars), Brihaspati or Guru (Jupiter) and Shani (Saturn). So Mangalvar, Tuesday, is the day of Mars, and Shanivar, Saturday, is the day of Saturn. English does the same thing with Saturday.

Uranus and Neptune can't be seen without a telescope and were only discovered in 1781 and 1846. In Hindi they are often called Arun and Varun. Ancient Indian astronomers, like those in many other cultures, tracked only the planets they could see with their own eyes.

How far apart they are

Diagrams usually show the planets spaced neatly, but real distances are huge and uneven. If the Sun were a football at one end of a cricket ground, Earth would be a peppercorn about 25 metres away. Jupiter would be a large marble about 125 metres away. Neptune would be a small bead about 750 metres off, far outside the stadium.

Neptune is about 30 times farther from the Sun than Earth. Light from the Sun takes about eight minutes to reach us, but about four hours to reach Neptune. A year on Neptune lasts about 165 Earth years, so it has gone round the Sun only about once since it was discovered.

Which planet is closest to Earth?

Most people say Venus, because Venus comes closer to us than any other planet. But in 2019, a small group of researchers worked out the average distance over many years. Venus spends long stretches on the far side of the Sun.

Mercury, circling tightly near the Sun, never gets very far from anyone. On average, it is the closest planet to Earth, and to every other planet too. Order in space depends on what question you ask.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The planets sometimes line up in a perfectly straight row.

Truth: The planets move on slightly tilted orbits at different speeds, so they never form a neat straight line in space. Sometimes several appear close together in our sky, which can look like a row from Earth.

Try this

Make a pocket solar system from a strip of paper 1 metre long. Mark the Sun at one end, then measure and mark each planet: Mercury 1.3 cm, Venus 2.4 cm, Earth 3.3 cm, Mars 5 cm, Jupiter 17 cm, Saturn 32 cm, Uranus 64 cm and Neptune at the far end. You'll find the four rocky planets squashed together near the Sun.
