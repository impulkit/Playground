What are the white lines planes leave in the sky?

The long white lines behind jets are clouds made by the plane itself, from the water in its own exhaust, freezing in air colder than minus 40°C.

The short answer

The white lines are contrails, short for condensation trails. When a plane's jet engine burns kerosene fuel, it gives off hot gases full of water vapour. High in the sky, about 8 to 12 km up, the air is usually colder than minus 40°C. The vapour cools in an instant, condenses around tiny soot particles and freezes into ice crystals.

The result is a long, thin cloud stretching behind the plane. It is the same thing that happens when your warm breath turns into a white puff on a cold morning, only bigger, colder and a lot higher.

Surprising but true

Burning one kilogram of jet fuel produces more than a kilogram of water, because the hydrogen in the fuel joins oxygen from the air.

Water from fuel

It seems odd that burning fuel makes water. But jet fuel is made of hydrogen and carbon. When it burns, the hydrogen joins with oxygen from the air to form water. Burning 1 kg of fuel makes more than 1 kg of water vapour.

A big airliner can burn several tonnes of fuel an hour, so it releases tonnes of water vapour every hour into the high, freezing air. Look closely at a fresh contrail and you may see a short clear gap right behind the engines. The exhaust needs a moment to cool before the ice appears.

Why some vanish and some stay

Sometimes contrails disappear within seconds. Other times they stretch across the sky and spread out for hours. The difference is the air around them.

If the upper air is dry, the ice crystals turn back into vapour quickly, and the trail fades. If the air is already moist, close to forming its own clouds, the crystals survive and even grow. The trail can spread into wide sheets that look like natural wispy clouds called cirrus. Old weather sayings that lasting trails mean rain may be coming have some truth to them, because they show moist air high up.

Do contrails warm the planet

This is where scientists are still working things out. During the day, contrails reflect some sunlight back into space, which cools the ground a little. At night, they act like a blanket, trapping heat that would otherwise escape. Overall, most studies find that contrails warm the Earth.

A large 2021 study suggested the warming from contrail clouds may be larger than the warming from the carbon dioxide that planes release, at least in the short term. Other researchers say the uncertainty is still big. What is clear is that they matter for climate change.

Fixing the problem

A surprising finding is that a small share of flights makes most of the warming contrails, those passing through cold, moist patches of air. If pilots fly a little higher or lower, they can avoid these patches.

In 2023, Google and American Airlines tested using computer predictions to steer some flights away from contrail-forming air. They reported about half as many contrails, with only a small increase in fuel. More trials are under way.

Chemtrails and wartime trails

Some people online claim that lasting trails are "chemtrails", chemicals sprayed in secret. Scientists who looked at the evidence found nothing to support this. It is a good example of why fake news spreads: the trails are real and visible, but the explanation is wrong.

During the Second World War, contrails were a danger for pilots, because the white lines gave away the position of bomber formations to the enemy. Planes still make them today, and on a busy day over India's air routes, you can sometimes see several criss-crossing the blue at once. They form at the same heights where powerful jet streams can blow at hundreds of kilometres an hour, which is why a trail can bend or drift within minutes.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Long-lasting trails behind planes are "chemtrails", chemicals sprayed in secret.

Truth: Scientists who have studied the claims found no evidence for it. Trails last longer simply when the upper air is cold and already moist, so the ice crystals do not evaporate quickly.

Try this

On a cold morning, breathe out slowly and watch your breath turn into a small white cloud. Or breathe on a cold mirror and watch it fog. Warm, wet air from your lungs is cooled quickly, and its water vapour condenses into tiny drops, just as a jet's exhaust does high up.
