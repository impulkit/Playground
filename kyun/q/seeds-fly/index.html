How do seeds fly?

Plants can't walk, so many of them build their seeds parachutes, propellers and gliders, and let the wind do the rest.

The short answer

Seeds fly by being shaped to catch the wind. A plant can't move, so if its seeds just fell straight down, the young plants would have to grow in their parent's shade, competing with it for light, water and soil. Seeds that travel get a better start.

Many plants solve this by building seeds that fall slowly. Some have fluffy parachutes, like the dandelion. Some have a wing that makes them spin like a helicopter, like the maple. A few have wide, papery wings that glide like aeroplanes. The slower a seed falls, the farther a breeze can carry it.

Surprising but true

A dandelion seed's fluffy parachute is mostly empty space, yet it holds the seed up by trapping a tiny ring of swirling air above it.

Parachutes made of fluff

Blow on a dandelion clock and a hundred tiny parachutes drift away. Each seed hangs below a crown of about 100 fine bristles.

For years, scientists weren't sure how such a gappy parachute worked. In 2018, researchers in Scotland filmed dandelion seeds in a wind tunnel. They found that air flowing through the bristles forms a stable ring of swirling air, a vortex, just above the crown. That ring lowers the pressure above the seed and helps hold it up. A dandelion seed can stay aloft long enough to travel a kilometre or more on a breezy day.

India has its own fluffy fliers. The aak, or madar, a tough roadside shrub, splits open to release flat brown seeds with tufts of silky white hair. In spring, the silk-cotton tree, or semal, drops pods that burst into clouds of fluff. People have used semal fluff to stuff pillows.

Spinning seeds

Maple seeds have a single wing and spin as they fall. In 2009, scientists showed that a small whirlwind forms along the front edge of the spinning wing, giving it extra lift and slowing its fall. On a breezy day, a seed dropped from a tall tree can land tens of metres away.

Many Indian trees use the same trick. Sal trees, which cover huge areas of central and northern India, drop seeds with several wing-like flaps that whirl to the ground. Mahogany seeds spin too. The drumstick tree, whose long pods go into sambar, makes seeds with three papery wings.

Can plants move?Touch a chhui-mui leaf and it folds shut in seconds. Plants move all the time; most are just too slow for us to notice.

The seed that inspired a plane

In the rainforests of Southeast Asia, a climbing plant called the Javan cucumber, Alsomitra macrocarpa, grows high in the trees. Its seeds have thin, curved wings about 13 cm wide. Released, they glide smoothly in wide circles, sometimes a long way from the parent.

In the early 1900s, the Austrian aircraft designer Igo Etrich studied these seeds and used their shape for his early gliders and planes. Seeds were teaching flight before most people had seen an aeroplane. Their gentle glide is a lot like a well-made paper plane.

Why bother travelling?

Seeds that travel can reach empty ground after a fire, a flood or a landslide, and start new plants there. Some find new islands. After the volcano of Krakatoa in Indonesia erupted in 1883 and wiped out life on the remaining island, ferns and grasses with wind-blown spores and seeds were among the first to return.

Not all seeds fly. Coconuts float across seas. Many fruits are eaten by birds, which drop the seeds far away, which is how many banyan trees get planted. Burrs hitch rides on animal fur. The garden balsam fires its seeds out of pods that burst when touched. Flying is just one of the ways plants have found to move without walking.

Quick facts

Parachutes
dandelion, aak (milkweed) and silk-cotton seeds
Spinners
maple, sal and mahogany seeds
Glider
the Javan cucumber, with a 13 cm wing
Why travel
to escape the parent plant's shade and reach new places
Distance
most land within metres, but some ride the wind for kilometres

Myth or fact?

Myth: The white fluff floating from cottonwood trees in spring causes hay fever.

Truth: The fluff, which carries the trees' seeds, is too big to get deep into your nose and lungs. The sneezing usually comes from tiny pollen grains released by grasses and other plants at the same time of year.

Try this

Collect some winged seeds from a park, such as sal, mahogany or drumstick seeds, or make your own from paper strips with a paper clip as the "seed". Drop each one from the same height with a plain paper clip beside it. Time how long each takes to land, then try again in front of a gentle fan.
