How does a thermometer measure heat?

Most thermometers work because almost everything grows a tiny bit bigger when it warms up.

The short answer

When most things warm up, their tiny particles jiggle faster and push a little further apart, so the material grows. A classic thermometer uses this. It has a small bulb of liquid, usually coloured alcohol or, in older ones, mercury, joined to a very thin glass tube. As the liquid warms, it expands and has nowhere to go but up the tube. As it cools, it shrinks back down.

The tube is so narrow that a tiny change in the liquid shows as a big change in height. Marks printed beside the tube turn that height into a number of degrees. Digital thermometers do the same job with electricity instead of liquid.

Surprising but true

When Anders Celsius made his temperature scale in 1742, he set 0 for boiling water and 100 for freezing. It was turned the other way round after his death.

Galileo's bubbling tube

Around 1593, the Italian scientist Galileo made a device called a thermoscope. It was a glass bulb with a long neck dipped into water. As the air in the bulb warmed or cooled, the water level in the neck went down or up. It showed that something was getting hotter, but it had no scale and was also affected by the weather.

Over the next century, people sealed the liquid inside the glass so air pressure couldn't change the reading. Then they needed a way to agree on the numbers. Early makers each chose their own marks, so a reading of 20 on one thermometer might mean something quite different on another. Scientists in different cities couldn't compare their results. The answer was to pick fixed points that anyone could check, such as melting ice and boiling water.

Fixing the numbers

In 1714, the German-Dutch instrument maker Daniel Gabriel Fahrenheit built a reliable mercury thermometer. His scale put water's freezing point at 32 degrees and boiling at 212. In 1742, the Swedish astronomer Anders Celsius proposed a scale with 100 steps between freezing and boiling, but he put 0 at boiling. Others soon flipped it.

Today almost every country uses Celsius. The USA mainly uses Fahrenheit for weather and cooking, so a hot summer day there might be reported as 95 degrees. That is about 35°C, a very ordinary May afternoon in Delhi. The story of how the scales split is in temperature scales.

Digital and infrared

A digital thermometer has a small part at its tip called a thermistor. Electricity passes through it more easily, or less easily, as its temperature changes. A tiny chip measures the change and shows a number on the screen.

Forehead and ear thermometers work differently. Every warm object gives off invisible infrared light, and warmer things give off more. These thermometers catch that light without touching anything at all. During the COVID-19 pandemic, people met them at the doors of offices, shops and schools in India and the USA.

Why mercury disappeared

Mercury is the only metal that is liquid at room temperature, and it expands very evenly, which made it perfect for thermometers. But it is poisonous, and a broken thermometer leaves silvery beads that are hard to clean up. Many countries have now stopped selling mercury thermometers for home use. If one ever breaks at home, don't touch the beads or sweep them with a broom. Tell an adult, who can clean it up safely. Most home thermometers today are digital, or use harmless coloured alcohol, which is the red or blue line you often see.

Some special thermometers still measure the far ends of temperature. Weather stations record the heat that matters in weather forecasts. Laboratory thermometers measure things colder than the coldest Antarctic night, and others measure molten steel at more than 1,400°C.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Normal body temperature is exactly 37°C (98.6°F) for everyone.

Truth: Body temperature differs from person to person and changes through the day, usually lowest early in the morning. Anywhere from about 36°C to 37.5°C is common. That old 37°C figure is an average from the 1800s, and some recent studies suggest many people today run slightly cooler.

Try this

Make a straw thermometer. Fill a small plastic bottle almost to the top with tap water and a few drops of food colouring. Push a clear straw in so it doesn't touch the bottom and seal the neck around it with modelling clay. Stand the bottle in a bowl of warm tap water and watch the coloured water creep up the straw. Move it to cold water and it sinks.
