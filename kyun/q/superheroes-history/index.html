Why do superheroes wear capes?

When Superman first appeared in 1938, he could not fly. He could only leap about 200 metres, an eighth of a mile, in a single bound.

The short answer

Superheroes wear capes mostly because of how comics began. The first big comic superhero, Superman, appeared in 1938 with a red cape, blue tights and boots. His creators borrowed from the costumes of circus strongmen, acrobats and stage heroes of the time, who wore tights, belts and sometimes cloaks.

Capes also solved a drawing problem. A comic is a set of still pictures. A cape flowing behind a hero shows speed and wind in a single frame. Because Superman was such a hit, many heroes who followed copied the look.

Surprising but true

Superman's ability to fly came later. Animated cartoons in the early 1940s found that showing him leaping looked silly, so they had him fly instead, and the comics followed.

Superman sets the style

Jerry Siegel and Joe Shuster were teenagers in Cleveland, Ohio, when they dreamed up Superman in the early 1930s. Publishers turned them down for years. Finally, Superman appeared on the cover of Action Comics #1 in June 1938, lifting a car above his head.

The comic sold so well that every publisher wanted its own hero. Batman arrived in 1939, with a dark cape shaped like bat wings. Captain Marvel, Wonder Woman and Captain America soon followed, during the years of the Second World War.

Borrowed from the circus

In the early 1900s, circus strongmen and acrobats performed in tight, stretchy costumes that showed off their muscles. Many wore belts and boots, and some wore short trunks over their tights, just like Superman. Readers already linked this look with strength and daring.

Capes, meanwhile, were the clothes of heroes in stage plays and adventure films, such as Zorro and Dracula. They suggested someone dramatic and a little bit royal. Kings and queens had worn long cloaks for centuries, so a cape quietly said: this person is important.

Drawing movement

In a comic, the artist has only still pictures. A flapping cape makes a jump look higher and a punch look faster. Artists draw it streaming behind a running hero or wrapped around one standing in shadow.

Capes are not for everyone, though. Spider-Man, created in 1962, never had one. Neither do many later heroes, who wear armour, masks or plain suits instead. In a 2004 Pixar film about a family of superheroes, a fierce little costume designer famously refuses to make capes. She lists heroes whose capes got caught in jet engines and lifts, and the joke has followed capes ever since.

Heroes before comics

The idea of a superhero is much older than capes. Ancient stories are full of heroes with powers. The Greek hero Hercules had enormous strength, as Greek myths tell. In the Ramayana, Hanuman leaps across the sea to Lanka and can change his size at will.

Many comic book writers have said they borrowed from these old stories. Thor, a Marvel hero, comes directly from Norse mythology. Wonder Woman's backstory draws on Greek myths about the Amazons.

Indian superheroes

India has its own caped and uncaped heroes. On TV, Shaktimaan (1997) wore a red and gold costume and became a favourite with Indian children. The film Krrish (2006) gave Bollywood a masked superhero. Comics such as Nagraj and Super Commando Dhruva created heroes rooted in Indian settings, as India's comics describes.

Superhero films are now among the biggest earners in cinemas worldwide. Some heroes still wear capes, some wear armour and some wear hoodies, but the old idea remains: an ordinary-looking person hiding extraordinary powers.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A cape would help a real person fly or glide.

Truth: An ordinary cape is too small and floppy to hold up a person. Wingsuits that let people glide have stiff fabric stretched between the arms and legs, and even they cannot climb or hover.
