How do helmets protect your head?

The most important part of a bicycle helmet is the plain white foam inside, and it is designed to be crushed just once.

The short answer

Your brain floats inside your skull, cushioned by a thin layer of fluid. In a fall, your head can stop in a split second while your brain keeps moving and hits the inside of the skull. That is what causes concussions and worse injuries.

A helmet slows that stop down. Its hard outer shell spreads the force of a blow over a wider area and lets the helmet slide on the road instead of snagging. Underneath, a layer of stiff foam crushes as it absorbs the energy. By making your head stop over a few extra milliseconds, a helmet sharply reduces the force that reaches your brain.

Surprising but true

A helmet that has taken one hard hit should be thrown away even if it looks perfect, because the foam inside may already be squashed.

Stopping slowly

A simple rule of physics is at work. The faster something stops, the bigger the force involved. Drop a glass onto a tiled floor and it shatters. Drop it onto a thick quilt and it survives, because the quilt lets it slow down gradually.

A helmet is a quilt for your head, made of foam called expanded polystyrene, or EPS. It is the same material used to pack televisions, only denser and shaped carefully. In a hard hit, millions of tiny air-filled beads in the foam collapse. That uses up the energy of the crash. Once crushed, the foam does not spring back, which is why a helmet works properly only once.

Foam that is too soft squashes flat and lets the head hit the road. Foam that is too hard barely crushes at all. Helmet makers test different densities by dropping helmets with metal heads inside and measuring the jolt.

Shell, liner and strap

The shell is usually tough plastic. On a bicycle helmet it is thin and light, while a motorcycle helmet has a thick shell to resist sharp objects and high-speed slides. The liner is the crushable foam. Comfort pads stop it rubbing your skin, but they do almost nothing in a crash.

The strap may be the most ignored part. An unfastened helmet can fly off before your head hits the ground. A good fit means two fingers between the strap and your chin, and the front edge sitting about two fingers above your eyebrows.

Helmets on Indian and American roads

India has more than 20 crore two-wheelers, and riders without helmets make up a large share of road deaths. Indian law requires both rider and pillion passenger to wear helmets that meet the standard IS 4151 and carry the ISI mark. The World Health Organization reports that wearing a proper motorcycle helmet cuts the risk of death by about 40% and the risk of serious head injury by about 70%.

In the USA, rules vary by state. Some require all motorcyclists to wear helmets, others only young riders. Bicycle helmets sold there must pass a federal test that involves dropping them, with a dummy head inside, onto metal anvils. Wearing a helmet is one of the simplest parts of road safety, along with seatbelts in cars.

Helmets in sport and history

Soldiers have worn helmets for thousands of years, from bronze helmets in ancient Greece to steel ones in the World Wars. In sport, helmets came later. Cricketers began wearing them widely in the late 1970s, when fast bowlers were hurling bouncers at well over 140 km/h. American football players wear thick helmets with face cages, and researchers are still working on ways to protect them from repeated smaller knocks.

New helmet designs add a slippery inner layer that lets the shell twist slightly on impact, reducing the twisting forces on the brain. But no helmet makes a rider invincible. It is a last line of defence, designed to matter in the one moment you hope never comes.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A helmet only needs to be worn on long rides or highways.

Truth: Many head injuries happen close to home, at low speeds, in falls from bicycles or skateboards. A helmet only helps if it is on and fastened before the fall.

Try this

Drop an egg onto a hard floor from knee height inside a sealed plastic bag (so nothing gets messy). Then wrap another egg in bubble wrap or a thick sponge and drop it the same way. The padded egg often survives because the padding makes it stop more slowly. Ask an adult before you try it.
