What is India studying with Aditya-L1?

India has parked a spacecraft 1.5 million km away, at a spot where it can stare at the Sun without ever being blocked.

The short answer

Aditya-L1 is India's first space observatory built to study the Sun. It was launched by ISRO in September 2023 and now watches the Sun from a special point in space called L1, about 1.5 million km from Earth. That is roughly four times as far as the Moon.

Its seven instruments study the Sun's surface, its outer layers and the stream of particles it blows into space. Scientists want to understand how the Sun works and how its storms affect Earth. Those storms can damage satellites, disturb radio and GPS signals, and in extreme cases harm power grids.

Surprising but true

The Sun's thin outer layer, the corona, is well over a million degrees Celsius, hundreds of times hotter than the Sun's visible surface. Nobody is sure why.

A balance point in space

Between Earth and the Sun there is a place where the pull of the two balances in a special way. A spacecraft there can stay lined up with Earth and the Sun, moving round the Sun with us, using very little fuel. This is the first Lagrange point, or L1, named after the 18th-century mathematician Joseph-Louis Lagrange, who worked out where such points must be.

Aditya-L1 doesn't sit perfectly still. It loops around L1 in a big, slow orbit called a halo orbit. It took about four months to get there after launch.

From L1, nothing ever blocks the Sun. A satellite near Earth passes through Earth's shadow, and even the biggest telescopes on the ground lose the Sun every night. Aditya-L1 watches it nonstop.

A blocked-out Sun on demand

The Sun's outer layer, the corona, is a puzzle. The Sun's visible surface is about 5,500°C. The corona, far above it, is well over a million degrees. It is a bit like walking away from a fire and feeling the air get hotter.

The corona is normally hidden by the glare of the Sun, and on Earth it can only be seen properly during a total eclipse, for a few minutes at a time. Aditya-L1 carries an instrument called the Visible Emission Line Coronagraph, built by the Indian Institute of Astrophysics in Bengaluru. It contains a small disc that blocks the bright face of the Sun, making an artificial eclipse that lasts as long as scientists like.

Another instrument, the Solar Ultraviolet Imaging Telescope, built with the Inter-University Centre for Astronomy and Astrophysics in Pune, photographs the Sun in ultraviolet light our eyes can't see.

Storms from the Sun

Sometimes the Sun throws out a huge cloud of electrically charged gas, called a coronal mass ejection. If one heads our way, it can reach Earth in one to three days. Aditya-L1 sits upstream, so it can sample these particles before they reach us.

When they hit Earth's magnetic field, they can trigger brilliant auroras. In May 2024, a powerful solar storm, which Aditya-L1 observed, produced auroras seen as far south as Ladakh.

How did the telegraph send messages across oceans?In 1858, Queen Victoria sent a 98-word message to the US president through a cable on the floor of the Atlantic. It took about 16 hours to get through.

The biggest storm on record struck in September 1859, now called the Carrington Event. Auroras appeared over the Caribbean, and telegraph wires sparked. Some operators found they could send messages with their batteries unplugged. A storm like that today could knock out satellites and power grids, so forecasting space weather matters.

India among the Sun watchers

Aditya-L1 joins other solar observatories at L1, including SOHO, run by the European Space Agency and NASA since 1996. Having several spacecraft watching with different instruments helps scientists cross-check what they see.

The mission also gives Indian students and scientists their own data. ISRO releases Aditya-L1's observations publicly, so researchers in universities across the country can study the Sun without waiting to borrow someone else's spacecraft.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The Sun is a giant ball of fire, burning like a campfire.

Truth: Nothing on the Sun is burning in the everyday sense, because burning needs oxygen. In its core, hydrogen is squeezed so hard that it fuses into helium, releasing enormous energy. That process is called nuclear fusion.

Try this

Tape a heavy coin to one end of a ruler and a light coin to the other. Balance the ruler on one finger and find the balance point. It will be much nearer the heavy coin. L1 is a balance point too, much nearer Earth than the Sun. Remember never to look directly at the Sun.
