How does the USA elect a president?

In five US elections, the candidate with the most votes across the country did not become president.

The short answer

Every four years, in November, American citizens aged 18 and over vote for president. But they are not voting for the president directly. In each state, they are really choosing a group of people called electors.

Together, the electors from all 50 states and Washington, D.C., make up the Electoral College. There are 538 of them. Whoever wins at least 270 electoral votes becomes president.

In almost every state, the candidate who wins the most votes in that state gets all of its electors. That is why a candidate can win the most votes across the country and still lose, which has happened five times.

Surprising but true

US presidential elections are always held on the Tuesday after the first Monday in November, a rule set by Congress in 1845, when many voters were farmers who needed a day to travel.

Why it works this way

The men who wrote the US Constitution in 1787 argued for weeks about how to choose a president. Some wanted Congress to pick. Others wanted a direct vote by the people. Small states feared big states would always win.

The Electoral College was a compromise. Each state gets as many electors as it has members of Congress: one for each of its seats in the House of Representatives, plus two for its two senators. Washington, D.C., got three electors in 1961. California has the most, with 54. Small states such as Wyoming have three.

The long road to November

The race begins more than a year before the vote. Candidates from each party compete in contests called primaries and caucuses, state by state, to win their party's support.

In the summer, each party holds a big convention and officially names its candidate. Then come months of rallies, adverts and televised debates. Polls try to measure who is ahead, often in percentages that change from week to week.

Election night

On Election Day, people vote at schools, churches and libraries, and many states let people vote early or by post. News organisations count results through the night and project winners state by state.

A few states, called swing states, could go either way and get most of the attention. Others reliably vote for one party year after year.

In December, the electors meet in their states and cast their votes. Congress counts them in January, and the new president is sworn in on 20 January, usually on the steps of the Capitol in Washington, D.C.

Who can vote

At first, in most states, only white men who owned property could vote. Over time, amendments to the Constitution widened the vote. The 15th Amendment (1870) said race could not be used to stop men voting, though many states found unfair ways around it for almost a century. The 19th Amendment (1920) gave women the vote. The 26th Amendment (1971) lowered the voting age from 21 to 18.

Today, citizens must usually register before voting, and each state sets its own rules for how.

How India does it differently

India's top leader, the prime minister, is not elected directly either. Voters choose Members of Parliament, and the party or group with a majority in the Lok Sabha forms the government. India's president is elected by MPs and members of state assemblies.

A system people argue about

Americans have debated the Electoral College for a long time. Supporters say it makes candidates pay attention to smaller states and rural areas, and keeps the states important. Critics say every person's vote should count equally across the country, and that the national winner should always win. Changing it would need a constitutional amendment, which is very hard to pass.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Americans vote directly for the president, and whoever gets the most votes nationwide wins.

Truth: Voters in each state are really choosing electors, who then cast the official votes. Usually the national winner also wins the Electoral College, but five times, most recently in 2000 and 2016, they were different people.
