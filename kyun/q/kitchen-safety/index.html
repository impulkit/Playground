How can you be safe in the kitchen?

The gas in an Indian cooking cylinder has no smell at all. The rotten-egg smell is added on purpose, so you can notice a leak.

The short answer

Kitchens are full of useful things that can also hurt you: hot pans, boiling water, sharp knives, gas flames and germs on raw food. Being safe comes down to a few habits that good cooks follow without thinking.

Wash your hands before you touch food. Keep raw meat away from food that will not be cooked. Turn pan handles inwards so nobody knocks them. Use oven gloves, not a thin cloth, for hot pots. Keep hot and sharp jobs for adults, or do them with an adult right beside you. If anything goes wrong, such as a burn, a cut, a fire or a smell of gas, tell an adult at once rather than trying to fix it yourself.

Surprising but true

Cooking gas is naturally odourless. Companies add a strong-smelling chemical so that a leak can be noticed long before it becomes dangerous.

Clean hands, clean food

Many cases of food poisoning start with germs that hitch a ride on hands, knives and chopping boards. Washing with soap for at least 20 seconds removes most of them, because soap breaks up the greasy layer that germs cling to.

The World Health Organization's "Five Keys to Safer Food" are simple: keep clean, separate raw and cooked food, cook thoroughly, keep food at safe temperatures, and use safe water and raw materials. Using one chopping board for raw chicken and then for salad, without washing it, can spread germs to food that will never be cooked.

The danger zone

Bacteria grow fastest at warm temperatures, between about 5 °C and 60 °C. In an Indian summer, the kitchen counter sits right in this zone. Cooked food left out for hours can become unsafe even if it looks and smells fine.

Health agencies advise putting leftovers in the fridge within about two hours, and sooner on hot days. A fridge should be colder than 5 °C. Reheated food should be steaming hot all the way through, rather than just warm on top. Rice, dal and meat dishes need extra care.

Heat, steam and fire

Burns are one of the most common kitchen injuries for children. Steam burns as badly as boiling water. A pressure cooker traps steam at high pressure, so it should only be opened by an adult after it has cooled and the pressure has dropped.

Oil fires are especially dangerous. Water must never go on burning oil, because it explodes into steam and sprays flaming oil around the room. The safe move is to turn off the heat and slide a lid or a damp, heavy cloth over the pan. Grown-ups should also keep long sleeves, dupattas and loose hair well away from open flames.

When you smell gas

LPG, the gas in Indian cooking cylinders, and the natural gas piped into many homes have no smell of their own. Suppliers add a sharp-smelling chemical so that leaks can be noticed. If you smell it, tell an adult immediately.

The usual advice is to turn off the regulator, open windows and doors, and not switch any lights or appliances on or off, since a tiny spark could ignite the gas. Nobody should light a match. Then the gas company's emergency number should be called.

Small cuts and burns

For a small burn, hold it under cool running water for 20 minutes, even if that feels like a long time. Do not use ice, butter or toothpaste. For a small cut, press a clean cloth on it until the bleeding stops, then cover it with a plaster. An adult should check every injury, and anything large, deep or on the face needs a doctor. A short course in first aid teaches these steps properly.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Putting toothpaste, butter or ice on a burn helps it heal.

Truth: These can trap heat or damage the skin further. The advice from doctors is to cool a small burn under cool running water for 20 minutes, and to tell an adult. Large or serious burns need a doctor straight away.

Try this

Test your handwashing. Rub a little turmeric paste or a few drops of oil mixed with turmeric over your hands, then wash with soap while singing "Happy Birthday" twice, which takes about 20 seconds. Check for yellow spots between your fingers, around your thumbs and under your nails, the places most people miss.
