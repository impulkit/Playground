Where does the water in your tap come from?

The water in your glass has been cloud, sea, ice and river many times over, for billions of years.

The short answer

The water in your tap comes from rivers, lakes, reservoirs or underground, and before that it fell as rain or snow. It is part of the water cycle: the Sun warms seas, lakes and plants, water evaporates into the air, cools into clouds, and falls again as rain. Then it flows back towards the sea, and the cycle starts again.

Your local water company collects some of that water on its way, cleans it, and pipes it to your home. After you use it, it goes down the drain, is cleaned again, and returns to rivers and the sea. Not one drop is new.

Surprising but true

On average, a water molecule that evaporates stays in the air for only about nine days before it falls again as rain or snow.

The great loop

The Sun drives the water cycle. It heats the oceans, and water rises into the air as invisible vapour. Plants add more, giving off water through tiny holes in their leaves. High up, the air is colder, and the vapour cools into tiny droplets around specks of dust. Millions of droplets together make a cloud.

When the droplets join up and get heavy enough, they fall as rain or snow. Some soaks into the ground. Some runs off into streams and rivers. Some freezes into glaciers and stays there for centuries. Sooner or later, almost all of it makes its way back to the sea.

Not much to drink

Earth has a lot of water, but about 97.5% of it is salty seawater. Most of the fresh water is locked up in ice sheets and glaciers or hidden deep underground. The fresh water in lakes, rivers and swamps is well under 1% of all the water on Earth.

That small share supplies most of the world's farms, factories and homes. So where your tap water comes from depends a great deal on where you live.

From the river to your tap

Bengaluru gets most of its water from the Kaveri river, about 100 km away. The city sits high on a plateau, so the water has to be pumped hundreds of metres uphill. Mumbai draws water from a chain of lakes and reservoirs in the hills to the north, filled each year by the monsoon.

New York City gets most of its water from reservoirs in the Catskill Mountains and the Delaware River area, well over 100 km away. Much of it flows to the city by gravity through long tunnels called aqueducts. The water is so clean at the source that most of it doesn't need filtering, only disinfecting.

How is dirty water made safe to drink?Your tap water may once have been muddy river water. A treatment plant can clean it in a few hours.

Water from underground

Not all tap water comes from rivers. Much of it comes from groundwater, water that has soaked into the soil and collected in cracks and spaces in rock. People reach it with wells and borewells.

India uses more groundwater than any other country. Farmers pump it to water their fields, and many towns depend on it. In some places, wells are being emptied faster than rain can refill them, and the water table is dropping. Rainwater harvesting, catching rain on roofs and guiding it into the ground, is one way people are trying to help.

Cleaning the water

River and lake water carries mud, leaves and germs, so it is cleaned before it reaches you. At a water treatment plant, the water is left to settle so mud sinks. Then it is filtered through sand and gravel, and disinfected, usually with a small amount of chlorine, to kill germs.

After you use water, it goes down the drain into sewers. In many cities, it is cleaned at a sewage treatment plant before being released into a river. Downstream, another town might take it in, clean it again, and pipe it to someone else's tap.

Very old water

The water cycle has been turning for billions of years. The same water has fallen as rain, flowed down rivers and frozen into ice again and again. Some of the water in your glass may once have been in a cloud over the Himalaya, a lake in Africa or a glacier in the last Ice Age.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The world is running out of water because we use it up.

Truth: The total amount of water on Earth stays almost the same, moving around the water cycle. What can run short is clean fresh water in the right place at the right time, which is why droughts and pollution matter so much.

Try this

Make a water cycle in a bowl. Put a little warm water from the tap in a large bowl and stand a small empty cup in the middle. Cover the bowl tightly with cling film and put an ice cube on top, right above the cup. Leave it in the sun. Water evaporates, condenses on the cold film like a cloud, and drips into the cup like rain.
