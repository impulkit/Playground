How fast can penguins swim?

On land a penguin waddles like a toddler in a winter coat. Underwater, a gentoo penguin can swim about four times faster than an Olympic champion.

The short answer

Penguins are birds that gave up flying in air to fly underwater. Most cruise at about 5 to 10 km/h, roughly a brisk walking or jogging pace. But in short bursts, the gentoo penguin can reach about 36 km/h, the fastest of any penguin.

That's about four times faster than the best human swimmers, who top out at roughly 8 to 9 km/h in a race. A penguin uses its wings as stiff flippers, flapping them up and down so they push water on both strokes. Its body is shaped like a torpedo, and its feet and tail steer like a rudder.

Surprising but true

Emperor penguins can release a cloud of tiny air bubbles from their feathers that cuts drag and lets them shoot up to launch themselves onto the ice.

Built like a submarine

On land, a penguin looks a little silly. In water, everything about it makes sense. Its short, stiff wings have flattened bones fused into a paddle. Its bones are dense rather than hollow, so it doesn't bob up like a cork. Short, tightly packed feathers form a smooth, waterproof coat.

Even the black-and-white colours help. From below, a penguin's white belly blends with the bright sky. From above, its black back blends with the dark water. This trick, called countershading, hides it from both hunters and prey. Some penguins also "porpoise", leaping out of the water as they swim, because moving through air for a moment takes less effort than pushing through water.

Deep divers

Emperor penguins are the champions of depth. They have been recorded diving to about 560 m, about two-thirds of the height of the Burj Khalifa. The longest recorded dive, measured in 2018, lasted about 32 minutes.

How do they hold their breath so long? They store oxygen in their blood and muscles, slow their heartbeat, and send blood only to the parts that need it most. Their muscles are packed with a protein that holds oxygen, which makes penguin meat very dark red.

The bubble boost

Emperor penguins have a special trick for getting out of the water. Before they leap onto the ice, they fluff their feathers to trap air, then dive. As they shoot back up, they release the air as a coat of tiny bubbles.

A 2011 study found that this bubble coat reduces drag, the water's resistance, and lets them roughly double their speed for a few seconds. They burst out of the water and land on the ice edge, sometimes a metre or more above the surface. Engineers are interested in using bubble coats to help ships move with less fuel.

Not just ice and snow

Many people picture penguins only on Antarctic ice. In fact, of about 18 species, only a few live on Antarctica itself. Others live on rocky coasts and islands in South America, South Africa, Australia and New Zealand. The African penguin nests on beaches near Cape Town, and the Galápagos penguin lives right on the equator.

Their speed matters, because they're both hunters and hunted. Penguins chase fish, squid and krill, and leopard seals, sea lions and orcas chase them. To survive, they need to be quick and to turn sharply, darting sideways to dodge a seal's jaws.

Humans have borrowed from penguins too. Swimsuit designers study smooth, streamlined bodies to cut drag, and some suits got so good at it that swimming's rule makers banned the fastest full-body ones in 2010. Sport is full of such arguments about gear, from swimsuits to the springy soles of racing sports shoes.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Penguins live at both the North and South Poles.

Truth: Wild penguins live only in the Southern Hemisphere, from Antarctica to South Africa, Australia and South America. The one exception, the Galápagos penguin, lives right on the equator and sometimes crosses just north of it. There are no wild penguins in the Arctic.

Try this

Fill a sink or bucket with water. Push a flat hand through it palm first, then edge first. Feel how much easier the narrow edge moves. Penguins are shaped like torpedoes for the same reason, so water slides past them with less push-back.
