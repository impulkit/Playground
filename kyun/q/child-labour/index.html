Why is child labour wrong?

About a hundred years ago, a photographer named Lewis Hine sneaked into American mills and mines. His photos of working children helped change the law.

The short answer

Child labour means work that is harmful for children or stops them going to school. It isn't the same as helping at home, running an errand or doing a few hours of light work that suits your age.

It is wrong because childhood is when bodies grow and brains learn fastest. A child carrying bricks or breathing dust in a workshop can be injured for life. A child who leaves school at ten may never learn to read well, and will probably earn less as an adult. Their own children are then more likely to work too, so the cycle repeats. Almost every country now bans the worst kinds of child work.

Surprising but true

Helping wash dishes at home is not child labour. The line is crossed when work harms a child's health or keeps them out of school.

Children in the mills

Children have always helped on farms. But during the Industrial Revolution, in the 1700s and 1800s, they were sent into factories and mines in large numbers. They were cheap to hire and small enough to crawl under machines. Some worked 12 hours a day.

The writer Charles Dickens was sent to work in a shoe-polish factory at the age of 12, when his father was jailed for debt. He never forgot it, and poor, overworked children appear all through his novels.

Who was Charles Dickens?At twelve, Charles Dickens was sticking labels on pots of boot polish in a rat-infested factory. He grew up to become the most famous writer of his age.

A camera as a weapon

In the United States, Lewis Hine worked for the National Child Labor Committee from 1908. He sometimes pretended to be a salesman or a fire inspector to get inside mills, mines and canneries. He photographed children at spinning machines and boys covered in coal dust, and wrote down their names and ages.

His photos, now kept by the Library of Congress, shocked many Americans. They helped build support for the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938, which set minimum ages for work across the country. Photography had shown people what words alone had not.

India's laws and campaigners

India's Constitution bans children under 14 from working in factories, mines and other dangerous jobs. Since 2016, the law has banned nearly all work for children under 14, apart from helping a family business after school hours. Teenagers aged 14 to 18 cannot do hazardous work.

Since 2010, the right to education has made school free and compulsory for children aged 6 to 14. Free school lunches through the mid-day meal scheme have given poor families another reason to send children to class. Kailash Satyarthi's group, Bachpan Bachao Andolan, has rescued many thousands of children from workshops and factories since 1980. He shared the Nobel Peace Prize in 2014.

Why it still happens

Most child labour today is not in factories but on farms, in homes and in small workshops. It usually comes from poverty. If a family can't afford food, a child's small wage can feel like the only choice. Debt, illness in the family, a disaster or a school too far away make it more likely.

That is why the solutions are often about families as much as children. Fair wages for parents, schools close to home, free meals and good inspections all help. The number of child labourers fell by tens of millions between 2000 and 2020. Even so, the ILO and UNICEF estimated about 138 million in 2024, and every 12 June the world marks a day against child labour to keep the problem in view. Each child back in a classroom is a cycle broken.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Child labour is a thing of the past, from Victorian times.

Truth: It has fallen a lot, but ILO and UNICEF estimated that about 138 million children were still in child labour in 2024, many of them in farming. The world missed its goal of ending it by 2025.
