What will batteries of the future be like?

Some new batteries use sodium, one of the two elements in table salt, and another kind works by letting iron rust and then un-rust.

The short answer

Most of today's rechargeable batteries, in phones, laptops and electric cars, are lithium-ion batteries. They are light and hold a lot of energy, and they have become much cheaper. But they use metals like lithium, cobalt and nickel that are mined in only a few countries, and they can catch fire if damaged.

So scientists are working on new kinds. Sodium-ion batteries use sodium, a cheap element found in salt. Solid-state batteries replace the liquid inside with a solid, which could make them safer and hold more energy. Iron-air batteries could store electricity from wind and sun for days. The future will probably have many types, each for a different job.

Surprising but true

The price of lithium-ion battery packs fell from more than $1,000 per kilowatt-hour in 2010 to about $108 in 2025, a drop of around 90%.

How a battery works

Every battery has two ends, called electrodes, separated by a material called an electrolyte. When you use the battery, tiny charged particles called ions travel through the electrolyte from one electrode to the other. At the same time, electrons flow through the wire outside, powering your device. Charging pushes everything back the other way.

Lithium is the lightest metal, and its ions are small and move easily. That is why lithium-ion batteries hold so much energy for their weight. In 2019, John Goodenough, Stanley Whittingham and Akira Yoshino won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for developing them.

Cheaper every year

In 2010, a lithium-ion battery pack cost more than $1,000 for each kilowatt-hour it could store. By 2025, according to BloombergNEF, the average had fallen to about $108. Factories got bigger, designs got better and chemists found cheaper recipes, such as batteries made with iron and phosphate instead of costly cobalt.

That price drop is a big reason electric cars and scooters have spread so quickly. It also helps countries like India store solar power made during the day for use at night.

Salt, solids and rust

Sodium sits just below lithium in the periodic table and behaves in a similar way. It is far more plentiful, since there is a huge amount of it in seawater and rock salt. Sodium-ion batteries hold less energy than the best lithium ones, but they cost less and work better in the cold. By 2025, Chinese companies were putting them in some small cars and electric scooters.

Solid-state batteries swap the flammable liquid electrolyte for a solid, such as a special ceramic. They could charge faster and be safer, but making them reliably in large numbers is hard. Several carmakers say they hope to sell cars with them around the end of this decade.

Iron-air batteries are heavy but very cheap. They are being tested by power companies in the USA to store electricity for around 100 hours, enough to cover several cloudy, windless days.

India's battery question

India wants millions of electric vehicles, but it has had to import almost all its lithium. In 2023, the Geological Survey of India announced a lithium deposit estimated at about 5.9 million tonnes in Reasi, Jammu and Kashmir. It is still being studied, and it is not yet clear how much can be mined. India is also building large battery factories of its own.

Old batteries, new life

When a battery wears out, its metals don't vanish. Recycling plants can recover lithium, cobalt, nickel and copper and use them to make new batteries. Car batteries that are too tired for driving can still store power for buildings for years.

Batteries thrown into ordinary rubbish can cause fires in garbage trucks and leak harmful materials, which is part of the problem of e-waste. Many scientists think the battery of the future will be designed from the start to be easy to take apart and reuse.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You should let your phone battery run down to zero before charging it.

Truth: That advice was true for some older types of battery. Lithium-ion batteries actually last longer if you keep them between about 20% and 80% and avoid draining them completely.
