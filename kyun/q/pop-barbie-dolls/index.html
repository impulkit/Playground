How did dolls change over time?

Children in the Indus Valley played with clay figures about 4,500 years ago, and a Barbie went to space as "Miss Astronaut" in 1965, before any American woman had.

The short answer

Dolls are some of the oldest toys in the world. Children in ancient Egypt, Rome, China and the Indus Valley played with figures made of clay, wood, cloth or bone. For thousands of years, most dolls were handmade at home or by local craftspeople.

In the 1800s, factories in Germany and France began making dolls with china heads and glass eyes. Then came rubber and plastic. In 1959, an American company launched Barbie, a grown-up fashion doll that changed the toy business.

Today dolls come in every shape, skin colour and job, from astronauts to doctors. They still tell us what each age thought children should dream about. Look at a shelf of dolls from different centuries and you are looking at a history of clothes, jobs and family life.

Surprising but true

Barbie was first sold in 1959 as a teenage fashion model, and her full name is Barbara Millicent Roberts.

Dolls from the deep past

Archaeologists have found small clay figures, animals on wheels and toy carts in the old Indus cities, from about 4,500 years ago. Some animal figures even have heads that nod when you pull a string. Ancient Egyptians buried wooden "paddle dolls" with painted faces and long strings of beads for hair.

In Rome, a girl named Crepereia Tryphaena was buried in the 100s CE with an ivory doll whose arms and legs could bend at the joints. It was found in 1889, still with tiny jewellery. Not every ancient figure was a toy, though. Some were used in religious rituals, and experts often argue over which was which.

Dolls for festivals

Many cultures display dolls on special days. In Japan, families set out tiered shelves of royal Hina dolls for Hinamatsuri, or Girls' Day, on 3 March. In south India, many homes arrange steps of dolls called golu or bommai kolu during Navaratri, showing gods, saints, animals and everyday village scenes.

Indian craft towns have their own traditions. Channapatna in Karnataka makes smooth wooden dolls coloured with natural dyes. Kondapalli in Andhra Pradesh carves figures from soft, light wood.

In Russia, the first matryoshka, or nesting doll, was made in the 1890s. Each doll opens to reveal a smaller one inside, sometimes eight or more. The first set showed a girl in a headscarf holding a rooster, with her family tucked inside her.

Enter Barbie

Ruth Handler, who started the toy company Mattel with her husband, noticed her daughter Barbara playing with paper dolls of grown-up women. Most dolls then were babies. Handler wanted a doll that let girls picture adult lives. She was partly inspired by a German doll called Bild Lilli.

Barbie was launched on 9 March 1959 at a toy fair in New York. Her boyfriend, Ken, named after Handler's son, arrived in 1961. Barbie quickly gained hundreds of outfits and jobs, from nurse to pilot to news reporter. In 1965 there was "Miss Astronaut" Barbie, long before the first American woman in space. Arguments and changes

Barbie has always had critics. Many people said her very thin body and tiny feet were impossible in real life, and might make children unhappy with their own bodies. Others said she showed girls they could do any job.

Mattel has changed her over time. A Black doll named Barbie came out in 1980. In 2016 the company added tall, petite and curvy body types. Later dolls included wheelchair users and dolls with hearing aids.

In the US, action figures like GI Joe arrived in 1964. Toy companies avoided the word "doll" to sell them to boys. And the strangest part? Children's playthings have changed with every new material and idea, as you can trace in the history of toys.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Dolls have always been toys mostly meant for girls.

Truth: In many cultures and times, dolls and figures were shared by children of all kinds, or used in festivals and ceremonies. Toy makers in the 1960s even called boy-targeted dolls "action figures" to sell them, and the line between them is a marketing choice.

Try this

Make a simple doll from a clothes peg, scraps of cloth and wool. Then make a second one that shows a job you might like to do one day. Notice how much a doll's clothes and tools tell you about what its maker thought was important.
