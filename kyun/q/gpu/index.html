What is a GPU, and why does AI need so many?

A GPU was invented to draw video games faster. Today, tens of thousands of them working together teach AI to write, talk and see.

The short answer

A GPU, or graphics processing unit, is a computer chip that does thousands of small sums at the same time. It was first made to draw pictures on a screen, quickly enough for smooth video games.

AI needs GPUs because teaching an AI is mostly a mountain of simple multiplications. A GPU can do huge numbers of them at once, so it learns in weeks instead of years. The biggest AI systems use tens of thousands of GPUs joined together.

Surprising but true

One of Nvidia's AI chips announced in 2024, called Blackwell, packs 208 billion tiny switches. That is about 25 for every person on Earth.

A kitchen with many cooks

Every computer has a CPU, its main brain. A CPU has a few powerful cores, each able to handle tricky jobs one after another, like a head chef.

A GPU is more like a giant wedding kitchen with thousands of helpers. Each helper can only do one simple job, such as rolling a roti. But a thousand helpers rolling at once beat any single chef. Working side by side like this is called parallel computing.

Pictures need exactly this. A screen is made of millions of pixels, tiny dots of colour. In a game, the colour of every pixel must be worked out again many times a second. Each pixel is a small sum, and they can all be done at once. Inside, a GPU holds billions of transistors, the tiny switches that computer chips are made of.

A GPU does not replace the CPU. The two work as a team. The CPU runs the computer, decides what needs doing and hands the big, repetitive jobs to the GPU. In a phone or laptop, both often sit side by side on the same chip.

From games to AI

In 1999, Nvidia, a company in California's Silicon Valley, launched the GeForce 256 and sold it as the world's first GPU. It took the job of drawing 3D scenes away from the CPU, so video games looked far better.

Scientists soon noticed that a GPU's thousands of helpers could do other maths too. In 2006, Nvidia introduced software called CUDA that let programmers use GPUs for any kind of number crunching.

The big moment came in 2012. A team at the University of Toronto trained an AI program on Nvidia GPUs to recognise objects in photos. It beat every other entry in a famous contest by a wide margin. Soon, AI researchers everywhere wanted GPUs.

What is a neural network?A neural network learns to spot a cat the way you learned to spot one, by seeing thousands of examples, not by reading a definition.

Why AI is hungry for GPUs

A neural network is made of millions or billions of numbers, joined in layers. To learn, it looks at an example, makes a guess, checks how wrong it was, and nudges all its numbers a little. Then it does it again, billions of times. Each step follows the same algorithm, and each step is mostly multiplying long lists of numbers, the perfect job for a GPU.

Large language models, the kind behind chatbots, learn from huge amounts of text. Training one can keep thousands of GPUs busy for months, inside buildings called data centres. These use lots of electricity and need powerful cooling, because busy chips get hot. Joined together, they work like a supercomputer.

India's GPUs

GPUs are expensive, so most students and small companies cannot buy many. In 2024, India started the IndiaAI Mission to share them. The government pays part of the cost so researchers and startups can rent time on powerful machines cheaply. By February 2026, about 38,000 GPUs were part of it, and the minister said 20,000 more were being added. Reports say Nvidia chips make up more than 85% of these shared machines.

Because so much of the AI world runs on one company's chips, news about Nvidia is watched closely, as the story of how a company can lose a trillion dollars in a week shows.

Myth or fact?

Myth: GPUs are only for gaming.

Truth: GPUs were built for games, but today they also train AI, forecast weather, design medicines and help scientists study space. The biggest buyers of GPUs now are companies running giant AI data centres.

Try this

Time yourself writing the 5 times table up to 5 x 20, alone. Then split it with four friends or family members, each writing four lines at the same time, and time it again. Which was faster? You have just worked like a GPU.
