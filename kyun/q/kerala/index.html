What makes Kerala unique?

Kerala is the first place in India to feel the monsoon each year, and its pepper drew Roman ships across the sea about 2,000 years ago.

The short answer

Kerala is a narrow state squeezed between the Arabian Sea and the green Western Ghats mountains, on India's south-west tip. It is about 590 km long but in places only 35 km wide. That shape gives it beaches, mountains and a maze of lagoons all within a few hours' drive.

What makes Kerala stand out is its mix. It has some of India's best health and education results, a long history of trade with the world, and Hindus, Muslims and Christians who have lived side by side for centuries. Its name is often linked to kera, the Malayalam word for coconut, and coconut palms are everywhere.

Surprising but true

Kochi has a synagogue built in 1568, one of the oldest working synagogues in the Commonwealth.

Where the monsoon knocks first

Every year, around 1 June, the southwest monsoon reaches Kerala before anywhere else in India. Clouds roll in from the sea and hit the Western Ghats, dropping heavy rain on the hills. The India Meteorological Department announces the date, and the whole country watches, because farmers everywhere depend on it.

All that rain feeds 44 rivers, thick forests, and tea and spice plantations on the hills. It also fills the backwaters.

The backwaters

Behind the beaches lies a network of lagoons, lakes, canals and rivers, called the backwaters. Fresh water from the hills meets salty water from the sea here. Vembanad, the biggest lake, and its connected wetlands cover more than 1,500 km². People live along the canals, travelling by boat to school and market, and farming rice in fields that lie below sea level.

Old rice barges called kettuvallams have been turned into houseboats for visitors. During the snake boat races, long boats with over 100 rowers each race to the beat of songs. The Nehru Trophy race has been held since 1952.

The spice coast

Kerala's black pepper, cardamom and cinnamon were famous long before Europeans arrived. Around 2,000 years ago, Roman and Arab traders sailed to ports such as Muziris to buy spices. In 1498, the Portuguese sailor Vasco da Gama landed near Kozhikode (Calicut), opening a sea route from Europe. The Portuguese, Dutch and British fought for control of this rich trade.

These trade routes brought people too. Christian communities in Kerala trace their faith back many centuries. Arab traders brought Islam, and Kerala has some of India's oldest mosques. In Kochi, Jewish families built the Paradesi Synagogue in 1568.

Dance, martial arts and Onam

Kathakali dancers wear huge skirts and painted faces, green for heroes and red for fierce characters, and act out stories from the epics with tiny eye and hand movements. It is one of India's great classical dance forms, along with Kerala's graceful Mohiniyattam. Kalaripayattu is a martial art with kicks, leaps and stick fighting that is still taught in Kerala today.

The biggest festival is Onam, the harvest festival, when families make flower carpets called pookalam and eat a feast called sadya, served on a banana leaf. A full sadya can have more than 20 dishes, from sambar and avial to sweet payasam, and there is a set order for where each one goes on the leaf.

Schools and the Gulf

In the 2011 census, about 94% of people in Kerala could read and write, the highest of any Indian state. Kerala also has long life expectancy. Many Keralites work in Gulf countries such as the UAE and Saudi Arabia and send money home, which has built houses and businesses across the state.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The monsoon arrives everywhere in India on the same day.

Truth: It reaches Kerala first, usually around 1 June, and moves north and west over the following weeks. It normally covers the whole country only by early July.
