What are human rights?

In 1948, a woman from India changed one word in a world document, and it now says "all human beings" instead of "all men".

The short answer

Human rights are the basic freedoms and protections every person should have, simply because they are human. They include the right to life and to be free from slavery and torture. They also include the right to speak your mind, follow a religion or none, go to school and be treated fairly by the law.

The most famous list is the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, agreed by the United Nations in 1948. It has 30 articles. The first says, "All human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights."

The declaration itself is not a law. But it has shaped treaties, courts and constitutions around the world, including India's Constitution.

Surprising but true

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights has been translated into more than 500 languages, more than almost any other document.

Why 1948?

The Second World War ended in 1945. Tens of millions of people had died, and the Holocaust had shown how a government could turn on its own people. Many leaders felt the world needed a shared promise that this must never happen again.

The new United Nations set up a commission to write that promise. It was chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt, widow of the American president Franklin D. Roosevelt. Members came from China, Lebanon, France, India, Chile and many other countries.

One word, changed

The draft's first article began, "All men are born free and equal." Hansa Mehta, India's delegate and a fighter for women's education, objected. In many countries, she pointed out, "men" could be used to mean only men.

The commission changed it to "all human beings". That small edit is still in the declaration, and the UN now credits her for it. On 10 December 1948, in Paris, the UN General Assembly voted to adopt the declaration. Nobody voted against it, though eight countries chose not to vote.

Kinds of rights

Some rights protect you from being harmed or silenced: freedom of speech, a fair trial and the right to vote. Others are about meeting needs, such as education, health care, food and a safe home.

Countries usually find the first kind easier to promise. The second kind costs money, and poorer countries may struggle to provide it. Both are in the declaration.

Rights in action

Ideas in the declaration grew into later treaties, such as the children's rights convention of 1989. Courts in many countries use them in their judgments.

People have often had to struggle for rights that were already written down. The American civil rights movement of the 1950s and 1960s fought for equal treatment that the US Constitution already seemed to promise.

Who checks?

The UN has a Human Rights Council, where countries review each other's records every few years. Independent experts write reports on problems such as child labour or the treatment of prisoners.

Many countries also have their own watchdogs. India set up the National Human Rights Commission in 1993. Anyone can complain to it, and it can investigate and recommend action, though it cannot punish people itself. Groups such as Amnesty International and Human Rights Watch, which are not run by any government, publish reports too. Governments often dislike these reports, which is partly why they matter.

Rights and responsibilities

Every right comes with a responsibility: respecting the same right in others. Your freedom of speech sits beside someone else's right not to be threatened. India's Constitution lists fundamental duties alongside its rights for this reason. Human Rights Day falls on 10 December each year, the day the declaration was adopted.

Myth or fact?

Myth: You have to earn human rights by being a citizen or behaving well.

Truth: Human rights belong to every person simply for being human. Laws may limit some freedoms in fair, set ways, such as sending someone to prison after a trial, but nobody loses their basic rights, like not being tortured.
