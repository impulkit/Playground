Why does ice cream need salt to freeze in old machines?

Plain ice can't freeze ice cream. Add a handful of salt, and the slushy mix around the tub can drop to about minus 20 °C.

The short answer

To freeze ice cream, you need something colder than 0 °C. That is because sugar in the cream mix lowers its freezing point to about minus 2 or 3 °C. Plain ice, sitting at around 0 °C, isn't cold enough to do the job.

Adding salt to the ice changes everything. Salt makes the ice melt, and melting soaks up heat. The salty slush can get as cold as about minus 21 °C. Packed around a metal tub of cream, it pulls heat out of the cream fast, and the mixture freezes.

Surprising but true

When salt makes ice melt, the mixture gets colder, not warmer. Melting soaks up heat from everything around it, including the tub of cream.

Why sugar fights freezing

When water freezes, its tiny particles line up into a neat pattern called a crystal. Sugar and other dissolved things get in the way, so the water has to be colder before it can line up. Scientists call this freezing point depression.

That's handy for ice cream. A mix that froze at 0 °C would turn into a solid block. Because of the sugar, part of the water stays liquid even in a freezer, which is why good ice cream is soft enough to scoop.

How salt makes ice colder

Ice is always melting and freezing a little at its surface. At 0 °C the two happen at the same rate, so nothing seems to change. Salt dissolves in the thin layer of water on the ice. Now that water can't refreeze easily, but the ice keeps melting.

Melting needs energy. Ice takes it as heat from whatever it touches: the air, the bucket and the tub of cream. So the temperature keeps falling until the salty water reaches its own, much lower, freezing point. It's the same science that helps salt melt ice on roads in snowy places like Chicago and Shimla.

The crank and the bucket

In 1843 Nancy Johnson of Philadelphia patented a hand-cranked ice cream freezer. A metal can full of cream sat inside a wooden bucket packed with ice and rock salt. Turning a handle spun a paddle inside the can. Makers used coarse rock salt, whose big chunks dissolve slowly and keep the bucket cold for longer.

The stirring did two jobs. It kept scraping frozen cream off the cold walls so new cream could touch them. It also beat in air and kept the ice crystals small, making the ice cream smooth. Machines like this made ice cream a treat American families could make at home on summer weekends.

India's frozen treats

Long before electric freezers, Indian kulfi makers used the same trick. They poured thick, sweet milk into small cone-shaped moulds and packed them in a big earthen pot, or matka, filled with ice and salt. Then they turned the moulds slowly until the kulfi set. Kulfi isn't churned, so it freezes dense and creamy rather than fluffy.

The Mughals prized cold treats. Records from Emperor Akbar's court describe cooling drinking water with saltpetre, a different salt that also chills water when it dissolves. Ice was sometimes carried down from the hills to the plains.

What changed with fridges

Today most ice cream is made in big electric freezers, and a home refrigerator does the cooling with a pump instead of salt. But hand-cranked and salt-and-ice methods are still used for fun, at fairs and at street kulfi stalls.

The coldest salty slush, at around minus 21 °C, is far below anything plain ice can reach. On the Fahrenheit scale used in the US, that is about minus 6 °F. A Dutch-German scientist named Daniel Fahrenheit is said to have used salt, ice and water to set the zero on his thermometer, in the early 1700s.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Salt melts ice because it warms it up.

Truth: Salt doesn't add heat. It stops water from freezing at 0 °C, so the ice melts. Melting pulls heat from the surroundings, which makes the salty water colder than the ice was.

Try this

Make ice cream in a bag. Pour half a cup of milk, a spoon of sugar and a drop of vanilla into a small zip bag and seal it. Put it inside a bigger bag filled with ice and three big spoons of salt. Wear gloves, shake for 10 minutes and taste.
