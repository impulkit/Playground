How can a frog freeze and come back to life?

Every winter in Alaska, a small frog turns as hard as an ice cube, its heart stops, and in spring it hops away.

The short answer

The wood frog, a small brown frog from North America, survives winter by freezing almost solid. When the cold arrives, it tucks itself under fallen leaves on the forest floor. Ice begins to form on its skin, and up to about two-thirds of the water in its body turns to ice. Its heart stops. It stops breathing. If you picked one up, it would feel like a frozen pebble.

The trick is sugar. The frog's liver floods its cells with glucose, which works as antifreeze. Ice forms only in the spaces between cells, not inside them. When spring comes, the frog thaws, its heart starts beating again, and it hops off to find a mate.

Surprising but true

In the wild in Alaska, wood frogs have stayed frozen for more than half the year and survived temperatures near −18 °C.

A frog-shaped ice cube

Wood frogs live farther north than any other North American frog, all the way into Alaska and beyond the Arctic Circle. They don't burrow deep or hide in ponds like many frogs. They spend the winter in shallow leaf litter, where temperatures drop far below freezing.

Once ice touches its skin, the frog reacts within minutes. Its liver turns stored starch into glucose and pours it into the blood. Sugar levels can rise to many times their normal level, a level that would be dangerous for a person. The sugar soaks into the cells and keeps them from losing too much water or forming ice inside.

Why ice inside cells is deadly

When water freezes, it forms sharp crystals and grows bigger, which is also why ice floats. Inside a cell, those crystals would rip through the thin walls like broken glass in a balloon. A cell full of ice would die.

The wood frog lets ice form in the spaces around its cells, inside its belly and under its skin. As that ice grows, it draws water out of the cells, which shrink but stay alive. The sugar inside them makes the leftover water harder to freeze. The frog also makes urea, another substance that protects cells.

Why does ice float?Almost every solid sinks in its own liquid. Water is the weird one.

Frozen for months

For a long time, scientists studied wood frogs in labs, where they survived about −3 to −6 °C. Then researchers in Alaska tracked frogs in their real winter homes. In a study published in 2014, the Alaskan frogs survived about −18 °C, colder than a home freezer.

They also stayed frozen for a very long time. Some frogs spent more than half the year frozen, with short thaws only in spring. During that time, they did not eat, drink, breathe or move.

Spring thaw

When the weather warms, the frog thaws. Within hours, its heart starts to flutter. Then it begins breathing, and soon it can move. Within a day or so, it is hopping again.

Wood frogs then rush to ponds of fresh snowmelt to breed. Their call sounds like a quacking duck. The tadpoles have to grow fast, before the shallow ponds dry up in summer. Wood frogs are among the first frogs to call in spring, sometimes while patches of ice still float on the water. Breeding so early gives their tadpoles a head start.

What doctors want to learn

When doctors move a heart or kidney from one person to another in a transplant, they have only hours before the organ is damaged. Freezing organs usually destroys them, for the same reason ice destroys cells.

Scientists study the wood frog's sugar trick to learn how organs might one day be stored longer. And the strangest part? Some tiny animals can survive freezing, drying out and even a trip into outer space, as the story of the toughest animal shows.

Myth or fact?

Myth: The wood frog survives because it doesn't really freeze.

Truth: It really does freeze. Ice forms under its skin, around its organs and in its belly. Its heart stops and it stops breathing. What it avoids is ice inside its cells, which would tear them apart.

Try this

Fill two small cups with water. Stir three spoons of sugar into one, and leave the other plain. Put both in the freezer and check every 30 minutes with an adult. The sugary water freezes later and goes slushy, a bit like the frog's antifreeze.
