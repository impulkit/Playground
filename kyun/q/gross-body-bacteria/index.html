How many bacteria live on your body?

You carry about 38 trillion bacteria, roughly one for every one of your own cells, and most of them are helping you right now.

The short answer

Your body is home to about 38 trillion bacteria, according to a careful 2016 estimate. That's roughly as many as all your own cells put together, which is about 30 trillion. Lined up, the number is so big that if you counted one bacterium every second, you'd need more than a million years.

Nearly all of them are harmless, and many are useful. They help digest food, make vitamins, train your immune system and crowd out harmful germs. Together, the microbes living with you are called your microbiome. All your bacteria together weigh only about 0.2 kg, about as much as a small mango.

Surprising but true

When scientists swabbed the belly buttons of 60 people in 2012, they found more than 2,300 kinds of bacteria, some possibly new to science.

Most of them live in your gut

The busiest home is your large intestine, the last part of your gut. It holds nearly all your bacteria, packed more densely than people on a Mumbai local train at rush hour. They feed on bits of food your body can't digest, like fibre from vegetables and grains.

In return, they make useful things. Some produce vitamin K, which helps blood clot. Others make substances that feed the cells lining your gut. Scientists are now studying whether gut bacteria affect mood, sleep and even how children grow.

A jungle on your skin

Your skin is a whole world of different neighbourhoods. Oily places like your forehead host different bacteria from dry ones like your forearm, and damp ones like your armpits. Body odour mostly comes from bacteria breaking down sweat.

In 2012, scientists at North Carolina State University swabbed the belly buttons of 60 volunteers. They found more than 2,300 kinds of bacteria, and some were possibly new to science. A few kinds appeared in most people, but every belly button had its own mix. Your belly button may be one of the least-washed places on your body.

Your mouth's crowd

Your mouth hosts hundreds of kinds of bacteria. Some cause trouble: they feed on sugar and make acid that eats tooth enamel, causing cavities. That's why brushing matters. But many mouth bacteria are harmless or helpful, keeping troublemakers in check.

Babies pick up their first bacteria during birth and from the people who hold and feed them. By about age three, a child's gut bacteria look much like an adult's. Pets, siblings, food and even playing in the mud all add new members to the community.

Where the 10-to-1 myth came from

For decades, textbooks said bacteria outnumbered our cells ten to one. That figure came from a rough calculation in 1972. In 2016, Ron Milo and colleagues at the Weizmann Institute in Israel did a careful recount. The ratio was closer to one to one. Interestingly, each trip to the toilet can lower your bacteria count noticeably, so the balance shifts through the day.

Friends worth keeping

Antibiotics kill harmful bacteria and save lives, but they can also kill helpful ones. That's why doctors prescribe them only when needed. After a course of antibiotics, the gut community usually recovers, though it can take weeks.

People have eaten foods full of friendly bacteria for thousands of years. Indian curd, idli batter and kanji all rely on them. Scientists are still learning which bacteria help most, and whether eating such foods changes the community inside you. The first person to actually see bacteria, in 1676, was a Dutch cloth merchant peering through tiny homemade microscopes.

Myth: Bacteria in your body outnumber your own cells ten to one.

Truth: This old figure came from a rough 1970s guess. A careful 2016 study found the numbers are about equal, roughly 38 trillion bacteria and 30 trillion human cells.

Try this

Make curd at home with an adult. Warm a cup of milk until it's comfortably warm to the touch, stir in a spoonful of curd and leave it covered in a warm spot overnight. Billions of friendly bacteria will turn the milk thick and tangy, the same kinds of microbes that live happily in your gut.
