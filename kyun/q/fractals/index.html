What are fractals?

Measure the coast of Britain with a shorter ruler and it gets longer. Keep shrinking the ruler, and the coastline keeps growing.

The short answer

A fractal is a shape that looks similar however closely you zoom in. Each small piece is a rough copy of the whole. Mathematicians call this self-similarity.

Look at a fern. Each leaf is made of smaller leaflets shaped like the whole leaf, and each leaflet has even smaller bumps shaped the same way. A head of Romanesco broccoli is made of cones built of smaller cones built of smaller cones. Trees, rivers, clouds, coastlines and your own lungs all have this kind of repeating, branching structure.

Ordinary geometry is about smooth lines and circles. Fractal geometry is about the rough, broken, wiggly shapes that nature is full of.

Surprising but true

The Koch snowflake, a fractal shape, has a border that is infinitely long, yet it fits inside a circle you can draw on a page.

The stretching coastline

In 1967, a Polish-born mathematician named Benoit Mandelbrot asked a simple question in the journal Science: how long is the coast of Britain?

The answer depends on your ruler. Measure with steps of 100 km and you skip over every small bay. Use 10 km steps and you follow more of the bends, so the total grows. With a 1-metre stick, you'd follow every rock. The smaller the ruler, the longer the coast.

This is called the coastline paradox. It happens because a coastline is wiggly at every scale. Mandelbrot later coined the word "fractal" in 1975, from the Latin fractus, meaning broken.

Infinite edges

Long before Mandelbrot, mathematicians had drawn strange shapes that puzzled them. In 1904, the Swedish mathematician Helge von Koch described what is now called the Koch snowflake.

Start with a triangle. On the middle third of each side, add a smaller triangle pointing out. Then do the same on every new side, again and again forever. Each round makes the border a third longer. After infinitely many rounds, the border is infinitely long. Yet the whole shape never grows beyond a circle drawn around the first triangle.

The Polish mathematician Wacław Sierpiński described his famous triangle full of holes in 1915. These shapes seemed like odd curiosities until computers showed how much they look like nature.

Fractals in nature

Branching is the most common fractal in living things. A tree trunk splits into branches, then twigs. Your lungs split into airways, then smaller airways, then tiny air sacs, about 20 times over. This packs a huge surface for breathing into your chest.

Lightning forks as it finds the easiest path through the air. Rivers gather streams that gather smaller streams. Seen from an aeroplane, a river system can look like the veins in a leaf.

Fractals in films and phones

In 1980, an engineer named Loren Carpenter showed a short computer animation of mountains built from fractal rules. Two years later, a similar method created a whole planet for the film Star Trek II: The Wrath of Khan. Fractals are still used for special effects like landscapes, clouds, fire and forests, because a few simple rules can create endless natural-looking detail.

Some mobile phone aerials use fractal shapes too. A fractal can pack a long wire into a small space, which helps it pick up signals of many different wavelengths.

Fractals in art and buildings

People have made fractal-like patterns for a long time. Many Hindu temples in India, such as the Kandariya Mahadeva temple at Khajuraho, have a tall central spire surrounded by smaller copies of itself, which are surrounded by smaller ones again. Scholars have compared this design to a fractal.

Traditional patterns in many cultures repeat a shape inside itself in the same way. When you look at temple towers, a fern or a coastline, you are looking at the same idea: small parts that echo the whole.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Fractals are just patterns made by computers.

Truth: Computers made fractals easy to draw, but fractal shapes were in nature long before. Ferns, river networks, lungs and coastlines all have them. Mathematicians were also studying fractal shapes on paper more than a hundred years ago.

Try this

Draw a large triangle. Mark the middle of each side and join those points to make four smaller triangles. Colour the middle one. Now do the same in each of the three corner triangles, then again in theirs. After a few rounds you've drawn the Sierpinski triangle, a famous fractal.
