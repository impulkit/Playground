How do glasses help you see?

Spectacles were invented in Italy around 1286. Before that, many people simply stopped being able to read up close as they got older.

The short answer

Light bends when it passes from air into glass or plastic and out again. This bending is called refraction. A lens is a piece of glass or plastic shaped so that it bends light in a planned way.

Your eye already has a lens of its own, just behind the coloured iris. Together with the clear front of the eye, it focuses light into a sharp picture on the retina, the light-sensitive layer at the back. If your eyeball is a little too long or too short, or the lens can't change shape enough, the picture lands slightly in the wrong place and looks blurry. Glasses add one more lens in front of your eye, nudging the light so the picture lands exactly on the retina again.

Surprising but true

Short sight is spreading fast among children worldwide. Studies suggest that spending more time outdoors in daylight helps protect children's eyes.

Short sight and long sight

If you can read a book easily but the board at school is fuzzy, you are short-sighted. Light from far away comes into focus just in front of the retina. Glasses for short sight are thinner in the middle than at the edges. These concave lenses spread light out a little.

If faraway things are clear but close-up reading is hard, you may be long-sighted. Then the focus falls behind the retina. Convex lenses, thicker in the middle, gather the light in sooner. A magnifying glass is a strong convex lens.

As people reach their forties, the lens inside the eye stiffens and can't change shape as easily. That's why many grandparents hold a phone at arm's length, or wear reading glasses.

The friar who mentioned it

Nobody knows exactly who made the first spectacles, but we know roughly when. In 1306, a friar named Giordano da Pisa told a crowd in Florence that the art of making eyeglasses had been invented less than twenty years earlier. Early spectacles were two convex lenses in frames of bone, metal or leather, joined at a rivet and perched on the nose. They helped older people read.

Before that, some readers used polished "reading stones" laid on top of a page to magnify the words. Lenses for short sight came later, in the 1400s. Arms that hook over the ears only became common in the 1700s.

How did microscopes reveal a hidden world?In the 1670s, a Dutch cloth seller peered through a lens smaller than a pea and saw tiny creatures swimming in a drop of pond water. Nobody had ever seen them before.

Lenses that changed science

Put two lenses together in the right way, and you can see things nobody had seen before. Around 1610, Galileo turned a telescope towards the night sky and spotted moons circling Jupiter. In the 1670s, Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, a Dutch cloth merchant, built tiny single-lens microscopes and saw bacteria swimming in drops of water.

Cameras, phone screens, projectors and car headlights all use lenses. So do your eyes, every second they are open.

A world growing short-sighted

Short sight has become much more common, especially in East Asia, where in some cities most young adults are short-sighted. It is rising in India and the USA too. One major study estimated that about half the world's people could be short-sighted by 2050.

Scientists think the change is partly about how children spend their days. Time outdoors in bright daylight seems to help protect against short sight, while long hours of close-up work, including screens, may add to the risk. If the board looks blurry, tell a parent or teacher. A quick eye test, which is painless, can sort it out, and the glasses that follow can make a school day much easier.

Myth or fact?

Myth: Wearing glasses makes your eyes weaker, so you'll need them more and more.

Truth: Glasses don't weaken eyes. Short sight in children often gets stronger as they grow, with or without glasses, which is why the prescription changes. Not wearing glasses you need just makes things blurry and can give you headaches.

Try this

Make a water-drop magnifier. Lay a piece of clear plastic, such as a sandwich bag or a plastic sheet, over a newspaper. Use a straw or your fingertip to place one small drop of water on it. Look at the letters through the drop. The curved drop bends light like a lens and makes them bigger.
