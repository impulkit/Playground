How do you read a food label?

The first ingredient on a packet is the one there is most of. Sometimes that turns out to be sugar.

The short answer

A food label is a small report about what is inside a packet. To read it well, look at three things. First, the ingredients list, which shows everything in the food, heaviest first. Second, the nutrition table, which shows how much energy, fat, sugar, salt and protein the food holds. Third, the small print: allergy warnings, the date and the vegetarian or non-vegetarian mark.

Indian labels usually give nutrition for every 100 g or 100 ml, and for one serving. American labels, called Nutrition Facts, give it per serving and add a "% Daily Value" to show how much of a day's need that serving covers. Once you know where to look, a packet can tell you a lot in under a minute.

Surprising but true

Sugar can hide on an ingredients list under dozens of names, such as glucose syrup, dextrose, maltodextrin, sucrose and invert syrup.

Heaviest first

Ingredients must be listed in order of weight at the time they went into the recipe. If sugar is the first or second ingredient in a breakfast cereal, that cereal is mostly sugar.

Companies sometimes split sugar into several different forms, such as sugar, glucose syrup and honey. Each appears lower down the list, even though together they might add up to the biggest ingredient. Reading the whole list, right to the end, gives the true picture.

The nutrition table

The nutrition table lists energy in kilocalories (kcal), along with protein, carbohydrate, sugar, fat and sodium, which comes mostly from salt. American labels, updated in 2016, also show "added sugars" on their own line, separate from the sugar found naturally in milk or fruit.

Comparing two foods is easiest using the per-100 g column, because servings can be different sizes. A packet with 30 g of sugar per 100 g is almost one-third sugar. Understanding percentages is half the skill here. The other half is noticing how small some "servings" are, and whether the packet holds three of them.

Green dot, brown triangle

India has a labelling rule that surprises many visitors. Every packet of food must carry a symbol. A green filled circle inside a green square means the food is vegetarian. A brown filled triangle inside a brown square means it contains meat, fish or egg. The non-veg mark used to be a brown circle. It was changed to a triangle, so the two marks now differ in shape as well as colour.

Families who are vegetarian, Jain or follow other food rules rely on these marks every day. Labels must also carry an FSSAI licence number, which shows the food maker is registered with India's food safety authority.

Warnings and dates

Allergy warnings matter a great deal for some people. Labels must name common allergens, such as milk, nuts, wheat, soy or egg. Many add "may contain traces of nuts" if the food was made in a factory that also handles nuts. For someone with a serious food allergy, that line can matter more than anything else on the packet.

"Best before" means the food tastes and looks its best until that date, but may still be safe after it. "Use by" or "expiry" is about safety, and food should not be eaten after it.

Why labels exist

In 1906, the American writer Upton Sinclair published The Jungle, a novel that described filthy conditions in Chicago's meat factories. Readers were horrified. The same year, the United States passed its first major food safety law. India's food laws were brought together under one act in 2006, which set up the FSSAI. Labels are one result of these rules: the right to know what you are eating.

Myth or fact?

Myth: A drink labelled "no added sugar" has no sugar in it.

Truth: The words "no added sugar" only mean none was added during making. Fruit juices and some milk drinks can still contain plenty of natural sugar, which you can see in the nutrition table.

Try this

Pick two packets of biscuits, chips or breakfast cereal at home. Find the sugar per 100 g on each and write the numbers side by side. Then measure that much sugar for each onto two plates with a teaspoon (one level teaspoon is about 4 g) and compare the piles.
