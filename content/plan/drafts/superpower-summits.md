## The short answer

A summit is a meeting where the top leaders of countries talk face to face. When the leaders of the USA, China and Russia meet, it matters because these three countries have more weapons, money and voting power than almost anyone else.

Together they hold most of the world's nuclear weapons. All three can block decisions at the [United Nations](united-nations). Their trade, or their trade fights, changes the prices of things families buy. Even a meeting that agrees nothing can show the world whether tensions are rising or cooling.

## How it works

Think of three big families who share one housing society. Their fights decide whether the gate stays open, the water tank gets fixed or the society meeting turns ugly. Everyone else in the building listens closely when they sit down to talk.

A summit usually works like this:

1. **Months of preparation.** Officials and diplomats, called "sherpas" after the mountain guides, agree most of the wording before the leaders arrive.
2. **The meeting.** Leaders talk, often with interpreters. Some meetings last only an hour.
3. **The statement.** Afterwards, the leaders may release a joint statement. Sometimes each side tells its own version instead.
4. **Back home.** Big promises, like a [treaty](?treaty), often need a parliament's approval. In the USA, two-thirds of the Senate must vote yes.

Leaders can also meet "on the sidelines" of a bigger gathering. APEC, short for Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation, is one such gathering. It began in 1989 and has 21 members around the Pacific Ocean, including the USA, China, Russia, Japan and Australia. It calls its members "economies", not countries, because its work is about trade. Its members include Hong Kong and Taiwan, which APEC calls "Chinese Taipei". APEC talks about trade, but it cannot force anyone to do anything. Its decisions are voluntary.

## Where it came from

For most of history, rulers rarely met. Travel was slow, and meetings could be dangerous. Diplomats carried messages instead. Air travel in the 1900s changed that.

**Yalta, 1945.** In February 1945, near the end of the [Second World War](world-war-two), the leaders of the USA, Britain and the Soviet Union met at Yalta, a resort town in Crimea. They agreed to split Germany into zones after the war, and they agreed on the veto at the new United Nations. Later, many people blamed Yalta for dividing Europe, and the word "Yalta" came to stand for big powers deciding the fate of smaller ones.

**Nixon in China, 1972.** For more than 20 years, the USA and Communist China had no friendly contact. A surprise friendship between table tennis players opened a door, a story called [ping-pong diplomacy](table-tennis). In February 1972, Richard Nixon became the first serving US president to visit China. He spent a week there and met Chairman Mao Zedong. The two countries set up full diplomatic ties in 1979.

**Reagan and Gorbachev, 1985 to 1987.** During the [Cold War](cold-war), US President Ronald Reagan and Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev met in Geneva in 1985 and Reykjavik in 1986. In December 1987, they signed the INF Treaty, which scrapped a whole class of nuclear missiles. Two years later, the [Berlin Wall](berlin-wall) fell.

## India's story

India's leaders have often refused to pick one big power. In the 1950s and 1960s, [Jawaharlal Nehru](nehru) helped lead the Non-Aligned Movement, which joined neither the American camp nor the Soviet one.

India's ties with each power have swung over the years. In 1971, the USA leaned towards Pakistan, and India signed a friendship treaty with the Soviet Union. In 1986, Gorbachev visited New Delhi. After a war with China in 1962, Rajiv Gandhi's visit to Beijing in 1988 was the first by an Indian prime minister in 34 years. In 2008, India and the USA signed a civil nuclear deal, a decade after India's 1998 [nuclear tests](pokhran) had led the USA to put [sanctions](?sanction) on India.

Today, India sits in groups with each side. It is part of BRICS with Russia and China, and the Quad with the USA, Japan and Australia. It is also a member of the [G20](g20), where all three powers meet. India has asked to join APEC, but APEC has not added any new member since 1998.

## The numbers that matter

- **21**: APEC's members. Together, they make about 6 of every 10 dollars of everything the world produces.
- **Nearly 9 in 10**: the share of the world's nuclear warheads held by the USA and Russia together, by expert estimates.
- **3 of 5**: the USA, China and Russia are three of the five permanent members of the UN Security Council. Any one of them can [veto](?veto), or block, a decision.
- **About 2,700**: missiles destroyed under the 1987 INF Treaty, the clearest example of a summit leading to real change.
- **About a week**: the length of Nixon's 1972 visit to China. It changed relations between the two countries for decades.

## What people argue about

**Helpful or dangerous for others?** Some say meetings between big powers lower the risk of war, because rivals who talk are less likely to misread each other. Others fear a "new Yalta", where big powers quietly share out the world and smaller countries have no say.

**Should leaders talk to rivals?** One side argues you must talk to the people you disagree with most. The other argues that a summit can reward a leader whose actions others object to, without getting anything back.

**Picking sides.** In India, some people think staying friendly with every big power gives India more freedom. Others worry that if the big three draw closer, or fall out badly, India could be squeezed in the middle. Both views come from the same goal: protecting India's interests.

## Myths and mix-ups

**"Summits are just photo opportunities."** Many end with friendly words and little else. But some, like the meetings that led to the INF Treaty, changed history. Even quiet meetings can build trust that helps later.

**"A handshake means the countries are now friends."** Leaders can smile for cameras and still disagree on almost everything. Rivals often meet precisely because they disagree.

**"APEC and the G20 can make laws."** Neither can. They are clubs where leaders talk and promise. Only countries' own governments and parliaments can turn promises into laws.

## What it means for you

What three faraway leaders say can reach your kitchen. Trade fights between big powers bring [tariffs](tariffs), taxes on imports, which can raise the price of phones, toys and machines. Tension around oil routes like the [Strait of Hormuz](strait-of-hormuz) can push up petrol prices, because [India buys most of its oil](india-oil) from abroad. And talks on cutting nuclear weapons touch everyone's safety.

Summits also show a useful life skill: rivals can still talk. The same ideas, knowing what you want, what you can give up and what the other side needs, work in a class debate or a family argument. [Why countries trade](trade-world) at all, and how [game theory](game-theory) explains bargaining, are good places to explore next.
