## The short answer

School looks different around the world, but its main aims are much the same: to teach children to read, write and count, to understand the world, and to get along with others. What changes is when school starts, how long the day lasts, what children wear and eat, how they learn and how they are tested.

In some countries, children begin formal lessons at five. In Finland they wait until seven. Some children walk to school, some ride buses, some take boats, and a few go to school over the internet from homes hundreds of kilometres from the nearest classroom.

## Cleaning and lunch in Japan

In Japan, the school year starts in April, when cherry trees blossom. Students take turns serving lunch to their classmates, often wearing white coats and caps. After lunch or at the end of the day, everyone cleans: sweeping, mopping, wiping windows and scrubbing sinks. Teachers say it teaches responsibility and respect for shared spaces.

## Late starts in Finland

Finnish children usually start formal school at seven, after a year of preschool. School days are fairly short, there are breaks between lessons for outdoor play, and students typically get less homework than in many countries. Teachers need a master's degree and are given a lot of freedom. Finland became famous after its students scored very well in international tests in the early 2000s.

## School over the air

In the Australian outback, some families live on cattle stations far from any town. Since 1951, their children have attended [School of the Air](australia), starting in Alice Springs. Students first used two-way [radios](radio-waves) to talk to teachers and classmates hundreds of kilometres away. Today they use the internet and [video calls](tech-video-calls), and they meet in person a few times a year.

## Boats as classrooms

In parts of [Bangladesh](bangladesh), monsoon [floods](floods-why) cut villages off for months. Since 2002, an organisation called Shidhulai Swanirvar Sangstha has run boat schools. Solar-powered boats pick children up from their villages, hold lessons on board, and drop them home again.

## India's schools

India has one of the largest school systems in the world, with crores of students in government and private schools. The school year usually begins in April or June, depending on the state. Many students wear uniforms, and board exams at the end of Classes 10 and 12 are big events in family life.

Under the PM POSHAN scheme, often still called the [mid-day meal](mid-day-meal), about 11 crore children in government schools (2025 figure) get a free cooked lunch each school day. Studies have found it helps attendance, especially among girls.

## Many languages, many timetables

[Children around the world](life-kids-around-world) learn in many languages. In India, many students learn in their mother tongue, Hindi and English, often all three. In the United States, most public schools do not require uniforms, and the school year usually starts in August or September. In France, [school lunches](lunch-world) are often several courses, with a starter, a main dish, cheese and a dessert.

Exams differ too. In China and [South Korea](seoul), a single university entrance exam can shape a student's future, and many go to extra classes in the evenings. Each system has supporters and critics. Comparing them helps educators borrow good ideas from each other, as [the history of school](school-history) shows they always have.
