## The short answer

Climate change is mostly caused by burning coal, oil and gas for electricity, transport and factories. That releases carbon dioxide and other gases that trap heat around the Earth. Fixing it needs big changes, like clean power stations and better buses and trains, and those are decided by governments and companies.

Kids can still help, in three main ways. They can waste less energy and food at home. They can learn how the problem works and explain it to others. And they can speak up, at school, in their neighbourhood and, one day, at the voting booth. The last one may matter most.

## Start with what you can see

Some of the biggest everyday sources of [greenhouse gases](?greenhouse-gas) are hidden inside ordinary things. An air conditioner set to 18 °C uses much more power than one set to 24 °C. In India, most electricity still comes from coal, so every unit saved means less coal burned.

Food matters too. About a fifth of the food that reaches shops, restaurants and homes worldwide is wasted, according to the UN's 2024 report. Much of it rots in dumps and gives off methane, which is why [food waste](food-waste) is a climate problem as well as a hunger one. Taking only what you'll eat, and finishing leftovers, is a small habit with a real effect. Travel adds up as well. Walking, cycling, taking the school bus or sharing a ride all cut fuel use.

## Small things that matter less than you'd think

Some actions get lots of attention but make only a tiny difference to warming. Unplugging a phone charger saves very little. Swapping a plastic straw for a paper one is good for sea turtles, but it barely changes the climate.

That doesn't make them pointless. Plastic pollution is a serious problem in its own right. But if you want to cut warming, energy and food are the big levers. Knowing the difference helps you spend effort where it counts.

## Using your voice

Many of the decisions that shape climate change are made in meetings far away, but those meetings respond to people. In 2018, a Swedish schoolgirl began sitting outside her country's parliament on Fridays. Within a year, millions of students in many countries had joined school strikes for climate, a story told in [young people changing the world](greta-and-youth).

You don't need a protest sign to speak up. Students have asked their schools to plant trees, set up composting or put solar panels on the roof. Some have written letters to local leaders about safe cycle lanes. Long before any of this, villagers in the Himalayas protected their forests in the [Chipko movement](chipko), showing that ordinary people can change official plans.

## Learning to tell good information from bad

Climate change attracts a lot of false claims online, both ones saying it isn't happening and ones saying it's already too late. Neither is true. Scientists, through groups like the IPCC, agree that warming is real, caused mainly by people, and that every bit of warming avoided reduces harm.

Learning to check sources is a climate skill. Ask who made a claim, what their evidence is, and whether scientists agree.

## Hope with numbers

There are real signs of progress. [Solar power](renewable-energy) has become much cheaper since 2010, and India has built huge solar parks in Rajasthan and Gujarat. India and many other countries have set targets for net zero, when a country's emissions are balanced by what is removed from the air. India aims for 2070. Countries' plans can change, and they need people watching to make sure they are kept. Today's 10-year-olds will be about 54 in 2070, and they will be the adults in charge of reaching that goal.
