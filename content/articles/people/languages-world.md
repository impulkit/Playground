## The short answer

People speak about 7,000 languages today. Ethnologue, a catalogue run by linguists (people who study languages), lists a little over 7,000 living ones.

That number hides a big imbalance. About two dozen languages, such as [Mandarin Chinese](chinese-characters), Spanish, English and [Hindi](hindi-language), are spoken by more than half the people on Earth. Thousands of others are spoken by a single village or a few hundred people.

The count is never exact. Linguists argue over where one language stops and a [dialect](?dialect) begins, and a few languages in remote forests have barely been recorded.

## Why the number is slippery

Hindi and [Urdu](arabic-script) sound almost the same in daily speech, yet they use different scripts and are counted as two languages. Chinese "dialects" such as Mandarin and Cantonese can be harder for each other's speakers to follow than Hindi and Urdu.

So the line between a language and a dialect is partly about history, politics and pride. A famous joke among linguists, made popular by the scholar Max Weinreich, says a language is a dialect with an army and a navy.

## The giants

Mandarin Chinese has the most native speakers, people who learned it as their first language at home. Spanish comes second. English has fewer native speakers than either, but if you add everyone who learned it at school or at work, it has the most speakers in total, well over a billion.

Hindi is among the top few too, especially when you count the many people in India who speak it as a second or third language. [Bengali](bengali-language), spoken in West Bengal and Bangladesh, also has more native speakers than Russian or Japanese.

A language often spreads because of trade, empire, films or the internet, not because it is easier to learn. English spread with British ships and later with American films, songs and computers.

## Where languages crowd together

Languages are not spread evenly. Papua New Guinea has more than 800, packed into steep valleys where villages stayed apart for thousands of years. Nigeria and Indonesia each have several hundred.

India is a giant of languages too. The 2011 [Census](census) grouped the country's mother tongues into 121 languages, and 22 of them are listed in the Constitution. In the United States, the Census Bureau has counted more than 350 languages spoken at home, from Spanish and Tagalog to [Navajo](navajo-nation) and Gujarati.

## Languages that are fading

Roughly 4 in 10 of the world's languages are [endangered](?endangered), meaning children are no longer learning them. When grandparents are the last fluent speakers, a language can vanish within a lifetime. With it go songs, jokes, names for plants and ways of seeing the world.

India has its own fading voices. Great Andamanese, spoken in the [Andaman Islands](andaman), now has only a handful of speakers left. In North America, many Native American languages are in the same position, though tribes are running schools and apps to bring them back.

## Languages without sound

Not every language is spoken aloud. Deaf communities around the world use well over a hundred [sign languages](sign-language), each with its own grammar. American Sign Language and Indian Sign Language are separate languages, and a signer of one cannot simply understand the other.

## Always changing

Languages borrow from one another all the time. English took "shampoo", "pyjamas" and "jungle" from Indian languages, and Hindi took "school" and "station" from English. Some languages have even been used as secret codes, as the Navajo [code talkers](code-talkers) showed in the [Second World War](world-war-two). A language that stops changing is usually one that nobody speaks any more.
