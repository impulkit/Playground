## The short answer

A passport is an official document that proves two things: who you are, and which country you are a citizen of. You show it when you leave your own country and when you arrive in another.

It also carries a polite request. Many passports contain a message from the government asking other countries to let the holder pass safely and to help them if needed. In return, your own country promises to take you back. A passport does not guarantee entry anywhere, though. The country you visit decides whether to let you in, often with a separate permission called a visa.

## Very old letters

Travellers have carried letters of safe passage for thousands of years. A story in the Hebrew Bible tells of a Persian king, around 450 BCE, giving his servant Nehemiah letters asking governors to let him travel to Judah. In medieval Europe, kings issued "safe conduct" papers. The word passport probably comes from French words meaning "to pass through a port" or gate.

For a long time, many people crossed borders with no papers at all. That changed during the [First World War](world-war-one), when countries worried about spies and began checking everyone.

## A booklet for the world

In 1920, the League of Nations, a forerunner of the United Nations, held a conference in Paris on passports. It agreed on a standard booklet with a photo, which is roughly what most countries still use.

Today an agency of the UN, the International Civil Aviation Organization, sets the rules so that a passport from India can be read at an [airport](airports) in Brazil or Japan. That is why the bottom of the photo page has two lines of letters, numbers and arrow-like `<<<` marks. Machines can scan them in a second.

## Chips and faces

Many countries now issue e-passports with a tiny chip and an antenna inside the cover. The chip holds your details and a digital photo, signed with a code that makes forgery very hard. At many [airports](airport-security), automatic gates read the chip and compare the photo with your face using a camera. India issues passports through Passport Seva Kendras, and it has begun issuing chip passports too. Similar ideas about proving identity with data are behind India's [Aadhaar](aadhaar) number.

## Colours and kinds

To get an Indian passport, you apply online, then visit a Passport Seva Kendra with documents proving your identity, address and date of birth. Your photo and [fingerprints](fingerprints) are taken, and police usually check your address before the passport is posted to you. Children need one too, even babies.

Most countries use a few colours for passport covers: dark blue, red, green or black. India's ordinary passport is dark blue. Government officials travelling for work get a white one, and diplomats get a maroon one. The US passport has been blue since 1976.

## Papers for people without a country

After the First World War and the Russian Revolution, hundreds of thousands of people found themselves with no country willing to give them papers. The Norwegian explorer Fridtjof Nansen, working for the League of Nations, created an identity document for these stateless [refugees](refugees) in 1922, which became known as the Nansen passport. It let them cross borders and look for work. Nansen won the [Nobel Peace Prize](nobel-peace) that year.

Today, refugees can receive travel documents under a UN agreement of 1951. For millions of people, the right piece of paper can make the difference between being stuck at a border and starting a new life on the other side.
