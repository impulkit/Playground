## The short answer

There is no perfect size for a parliament. Too small, and each member has to speak for so many people that ordinary voters feel far away from them. Too big, and the house gets expensive, slow and noisy, and each member has less time to speak.

Most countries settle somewhere in between. India's [Lok Sabha](lok-sabha-rajya-sabha) is large, but India has so many people that each of its 543 MPs still speaks for more people than in almost any other country. That is why the question keeps coming back.

## How it works

Every MP represents one area, called a [constituency](?constituency). Divide a country's people by its number of seats, and you get how many people one MP speaks for.

1. **Fewer seats** means bigger constituencies. One MP must listen to, visit and answer far more people.
2. **More seats** means smaller constituencies. MPs can know their area better, and more kinds of people can win seats.
3. **But every extra seat** adds salaries, offices and staff, and shares out the same hours of debate among more people.

Think of a school with one class monitor for every 200 children. The monitor could never learn everyone's name. With one for every 20, they could. But a meeting of a hundred monitors would take all day, and most would never get a turn to speak.

## Where it came from

Ancient Athens had a council of 500 citizens, chosen by lottery, to prepare business for its assembly, as the story of [Athenian democracy](democracy-athens) explains. Modern parliaments have often grown as their countries grew.

The US House of Representatives began with 65 members in 1789 and added seats after most of its ten-yearly [census](census) counts. A 1911 law raised it to 435 seats from 1913, once two new states had joined. A 1929 law kept that number. Apart from a short time with 437 seats after Alaska and Hawaii joined in 1959, it has stayed there ever since. So each American representative now speaks for far more people than in 1913.

Germany went the other way. Its voting rules kept adding extra seats until its Bundestag passed 730 members. A reform made 630 the limit from the 2025 election.

India's first Lok Sabha, elected in 1951–52, had 489 elected seats. After each census, it was enlarged. It had 542 seats at the 1977 election, and 543 at every election since 1989.

## India's story

In 1976, the 42nd [amendment](?amendment) froze each state's number of seats, using the 1971 census. The idea was that states working to slow population growth should not lose seats for succeeding. In 2001, the freeze was stretched until after the first census taken after 2026. The full story of how seats are shared out is told in the entry on [delimitation](delimitation).

So while India's population more than doubled, the number of seats stayed the same. Article 81 of the [Constitution](constitution) sets the most members the Lok Sabha may have, so making it much bigger needs a change to the Constitution itself.

When [Parliament](parliament-india) moved into its new building in Delhi in 2023, the Lok Sabha chamber was built with 888 seats, far more than the house needed then. Its planners left room for the house to grow.

## The numbers that matter

- **About 27 lakh**: people for each of the Lok Sabha's 543 seats, using the UN's 2025 estimate of about 146 crore Indians. That is more than the whole population of some countries.
- **About 1 lakh**: people per MP in the UK, where 650 MPs represent about 7 crore people, as the entry on [the UK's four countries](uk-countries) describes.
- **761,169**: people per US representative after the 2020 census, more than three times the figure of about 2 lakh after the 1910 census.
- **2,977**: delegates in China's National People's Congress in 2023, one of the largest lawmaking bodies in the world. It meets in full for only about ten days a year, which shows that size alone does not decide how much power a parliament has. [China today](china-today) explains how its government works.
- **About 1,100**: the size the [cube root](?cube-root) rule suggests for a country of India's size. Political scientist Rein Taagepera noticed in 1972 that many lower houses are close to the cube root of their country's population. Britain is well above it. Some scientists argue a different formula fits better.

## What people argue about

**Bigger or not?** Supporters of a larger house say Indian MPs are stretched far too thin, and that more seats would bring MPs closer to voters. They add that the women's reservation law of 2023 will keep a third of seats for women once it starts. That is easier to bring in if the house grows, because fewer sitting MPs would lose their seats. Critics reply that a bigger house costs more, and that debates are already short. A day with 6 hours of debate gives 543 members less than a minute each.

Some also worry that when there are many more MPs, each one has less say, and party leaders gain more control. In India, a rule from 2003 lets ministers number up to 15% of Lok Sabha seats, so a bigger house also allows a bigger government.

**Who gets the new seats?** If seats followed today's population, fast-growing northern states such as Uttar Pradesh and Bihar would gain the most. Many southern states, such as Tamil Nadu and Kerala, say they would lose influence for having slowed their growth. One middle path is to raise every state's seats by the same [percentage](percentages). That keeps each state's share the same. But it also keeps votes unequal in weight: an MP in a crowded state would still speak for many more people than one in a less crowded state.

The argument is really about two kinds of fairness in a [federal](?federal) country. One says every voter should count equally. The other says every state should keep its voice.

## Myths and mix-ups

**"Every MP speaks for the same number of people."** Not in India. Because seats are frozen and some cities grew fast, the biggest constituencies have more than 30 lakh voters, while Lakshadweep has well under 1 lakh.

**"More seats always give a state more power."** Power in a vote depends on a state's share of the house. If every state grows by the same share, the balance stays where it was.

**"Parliament can add seats with an ordinary vote."** Changing the Lok Sabha's size needs a [special majority](?special-majority) under Article 368. In each House, two-thirds of members present and voting must agree, and they must also be more than half of all members. If a change touches "the representation of States in Parliament", Article 368 says at least half of the state assemblies must also approve it. The entry on [changing constitutions](constitution-amendments) explains more.

## What it means for you

The size of Parliament decides how easy it is to reach your MP. With a smaller constituency, your MP is more likely to visit your town, hear about your school's broken road or answer your family's letter.

It also decides how your state's voice compares with others when laws and budgets are voted on. Next to the [population](population) of India, a house of a few hundred members is small. Whether it should grow, and how, is one of the biggest choices a democracy like India's can make about itself. Every [election](elections-india) you vote in one day will be shaped by it.
