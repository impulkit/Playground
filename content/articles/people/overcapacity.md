## The short answer

Yes. A country can build so many factories that they can make far more than people want to buy. Economists call this overcapacity, or excess [capacity](?capacity).

The extra goods have to go somewhere. Often they are sold abroad cheaply, which pushes down prices around the world. Shoppers may enjoy the low prices, but factories in other countries can struggle or close. That is why governments argue so fiercely about it.

## How it works

Think of a mango market in May. In a good season, so many trucks arrive that there are more mangoes than buyers. Sellers drop their prices to clear their crates before the fruit spoils. Buyers are happy, but some farmers lose money. This tug between [supply and demand](supply-demand) sets the price of almost everything.

{{story:supply-demand}}

Factories work in a similar way, but a steel plant or a solar panel factory cannot be switched off as easily as a fruit stall. Here is how a glut builds up:

1. **Cheap help.** A government gives companies a [subsidy](?subsidy), cheap land, low-cost electricity or bank loans at easy rates. Building a factory becomes cheaper than it should be.
2. **Everyone builds.** Many companies rush in, often encouraged by plans to create jobs or lead a new industry.
3. **Demand slows.** A building boom ends, or buyers simply cannot use that much.
4. **The extra spills out.** Factories keep running to pay their loans and keep workers, so they [export](?export) the extra at low prices.
5. **Prices fall worldwide.** Makers in other countries cannot match those prices, and some shut down.

Not every glut needs government help. A boom can tempt companies to overbuild on their own. When the boom ends, there are too many factories for too few buyers.

## How countries push back

Countries have several tools when cheap goods flood in.

- **Anti-dumping duties.** Under World Trade Organization rules, [dumping](?dumping) means selling goods abroad for less than their normal value. A country may charge an extra duty only after an investigation shows dumping, real harm to its own makers, and a link between the two.
- **Safeguard duties.** These are short-term extra taxes when imports suddenly surge, giving local makers time to adjust.
- **Tariffs.** A [tariff](tariffs) is a tax at the border. Some countries add broad tariffs on goods from places they accuse of unfair practices.
- **Trade investigations.** The USA has a 1974 trade law with a part called Section 301. It lets the US government investigate other countries' trade practices and respond with tariffs if it finds them unfair to American businesses. The USA has used it to look into whether other countries' factory plans create too much capacity.

## Where it came from

Gluts are as old as markets, but the modern fight is mostly about [steel](steel). Steel plants are huge and costly, so countries hate to close them. China's steel making grew so fast that by 2019 it made more than half of the world's steel.

US officials said world steel excess capacity more than doubled between 2000 and 2014. In the USA, they blamed it for falling prices, plant closures and more than 15,000 lost jobs. In December 2016, more than 30 countries launched the Global Forum on Steel Excess Capacity in Berlin to talk about the problem. Its members made about 90% of the world's steel.

Then came solar panels. Since 2011, China has invested more than 50 billion US dollars in new [solar panel](solar-panels) factories, ten times more than Europe, and became by far the biggest maker. Panels became much cheaper for everyone. But in 2022, the International Energy Agency found that China's costs were about 35% lower than Europe's, and many factories elsewhere found it hard to compete.

## India's story

India sits on both sides of this story.

First, India often feels the flood. India is one of the world's biggest steel makers, second only to China in 2020. When cheap steel from abroad poured in, Indian steel prices fell to a near four-year low in September 2024. In April 2025, India put a 12% safeguard duty on some flat steel imports, including from China and Vietnam, after Indian steel makers asked for protection from cheaper imports.

India has used anti-dumping rules for a long time. In the second half of 2008, India started 42 of the world's 120 new anti-dumping investigations, more than any other country, according to the WTO.

Second, India wants more factories of its own. In 2020, the government started the Production Linked Incentive (PLI) scheme, which later grew to cover 14 sectors. It rewards companies when they invest and make more in India. Sectors include [solar panels](solar-india), [medicines](medicine-prices), special steel and electronics.

Some trading partners see schemes like these as help that could create too much capacity. India's government disagrees, and says the country does not have excess capacity.

## The numbers that matter

- **More than half**: China's share of all the world's steel. In 2020, it made 57.6% of it.
- **More than 80%**: China's share of every stage of making solar panels, from raw material to finished panel, according to the International Energy Agency.
- **At least double**: how much the world's solar wafer, cell and panel factories could make at the end of 2021, compared with what the world bought.
- **More than 80%**: how much the cost of solar power fell in about ten years, according to the IEA.
- **10% lower**: how much cheaper solar panels cost to make in China than in India, according to the same 2022 IEA study.

## What people argue about

**Unfair help or just good at making things?** One side says some governments pour in subsidies and cheap loans, so their factories can sell below fair prices and knock out rivals. The other side says some countries are simply efficient, with big factories, skilled workers and low costs, and that is how trade is meant to work.

**Cheap panels for the planet, or lost jobs?** Cheap solar panels make it cheaper for countries to switch to clean power and fight [climate change](climate-change). But factory workers in countries that cannot compete may lose their jobs.

**Who decides what is "too much"?** A country that exports a lot is not always overbuilding. Some people say only the market can judge, through prices. Others say governments should watch for gluts before they cause damage.

**Do the fixes help?** Supporters say duties and tariffs give local makers a fair chance. Critics say they make goods dearer for families and for other factories that need cheap steel or parts. They also warn that tariffs can lead to trade fights.

## Myths and mix-ups

**"Making a lot means making too much."** A country can make far more than it uses at home and sell it all abroad. Overcapacity is about the whole world's buyers, not one country's.

**"Any cheap import is dumping."** Dumping has a set meaning: selling below normal value. It has to be proved in an investigation.

**"Only one country overbuilds."** Any country can build too many factories during a boom. Gluts have hit many industries in many places.

## What it means for you

Overcapacity can make some things cheaper for your family, like steel utensils, phones or solar panels on a rooftop. But it can also put jobs at risk in towns that make those things, and lead to new taxes at the border.

When countries argue about overcapacity, they are really arguing about [why countries trade](trade-world) and who gets the factories and jobs. The answers shape prices in your local market, the [trade deficit](trade-deficit) and the future of [India's economy](india-economy).
