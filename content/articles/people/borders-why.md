## The short answer

A border is an agreed line where one country's laws stop and another's begin. On one side, you use one currency, follow one set of rules and carry one passport. Step across, and all of that can change.

Borders are decided by people. Some follow natural features like mountains and rivers. Some are straight lines drawn on [maps](maps-flat). Many were settled by treaties after long talks, and some after wars.

Borders have changed many times in history. Maps from different countries sometimes show the same border differently, because the countries disagree.

## Mountains, rivers and straight lines

Natural borders are the oldest kind. The [Himalaya](himalaya-form) separate India from Tibet for long stretches, and rivers divide many countries. But rivers change course after floods, which can leave land on the "wrong" side.

Straight-line borders were usually drawn by distant governments. Much of the US–[Canada](canada) border follows the 49th parallel, a line of latitude, one of the map [coordinates](coordinates) used to find places. In Africa, European empires drew many straight borders in the late 1800s, often splitting communities who spoke the same language.

## A line drawn in five weeks

In 1947, as British rule ended, the border between [India and Pakistan](pakistan-history) had to be drawn fast. A British lawyer named Cyril Radcliffe, who had never been to India before, was given about five weeks.

He worked with old maps and census figures, deciding which villages went where. The line was published two days after independence. Millions of people found themselves on the side they did not want, and the [Partition](partition) that followed was one of the largest movements of people in history.

## Islands of one country inside another

For centuries, the border between India and [Bangladesh](bangladesh) near Cooch Behar was a tangle. There were 162 enclaves: small pockets of Indian land inside Bangladesh, and Bangladeshi land inside India. One was even an Indian pocket inside a Bangladeshi pocket inside India.

People living in them often could not easily reach schools or hospitals in their own country. In 2015, the two governments agreed to swap the enclaves. Residents could choose which country to belong to. Thousands of people became citizens of a country for the first time.

## Borders on the sea and in the sky

Borders don't stop at the coast. Under a UN agreement called the Law of the Sea, a country controls the waters up to about 22 km (12 nautical miles) from its shore, almost as if they were land. Further out, up to about 370 km, it has special rights to fish and to drill for oil and gas.

This is why tiny islands can matter so much. Owning one can mean owning a huge area of sea around it. India's Andaman and Nicobar Islands and [Lakshadweep](lakshadweep) give it a large share of the Indian Ocean.

Countries also control the air above them. Planes need permission to fly over another country, and [pilots](jobs-pilot) radio ahead as they cross from one country's airspace into the next.

## Strange borders around the world

In the Belgian town of Baarle-Hertog, the border with the Netherlands runs through shops and homes. Some front doors are in one country and the back rooms in the other.

At Attari–Wagah, between Amritsar in India and Lahore in Pakistan, soldiers from both sides perform a flag-lowering ceremony every evening, with high kicks and loud cheers from crowds.

## Settling disputes

Today, most countries try to settle border questions by talking, through treaties or international courts. Some borders, including parts of India's, are still being discussed. Crossing most borders legally means showing a [passport](passports), a reminder that these invisible lines still matter to everyone who travels.
