## The short answer

The police keep people safe and help make sure laws are followed. They patrol streets, respond to emergencies, investigate crimes, manage traffic, find missing people, and help at big crowds like festivals and cricket matches.

Police can arrest people they believe have broken the law, but they cannot punish them. Deciding guilt and punishment is the job of [judges](courts-judges). That split matters: it stops any single group from having too much power over people's lives.

## Who runs the police

In India, [the Constitution](constitution) makes police a state subject. So the Kerala Police, the Maharashtra Police and others are separate forces run by state governments. Senior officers usually come from the Indian Police Service, recruited nationally.

In the United States, policing is even more local. Cities, counties and states run their own forces, and there are thousands of separate police departments. National agencies like the FBI handle certain serious crimes that cross state lines.

## The first modern force

Many cities had watchmen for centuries, but the first modern, uniformed police force is often traced to [London](london). Home Secretary Robert Peel set up the Metropolitan Police in 1829. Londoners nicknamed the officers "bobbies" or "peelers" after him, and the first name is still used.

In India, the British passed the Police Act of 1861 after the [revolt of 1857](revolt-1857). Much of the structure of Indian policing still dates from that law, and many experts say it needs updating.

## Clues and science

Police detectives gather evidence: CCTV footage, phone records, witness statements and [fingerprints](fingerprints). In 1897, the British official Edward Henry set up the world's first [fingerprint bureau](fingerprint-sensors) in Calcutta, now [Kolkata](kolkata). Two Indian officers, Azizul Haque and Hem Chandra Bose, worked out much of the system for sorting prints so they could be found quickly. Versions of it were used around the world for about a century.

For a long time, police forces were almost entirely male. In 1972, Kiran Bedi became the first woman to join the Indian Police Service. Today many Indian states have reserved a share of police posts for women and run all-women police stations, where women can report crimes more comfortably.

## Answering the call

Most police work is not chasing criminals. Officers spend much of their time answering calls, sorting out arguments, helping at accidents and guiding traffic. Before electric signals, police stood at crossings and waved cars through by hand.

Police uniforms in India are usually khaki, a word that means "dust-coloured" in Persian and Hindi. British Indian army units began dyeing their uniforms this colour in the 1840s because it blended into dusty ground, and police forces later took it up. Many American police wear dark blue instead, a colour that goes back to early forces in cities like New York.

Emergency numbers make it easy to call for help. The first 911 call in the US was made in Haleyville, Alabama, in 1968. India now uses 112 for police, fire and ambulance together.

## Trust both ways

Police have special powers, so they must follow strict rules. People have [rights](rights-duties) when they are stopped or arrested, such as being told why. In both India and the USA there have been serious complaints about police misusing force or treating some groups unfairly. Courts, human rights commissions, body cameras and community policing are all ways societies try to keep police honest. Policing tends to work best when people trust officers enough to report problems and help with investigations.
