## The short answer

UN peacekeepers are soldiers, police and civilian experts sent by the United Nations to help countries move from war towards peace. They might watch over a ceasefire line, protect villagers, help refugees return home, clear landmines, train police or help run elections.

The UN has no army of its own. Countries lend their troops and police for each mission, and they wear blue helmets or berets, which is why they are often called the "Blue Helmets". Peacekeepers are supposed to be neutral. They usually go only where the main sides in a conflict have agreed to let them in.

## How it began

The [United Nations](united-nations) was set up in 1945, after the Second World War, to prevent future wars. In 1948, it sent unarmed military observers to the Middle East to watch over a truce. That was the first peacekeeping mission, and it still operates today.

The first armed peacekeeping force came in 1956, during the Suez Crisis in Egypt. Its soldiers needed to be recognised at a glance, so blue helmet covers were quickly made, using the UN's own colour. In 1988, UN peacekeepers as a whole were awarded the [Nobel Peace Prize](nobel-peace).

## India's long role

India has been one of the biggest contributors of peacekeepers in UN history. More than 2.5 lakh Indian soldiers and police have served since the 1950s, in places from [Korea](seoul) and the Congo to Lebanon, South Sudan and Cambodia. More than 150 Indians have died while serving. In the Congo in 1961, Captain Gurbachan Singh Salaria was killed leading a charge against armed fighters. He was awarded the Param Vir Chakra, India's highest military honour, the only time it has been given for a UN mission.

In 2007, India sent the first all-women formed police unit to a UN mission, in Liberia, which was recovering from a long civil war. The officers patrolled streets, guarded government buildings and ran night patrols. Liberian women reportedly joined the country's police in larger numbers after seeing them at work. The unit served until 2016.

## A hard job

Peacekeeping is dangerous. Missions often work in places where fighting hasn't fully stopped, with armed groups that don't respect the rules. Since 1948, more than 4,000 peacekeepers from many countries have died in service.

There have also been painful failures. In Rwanda in 1994 and at Srebrenica in Bosnia in 1995, small UN forces were unable to stop terrible mass killings. Peacekeepers in Haiti were linked to a [cholera](disease-history) outbreak in 2010, and the UN later apologised. There have also been cases of peacekeepers abusing the people they were meant to protect. The UN has introduced stricter rules, though critics say more needs to be done.

## Who pays and who goes

All UN member countries share the cost, with richer countries paying more. The USA is the largest single funder. Most troops, though, come from countries in South Asia and Africa, including India, Bangladesh, Nepal, Rwanda and Ethiopia.

Today most missions are in Africa and the Middle East. They often last many years. The mission in Cyprus, for example, began in 1964 and still patrols a buffer zone across the island. Many peacekeepers say the job is worth it when a market reopens or children go back to school. The work is tied closely to [human rights](human-rights), because a ceasefire matters most when ordinary people can live safely again.
