## The short answer

Medicine is about treating one sick person. Public health is about keeping whole populations from getting sick in the first place. It includes clean drinking water, [sewers](toilets-sewers), vaccines, food safety checks, [mosquito control](mosquitoes-bite), clean air, [road safety](road-safety) and advice on healthy eating.

Most public health work is invisible when it succeeds. You don't notice the [cholera](disease-history) that never arrived or the measles outbreak that didn't happen. Yet these efforts have saved more lives than almost anything else people have done. In India, life expectancy was around 32 years at independence in 1947. Today it is about 70.

## A map and a pump

In August 1854, cholera swept through Soho in London, killing hundreds of people in a few days. Most doctors then believed diseases spread through bad air. A doctor named John Snow had a different idea: that cholera spread through dirty water.

He knocked on doors, asked where families drew their water and marked each death on a street map. The deaths clustered around a pump on Broad Street. Snow persuaded local officials to remove its handle. The outbreak was already fading, but his map became one of the most famous pieces of evidence in health history. It helped convince people that clean water could stop cholera.

## Wiping out a disease

Smallpox killed hundreds of millions of people over the centuries. From 1967, the World Health Organization led a huge campaign to find every case and vaccinate everyone around it. India was a major battleground, and health workers searched village by village, house by house. India's last case was in 1975. In 1980, WHO declared [smallpox](smallpox) gone from the whole planet, the first human disease ever wiped out.

Polio came next. India's Pulse Polio campaign, begun in 1995, sent vaccinators to give drops to every child under five, on set days, wherever they were. India's last case of wild polio was a girl in Howrah, West Bengal, in January 2011.

## The everyday work

Most public health is less dramatic. Inspectors check that milk is safe. Teams spray to control the [mosquitoes](what-if-no-mosquitoes) that carry [malaria and dengue](mosquito-diseases). Iodine added to salt has helped prevent goitre, a swelling in the neck. Laws on [seat belts](seatbelts), smoking and clean air are public health too.

Cities learned many of these lessons the hard way. When [plague](black-death) reached Bombay in 1896, it killed many thousands of people over the following years. In response, the city set up an Improvement Trust in 1898 to clear crowded, airless lanes and build better housing. Clean water and drains changed city life more than almost any medicine.

In the USA, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention began in 1946 with fighting malaria in the southern states. Today it tracks outbreaks and studies everything from food poisoning to road injuries.

## Health for everyone

Public health depends on trust and cooperation. A vaccine only stops an outbreak if enough people take it. During the COVID-19 pandemic, people around the world wore masks, kept apart and got vaccinated. Those choices showed how one person's health is tied to everyone else's.

Since 1948, the World Health Organization has helped countries share information and respond to outbreaks. Its founding day, 7 April, is now celebrated as World Health Day. Some of the biggest health victories of the next century may come from something as simple as a clean tap.
