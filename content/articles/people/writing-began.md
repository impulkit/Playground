## The short answer

Writing was invented separately in several places, including [Mesopotamia](mesopotamia), Egypt, [China](china-today) and Central America. The oldest known comes from [Sumer](sumer), in today's Iraq, about 5,200 years ago.

It began as a way to keep accounts. Temple officials needed to track how much grain, beer and how many sheep came in and went out. Over centuries, the marks grew into full writing that could record laws, letters, prayers and stories.

The [Indus script](indus-script) is one of history's great unsolved puzzles. Thousands of seals exist, but nobody can read them. Brahmi script, used by Emperor [Ashoka](ashoka), grew into Devanagari, Tamil, [Bengali](bengali-language) and many other scripts used today.

## From tokens to tablets

Before writing, people in Mesopotamia used small clay tokens to count goods: a cone for a measure of grain, perhaps a disc for a sheep. Some tokens were sealed inside clay envelopes, with their shapes pressed on the outside so people could check without breaking them.

The archaeologist Denise Schmandt-Besserat argued that this led to writing. Why make tokens at all, if marks on clay could do the job? Soon scribes were pressing signs into flat tablets with a cut reed. This wedge-shaped writing is called cuneiform.

## Signs that became sounds

The earliest signs were pictures: a drawing of a fish meant "fish". But pictures can't easily show names or ideas like "yesterday". So scribes began using signs for their sounds, like a rebus puzzle where a picture of a bee and a leaf spells "belief".

This trick let writing capture any spoken word. The first author known by name, Enheduanna, a priestess in the city of Ur, wrote hymns more than 4,000 years ago.

## Egypt and China

In Egypt, picture-writing called [hieroglyphs](hieroglyphs) appeared at about the same time as cuneiform. Nobody could read it for over 1,400 years, until the Rosetta Stone, found in 1799, carried the same message in hieroglyphs and Greek. Jean-François Champollion cracked it in 1822.

In China, the oldest known writing appears on "oracle bones" from over 3,000 years ago, used to ask questions of ancestors. Modern [Chinese characters](chinese-characters) descend from them. In Central America, [the Maya](myths-maya) developed a full writing system that scholars mostly decoded only in the late 1900s.

## The alphabet

About 3,000 years ago, traders called [the Phoenicians](phoenicians), living on the coast of today's Lebanon, used a short set of letters, each standing for one sound. It had only about 22 signs, far fewer than cuneiform's hundreds.

The Greeks borrowed it and added vowels. Latin came from Greek, and the letters you are reading now come from Latin. Some secret writing systems later scrambled these letters into [codes](cipher-codes).

## India's scripts

The Indus seals, about 4,600 years old, carry short rows of signs, usually only about five long. Some scholars think they are full writing, others think they are symbols, like logos. Without a longer text or a stone carrying the same words in a known language, nobody can be sure. Computer scientists and linguists keep trying, and new seals still turn up in excavations.

Brahmi appears on Ashoka's rock and pillar messages from about 2,300 years ago. It too was forgotten, until a British scholar, James Prinsep, worked it out in 1837. From Brahmi grew most of the [scripts of India](scripts-india) and many in Southeast Asia, from Thai to Tibetan.
