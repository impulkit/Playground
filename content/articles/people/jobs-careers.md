## The short answer

Most people choose jobs by mixing what they enjoy, what they are good at, what they can train for, and what work is available where they live. Money matters, and so do family hopes, the example of people they admire, and plain luck.

Few people pick one job at 15 and keep it forever. Many change direction several times. In a long American study, people born between 1957 and 1964 held about 12 different jobs, on average, by their mid-50s. Careers often look like a winding path rather than a straight road.

## Interests, skills and values

Career counsellors often start with three questions. What do you like doing? What are you good at, or willing to get good at? And what matters to you: helping people, making things, earning well, being outdoors, or having a steady routine?

An American psychologist, John Holland, sorted interests into six broad types, such as people who like working with tools, with ideas, with people or with data. Many career tests still use his ideas. They are not fortune-tellers, but they can point to jobs worth exploring.

## Family, place and chance

In India, many people follow their family's trade, such as farming, running a shop or [weaving](knitting-weaving). More than half of India's workers are self-employed, running their own small farms or businesses instead of working for a company. In the past, caste often decided what work a person could do. [The Constitution](constitution) banned that discrimination, though its effects have not fully disappeared.

Where you live matters too. A coastal town offers [fishing](fishing-boats) and shipping work. A big city has offices, hospitals and film studios. And chance plays a part: [a good teacher](jobs-teacher), a summer job or a book can change someone's direction completely.

## Jobs that vanish

Jobs come and go as technology changes. Before alarm clocks were common, "knocker-uppers" in British industrial towns tapped on windows with long poles to wake factory workers. Lamplighters lit gas streetlamps every evening. Telephone operators connected calls by plugging wires into boards. Most of these jobs are gone.

Once, "computer" meant a person. At NASA, [human computers](moon-race-women) like [Katherine Johnson](usa-nasa-hidden-figures) calculated flight paths by hand. When electronic computers arrived, some of these women became early programmers.

## Jobs that appear

Some jobs keep their names but change completely. A farmer today may use a phone app to check the weather and crop prices, and a doctor may study scans made by machines that did not exist 50 years ago. Even teachers now use smartboards and online lessons.

New technologies create jobs that nobody imagined before. [Pilots](jobs-pilot) appeared after the [Wright brothers](wright-brothers) flew. App designers, video editors and [drone operators](drones-delivery) are recent. The [Industrial Revolution](industrial-revolution) wiped out some hand crafts but created millions of factory jobs. Today, people are debating what [robots](robots) and [artificial intelligence](ai-what) will do. Some jobs will shrink, some will change, and new ones will appear. Experts disagree on how fast.

## Trying things out

Nobody can predict exactly which jobs will exist. So many advisers suggest building skills that travel well: reading carefully, writing clearly, working with numbers, getting along with others and learning new things quickly. Trying things out helps too. A school project, a hobby or [volunteering](volunteering) can show you what you enjoy long before you have to choose. Grace Hopper, a pioneer of computer programming, started as a maths teacher.
