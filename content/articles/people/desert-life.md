## The short answer

The [Thar Desert](thar-formed) in Rajasthan gets little rain, and only during the [monsoon](monsoon). In the far west, near Jaisalmer, a whole year's rain can be less than 25 cm, while summer days often pass 45 °C.

For centuries, people have survived by saving rainwater cleverly, in tanks, ponds and [stepwells](india-wonder-chand-baori). They built thick-walled houses, herded animals that could cope with drought, and grew hardy crops like bajra, a kind of [millet](millets).

The golden city of Jaisalmer is built from local yellow sandstone. Rocks of the same region hold [dinosaur footprints](dino-feet-tracks), left long before there was any desert at all.

## Crowded for a desert

The Thar covers about 2 lakh km², mostly in Rajasthan, with parts in Gujarat, Haryana, Punjab and Pakistan. Many deserts around the world are almost empty. The Thar is one of the most crowded, with towns, villages and farms scattered across it.

Part of the reason is that it gets some rain each year, unlike the driest deserts. Another is that people here learned, over hundreds of years, how to make every drop count.

The desert is also full of colour. Women wear bright odhnis and heavy silver jewellery, and men wear tall turbans in reds, oranges and yellows. Folk singers called Manganiyars and Langas have sung ballads of desert kings and lovers for generations, often for the same families their ancestors sang for.

## Every drop saved

Many homes and villages have a kund, or tanka: a round, covered underground tank. The ground around it slopes gently inwards, so rain runs in. The cover keeps the water cool and clean and stops it evaporating.

Villages also dug ponds called [johads](india-water-man) and talabs, and wealthy patrons built stepwells. Chand Baori at Abhaneri, near Jaipur, is more than a thousand years old. Its 3,500 steps zigzag down 13 storeys, about 30 m, to the water. People still visit it today.

## Animals of the sand

The [camel](camels) has been the Thar's truck, tractor and taxi for centuries. It can go days without drinking, carry heavy loads and walk on soft sand with its wide, padded feet. Rajasthan made it the state animal in 2014.

Herding communities such as the Rabari move with their camels, goats and sheep to find grazing. Every year, [the town of Pushkar](india-wonder-pushkar) on the desert's edge holds one of the world's largest camel fairs.

## Keeping cool

Desert houses have thick walls of stone or mud that soak up the day's heat slowly, so rooms stay cooler inside. Small windows keep out hot winds and blowing sand. Courtyards give shade.

In Jaisalmer, carved stone screens called jaalis let breezes through while blocking the glare. Jaisalmer Fort, a [UNESCO World Heritage Site](unesco-sites), is one of the few forts in the world where thousands of people still live.

## Guardians of the trees

[The Bishnoi community](wild-blackbuck) of the Thar protects trees and animals as part of its faith. [Blackbuck and chinkara deer](deer-india) often wander fearlessly near Bishnoi villages. Their story of sacrifice at Khejarli in 1730, to save khejri trees, later inspired India's [Chipko](chipko) movement.

## A canal through the sand

Since the 1950s, the Indira Gandhi Canal has carried water from rivers in Punjab hundreds of kilometres into the desert. Fields of wheat, cotton and mustard now grow where there was once only scrub. It changed many lives, though it also brought problems, such as waterlogged and salty soil in some places.
