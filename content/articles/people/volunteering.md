## The short answer

Volunteering means giving your time and effort to help others, without being paid. Volunteers teach children to read, plant trees, serve food, clean beaches, help at hospitals, rescue animals and assist after disasters. Some give an hour a month, and some give years.

People volunteer for many reasons: to help a cause they care about, to meet people, to learn skills or to give something back to their community. Many organisations, from small local groups to huge international [charities](charity), would struggle to work without volunteers.

## Helpers in uniform

Some volunteers do serious, risky jobs. In the United States, about two in three [firefighters](jobs-firefighter) are volunteers, especially in small towns where there are not enough emergencies to pay a full-time crew. They train for many hours and leave work or bed when the alarm sounds.

In India, the National Service Scheme was launched in 1969, the centenary year of [Gandhi](gandhi)'s birth. College and higher-secondary students in the scheme spend time on community work, such as health camps, literacy drives and cleanliness campaigns. Millions have taken part. The [Scouts and Guides](scouts-guides) movement also trains young people to help others.

## Volunteers for science

You do not have to leave home to volunteer. Through "citizen science" projects, ordinary people help researchers by counting birds, recording rainfall or sorting photos.

In 2007, a Dutch schoolteacher named Hanny van Arkel was classifying photos of [galaxies](galaxies) for an online project called Galaxy Zoo. She noticed a strange green blob next to one galaxy. Astronomers studied it and found a huge glowing gas cloud, now known as Hanny's Voorwerp, Dutch for "Hanny's object". Volunteers in projects like this have helped discover new planets around other stars too.

Birdwatchers around the world, including many in India, record what they see in online databases. Scientists use these records to track bird [migration](migration) and how populations change.

## Volunteers for nature

Some of the world's biggest environmental movements were led by volunteers. In the 1970s, villagers in the Himalayan foothills, many of them women, protected their forests by hugging trees to stop them being cut, in what became known as the [Chipko movement](chipko). Across India, groups of volunteers now clean beaches, revive lakes and plant saplings on weekends.

## Going abroad

The US Peace Corps, founded in 1961, sends American volunteers to live and work in other countries for two years, teaching, farming or supporting health projects. Other countries run similar programmes. The United Nations has its own volunteer service, and since 1985 it has marked 5 December as International Volunteer Day.

## What volunteers get back

In emergencies, volunteers often arrive before anyone else. After floods in [Kerala](kerala) in 2018, fishermen took their own boats into flooded towns and rescued thousands of people. Students and neighbours ran relief camps, cooked meals and sorted donations. Volunteers also help run huge gatherings like the Kumbh Mela, guiding pilgrims and reuniting lost family members.

Volunteering is unpaid, but people often say they gain a lot: new friends, new skills and a sense of purpose. Studies find that people who volunteer tend to report feeling happier, though it is hard to tell how much volunteering causes that. You can read more about this in [Is kindness good for you?](kindness-science). For young people, volunteering can also be a way to try out a kind of work before choosing a career.
