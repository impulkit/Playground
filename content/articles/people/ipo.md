## The short answer

IPO stands for Initial Public Offering. It is the first time a company sells its shares to the public.

A share is a small piece of a company. Before an IPO, the company belongs to its founders and a few big investors. After it, anyone with a demat account and a few thousand rupees can buy a piece, and buy or sell it again on the [stock market](stock-market).

## How it works

Think of a family that runs a popular sweet shop. They want to open ten new branches, but they do not have the money. They could borrow from a [bank](money-banks), but a loan must be paid back with [interest](?interest). Or they could cut the business into a thousand pieces, keep some, and sell the rest to people in town. That second choice is an IPO, on a much bigger scale.

Here is how it happens, step by step.

First, the company hires investment banks to help. Together they decide how much money to raise and roughly what each share is worth.

Next, the company writes a long document called a prospectus. It explains the business, its profits, its debts and its risks. In India, it goes to SEBI, the market regulator, which checks that nothing important is hidden.

Then the company announces a price band, such as ₹1,065 to ₹1,119 a share, and opens the IPO for about three working days. People apply through a broker app or their bank. Shares come in fixed bundles called lots, so you apply for one lot or more, not for a single share.

The shares are split between groups: big institutions, wealthy investors and ordinary people. In most big Indian IPOs, at least 35% is kept for small investors.

If people ask for more shares than there are, the IPO is "oversubscribed". Small investors may then be picked by lottery, so getting shares is a matter of [probability](?probability), not of who applied first.

Finally, a few working days after the IPO closes, the shares are listed on the BSE and NSE. From that moment the price is set by buyers and sellers. When more people want to buy than sell, the price rises. That is [supply and demand](supply-demand) at work.

## Where it came from

In 1602, the Dutch East India Company sold shares to ordinary people in [Amsterdam](amsterdam) to pay for spice voyages to Asia. It is often called the world's first IPO. Buyers later traded those shares with each other, and Amsterdam became home to one of the first stock exchanges. Britain's [East India Company](east-india-company), which later ruled much of India, also raised money from shareholders.

In India, traders in Bombay began meeting under banyan trees in the 1850s to buy and sell shares. In 1875 their group became the Bombay Stock Exchange, the oldest stock exchange in Asia.

## India's story

For a long time, the government decided the price of every Indian IPO, through an office called the Controller of Capital Issues. After the [reforms of 1991](economic-reforms-1991) opened up India's [economy](?economy), that office was closed in 1992. SEBI, set up in 1988, got the power to watch over the markets. Companies could now set their own prices, as long as they told buyers the truth.

The same year, the National Stock Exchange was set up. It brought trading onto computer screens, so a buyer in Kochi and a seller in Kanpur could trade in seconds.

Big IPOs followed. In 2021, the food delivery company Zomato listed, one of many [Indian startups](indian-startups) to do so. In 2022, LIC, the government's life insurance company, raised about ₹21,000 crore. In 2024, Hyundai Motor India raised about ₹27,870 crore. Then, in October 2026, Jio Platforms raised about ₹30,213 crore, India's largest IPO so far, and was valued at over ₹12 [lakh crore](lakh-crore) on its first day.

## The numbers that matter

**More than 1.3 crore.** That is how many applications came in for the Jio IPO, reports say. No IPO anywhere in the world had ever crossed one crore.

**22 times.** People bid for about 22 times as many Jio shares as were on sale, worth more than ₹4.9 lakh crore, not counting the big "anchor" investors. So most applicants got far fewer shares than they asked for, or none at all.

**₹1.95 lakh crore.** That is what more than 365 Indian IPOs raised in 2025, a record. About 106 were big companies. The rest were small firms listing on special exchanges for small businesses.

**About 23 crore.** That is roughly how many demat accounts, the online lockers that hold shares, India had by mid-2026. Some people have more than one, so the number of investors is smaller.

**21%.** Jio shares sold at ₹1,119 opened at ₹1,358. Price changes are always shown as [percentages](percentages), so you can compare a ₹100 share with a ₹2,000 one.

## What people argue about

Some people say IPOs are good for ordinary families. Owning big companies used to be only for the rich. Now a shopkeeper or teacher can own a small piece, and SEBI's rules on what companies must reveal help protect them.

Others worry that many people apply only to sell on the first day, treating an IPO like a lottery ticket. They say popular IPOs are sometimes priced so high that people who buy later lose money. Both sides agree that buyers should read about the company first.

## Myths and mix-ups

**"All the IPO money goes to the company."** Not always. Part of an IPO can be new shares, and that money goes to the company. Part can be early owners selling their own shares, called an offer for sale, and that money goes to them.

**"A huge IPO always rises on day one."** Hyundai Motor India's IPO in 2024 was India's biggest at the time. Its shares opened about 1.3% below their issue price.

**"A ₹100 share is cheaper than a ₹1,000 share."** Not really. What matters is the value of the whole company divided by the number of shares. A company with more, smaller shares is not a better bargain.

## What it means for you

Even families who never buy a share are touched by IPOs. Provident funds, mutual funds and [insurance](insurance) companies put part of people's savings into shares. When a company raises money, it may build factories, open shops and hire workers.

If a grown-up in your family applies for an IPO, a few rules help. Read what the company does and how it earns money. Never borrow to apply. Remember that prices can fall as well as rise. Over many years, owning good companies is one way people try to [grow their savings](maths-money-compound), but nobody can promise a share will go up.
