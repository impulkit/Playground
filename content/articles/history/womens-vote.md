## The short answer

For most of history, women were not allowed to vote in elections, even where men could. Starting in the 1800s, women and their supporters campaigned to change this. They wrote petitions, held marches, gave speeches and, in some places, broke laws and went to prison.

The campaign for the vote is called suffrage, and its campaigners were suffragists. [New Zealand](new-zealand) became the first self-governing country where women could vote in national elections, in 1893. The USA followed in 1920 and Britain fully in 1928. India took a different path: its Constitution gave every adult the vote, women included, from the start.

## New Zealand goes first

In New Zealand, Kate Sheppard led a campaign that gathered signatures on huge petitions. The biggest, in 1893, was signed by nearly 24,000 women. Its pages were glued into one long roll, which was unrolled across the floor of Parliament.

Later that year, women won the vote. In the election a few weeks afterwards, most adult women in the country took part. Kate Sheppard's face is now on New Zealand's 10-dollar note.

## The American fight

In 1848, about 300 people met at Seneca Falls, New York, for the first [women's rights](womens-rights) convention. Elizabeth Cady Stanton wrote a declaration based on the Declaration of Independence, saying that "all men and women are created equal".

[Susan B. Anthony](susan-b-anthony) was arrested in 1872 for voting. Black women such as Sojourner Truth and Ida B. Wells campaigned too, though some white suffragists pushed them aside. Some western states and territories, starting with Wyoming in 1869, let women vote in their own elections.

In 1917, women stood silently outside the White House holding banners. Many were arrested. In August 1920, the 19th Amendment to the Constitution was ratified, after Tennessee approved it by one vote. About 2.6 crore American women could now take part in [US elections](elections-usa).

## Suffragettes in Britain

In Britain, Emmeline Pankhurst founded a group in 1903 whose members became known as suffragettes. Tired of being ignored, some smashed windows and chained themselves to railings. In prison, many went on hunger strike.

One active suffragette was Sophia Duleep Singh, a Punjabi princess and goddaughter of Queen Victoria, who sold suffragette [newspapers](newspapers) outside her home near London.

In 1918, women over 30 who met certain conditions won the vote. Ten years later, women got the vote on the same terms as men.

## India's choice

In British India, women campaigned from 1917, with [Sarojini Naidu](sarojini-naidu) among those who spoke for them. From 1921 some provinces, starting with Madras, let a small number of women vote, usually those who owned property or had education.

When India wrote its [Constitution](constitution), its makers chose universal adult franchise: every citizen aged 21 or over could vote, whatever their sex, religion, caste, wealth or education. Many people abroad doubted it would work in a country where most people could not read. In the first general election of 1951–52, [election officials](election-commission) used picture symbols for parties and separate ballot boxes for each candidate.

## Still changing

Women's voting rights kept spreading through the 20th century. Saudi Arabia let women vote in local elections for the first time in 2015.

In India, women now vote in very large numbers. In recent national elections, the share of women voting has been about the same as men's, and in some states higher. In 1966, [Indira Gandhi](indira-gandhi) became India's first woman Prime Minister.
