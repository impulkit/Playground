## The short answer

Archaeologists find out about the past by studying the things people left behind: buildings, tools, pots, bones, seeds and rubbish. Much of human history happened before writing, or in places where writing did not survive. Objects are often the only evidence.

They dig carefully, layer by layer, and record exactly where each object lies. The order of the layers tells them what came first. Historians, who mostly study [written sources](history-sources), often work alongside them. Archaeologists use science to date objects, test what people ate and even where they grew up. Bit by bit, broken pieces become a story.

## Reading the layers

Over time, dust, mud and rubbish build up on the ground. People build new houses on top of old ones. This creates layers, a bit like pages stacked in a pile. Usually, the deeper the layer, the older it is. Studying layers is called stratigraphy.

That is why archaeologists don't just grab interesting objects. A pot is useful, but knowing which layer it came from, and what lay next to it, tells much more. Once something is dug up, its exact place can never be checked again, so diggers draw, photograph and measure everything.

## Clocks in the ground

Living things take in a tiny amount of a special kind of carbon. When they die, it slowly turns into something else at a steady rate, losing half every 5,730 years. Measuring what's left in a bone, seed or piece of charcoal tells scientists roughly when it died. This is radiocarbon dating, invented by the American chemist Willard Libby in the late 1940s. It works for things up to about 50,000 years old. The steady rate of loss is called a [half-life](?halflife).

Tree rings help too. Each year a tree adds a ring, wide in good years and thin in bad ones. Matching patterns between old timbers can date a wooden beam to the exact year it was cut.

## Rubbish is treasure

Some of archaeologists' favourite finds are rubbish heaps. Broken pots, animal bones and burnt seeds show what people cooked and ate. Tiny grains of pollen show what plants grew nearby.

Bones and teeth hold clues too. Unlike [fossils](fossils-form), which have turned to stone over millions of years, the bones archaeologists study are usually only hundreds or thousands of years old. Chemical traces in tooth enamel can show where a person spent their childhood, which helps track how people moved.

## Finds in India

In the 1920s, digs at Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, now in Pakistan, revealed the [Indus civilisation](indus-cities), with planned streets, brick houses and [drains](oddity-ancient-toilets), more than 4,500 years old. Until then, nobody knew it had existed.

Later finds include Lothal in Gujarat, with what may be an ancient dockyard, and Rakhigarhi in Haryana, one of the largest Indus sites. At Keeladi in Tamil Nadu, recent digs have found pottery with early Tamil writing. The Archaeological Survey of India, founded in 1861, looks after thousands of protected monuments.

## New tools

Today some archaeology starts in the air. [Lasers](lasers) fired from aircraft, called lidar, can see through forest to the shape of the ground below. In 2018, lidar revealed tens of thousands of hidden Maya structures in the jungles of Guatemala, adding to the list of [lost cities](lost-cities) found again. Ground-penetrating radar can find walls under fields without any digging.

Even so, much of the work still happens on hands and knees with a small trowel and a soft brush, one thin layer at a time. Some finds are pure luck, like the farmers in China whose well-digging struck a clay soldier's head.
