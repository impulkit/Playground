## The short answer

For hundreds of thousands of years, people lived by hunting and gathering. Then, starting around 11,500 years ago, some groups in the Middle East began to plant seeds of wild grasses and keep herds of [goats and sheep](goats-sheep). Over many generations, they chose the best plants and the calmest animals, and those slowly changed. This is called domestication.

Farming was not invented once. It began in at least half a dozen regions, at different times, with different crops. Within a few thousand years, farmers had settled villages, food stores and far more children than hunters could feed. That set the stage for towns and cities.

## The Fertile Crescent

The first known farmers lived in an arc of land from today's Israel and Jordan, up through Syria and Turkey and down to Iraq and Iran. Historians call it the Fertile Crescent. Here grew wild [wheat](wheat-bread), barley, lentils and peas.

Wild wheat has a problem for people: when ripe, the seeds fall off and scatter. A few plants, by chance, held on to their seeds. When people harvested with sickles, those were the ones they carried home and planted. Over time, fields filled with wheat that waited to be picked.

Animals changed too. Goats and sheep were tamed here, and cattle soon after. Dogs had joined people even earlier, while everyone was still hunting. Taming an animal took many generations of keeping the gentlest ones and letting them breed, until the herd behaved very differently from its wild cousins.

## Farmers around the world

In China, people began growing [rice](rice) along the Yangtze River and millet along the Yellow River. In Mexico, they turned teosinte into [maize](corn-maize), and later grew beans and squash beside it. In the Andes of South America, they grew [potatoes](potatoes) and kept llamas. In New Guinea, they grew taro and [bananas](bananas). In Africa, they grew sorghum and some kinds of [millets](millets).

In South Asia, one of the oldest farming sites is Mehrgarh, in the hills of Balochistan, now in Pakistan. About 9,000 years ago, people there grew barley and wheat and kept humped zebu cattle, the kind still seen across India today. Their descendants helped build the [Indus cities](indus-cities).

## Why start farming?

Farming is hard work. So why did people choose it? Nobody knows for sure. As the last ice age ended, the climate grew warmer and wetter, and wild grains became plentiful in some places. People may have settled near these grains, grown in number, and then needed to plant more to feed everyone.

Some groups built big stone monuments before they farmed. At [Göbekli Tepe](mystery-gobekli) in Turkey, people carved huge stone pillars about 11,000 years ago. Gatherings at such places may have needed a lot of food, which could have pushed people to grow it.

## A mixed blessing

Farming fed more people, but life was not always better. Skeletons show that many early farmers were shorter than hunters before them, and had more tooth decay and disease. Living close to animals and to each other spread germs. A failed harvest could mean famine.

The writer Jared Diamond once called farming "the worst mistake in the history of the human race". Many historians disagree, pointing to what farming made possible: food stores, trades, writing and [cities](first-cities). Today about 8 billion people depend on the crops those first farmers chose, grown on fields that still need good soil and steady rain.
