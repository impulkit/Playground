## The short answer

Yes, almost certainly. Indus seals, beads and weights have been found in Mesopotamian cities such as [Ur](sumer) and Kish, in what is now Iraq. Mesopotamian clay tablets talk about ships from a faraway land called Meluhha, which carried carnelian, ivory and fine wood. Most historians think Meluhha was the Indus region.

The trade was at its height between about 2600 and 1900 BCE. Ships seem to have hugged the coast of the Arabian Sea and the Persian Gulf, stopping at Magan, today's Oman, and Dilmun, today's Bahrain. It was one of the longest trade links in the world at the time.

## The interpreter's seal

The best clue is a tiny stone cylinder in the Louvre museum in Paris. It belonged to a man named Shu-ilishu, who lived in Mesopotamia around 2200 BCE. The writing on it calls him an interpreter of the Meluhhan language.

That small detail says a lot. People from Meluhha were visiting often enough, or even living in Mesopotamia, that someone made a career of translating for them. Sargon of Akkad, a powerful Mesopotamian king, boasted that ships from Meluhha, Magan and Dilmun tied up at the quay of his capital.

## Red beads and long drills

Indus craftspeople were famous for beads made of carnelian, a red-orange stone. They heated the stone to deepen its colour, then drilled long, narrow holes through it with special stone drills. Some long beads took days of patient work.

Etched carnelian beads, with white patterns burned onto the red stone, have been found in the royal graves at Ur. Archaeologists think many were made in the Indus or by Indus craftspeople living abroad. Shell bangles, ivory and timber probably travelled west too. What the [Indus cities](indus-cities) got back is harder to trace, but metals such as tin and silver may have been part of it.

## Weights everyone could trust

Indus traders used cube-shaped stone weights, often made of a banded stone called chert. The smaller ones double each time: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64. The same set turns up in cities hundreds of kilometres apart, so a trader in Harappa and a trader in Lothal measured goods in the same way.

Fair [weights](weights-mass) matter in any market, from an ancient quay to a sabzi mandi today. A standard set is a sign of an organised trading system, perhaps with officials to check it. The Indus people also used a decimal system for their larger weights, much as we count in tens today.

## A dock at Lothal

At Lothal in Gujarat, archaeologists found a large brick basin, about 220 metres long, roughly as long as two football fields. The excavator, S.R. Rao, argued that it was a dockyard joined to a river channel, where boats could load and unload.

Not everyone agrees. Some experts think it was a reservoir for storing water. Either way, Lothal had bead workshops, a warehouse and a seal from the Persian Gulf, so it was clearly tied into long-distance trade. Its position near the sea made it a natural [port](sea-ports).

## Why the trade faded

After about 1900 BCE, mentions of Meluhha in Mesopotamian texts grow rare. The big Indus cities were shrinking at the same time, perhaps because rivers shifted and the [monsoon](monsoon) rains weakened. As the cities emptied, the ships stopped coming, and the memory of Meluhha slowly faded from the records.
