## The short answer

The civil rights movement was a struggle, mostly in the 1950s and 1960s, by Black Americans and their allies to end unfair treatment under the law in the United States.

A century after slavery ended, many southern states still had laws, nicknamed "Jim Crow laws", that kept Black and white people apart. This is called segregation. Black children went to separate, poorer schools. Buses, parks and even drinking fountains were divided. Unfair tests and threats kept most Black southerners from voting. Through peaceful protest, court cases and huge marches, the movement got these laws struck down and new ones passed to protect equal rights.

## Separate and unequal

In 1896 the US [Supreme Court](supreme-courts) ruled that separate facilities were legal as long as they were "equal". In practice, they almost never were. Black schools often had broken buildings and old books.

Lawyers from a group called the NAACP spent years fighting this in court. In 1954, in a case called Brown v. Board of Education, the Supreme Court ruled that separating schoolchildren by race was against [the Constitution](us-constitution). Thurgood Marshall, the lawyer who argued the case, later became the first Black judge on the Supreme Court. Many southern states refused to obey. In 1957, nine Black students needed soldiers to protect them as they entered a high school in Little Rock, Arkansas.

## The bus boycott

On 1 December 1955, [Rosa Parks](rosa-parks) was arrested in Montgomery, Alabama, for refusing to give up her bus seat to a white man. Black residents responded by refusing to ride the city buses. They walked, shared cars and organised rides for 381 days. A young preacher, [Martin Luther King Jr.](martin-luther-king), became the boycott's leader. In 1956 the courts ended bus segregation there.

## Learning from India

Many leaders of the movement studied [Gandhi](gandhi), who had used non-violent protest against British rule. The idea was to break unfair laws openly and peacefully, accept arrest, and never hit back. King visited India in 1959 to learn more.

Protesters trained hard for this. They practised staying calm while others shouted at them. Students held sit-ins at segregated lunch counters. In 1963, in Birmingham, Alabama, hundreds of schoolchildren marched, and many were arrested. Police turned fire hoses and dogs on them, and the pictures on [television](television-invented) turned many Americans in favour of the protesters. "Freedom Riders" travelled on buses through the South to test laws against segregated travel.

## The march and the laws

On 28 August 1963, about 2.5 lakh people gathered in Washington, D.C. for the March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom. There, King gave his "I Have a Dream" speech.

The next year, Congress passed the Civil Rights Act of 1964, banning segregation in public places and unfair treatment at work. In 1965, marchers set out from Selma, Alabama, to demand the vote. Police attacked them on the Edmund Pettus Bridge, and the images shocked the country. Months later, the Voting Rights Act made it illegal to block people from [voting](vote-how) through unfair tests.

## What changed and what didn't

Within a few years, the number of Black voters in the South rose sharply, and Black Americans were elected to offices that had been closed to them. The movement also inspired other groups, including women, Latino farm workers and disabled people, to demand equal rights.

It did not end unfairness overnight. Arguments about equality in schools, jobs and policing continue. But the laws of the country had changed, and so had what most Americans thought was acceptable.
