## The short answer

The Renaissance was a time of great change in European art, science and ideas, from about 1350 to 1600. It began in Italy, especially in the city of Florence, and spread across Europe.

The word means "rebirth". Renaissance scholars believed they were bringing back the learning of [ancient Greece](ancient-greece) and Rome. They studied old texts, asked questions about the world and celebrated what humans could achieve, an outlook called humanism. Artists such as [Leonardo](leonardo) da Vinci and [Michelangelo](michelangelo) made paintings and sculptures that looked startlingly real. Architects designed buildings inspired by Roman ruins, and the [printing press](printing-press) let new ideas spread faster than ever before.

## Why Florence?

In the 1400s, Florence was rich from banking and the wool and cloth trade. Wealthy families, especially the Medici, competed to show off their money and taste. They paid artists, architects and scholars to work for them. Someone who funds artists in this way is called a patron.

Florence was also a republic of proud citizens and busy workshops. Young artists trained as apprentices, grinding colours and copying their masters before taking on work of their own. Talent, money and rivalry came together in one small city.

## A dome and a vanishing point

Florence's cathedral had a gap in its roof for decades, because nobody knew how to build a dome that wide. In 1420, the goldsmith-turned-architect Filippo Brunelleschi took on the job. He finished it in 1436 without wooden supports underneath, using clever brick patterns and two shells. It is still the largest brick dome in the world.

Brunelleschi also showed painters how to use [perspective](perspective). Lines that run away from you, like the edges of a road, seem to meet at one point on the horizon. Paintings using this rule suddenly looked like windows into real space.

## Ideas from far away

The Renaissance did not come only from Europe's own past. Many ancient Greek books had been preserved and studied in Arabic by scholars in Baghdad, [Cairo](world-cairo) and Spain. Their own work in medicine, astronomy and mathematics was translated into Latin.

[Indian numerals](indian-numerals), including zero, travelled the same way. In 1202 the Italian mathematician [Fibonacci](maths-golden-spiral) wrote a book explaining these "Hindu-Arabic" [numbers](number-systems) to European merchants. They were far easier to use than [Roman numerals](roman-numerals). When Constantinople fell to the [Ottomans](ottomans) in 1453, Greek scholars fled to Italy, bringing more texts.

## Painters, sculptors and stargazers

Leonardo da Vinci painted the *[Mona Lisa](famous-paintings)* and filled notebooks with drawings of machines, birds and the human body. Michelangelo carved his marble *David*, over 5 metres tall, and spent four years painting the ceiling of the [Sistine Chapel](vatican) in Rome, from 1508 to 1512.

Scientists began to test old ideas with observation. In 1543 Nicolaus Copernicus argued that the Earth goes around the Sun. Later, [Galileo](galileo) pointed a telescope at the sky and found moons orbiting [Jupiter](jupiter-giant).

## Who named it?

People at the time did talk about a revival of learning. But the word "Renaissance" for the whole era became popular only in the 19th century, especially after the Swiss historian Jacob Burckhardt wrote about it in 1860.

Today many historians are cautious about the label. Change came slowly, and mostly reached the rich and educated. Ordinary farmers' lives changed little. Still, the art of this age, from the dome of Florence to the *Mona Lisa*, draws millions of visitors every year.
