## The short answer

Rome grew from a small town in central Italy into one of the largest empires in history. It did this over hundreds of years with a powerful army, a habit of turning defeated enemies into allies, and very practical engineering: roads, [bridges](bridges), aqueducts and concrete.

Rome also offered something to the peoples it conquered. Local leaders who cooperated could become Roman citizens, enjoy Roman law and trade across the empire. Many cities came to see themselves as Roman. That loyalty helped the empire last for centuries.

## Kings, a republic, then emperors

Legend says Rome was founded in 753 BCE by Romulus and Remus, twin brothers raised by a she-wolf. For about 250 years, kings ruled. Then, in 509 BCE, the Romans threw out their last king and set up a republic. Two leaders called consuls were elected each year, advised by a council of older men, the Senate.

The republic conquered Italy, then fought [Carthage](carthage), a rich trading city in North Africa. The Carthaginian general Hannibal famously marched [war elephants](oddity-elephants-war) over the Alps, but Rome won in the end. After years of civil war, Julius Caesar took power and was murdered in 44 BCE. His adopted son Octavian became the first emperor, Augustus, in 27 BCE.

## The army

Rome's soldiers were trained professionals. A legion had about 5,000 men, organised into smaller units with clear ranks. They drilled constantly, marched about 30 km in a day carrying heavy packs, and built a fortified camp each night.

Wherever the army went, it built [roads](roman-roads) to move troops and supplies quickly. Soldiers who served 25 years were given land or money, and non-citizen soldiers received citizenship. In 212 CE, Emperor Caracalla made almost all free people in the empire Roman citizens.

## Concrete and water

Romans mixed lime with volcanic ash to make a concrete that could even set underwater. With it they built harbours, domes and the arches of the [Colosseum](colosseum). Aqueducts carried fresh water for kilometres into cities, mostly by gentle slopes underground. Rome itself had public baths, fountains and [toilets](oddity-ancient-toilets) flushed by running water.

## Trading with India

Roman ships sailed from Egypt across the Arabian Sea, riding [the monsoon](monsoon) winds, to ports on India's west coast. One of the most important was Muziris, in today's [Kerala](kerala). They brought gold and silver coins, wine and glass, and took home [pepper](food-pepper-black-gold) and other [spices](spices), pearls, ivory and fine [cotton](clothes-cotton) cloth.

Hoards of Roman coins have been dug up across South India. At Arikamedu, near [Puducherry](puducherry), archaeologists found Roman pottery and wine jars. The writer Pliny the Elder grumbled that India drained Rome of vast sums every year in exchange for luxuries.

## Why the west fell

By the 200s CE, the empire was huge and hard to defend. Emperors came and went quickly, often murdered by their own soldiers. Taxes rose, money lost its value, and groups from outside the borders pressed in. The empire was split into eastern and western halves.

In 476 CE, a Germanic general removed the last western emperor. The eastern half, ruled from Constantinople, now [Istanbul](istanbul), carried on for nearly 1,000 more years, until 1453.

## Rome is still here

[Latin](latin-roots), the Roman language, grew into Italian, Spanish, French, Portuguese and Romanian, and gave English thousands of words. July and August are named after Julius Caesar and Augustus. The USA named its Senate after Rome's, and the US Capitol takes its name from Rome's Capitoline Hill. Clock faces and book chapters still use [Roman numerals](roman-numerals), more than 1,500 years after the last western emperor.
