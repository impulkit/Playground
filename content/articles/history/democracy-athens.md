## The short answer

Around 508 BCE, in the [ancient Greek](ancient-greece) city-state of Athens, and after years of rule by powerful families and tyrants, a leader named Cleisthenes reorganised Athens. He gave power to an assembly of ordinary male citizens, who met on a hillside to debate and vote on laws, war, peace and taxes. The Athenians called this *demokratia*, from *demos*, the people, and *kratos*, power.

Unlike most democracies today, Athens was direct. Citizens did not elect representatives to decide for them. They voted on decisions themselves. And many officials were picked by lot, a random draw, so that any citizen might end up in charge for a while.

## From tyrants to the people

Before democracy, Athens was ruled by rich landowners, and at times by a single strongman called a tyrant. Poor farmers fell into debt and some were sold into slavery. In 594 BCE a lawmaker named Solon cancelled debts and banned enslaving Athenians for debt.

After the last tyrant was thrown out, Cleisthenes went further. He split citizens into ten new tribes mixed from different parts of the region, so that old family loyalties mattered less. Each tribe sent 50 men, chosen by lot, to a Council of 500 that prepared business for the assembly.

## A meeting on a hill

The assembly met about 40 times a year on the Pnyx, a rocky hill facing the [Acropolis](acropolis). Any citizen could come, and some votes needed at least 6,000 to be valid. Anyone could stand up and speak. Votes were usually by a show of hands, and officials judged which side won by eye.

Athens may have had around 30,000 adult male citizens in the 400s BCE. That is fewer than the number of people who can sit in a large cricket stadium today. In India's [general election](election-commission) in 2024, by comparison, more than 60 crore people cast a vote.

## Choosing by lottery

Athenians believed elections favoured the rich and the famous. Picking officials by chance felt fairer, because it gave every citizen the same odds. In the language of [probability](probability), each name had an equal chance.

They even built a machine for it: the kleroterion, a stone slab with rows of slots. Citizens slotted in bronze name tickets. Coloured balls dropped through a tube decided which rows were chosen. Jurors for trials were picked this way too, and juries could have hundreds of members. In 399 BCE, a [jury](jury) of about 500 found the philosopher [Socrates](socrates) guilty, and sentenced him to death.

Generals, though, were elected, because the city needed skill, not luck, to win wars.

## Sending someone away

Once a year, citizens could vote to ostracise someone they thought was becoming too powerful. Each voter scratched a name on a pottery shard. If at least 6,000 votes were cast, the man with the most had to leave Athens for ten years. He kept his property and could return afterwards. It was a way to remove a threat without a trial or bloodshed.

## Who was left out

Athenian democracy had big limits. Women could not vote. Neither could the many enslaved people whose work kept the city running, or foreigners who lived there for generations. Critics at the time, including Plato, thought crowds could be swayed by clever speakers into bad decisions.

## What came after

Athens lost its independence to Macedonia in 322 BCE, and its democracy ended. Other ancient peoples also had councils and assemblies. In India, around 2,500 years ago, some states such as the Licchavis of Vaishali were ruled by assemblies of leading families, which some historians call republics.

Modern democracies, from India's [Parliament](parliament-india) to the [US Congress](congress-usa), mostly use elected representatives instead of mass meetings. But traces of Athens remain. Jury service, and the [gram sabha](panchayat), where villagers meet to discuss local matters, both carry the old idea that ordinary people can decide.
