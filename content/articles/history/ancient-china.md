## The short answer

Ancient China gave the world many inventions, but four are so famous that they are called the Four Great Inventions: paper, the magnetic compass, gunpowder and printing. Each spread slowly westward along trade routes and changed how people wrote, travelled, fought and shared ideas.

China also gave the world [silk](silk), porcelain, tea drinking, kites, the wheelbarrow, cast iron and an early earthquake detector. Many of these came from more than 2,000 years of record-keeping, careful craftsmanship and government support for scholars and engineers.

## Paper

Before paper, Chinese scribes wrote on strips of [bamboo](bamboo) tied together, or on silk, which was costly. Bamboo books were heavy. One story says an official needed two men to carry the reports he wrote to the emperor.

Early forms of paper existed by the 100s BCE. In 105 CE, an official named Cai Lun presented the emperor with an improved way of making it. Tree bark, hemp, old rags and fishing nets were soaked, pounded and spread thin to dry. [Paper](paper-invention) was cheap and light, and its use spread across China, then to [Korea](ancient-korea), Japan, the Islamic world and, by about the 1100s, to Europe.

## Printing

Chinese printers carved a whole page of text backwards into a wooden block, inked it, and pressed paper onto it. The Diamond Sutra of 868 CE, found in a cave at Dunhuang, is the oldest printed book that carries a date.

In the 1040s, a craftsman named Bi Sheng made movable type: single characters baked from clay that could be arranged into any page and reused. Chinese has thousands of characters, so wooden blocks often remained more practical. In Europe, with a small alphabet, [Gutenberg](printing-revolution)'s [printing press](printing-press) of the 1450s made movable type a huge success.

## The compass

Chinese thinkers noticed that a lodestone, a naturally magnetic rock, always turned to point north and south. More than 2,000 years ago, they made spoon-shaped lodestones that spun on a smooth plate. These were used at first for fortune-telling and for planning buildings.

By the 1000s CE, Chinese sailors were using magnetised needles to find their way at sea when clouds hid the stars. The scholar Shen Kuo described such a needle in 1088. It works because Earth itself acts like a giant [magnet](magnets-how).

## Gunpowder

Alchemists in the 800s CE mixed saltpetre, sulphur and charcoal while searching for medicines and a potion for long life. Instead, the mixture flared and burned. A military book from 1044 CE gives the first known written recipe.

At first, gunpowder was used in fire arrows and simple bombs, and later in [rockets](rockets-how) and guns. It also gave the world [fireworks](fireworks), which are still lit to celebrate Chinese [New Year](new-year), Diwali and the Fourth of July.

## A box that sensed earthquakes

In 132 CE, the scholar Zhang Heng built a large bronze vessel with eight dragons around the top, each holding a ball in its mouth. Below sat eight bronze toads with open mouths. When the ground shook, even far away, a ball dropped from one dragon into a toad, showing the direction of the earthquake. Nobody knows exactly how the inside worked, and modern engineers have built several possible versions.

## Ideas travelling both ways

China and India swapped ideas for centuries. Buddhism travelled from India to China, and Chinese monks such as Xuanzang walked across mountains and deserts in the 600s CE to study at [Nalanda](nalanda). He carried hundreds of texts home. Paper and silk, in turn, made their way to India.
