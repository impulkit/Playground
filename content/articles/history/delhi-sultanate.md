## The short answer

The Delhi Sultanate was a series of Muslim kingdoms that ruled much of north India from Delhi between 1206 and 1526. Its rulers were called sultans.

Five dynasties held power one after another: the Mamluks (sometimes called the Slave dynasty), the Khaljis, the Tughlaqs, the Sayyids and the Lodis. At its largest, under the Khaljis and the Tughlaqs, the sultanate reached deep into south India. The period brought new styles of building with arches and domes, new kinds of music and poetry, and a new mixed language that grew into [Hindi](hindi-language) and [Urdu](urdu). It ended in 1526, when Babur defeated the last sultan at Panipat and founded the [Mughal](mughals) Empire.

## A slave who became a king

The first sultan, Qutb-ud-din Aibak, began life as an enslaved soldier. In Central Asia at that time, slaves were often trained as soldiers and officials, and some rose very high. Aibak became a general for the ruler Muhammad of Ghor, and took charge of his Indian lands.

Around 1199, Aibak began building the Qutb Minar in [Delhi](delhi), a tower of red and buff sandstone. His successor, Iltutmish, finished it. At about 73 metres, it is as tall as a 24-storey building, and it is now a UNESCO [World Heritage](unesco-sites) Site.

## Razia, the sultan who was a woman

Iltutmish chose his daughter Razia to rule after him, saying she was more able than his sons. In 1236 she became sultan, the only woman to rule from Delhi's throne in this era.

Razia dressed in a cloak and turban, held court in public and rode an elephant at the head of her army. Many nobles disliked taking orders from a woman. After about four years she was overthrown and killed. Her story has inspired books, plays and a film.

## Market rules and Mongol armies

Alauddin Khalji, who ruled from 1296 to 1316, faced repeated invasions by [Mongol](mongols) armies from Central Asia. To pay a large standing army, he fixed the prices of grain, cloth and horses in Delhi's markets, and punished shopkeepers who cheated on weights.

His armies also marched south and brought back great wealth. Court historians praised his control, while others recorded that people found his rule harsh. He was one of the few rulers of the time to keep the Mongols out of India.

## A sultan with big ideas

Muhammad bin Tughlaq, who ruled from 1325 to 1351, was clever and well read, yet many of his plans failed. In 1327 he ordered the capital moved from Delhi to Daulatabad in the Deccan, about 1,100 km away. Many people suffered on the march, and the court later returned.

He also issued copper and brass token coins to stand in for silver, an idea a bit like modern [money](money-worth). But they were easy to forge, and the plan collapsed. The Moroccan traveller [Ibn Battuta](ibn-battuta) worked for him as a judge and described his generosity and his sudden anger.

## Pillars, poets and the end

The next sultan, Firoz Shah Tughlaq, built canals, gardens and towns. He also had two of [Ashoka](ashoka)'s ancient stone pillars carried to Delhi, wrapped in silk cotton and moved on a huge cart.

The poet and musician Amir Khusrau lived in this era. He wrote in Persian and in Hindavi, an early form of Hindi and Urdu, and he is linked to the growth of [qawwali](qawwali) [singing](singing). In 1398, the Central Asian conqueror Timur sacked Delhi, and the sultanate never fully recovered. In 1526, Babur's guns and cavalry defeated Ibrahim Lodi at Panipat, and a new empire began.
