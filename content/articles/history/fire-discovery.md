## The short answer

Nobody invented fire. It was already there, in grass fires lit by [lightning](lightning) and in smouldering logs after a storm. Early humans first learned to use it: carrying a burning branch, feeding it with wood and keeping it alive. Much later, they learned to start a fire whenever they wanted.

The evidence comes from charcoal, ash, burnt bones and stones changed by heat, found in [caves](caves-form) and old campsites. The oldest strong clues are about a million years old, from a cave in [South Africa](south-africa). By around 400,000 years ago, fire shows up at many sites in Africa, Europe and Asia.

## Clues in the ash

Finding ancient fire is tricky. A wildfire can scorch bones and soil too. So scientists look for signs that a fire burned in one spot, again and again, inside a shelter where lightning could not reach.

At Wonderwerk Cave, researchers found fine ash and burnt bone fragments about 30 metres inside the cave, layered as if from small fires. At Gesher Benot Ya'aqov in Israel, burnt seeds, wood and flint about 790,000 years old cluster in patches, like old hearths. Some claims of even older fires exist, but many scientists are not convinced.

## Rubbing and striking

Starting a fire takes skill. One way uses [friction](friction): spinning a hard stick fast against a softer piece of wood until the dust gets hot enough to glow. Tiny embers are then dropped into dry grass and blown into flame. A bow, like a small archer's bow, makes the spinning easier.

The other way strikes certain stones together, such as flint against a mineral called pyrite, to throw sparks onto dry tinder. Some flint tools left by [Neanderthals](neanderthals) in France, about 50,000 years old, have marks that may come from exactly this.

## What fire changed

Fire kept people warm and let them live in colder lands. It scared away animals at night. Its light stretched the day, giving hours for talking, making tools and telling stories.

It also changed food. [Cooking](cooking-science) softens meat and roots, kills many germs and makes food easier to digest. The scientist Richard Wrangham has argued that cooking gave early humans so much extra energy that it helped their brains grow larger. Others think brains began growing before regular fire use, so the idea is still debated.

People also used fire as a tool. They hardened the tips of wooden spears, and in South Africa, at least 160,000 years ago, people heated stone to make it easier to chip into sharp blades.

## Fire in stories and belief

Because fire mattered so much, many cultures tell stories about it. In [Greek myths](greek-myths), Prometheus steals fire from the gods and gives it to humans. Many [Native American stories](myths-native-american) tell of an animal, such as a spider or a coyote, carrying fire to people.

In India, fire is sacred in Hindu worship. The very first hymn of the Rigveda, one of the oldest texts in any Indian language, praises Agni, the god of fire. Today, fire still sits at the centre of Hindu weddings, Parsi fire temples and the oil lamps of [Diwali](diwali-lights).

## Matches arrive

For most of history, starting a fire was slow work, so people kept embers glowing or borrowed fire from neighbours. [Friction matches](made-matchsticks) were invented in England in 1826. Safety matches, which light only when struck on the box, followed in Sweden in the 1840s and 1850s. A trick that once took minutes of hard work now takes one flick of the wrist.
