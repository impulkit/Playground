## The short answer

Around 261 BCE, Ashoka's army conquered Kalinga, a kingdom roughly where [Odisha](odisha) is today. The war was terrible. Ashoka himself later wrote that 1,50,000 people were carried away, 1,00,000 were killed and many more died afterwards.

Seeing this, he felt deep sorrow. He turned to the teachings of the [Buddha](buddha) and decided to rule by *dhamma*, a word for right conduct: kindness, honesty and respect for all living things. Instead of conquering with swords, he said, he would win people over with good behaviour. We know this because he said so in his own words, carved on a rock.

## A confession in stone

Ashoka ordered messages called [edicts](?edict) to be carved on rocks and polished stone pillars across his empire. About thirty sites have been found, from Afghanistan to Karnataka.

Rock Edict XIII is the one about Kalinga. It is unusual: a king admitting that his victory caused grief. Strangely, this edict was left out in Kalinga itself. At Dhauli, near Bhubaneswar, the carvers placed special edicts instead, telling officials to treat the local people fairly. Perhaps it felt wrong to remind the Kalinga survivors of what had happened.

## Rules for a kinder kingdom

The edicts are practical as well as moral. Ashoka says he planted shade trees and dug wells along [roads](roads-built), and built rest houses for travellers. He arranged medical treatment for people and for animals, and grew healing herbs where they were missing.

He cut down on killing animals in the royal kitchen and banned some animal sacrifices. He asked people to respect parents, teachers and servants. He also asked followers of different faiths to speak well of each other's beliefs, a message that still sounds fresh today.

## Spreading the message

Ashoka sent messengers to kingdoms as far away as Greece and Egypt. Buddhist tradition says his son Mahinda carried Buddhism to [Sri Lanka](sri-lanka), where it is still the main religion.

He supported Buddhist monks and built many [stupas](?stupa), dome-shaped mounds holding relics. Buddhist stories later claimed he built 84,000 of them, a number meant to sound endless rather than to be counted. The great stupa at Sanchi in [Madhya Pradesh](madhya-pradesh) began in his reign. Most edicts are in Prakrit, a spoken language of the time, written in the Brahmi script. In the far northwest he used Greek and Aramaic so local people could read them.

## The lion and the wheel

At Sarnath, where the Buddha gave his first sermon, Ashoka raised a pillar topped by four lions facing four directions. The lions stand above a wheel, the symbol of dhamma.

When India became a republic in 1950, the Lion Capital became the national emblem. The wheel, called the Ashoka Chakra, sits in the middle of the [national flag](flags). A king who gave up war is now on every passport and coin.

## Forgotten, then found

After the Mauryas fell, people forgot how to read Brahmi. Ashoka became a hazy name in Buddhist legends. In 1837, James Prinsep, a British official at the Calcutta mint, worked out the script by comparing inscriptions and coins.

At first, the king in the edicts was a mystery called Piyadasi. Only in 1915 did a newly found edict name him as Ashoka. Historians still argue about how sincere he was. Some see a true change of heart. Others see a clever ruler using kind words to hold a vast empire together. Either way, few kings have left such a personal record.
