## The short answer

A caterpillar becomes a butterfly through a complete change of body called [metamorphosis](?metamorphosis). It happens in four stages: egg, caterpillar, pupa and adult.

The caterpillar's job is to eat and grow. When it is big enough, it hangs up and sheds its skin one last time, revealing a hard case called a chrysalis, or pupa. Inside, its body is taken apart. Special juices break down much of the caterpillar into a soupy mush.

Then groups of cells that have been waiting quietly since the caterpillar was in its egg spring into action. They use the mush as building material to make wings, long legs, big eyes and a coiled drinking tube. Days or weeks later, a butterfly climbs out.

## An eating machine

A caterpillar does almost nothing but eat. Some kinds grow to more than a thousand times their weight at hatching in just two or three weeks. If a human baby grew that fast, it would end up as heavy as an elephant.

Its skin can't stretch that much, so the caterpillar sheds it several times, wriggling out of the old skin with a new, looser one underneath. Each caterpillar can only eat certain plants. That is why the mother chooses so carefully where to lay her eggs.

Many caterpillars protect themselves with tricks. Some look like bird droppings. Others have fake eye spots, spines or bright colours that warn birds they taste bad.

## Inside the chrysalis

For a long time, people had no idea what happened inside a chrysalis. Now scientists can scan them and watch. Most of the caterpillar's [muscles](muscles) and gut dissolve. But small clusters of cells, called imaginal discs, survive. Each one grows into a particular part: one pair becomes the wings, others the legs, antennae or eyes.

Some parts, like the breathing tubes and parts of the [nervous system](nervous-system), are kept and rebuilt rather than destroyed. And some memories may survive too. In 2008, scientists in the USA trained moth caterpillars to avoid a certain smell. When the moths came out of their cases, many still avoided it.

## Wings made of scales

When the butterfly first climbs out, its wings are crumpled and soft. It pumps blood into the veins to stretch them out, then waits for them to dry and harden before it can fly.

Butterfly wings are covered in thousands of tiny overlapping scales, like roof tiles. That is the "dust" that rubs off on your fingers. Some colours come from pigments. Others, like the shimmering blue of some swallowtails, come from the way tiny ridges on the scales bounce light around. A butterfly's two wings are almost perfect mirror images, a good example of [symmetry](symmetry).

## India's butterflies

India has about 1,300 kinds of butterfly. In 2015, Maharashtra became the first state to choose a state butterfly, the blue Mormon, a big black swallowtail with glowing blue patches. Karnataka chose the Southern birdwing, one of India's largest butterflies, whose wings can stretch about as wide as an adult's hand.

After [the monsoon](monsoon), you may see groups of butterflies sipping at damp mud. This is called puddling. They are drinking salts and minerals that the males need. In the USA, the monarch butterfly makes one of the longest insect journeys on Earth, flying thousands of kilometres to Mexico, as told in [monarch migration](monarch-migration).

Moths go through the same change. Many moth caterpillars spin a silk case called a cocoon around themselves. One of them, the silkworm, spins the thread used to weave [silk](silk) sarees.
