## The short answer

Trees don't have a heart to pump water. Instead, water evaporates from tiny holes in the leaves, and that pulls the next bit of water up behind it, all the way from the roots, like a very long, thin straw. Redwoods are champions at this.

The tallest known tree, a coast redwood named Hyperion, is about 116 m tall, taller than a 30-storey building. That's about one and a half times the height of the Qutub Minar in Delhi, and taller than the [Statue of Liberty](statue-liberty) from the ground to the tip of her torch.

## Pulling water to the sky

Water moves up a tree through bundles of microscopic tubes in the wood. Water molecules cling tightly to each other, so a column of water behaves a bit like a chain. When one molecule evaporates from a leaf, it tugs on the one below, and that tugs on the next, all the way down to the roots.

There is a limit. The higher the water climbs, the harder [gravity](gravity-fall) pulls against it, and the more slowly it reaches the top. In 2004, a team led by George Koch climbed some of the tallest redwoods to study their highest leaves. Those leaves were small and thirsty. The scientists calculated that redwoods probably can't grow much taller than about 122 to 130 metres.

## Drinking fog

Coastal California is often foggy. Redwoods can soak up [fog water](fog-smog) directly through their needles. This helps the tops of the trees, where pulling water up from the ground is hardest.

Summer fog rolls in from the cold Pacific almost every day, especially near [San Francisco](san-francisco) and further north. Fog dripping off redwood needles can also soak the forest floor, watering ferns and young trees even when it hasn't rained for weeks.

## Finding Hyperion

In 2006, two naturalists, Chris Atkins and Michael Taylor, were hiking through a remote valley in Redwood [National Park](national-parks) when they spotted an unusually tall tree. A scientist later climbed it and dropped a tape measure from the top. It measured about 116 metres, making it the tallest known living tree.

The park has kept its location secret to protect it. When too many visitors went looking for it anyway, trampling the ground around its roots, the park closed the area. Today visitors who go there can be fined.

## Built to last

Redwood bark can be 30 cm thick and contains chemicals that resist fire and insects. Some redwoods are [more than 2,000 years old](trees-oldest).

[Forest fires](forest-fires) that kill other trees often leave redwoods scarred but alive. When a redwood is cut down or knocked over, new shoots sprout from its stump and roots. Over many years they grow into a circle of trees around the old one, which people call a "fairy ring".

## Saving the giants

In the 1800s and early 1900s, loggers cut most of the old redwood forests for timber. Today only a small fraction of the original old-growth forest is left, mostly in protected parks. Groups such as the Save the Redwoods League, founded in 1918, bought land to protect, and Redwood National Park was set up in 1968.

Redwoods have cousins. The giant sequoia, which grows inland in the Sierra Nevada, is shorter but much thicker, the biggest tree on Earth by volume. A third relative, the dawn redwood, was known only from [fossils](?fossil) until living trees were found in China in the 1940s.

## The widest tree

The tallest tree is in California. The tree that spreads over the most ground is in India: the [Great Banyan](banyan) near Kolkata, or Thimmamma Marrimanu in Andhra Pradesh, which holds the world record. One reaches up, the other reaches out.
