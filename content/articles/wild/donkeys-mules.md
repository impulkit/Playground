## The short answer

Donkeys can carry heavy loads over rough, dry ground, on little food and water, for hours. They are calm, sure-footed and tough. For thousands of years, that made them the most practical helpers a poor farmer, trader or potter could own.

Their wild ancestor, the African wild ass, lived in the deserts and stony hills of north-eastern Africa. Animals from such harsh places learn to cope with heat, thirst and thin grass. Donkeys kept those talents when people tamed them, which is why they still work in deserts and mountains across Asia, Africa and Latin America.

## Tamed in Africa

A big study of donkey DNA, published in 2022, found that donkeys were [domesticated](?domesticate) about 7,000 years ago in eastern Africa, around today's Kenya and the Horn of Africa. Early herders may have used them as the deserts there grew drier.

From Africa, donkeys spread to Egypt, West Asia and beyond. They carried goods on early trade routes long before [camels](camels) were tamed. A donkey caravan could cross country with no roads, stopping each night at wells it knew. Egyptian pharaohs had donkeys buried near their tombs. In the Bible, the Quran and Hindu stories, donkeys appear as ordinary working animals, carrying people, water and grain.

## Built for hard country

A donkey's hooves are small, hard and upright, good for stony slopes. Its big ears help it hear other donkeys across wide, empty land, and give off heat on hot days. Its loud bray carries a long way, useful when herds spread out to find food.

Donkeys also get much of the water they need from dry plants and can drink a lot quickly when they find it. Instead of running from danger as horses do, a donkey often freezes, studies the problem, and may stand its ground or kick. People called that stubbornness. On a narrow mountain path, it can save a life.

## Mules: the best of both

A mule is what you get when a male donkey and a female horse have a baby. Mules are often bigger and stronger than donkeys, but keep their steady temper and tough feet. From their mothers they get size and speed, much like [horses](horses). From their fathers they get patience, a careful way of placing each hoof, and the ability to work on less food. Horses have 64 [chromosomes](?chromosome), donkeys 62, and mules end up with 63. Because the numbers don't pair up neatly, mules are almost always unable to have foals.

Armies have used mules for centuries to carry supplies in mountains. The Indian Army still keeps mules for high Himalayan posts where trucks can't go. In 2024 it also took delivery of 100 [robotic "mules"](robot-dogs), four-legged machines that can carry loads and keep watch. In the USA, visitors have ridden mules down narrow trails into the Grand Canyon since the late 1800s. Mule trains still carry the post to Supai, a village of the Havasupai people deep in a side canyon that no road reaches.

## Fewer donkeys in India

Donkeys still carry bricks at kilns, sand from riverbeds and goods up hill paths. But as trucks and tractors spread, their numbers have fallen fast. India's 2019 livestock census counted about 1.2 lakh donkeys, less than half the number in 2012. Some breeds, such as the Halari donkey of Gujarat, are now rare. Its milk is sold for soap and skin creams, which has given some herders a new reason to keep them.

India also has a wild relative. The Indian wild ass, or *khur*, lives on the salt flats of the [Rann of Kutch](gujarat-rann) in Gujarat. It can run at about 50 km/h. In the 1960s only a few hundred were left. A 2024 count found 7,672, thanks to strict protection of its home.
