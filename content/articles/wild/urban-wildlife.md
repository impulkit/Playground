## The short answer

Cities are full of wild animals. Pigeons, crows, sparrows and [squirrels](squirrels) are easy to spot. Look harder and you'll find much more: bats under bridges, [falcons on skyscrapers](sky-peregrine), foxes and coyotes slinking through parks, and in a few places, even leopards and mountain lions.

Cities offer food, water, warm buildings to shelter in and, in many places, fewer large predators. Some animals manage well among people and traffic. Others struggle with roads, glass windows, bright lights and noise. Scientists study city wildlife to learn how animals adapt and how cities can share space with them.

## Leopards in Mumbai

Sanjay Gandhi National Park covers about 100 square kilometres of forested hills in the north of [Mumbai](mumbai), surrounded by tower blocks and busy roads. It is home to [dozens of leopards](leopards-cities), along with spotted deer, monkeys, pythons and more than 250 kinds of birds.

Leopards mostly hunt at night and sometimes wander into nearby housing colonies, where stray dogs are easy prey. Forest officials, scientists and local residents have worked together on campaigns teaching people how to stay safe, such as keeping rubbish cleared and walking in groups after dark.

## A mountain lion in Hollywood

Around 2012, a young mountain lion crossed two of the busiest freeways in [Los Angeles](los-angeles) and settled in Griffith Park, a large urban park near the Hollywood sign. Scientists called him P-22 and fitted him with a tracking collar. He lived there for about ten years, often caught on hidden cameras.

P-22 became a local celebrity. His story helped win support for a wildlife bridge over a busy freeway near Los Angeles, designed so mountain lions and other animals can cross safely. He died in 2022, old and injured, but his story changed how many people saw their city.

## Birds over the city

In Delhi, thousands of black [kites](kites-birds) circle in the sky. They feed on scraps from markets and rubbish, and in some neighbourhoods, people throw meat to them as a traditional act of charity. Brothers in Delhi who have spent years rescuing injured kites were the subject of an award-winning documentary in 2022.

Near Mumbai, tens of thousands of [flamingos](flamingos-pink) spend the winter on the mudflats of Thane Creek, feeding beside factories and flyovers. In New York and Chicago, peregrine falcons nest on tall buildings and bridges, diving on pigeons from high ledges as if from cliffs.

## Bats, coyotes and raccoons

In Austin, Texas, about 1.5 million Mexican free-tailed bats roost under the Congress Avenue Bridge in summer. At sunset they stream out in long ribbons to hunt insects, and crowds gather on the bridge to watch.

In Chicago, scientists have tracked coyotes that live among car parks and railways, mostly unseen. Raccoons in many North American cities open bins and even learn to open doors. In Indian towns, rhesus macaques and langurs leap across rooftops. Some animals are good at city life, and people are still learning how to live alongside them.

## Making room in the city

City planners now think about animals as well as people. Green roofs, tree-lined streets and parks joined by leafy strips give birds and insects places to feed and travel. Some cities dim their lights during bird migration seasons, because bright windows confuse birds flying at night. In Bengaluru and other Indian cities, volunteers have helped clean and restore old lakes, which bring back herons, kingfishers and dragonflies. Good [urban planning](urban-planning) can make a city better for its wild neighbours and its people at the same time.
