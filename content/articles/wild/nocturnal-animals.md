## The short answer

Night animals see in the dark by catching every scrap of light they can. Many have huge eyes with wide pupils that let in more light. The back of their eyes, the [retina](?retina), is packed with rod cells, which work well in dim light. And many have a mirror-like layer behind the retina that bounces light back for a second chance to be seen.

Even so, no animal can see in total darkness. Night animals also rely on other senses. Owls and foxes listen. Bats use [echoes](?echolocation). Some snakes sense the heat of a warm body. A night animal's world is built from many senses at once.

## Big eyes, wide pupils

The bigger the eye, the more light it can gather. The tarsier, a tiny primate from Southeast Asia, has eyes so large that each one is about as big as its brain. It can't move them, so like an [owl](owl-eyes) it turns its head instead.

India has its own big-eyed night specialist. The slender loris, a small, slow-moving primate found in southern India and Sri Lanka, creeps along branches at night hunting insects. Its round, staring eyes take up much of its face.

Pupils matter too. A cat's pupils close to narrow slits in sunlight to protect the eye, then open into wide circles at night. That lets them work well across a huge range of brightness.

## A mirror in the eye

Your [eyes](eyes-see) use two kinds of light-sensing cells: cones for colour and detail, and rods for dim light. Night animals have far more rods. Many also have the tapetum lucidum, a shiny layer behind the retina. Light that slips past the rods hits the mirror and bounces back through them again.

That is why the eyes of cats, dogs, deer, cows and crocodiles glow in headlights. The colour of the glow can be green, yellow, blue or red, depending on the animal. People have no tapetum, so our eyes don't shine back, though a camera flash can make them look red.

## Colour in moonlight

For a long time, scientists thought that seeing colour at night was impossible. In dim light, people see mostly in shades of grey. Then, in 2002, a team in Sweden tested elephant hawkmoths. The moths could pick out coloured flowers by starlight.

Later, the same group found that some geckos can also see colour in light too dim for human colour vision. Their eyes work differently from ours, with larger light-catching cells.

There is a trade-off. Eyes tuned for the dark are often less sharp in daylight, and many night animals see fewer colours than we do. Being active at night has rewards that make up for it: cooler air in hot places like the Thar Desert, fewer daytime hunters and less competition for food.

## When eyes are not enough

[Bats](bats-fly) hunt by sending out squeaks and listening for echoes. Pythons and pit vipers have small pits on their faces that sense [infrared](?infrared) heat from warm-blooded prey. Cats sweep their whiskers to feel gaps and objects. Kiwis in New Zealand sniff for worms through nostrils at the tip of their beaks.

Night animals live closer to people than you might think. [Leopards prowl](leopards-cities) the forest of Sanjay Gandhi National Park on the edge of [Mumbai](mumbai). In American cities, raccoons and opossums raid bins after dark. In many places, a [cat](cats-domestic) on a wall at midnight is the night animal you are most likely to meet.
