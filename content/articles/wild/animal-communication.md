## The short answer

Animals talk without words, using every sense they have. Birds sing and call, wolves howl, and frogs croak to attract mates. [Ants lay trails of scent](ants-world). [Cuttlefish](power-cuttlefish) flash colour patterns across their skin. Elephants rumble so low that the sound can travel through the ground. Bees dance.

Each message has a job: "danger", "food over here", "this is my patch", "come closer" or "stay away". Some signals are simple. Others carry surprisingly detailed information. Whether any animal has a true language like ours is still argued about, but animals clearly share more than scientists once thought.

## Dances and directions

From the 1920s to the 1940s, the Austrian scientist Karl von Frisch spent years watching honeybees in glass-sided hives. He noticed that a bee returning from good flowers would run in a figure-of-eight on the honeycomb, wiggling its body along the middle line.

He worked out that the angle of the wiggle showed the direction to fly compared with the Sun, and its length showed the distance. Other bees followed the dancer, picked up the scent of the flowers and set off. Many scientists at first doubted that an insect could pass on a map. Later experiments confirmed it, and in 1973 von Frisch shared the Nobel Prize. Young bees even seem to get better at [the dance](bees-dance) with practice, by following older dancers.

## Words for danger

In Kenya in the 1970s, the scientists Robert Seyfarth and Dorothy Cheney recorded vervet monkeys. They found the monkeys made different alarm calls for different predators. A leopard call sent monkeys up into trees. An eagle call made them look up and rush into bushes. A snake call made them stand up and peer at the ground. When the scientists played the recordings from a hidden speaker, the monkeys reacted the same way, even with no predator in sight.

In Indian forests, other animals eavesdrop. Langurs watch from the treetops and give loud alarm barks when they spot a tiger or leopard. [Chital deer](deer-india) below listen and run. In return, the deer's alarm calls warn [langurs](monkeys-langur) on the ground.

## Songs, rumbles and smells

Some messages travel far. [Humpback whales](whale-songs) sing long songs that can be heard many kilometres away, and all the males in one area sing nearly the same song, which slowly changes each year. Elephants make deep rumbles, some below what human ears can hear, that other elephants can pick up from a few kilometres away.

Other messages stay close. Ants mark a path to food with chemicals called pheromones, and each ant that walks it adds more. Cats rub their cheeks on furniture to leave their scent. Fireflies flash in patterns that tell other fireflies which kind they are.

## Is it language?

Human language lets us combine words in endless new ways, and talk about the past, the future and things far away. Bees come close when they describe a place out of sight. Some parrots and apes have learned to use words or symbols taught by people, and [parrots](parrots-talk) can copy speech very well.

Most scientists say no animal has yet shown everything human language does. Others think we have simply not understood animal signals well enough. People who use [sign language](sign-language) show that language doesn't need sound at all, which is one reason researchers keep watching gestures, faces and bodies in animals too.
