## The short answer

An animal is [endangered](?endangered) when so few are left that it could disappear for ever. Scientists at the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) keep a "Red List" that sorts species from *least concern* to *critically endangered* and finally *extinct*. More than 45,000 species on it are now threatened, from tigers and tortoises to frogs, sharks and trees.

There are a handful of big reasons. Forests and grasslands are cleared, so animals lose their homes, or [habitat](?habitat). Some are hunted or caught for trade. Pollution poisons rivers and food. Animals brought from elsewhere can eat or outcompete local ones. And a warming [climate](climate-change) is shifting where many creatures can survive.

## The vultures and the painkiller

In the 1980s, Indian cities and villages had tens of millions of vultures. They cleaned up dead cattle within hours. Then, through the 1990s, they began dropping dead. Within about a decade, more than 95% of some species were gone.

Scientists finally traced the cause to diclofenac, a painkiller given to sick cows. When vultures ate a treated cow that had died, the medicine destroyed their kidneys. With the vultures gone, carcasses lay rotting for days. Stray dogs and rats multiplied to feed on them, which brought new health worries for the people living nearby. India banned diclofenac for animals in 2006. Today [vultures](vultures-india) are being bred in centres in Haryana, Assam and elsewhere, and released slowly back into the wild.

## Birds of the dry grasslands

The great Indian bustard is a tall, heavy bird that walks the grasslands of Rajasthan and Gujarat. Fewer than about 150 remain, most in and around the Desert National Park near Jaisalmer. Grasslands look empty to many people, so they are often ploughed or covered with roads, [wind farms](wind-turbines) and solar parks.

Bustards also have poor forward vision and fly into overhead power lines. Courts and conservationists have pushed for lines in key areas to be buried underground or marked so the birds can see them. Eggs are now being hatched in a breeding centre to build a backup flock.

## Saved at the last minute

Some stories turn around. By 1987 only 27 California condors were left, so American scientists caught every last one. They bred them in zoos and began releasing them in 1992. Today there are over 500, more than half of them flying free in California, Arizona, Utah and Mexico.

The [bald eagle](bald-eagle), poisoned by the pesticide DDT, recovered after the chemical was banned in the USA in 1972. In India, one-horned [rhinos](rhino-kaziranga) in Kaziranga, Assam, climbed from perhaps a few dozen in the early 1900s to more than 2,500.

## Gone means gone

Extinction is natural. Most species that ever lived died out long before humans existed. The difference today is speed. Species are vanishing many times faster than they did over most of Earth's history.

[The passenger pigeon](lost-passenger-pigeon) shows how quickly it can happen. In the 1800s, flocks of billions crossed North America. Hunted for cheap meat and with their forests cut down, they crashed within a few decades. Martha, the last one, died at Cincinnati Zoo in 1914. Nobody had thought such a common bird could disappear. Its story helped push the USA to pass some of its first laws protecting wild birds. Today, Red List scientists hope to spot the next passenger pigeon while there is still time to act, and the list is how they keep watch.
