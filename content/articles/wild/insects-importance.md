## The short answer

We need insects because they keep the living world working. They pollinate many of the fruits, vegetables and nuts we eat. They break down dead plants, dead animals and dung, turning them back into [soil](soil-made). They are food for birds, frogs, fish, lizards and bats. Some even give us useful things such as honey, silk and lac.

Insects are also everywhere. About a million kinds have been named, more than all other animals put together, and there may be millions more still unknown. If [insects disappeared](what-if-no-insects), many plants and animals, and the people who depend on them, would be in deep trouble.

## Pollinators and food

Bees, butterflies, moths, flies and beetles carry pollen from flower to flower. That is how many plants make fruit and seeds. A major global report in 2016 found that more than three-quarters of the leading kinds of food crops benefit at least partly from animal [pollinators](pollinators), most of them insects.

Mangoes, apples, coffee, cashews, chillies, cucumbers and cocoa all rely on insect visits to some degree. Insects also make food directly. Honeybees turn flower nectar into [honey](honey), and in many countries [people eat insects](food-weird-world) themselves. The Food and Agriculture Organization estimates that about 2 billion people eat insects as part of their usual diet. In [Odisha](odisha), a tangy chutney made from red weaver ants is a traditional dish around Similipal. In 2024 it received a GI tag, a label that protects foods and crafts linked to one region.

## Nature's clean-up crew

Without insects, the world would be buried in waste. Termites and beetles break down fallen wood. Fly maggots and beetles clear away dead animals. [Dung beetles](power-dung-beetle) roll, bury and eat animal droppings, putting nutrients back into the ground.

Australia learned this the hard way. When settlers brought cattle, the native dung beetles, used to dry kangaroo droppings, could not cope with large, wet cow pats. Dung piled up on pastures and bush flies multiplied. From 1965, the Australian Dung Beetle Project brought in dozens of species from Africa and Europe. Many settled in, and pastures recovered.

## Silk, lac and more

India has a long history with useful insects. [Silk](silk) comes from the cocoons of silkworm caterpillars, and India makes several kinds, including mulberry, tussar, eri and golden muga silk from Assam.

Lac comes from tiny insects that live on trees such as kusum and palash. They give off a resin that people scrape off and turn into shellac, used for varnish, polish and traditional lac bangles. Jharkhand is one of India's biggest lac-producing states. Red dyes once came from insects too. Lac dye coloured cloth and leather in India for centuries, and cochineal, from a small bug living on cactus in Mexico, still colours some sweets and lipsticks today.

## Are insects in trouble?

In 2017, a study from German nature reserves reported that the weight of flying insects caught in traps had fallen by about three-quarters over 27 years. Other studies have found declines in some places and little change in others. Scientists agree that habitat loss, pesticides, [light pollution](night-sky-dark) and climate change all harm insects. They still debate how fast insects are declining worldwide, because careful long-term counts are rare.

Simple steps help: planting native flowers, leaving some wild corners and dead wood, and using fewer pesticides. Insects are small, but they make up a large share of animal life on land.
