## The short answer

[Some ants](ants-world) grow fungus, some herd insects, and some build homes with [living glue](glue-sticky).

Leafcutter ants have been farming for tens of millions of years. Humans began [farming](farming-began) only about 12,000 years ago. Ants were already around with the dinosaurs, and some are trapped in 99-million-year-old [amber](amber-fossils).

Farming ants don't plan the way people do. Each ant follows simple rules, set off by smells and touch. But a whole colony, working together, manages its crops as carefully as any farmer.

## Gardeners underground

In the [rainforests](rainforests) and dry scrub of Central and South America, and as far north as Texas and Louisiana in the USA, leafcutter ants march in long lines. Each worker carries a piece of leaf above her head like a green sail.

The leaves don't get eaten. Underground, smaller workers chew them into a pulp and spread it in chambers as compost for a special [fungus](?fungus). The fungus grows, and the ants eat its swollen tips. The ants weed their gardens, pull out unwanted moulds, and even carry bacteria on their bodies that make natural medicines against pests. A big colony can hold millions of ants, with fungus gardens spread through hundreds of chambers.

When a young queen leaves to start a new nest, she carries a tiny piece of fungus in a pouch in her mouth. It's the seed for her colony's first crop.

## Ant cowboys

Aphids are tiny insects that suck sap from plants. They squirt out a sugary liquid called honeydew. Many ants love it. Some ants stroke the aphids with their antennae to make them release a drop, a little like milking.

In return, the ants guard their "herds" from ladybirds and other hunters. Some carry aphids to fresh plants, or shelter their eggs in the nest over winter. Gardeners in India and the USA often notice that where there are lots of aphids, there are lots of ants. Some ants go further. Certain species in Asia carry mealybugs, another sap-sucking insect, with them when they move house, like herders driving cattle to new pasture. The ants and their "cattle" can depend on each other so completely that neither does well alone.

## India's weaver ants

In [mango](mango-tree), cashew and other trees across India, red weaver ants build nests by stitching leaves together. Workers line up along a leaf's edge and grab the next leaf with their jaws. When the gap is too wide, they hang onto each other to make [a living chain of bodies](power-ant-bridges) and pull the leaves close.

Then other workers bring larvae, the ants' young, and squeeze them gently. The larvae give out silk, and the workers dab it back and forth like a glue stick until the leaves are sewn tight. Weaver ants are fierce defenders and eat many insect pests. In parts of Asia, farmers have welcomed them into fruit orchards for centuries.

## Ants on the menu

In Odisha, Jharkhand and Chhattisgarh, some communities make a sour, spicy chutney from red weaver ants and their eggs. In 2024, the red ant chutney of Similipal in Odisha received a Geographical Indication tag.

Ants live alongside many other insect partners. Fig trees have their own [wasps](fig-wasps), and termites, which are more closely related to cockroaches than to ants, grow fungus gardens of their own inside their mounds. Farming has been invented at least three times by insects, long before people tried it.
