## The short answer

Rhythm is how sounds are arranged in time. Underneath most music runs a beat, a steady pulse like a clock ticking, the thing you tap your foot to. Rhythm is the pattern of long and short sounds, and silences, laid over that beat.

Think of a cricket crowd chanting. The beat is the steady thump of clapping hands. The rhythm is the pattern of the words on top: some quick, some stretched out, some landing between the claps. Speed matters too. Musicians call it tempo and measure it in beats per minute.

## Beats in groups

Beats usually come in groups. In much Western music, they are [counted in fours](maths-music-rhythm), "ONE two three four", with the first beat a little stronger. A waltz counts in threes: "ONE two three". Each group is called a bar.

Indian classical music uses longer cycles called *tala*. Teentaal has 16 beats in four groups of four. Musicians keep track by clapping on some beats and waving on others. The first beat, *sam*, is the home base. A drummer may play a long, twisting pattern that seems to get lost, then lands exactly on sam, and the audience cheers.

## Keeping time

In 1815, Johann Maelzel patented the metronome, a ticking machine with a swinging arm that could be set to any speed. [Beethoven](beethoven) was one of the first composers to mark metronome speeds on his music.

Today, musicians use metronome apps. A slow piece might be 60 beats per minute, one per second. A fast dance track might be 130 or more. Your resting heart beats at roughly 60 to 100 times a minute, and it speeds up when you run, as explained in [your heartbeat](heartbeat-exercise). Some people find music near a walking pace especially easy to move to.

## Off the beat

Some of the most exciting rhythms play against the beat instead of on it. This is called syncopation. A note lands just before or after where you expect it, which creates a little jolt of surprise.

[Jazz](jazz) is full of syncopation, and so are Latin American dance music, [bhangra](bhangra) and hip-hop. In West African drumming, several drummers often play different rhythms at the same time, such as groups of three against groups of two. This is called polyrhythm. Each drummer must hold their own part while the others pull in different directions.

## Rhythm you can speak

Indian musicians learn rhythm by speaking it. In South India, the art of *konnakol* turns rhythms into syllables like "ta ka di mi". Tabla players learn strokes as spoken *bols*, such as "dha dhin dhin dha", before they touch the drum, as described in [the tabla](tabla).

Speaking a rhythm helps [the brain](brain-works) remember it, the same way a rhyme helps you remember a poem. Rappers do something similar, packing words so their syllables land on and between the beats.

## A dancing cockatoo

For a long time, scientists thought only humans could move in time with music. Then, in 2009, a team in the USA studied a cockatoo named Snowball. They played him the same song at different speeds. Often, he sped up or slowed down his head-bobbing and foot-lifting to match.

Other studies have found that parrots, and a sea lion named Ronan, can keep a beat. All of them are good at learning new sounds or movements. Dogs and cats, for all their charms, have not yet shown the same skill in careful tests.
