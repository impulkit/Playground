## The short answer

The Statue of Liberty is covered in copper, the same reddish-brown metal used in electric wires. When copper sits in air and rain for years, it slowly reacts with water, oxygen and other gases. A thin green crust called a [patina](?patina) forms on top.

The statue arrived in New York shiny and brown, like a new coin, and was dedicated in 1886. Within a few decades, it had turned completely green. The colour you see in photos today is the work of chemistry and weather, not paint.

## A protective skin

The patina isn't damage. Scientists who studied it found it is made mostly of minerals containing copper, sulfur and oxygen, formed partly from sulfur in the polluted air of a big city. It clings tightly to the metal and slows further change, a bit like a raincoat.

[Iron behaves differently](mystery-iron-pillar). When iron [rusts](rust), the flaky rust falls away and exposes fresh metal, so rust keeps eating deeper. Copper's green coat stays put. In the early 1900s, when the US government discussed painting the statue, [newspapers](newspapers) and members of the public objected. The green stayed, and it has become part of how people everywhere picture the statue.

Inside, visitors can climb a narrow spiral staircase of 162 steps from the top of the pedestal to the crown, which has 25 windows. The torch has been closed to visitors since 1916, after an explosion at a nearby store of wartime ammunition damaged the arm.

## A gift from France

The statue's official name is *Liberty Enlightening the World*. French thinkers proposed it to celebrate liberty and the friendship between France and the United States, which had won independence with French help during the [American Revolution](american-revolution). The sculptor Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi designed it. Gustave Eiffel, who later built the [Eiffel Tower](eiffel-tower), designed the iron skeleton that holds the copper skin up.

The statue was built in [Paris](paris), taken apart and shipped across the Atlantic in 1885, in 350 pieces packed into more than 200 crates. In her left hand, Liberty holds a tablet marked with the date July 4, 1776, in [Roman numerals](roman-numerals). A broken chain lies at her feet.

## Built with small change

France paid for the statue, but Americans had to pay for the pedestal, the tall stone base. Money ran short. In 1885, the newspaper publisher Joseph Pulitzer promised to print the name of every donor in his paper, the *New York World*, however small the gift. More than 120,000 people gave, many of them less than a dollar, and the pedestal was finished.

In 1903, a plaque with a poem by Emma Lazarus, "The New Colossus", was placed inside the pedestal. Its lines about welcoming the "huddled masses" tied the statue to the millions of [immigrants](immigration-stories) who sailed past it on their way to nearby [Ellis Island](ellis-island).

## Colours from chemistry

Artists have long used copper's colours on purpose. Copper minerals such as malachite gave old painters a rich green, and azurite gave them blue. The painters of [Ajanta](ajanta-colours) ground minerals from the earth for their paints in much the same way.

Copper roofs on old churches and government buildings around the world turn green too. On the statue, the change took decades. A cheap copper ring can do it far faster, leaving a faint green mark on a finger within days: a tiny patina of your own.
