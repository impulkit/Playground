## The short answer

A kolam is a pattern drawn at the doorstep, usually with rice flour, every morning across Tamil Nadu and much of South India. Many kolams start with a grid of dots, called *pulli*. The artist then draws lines around and between the dots, often as one unbroken line that loops back to where it began.

Hidden in these patterns is a lot of maths. Most kolams have [symmetry](symmetry): turn them a quarter turn, or flip them like a mirror image, and they look the same. Some can be built from a few simple rules repeated over and over, the way a computer program builds a picture.

That's why mathematicians and computer scientists have studied kolams for more than 50 years.

## Many names, one habit

Different parts of India have their own floor art. In Tamil Nadu it's kolam, in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana muggu, in Bengal alpana and in Rajasthan mandana. [Rangoli](rangoli-art) is the name used in Maharashtra, Gujarat and much of the north, often with bright coloured powders at festivals such as [Diwali](diwali-lights). In [Kerala](kerala), during Onam, families make the *pookalam*, a circle of fresh flower petals.

The daily kolam is usually white and drawn fresh at dawn, after the doorstep is swept and sprinkled with water. During the Tamil month of Margazhi, from mid-December to mid-January, kolams grow huge and competitive, sometimes filling whole streets. At [Pongal](onam-pongal), they often show pots of boiling rice, sugarcane and the sun.

## The maths of dots and loops

Take a square grid of dots and try to draw one line that weaves around every dot and ends where it started. This kind of kolam, called a *sikku* or *kambi* kolam, is a real puzzle. Experienced artists draw them quickly from memory, sometimes with dozens of dots.

[Mathematicians describe these patterns](maths-art) using symmetry and graph theory, the maths of points and the lines joining them. Many kolams match themselves four times in one full turn, like a plus sign. Others have mirror lines running across them. Some grow outward in repeated loops that look a little like [fractals](fractals), patterns built from smaller copies of themselves.

## Kolams and computers

In the 1970s, Gift Siromoney, a mathematician at Madras Christian College in [Chennai](chennai), began studying kolams with his colleagues. They showed that kolam patterns could be described with "array grammars": sets of rules a computer can follow to build a picture step by step. A few simple rules can produce surprisingly complicated designs.

Later, the American mathematician Marcia Ascher wrote about the kolam as a living tradition of mathematical thinking. Computer scientists have since written programs that draw kolams, and designers borrow them for fabrics and floor [tiles](tessellations).

## Flour for the ants

Traditional kolams are drawn with rice flour, and that choice is no accident. By midday, ants, birds and other small creatures have often eaten parts of the pattern. Many families see this as a small daily act of feeding other living things.

The kolam is also meant to be temporary. Feet, wind and rain wipe it away, and the next morning it is drawn again. Children often learn by watching mothers and grandmothers, practising until their fingers can let the flour fall in a thin, steady line. Today people of all ages draw them, from village lanes in Thanjavur to apartment doorways in New Jersey.
