## The short answer

For more than two thousand years, Indian cloth was some of the most wanted in the world. Indian weavers made cotton so fine it floated like mist, and dyers made colours that stayed bright after washing. Traders carried Indian textiles to Rome, Egypt, China, Southeast Asia, Africa and Europe.

Indian cotton changed what the world wore. Even the English language is full of words for cloth that came from India. The story also has a hard side. When British factories began making cheap cloth with machines, India's handweavers suffered badly, and that became a big part of [India's freedom struggle](freedom-struggle).

## Cotton from the Indus

India is one of the first places where people grew cotton and spun it into thread. At Mohenjo-daro, one of the great [Indus Valley cities](indus-cities), archaeologists found a scrap of cotton cloth over 4,000 years old. It had been dyed red with madder, a plant root.

Cotton is tricky to spin, because its fibres are short. Indian spinners learned to twist them into long, strong threads by hand. Weavers then turned that thread into cloth on wooden [looms](?loom).

## Cloth for emperors

The finest Indian cloth was muslin, especially from around Dhaka, in what is now [Bangladesh](bangladesh). The best muslins were so thin and light that they seemed almost see-through. Some had poetic names that meant "running water" or "evening dew".

Ancient Romans bought so much Indian cloth, [spice](cardamom-pepper) and jewellery that the writer Pliny the Elder complained, nearly 2,000 years ago, that Rome's gold was flowing east to pay for it. Much later, Mughal emperors and their courts wore Indian silks and muslins, and gave them as precious gifts.

## Colours that didn't fade

Indian dyers were masters of colour. They used plants like indigo for blue, madder for red and [turmeric](turmeric) or pomegranate rind for yellow. The secret was the [mordant](?mordant), a substance that helps dye bond to cotton. With mordants, Indian colours stayed bright after many washes.

Indigo was so linked with India that its name comes from a Greek word meaning "Indian". When Europeans saw [printed and painted Indian cottons](kalamkari) in the 1600s, they went wild for them. They called them calico, after the [port of Calicut](sea-ports) (Kozhikode), and chintz, from the Hindi word *chhint*, meaning spotted or sprinkled.

## Too popular for Britain

The [East India Company](east-india-company) shipped huge amounts of Indian cotton to Britain. Soon everyone wanted calico curtains, dresses and bedspreads. British wool and silk weavers were furious. In 1700 and 1721, Parliament passed laws that banned importing or wearing most printed Indian cottons.

British inventors then built machines to spin and weave cotton cheaply, a key part of the [Industrial Revolution](industrial-revolution). Raw cotton was shipped from India to British mills, and machine-made cloth was sold back to India.

## What happened to India's weavers

Machine-made cloth was cheaper than handwoven cloth. Under British rule, Indian weavers could not compete, and many lost their living. Towns that had been famous for weaving shrank.

In the early 1900s, leaders of the freedom movement urged Indians to buy Indian cloth instead of British. [Gandhi](gandhi) went further, spinning his own thread and asking everyone to wear handmade khadi. Today India's [handloom weavers](knitting-weaving) still make famous fabrics, from [Banarasi silk](art-banarasi) to [Pochampally ikat](art-ikat), and many of them have legal protection for their names.
