## The short answer

When a song gives you chills, your brain is doing two things at once. It is predicting what comes next, and it is rewarding you when the music delivers.

Brain scans show that the brain releases [dopamine](?dopamine), a chemical linked to pleasure and wanting, both while you wait for a favourite moment and when it arrives. That rush can set off the same reflex that makes your skin prickle in the cold. Tiny [muscles](muscles) at the base of each hair tighten, and the hairs stand up.

Not everyone gets [music chills](body-music-chills). Many people do, and some never have. Scientists are still finding out why.

## The science of a shiver

Scientists call these musical chills "frisson", a French word for a shiver. In a well-known study published in 2011, researchers at McGill University in Montreal asked people to bring their own favourite pieces of music. Then they played the music while scanning the listeners' brains.

They found dopamine released in two different places. One area became active in the seconds before a chill, as the listener sensed a big moment coming. Another became active at the peak itself. In other words, the waiting was part of the pleasure. The same reward system in the [brain](brain-works) helps us enjoy food and other treats, which may be why a great song can feel almost delicious.

## What sets them off

Chills tend to come at surprising or powerful moments. A singer jumps to a high note. A quiet passage suddenly bursts into a [full orchestra](orchestra). A new instrument enters, or the harmony shifts in an unexpected direction. In a cricket stadium, it might be thousands of people singing the same line together.

Film composers know this well. A soundtrack often swells just as the hero wins, and in a dark cinema hall you can almost feel the audience shiver. In Indian classical music, a long, slow build in a [raga](raga-time) can end in a fast, dazzling passage that makes listeners gasp and call out "wah".

## Why some people and not others

Researchers have found that people who often get chills tend to score high on a personality trait called "openness to experience". They enjoy new ideas, art and daydreaming. A small brain-scan study in 2016 suggested that people who get chills may have stronger connections between the parts of the brain that process sound and those that handle feelings.

Culture and memory matter too. A song tied to a wedding, a festival or a [Diwali](diwali-lights) night can move you more than a song you hear for the first time. So the same piece might give one person goosebumps and leave the friend beside them yawning.

## An old reflex, borrowed

Goosebumps are left over from our furry ancestors. When a cat is frightened, its fur puffs up to make it look bigger. When a bird is cold, it fluffs its feathers to trap warm air. Humans have too little hair for either trick to work, but the reflex survived.

Why music should borrow it is still debated. One idea is that powerful music stirs ancient alarm and reward systems in the brain, the ones that once reacted to danger or to a parent's call. Whatever the reason, [sound waves](sound-travel) arriving at your ears can make the skin on your arms react. That is a long way for a tremble in the air to travel.
