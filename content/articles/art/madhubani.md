## The short answer

Madhubani painting is a folk art from the Mithila region of [northern Bihar](bihar-history) and southern [Nepal](nepal). It is named after the district of Madhubani, one of its main centres. The paintings are full of bold outlines, bright colours and patterns that fill every bit of space.

For centuries, women painted these pictures on the freshly plastered mud walls and floors of their homes. They painted for festivals, for prayers and especially for [weddings](life-weddings-world). The pictures show gods and goddesses, scenes from the epics, and symbols of nature such as fish, peacocks, the sun, the moon and trees.

## Painting for a wedding

One of the most important places for Madhubani painting was the *kohbar ghar*, the room where a newly married couple would stay. Women covered its walls with paintings full of symbols of love, long life and children.

Fish stood for good luck and fertility. Parrots and peacocks stood for love. [Bamboo](bamboo) and [lotus](lotus) plants, painted together, stood for the couple. Mothers taught daughters, and each family had its own favourite designs. Many paintings show stories from the [Ramayana](ramayana). Sita, the heroine, is said to have come from Mithila, and her wedding to Rama is a favourite scene.

## Colours from the kitchen and garden

Traditionally, painters made their own colours. Black came from soot or burnt rice. Yellow came from [turmeric](turmeric) or pollen. Red came from flowers or a red powder, and white from rice paste. Green came from leaves.

Instead of [bought brushes](art-materials), painters used twigs, bamboo sticks, cotton wrapped on sticks and even fingers. Today, many artists use pens, paper and bought paints, but they keep the old style: a strong double outline, and no empty space left blank.

## Found after an earthquake

In January 1934, a powerful [earthquake](earthquakes) struck Bihar and Nepal. A British official named William G. Archer was sent to inspect the damage around Madhubani. In the broken houses, he saw that the inner walls were covered with paintings.

Archer photographed them and, in 1949, wrote an article about them in the Indian art magazine *Marg*. For the first time, people outside Mithila began to take notice.

## From walls to paper

In the mid-1960s, a severe [drought](droughts) hit Bihar. Crops failed and families went hungry. The All India Handicrafts Board sent the artist Bhaskar Kulkarni to Mithila to encourage women to paint on paper, so their work could be sold.

It worked. Women who had only ever painted at home began selling their paintings across India and abroad. Some became famous, and painters began to sign their work and sell it in city exhibitions. In 1975, Jagdamba Devi became the first Madhubani artist to receive the Padma Shri, one of India's top civilian honours. Sita Devi and Ganga Devi also became well known.

## Many styles, one tradition

Madhubani is often divided into styles, such as *bharni*, which fills shapes with solid colour, and *kachni*, which uses fine lines. Artists from different communities added their own styles, such as *godna*, inspired by tattoo designs.

In 2007, Madhubani painting received a Geographical Indication tag, which protects its name as an art from that region. Today, you can find it on saris, bags and even the walls of Madhubani railway station. It belongs to a family of Indian folk arts, alongside [Warli painting](warli) from Maharashtra.
