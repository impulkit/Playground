## The short answer

Kuchipudi and Mohiniyattam are two of India's officially recognised classical dances, both from the south. Kuchipudi comes from Andhra Pradesh and is quick, bouncy and dramatic, mixing fast footwork with acting. Mohiniyattam comes from [Kerala](kerala) and is slow, gentle and swaying, danced traditionally by women.

Both tell stories from Hindu mythology through hand gestures and facial expressions, like other classical dances. Watching them one after the other is a good way to see how differently two neighbouring regions can express similar stories. Kuchipudi feels like lively theatre. Mohiniyattam feels like the sway of palm trees and the roll of the sea.

## A dance named after a village

Kuchipudi takes its name from a village in the Krishna district of Andhra Pradesh. Tradition says that a saint named Siddhendra Yogi created a dance-drama called *Bhama Kalapam* there, about Satyabhama, a wife of Krishna, and taught it to the young men of the village. Historians are not sure exactly when he lived.

For centuries, troupes of men from a few Brahmin families of Kuchipudi travelled from village to village, performing long dance-dramas. Men played the female roles too. The songs were in Telugu, the language of the region, and the actors sometimes spoke lines as well as dancing.

## From drama to solo stage

In the 20th century, gurus such as Vempati Chinna Satyam reshaped Kuchipudi for the concert stage. They created solo pieces, and women began to learn and perform it. Today most Kuchipudi dancers are women. Many Telugu families in the United States send their children to Kuchipudi classes, so the dance now has students from [Hyderabad](hyderabad) to Texas.

The best-known showpiece is the *tarangam*. The dancer stands on the raised rim of a brass plate and moves it across the floor in [rhythm](rhythm-beat) with their feet. Sometimes they also balance a small pot of water on their head. It needs balance, strength and perfect timing.

## The enchantress of Kerala

Mohiniyattam means "dance of the enchantress". In Hindu stories, the god Vishnu took the form of Mohini, a beautiful woman, to trick the demons out of the nectar of immortality. The dance tries to capture her soft grace.

Mohiniyattam's movements are slow and circular. The upper body sways from side to side in gentle curves, like palm fronds in a breeze or waves rolling onto a beach. The footwork is light and smooth, never stamping. Dancers wear a white and gold *kasavu* sari, the traditional handloom of Kerala, with their hair tied in a bun on the left side of the head and wrapped with jasmine flowers.

## Saved from fading away

Mohiniyattam was supported by the [kings of Travancore](princely-states). Maharaja Swathi Thirunal, a 19th-century ruler and composer, wrote many songs that are still danced today. But by the early 20th century, the dance had lost respect, and few people learned it.

The poet Vallathol Narayana Menon, who founded the Kerala Kalamandalam school in 1930, helped revive it there along with [Kathakali](kathakali). Dancers such as Kalyanikutty Amma studied with older performers and rebuilt the dance for new students.

## Two sisters of Bharatanatyam

Kuchipudi and Mohiniyattam both share roots with [Bharatanatyam](bharatanatyam), the classical dance of Tamil Nadu. All three use similar hand gestures, and some of the same songs and rhythms. A trained eye can still tell them apart within seconds. Bharatanatyam is crisp and angular. Kuchipudi is quick and playful. Mohiniyattam flows like water.
