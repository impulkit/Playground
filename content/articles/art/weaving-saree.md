## The short answer

A saree is woven on a [loom](?loom), a frame that holds hundreds or thousands of threads tight. The long threads that run the length of the saree are called the warp. The weaver passes another thread, the weft, across them, going over some warp threads and under others. Row after row, the cloth grows.

To make patterns, the weaver lifts different warp threads for each row, or adds extra coloured or golden threads in certain spots. A plain [cotton saree](clothes-cotton) can be woven in a day or two. A richly patterned silk wedding saree can take a team of weavers weeks or months.

## Warp and weft

Before weaving starts, the warp has to be set up. Thousands of threads, each perhaps 6 to 9 metres long, are stretched in perfect order and wound onto the loom. This alone can take days.

On a [traditional pit loom](knitting-weaving), the weaver sits on the edge of a shallow pit, with foot pedals below. Pressing a pedal lifts some warp threads, making a gap called a shed. The weaver throws a shuttle carrying the weft thread through the gap, then pulls a comb-like beater forward to pack it tight. Then the next pedal, the next throw, the next beat, thousands of times.

## Patterns from punched cards

In 1804, a French weaver named Joseph-Marie Jacquard invented a loom attachment that used a chain of cards with holes punched in them. Each card controlled which threads lifted for one row. Change the cards and you changed the pattern.

Many Indian handlooms still use Jacquard-style card systems for complex designs. The idea also travelled into science. The English inventor [Charles Babbage](charles-babbage) planned to use punched cards to give instructions to his calculating machine. A hole or no hole is like a yes or a no, which is the basic idea behind [binary code](binary-code).

## Gold threads of Banaras

In [Varanasi](varanasi), weavers make [Banarasi sarees](art-banarasi) with patterns of flowers, vines and paisleys in *[zari](art-zardozi)*, thread wrapped in thin metal that looks gold or silver. These designs are woven in as extra weft threads, so they are part of the cloth itself, not embroidered on top.

A heavy Banarasi brocade can take a few weeks to several months. Families pass designs and skills from parent to child, and many sarees go to brides as part of a [wedding](weddings-india).

## Kanchipuram and Patola

In Kanchipuram, Tamil Nadu, weavers make thick, shining [silk](silk) sarees with wide borders in contrasting colours. Using a method called *korvai*, the border and body are woven separately and interlocked by hand, so the join is very strong. Tradition says a good Kanchipuram saree lasts for generations.

In Patan, Gujarat, a few families weave Patola sarees by [double ikat](art-ikat). Each thread of both warp and weft is tied and dyed in sections before weaving, so the pattern only appears as the threads cross. A tiny mistake in dyeing shows up as a blur in the cloth.

## Handloom and powerloom

Today many sarees are made on powerlooms, machine looms that work much faster and cost less. They can copy handloom designs closely.

Handloom weavers find it hard to compete, and many young people from weaving families now choose other work. To support them, India marks National Handloom Day every 7 August. The date recalls the Swadeshi movement launched on that day in 1905, when Indians were urged to buy Indian-made cloth. Some sarees now carry a Handloom Mark label so buyers can tell the difference.
