## The short answer

Most of the sounds in a film aren't recorded while the cameras roll. They're added later by sound designers and Foley artists. Sound designers invent big sounds that don't exist in real life, like spaceship engines or dinosaur roars, often by mixing several real sounds together. Foley artists perform small everyday sounds, like footsteps, rustling clothes and doors, while watching the film on a screen.

Many of these sounds come from unlikely places. A punch might be a fist hitting a cabbage. Breaking bones are often snapped celery. Bird wings are a pair of leather gloves flapped in the air.

## Why films need fake sounds

A film set is full of noise: generators, crew whispering, traffic outside. [Microphones](microphones) are aimed at actors' mouths, so they catch voices well but quieter sounds badly. And some things can't be recorded at all. Nobody has ever heard a real dragon, a laser blast or a starship.

So after filming, the picture goes to a team who rebuild the sound, layer by layer. A single action scene can hold hundreds of separate sounds.

## The art of Foley

[Foley](?foley) is named after Jack Foley, who worked at Universal Studios in Hollywood from the late 1920s, when films first got sound. He found it was easier to act out sounds while watching the film than to record them on set.

Foley studios look like junk shops. They have patches of floor made of gravel, wood, tiles and grass, plus shelves of shoes, chains, keys and old phones. Artists walk on the spot in the right shoes, matching every step of the actor on screen. A good Foley artist can make you believe someone is walking on ice, in high heels, while carrying shopping.

## Building a monster

For the 1993 film *Jurassic Park*, sound designer Gary Rydstrom built the T. rex roar from a baby elephant's scream, a tiger and an alligator, slowed down and mixed. Nobody knows what a real T. rex sounded like. Some scientists think big [dinosaurs](dino-sounds) may have made deep booming calls with their mouths closed, more like a pigeon's coo than a roar.

For *Star Wars* in 1977, Ben Burtt made the lightsaber hum from the idling motor of old cinema projectors. He added a buzz caught by accident when his microphone passed near a television set. Chewbacca's voice mixed recordings of bears, a walrus and other animals.

## Cartoons and games

Cartoons need sounds that are bigger than life: a boing when a character bounces, a slide whistle when someone falls off a cliff. Early animation studios built their own libraries of these effects, and many are still used.

Video games need thousands of sounds that change as you play. A single footstep might be recorded on dozens of surfaces, so the game can pick the right one whether your character is walking on grass, metal, wood or snow.

## The scream that won't stop

One famous scream was recorded for a 1951 American film called *Distant Drums*. It ended up in a sound library and has since been used in hundreds of films and video games, often as a hidden joke among sound editors. It's called the Wilhelm scream, after a character named Private Wilhelm in a 1953 film who let it out after being hit by an arrow.

Sound designers also have to decide when to break the rules. Real space is silent, because there's no air to carry sound to your [ears](ears-hear). But a spaceship battle with no noise feels wrong to most audiences, so films add explosions anyway.
