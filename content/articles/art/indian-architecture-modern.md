## The short answer

After independence in 1947, India's leaders wanted buildings that looked forward, not back to the palaces of kings or the offices of the British. Prime Minister [Nehru](nehru) invited the Swiss-French architect Le Corbusier to design [Chandigarh](chandigarh), a brand-new city. The American Louis Kahn designed the Indian Institute of Management in [Ahmedabad](ahmedabad).

[Indian architects](jobs-architect) learned from these masters and then went their own way. Balkrishna Doshi, Charles Correa, Raj Rewal and the British-born Laurie Baker designed buildings suited to India's heat, monsoons and budgets. Their ideas still shape how architects in India think.

## The concrete giants

Le Corbusier spent much of the 1950s working in India. In Chandigarh he designed the Capitol Complex: the High Court, the Assembly and the Secretariat, built in rough concrete with huge sunshades. In Ahmedabad he designed the Mill Owners' Association building and the Sanskar Kendra museum. Seventeen of his works around the world, including the Capitol Complex, were made a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2016.

Louis Kahn's IIM Ahmedabad campus, begun in the 1960s, is built of red brick with giant round and arched openings. The openings let in light but keep out the direct sun, so the long corridors stay shady even in a Gujarat summer.

## Doshi and Correa

Balkrishna Doshi worked for Le Corbusier in Paris and helped on his Indian projects. Back in India, he designed IIM Bangalore and his own studio in Ahmedabad, Sangath, with curved vaults half sunk into the ground to stay cool. His Aranya housing in Indore gave [low-income families](slums-housing) a basic plot and core house that they could extend as they earned more. He won the Pritzker Prize in 2018.

Charles Correa designed the Gandhi Smarak Sangrahalaya at [Gandhi](gandhi)'s Sabarmati Ashram in 1963. It is a cluster of small tiled rooms around open courts and a pool, gentle and modest like the man it remembers. He also designed the Jawahar Kala Kendra in [Jaipur](jaipur) and helped plan Navi Mumbai.

## The brick man of Kerala

Laurie Baker was born in England and came to India in 1945. He settled in [Kerala](kerala) and became an Indian citizen. He believed buildings should be cheap, cool and made from local materials.

Baker used bare brick walls, often with a rat-trap bond that leaves hollow spaces inside to save bricks. He built *jaali* walls full of holes so breezes could flow through. His Centre for Development Studies campus in Thiruvananthapuram is full of these ideas. Many architects today, worried about energy and climate, look back at his work with new interest.

Baker liked to say that a building should grow from its site, using what is close by. He visited each site, studied the slope and the trees, and often changed the design as the walls went up. Some of his houses curve around old trees rather than cutting them down.

## New shapes on the skyline

Later buildings added new forms. Raj Rewal's Hall of Nations in Delhi, opened in 1972, was a giant concrete space frame. It was demolished in 2017, despite protests from architects worldwide. The Lotus Temple in Delhi, designed by the Iranian-born architect Fariborz Sahba and opened in 1986, is shaped like a white [lotus](lotus) flower with 27 marble-clad petals.

Today, Indian architects work on everything from metro stations to green office towers. Many still borrow from old ideas such as courtyards, verandas and thick walls, which kept homes cool long before air conditioning existed.
