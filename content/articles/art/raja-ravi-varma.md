## The short answer

Raja Ravi Varma (1848–1906) was a painter from Kerala who changed how Indians pictured their gods, goddesses and heroes. He used oil paint and the realistic style of European art, but he painted Indian stories, Indian faces and Indian clothes.

His paintings of scenes from the [Mahabharata](mahabharata) and [Ramayana](ramayana) made gods look like real people you might meet. Then he did something unusual for an artist. He opened a printing press and sold cheap copies of his pictures. They spread to homes, shops and [calendars](calendars) across India, and many images of gods that families pray to today follow his style.

## A boy who drew on palace walls

Ravi Varma was born in Kilimanoor, a small town in the kingdom of Travancore, in what is now [Kerala](kerala). His family was connected to the royal family. As a child he is said to have drawn on walls with charcoal, and an uncle who was a painter encouraged him.

At the Travancore palace he saw European paintings and watched a visiting British portrait painter work in oils. Oil paint was rare in India then. Ravi Varma had little formal training, so he taught himself to mix colours and use the medium, through years of practice.

## Painting stories people knew

Ravi Varma became famous for portraits of royal families. His real fame, though, came from scenes of old stories: Shakuntala writing a love letter on a [lotus](lotus) leaf, Damayanti talking to a swan, Arjuna and Subhadra. One of his paintings won a prize at an international exhibition in Vienna in 1873.

He travelled across India looking at clothes, jewellery and faces to make his pictures feel real. He often dressed his women in a sari draped in a style common in Maharashtra, and some historians think his pictures helped make that drape popular across the country.

## Gods for every home

In 1894 Ravi Varma opened a press near Bombay to print [lithographs](?lithograph) of his paintings. In his day, temple images were mostly carved statues inside temples, and fine paintings belonged to kings. Suddenly, a family could buy a colourful print of Lakshmi, Saraswati or Krishna for a small price.

The prints sold in huge numbers. They decorated homes, shops and later calendars and matchboxes. Like the [printing press](printing-press) that spread books in Europe, his press spread pictures, and his way of showing the gods became the way millions imagined them.

## Praise and criticism

Ravi Varma was hugely popular in his lifetime and is often called the father of modern Indian art. Still, not everyone admired him. Some later artists and critics felt he copied European styles too closely, making Indian gods look like figures from Western theatre. They wanted an art that grew from Indian traditions such as Mughal painting or temple murals.

His pictures have also been criticised for showing only one idea of beauty, mostly fair-skinned and upper-class. Supporters reply that he made art available to ordinary people for the first time, which few painters before him had done.

## His pictures on screen

Ravi Varma died in 1906. His influence did not. The look of early Indian films, with gods in glittering crowns and painted backdrops, borrowed from his prints. Many of India's early [film-makers](bollywood) grew up surrounded by his pictures. More than a century later, his images still appear on posters and in TV serials about the epics, and the government named an award for artists after him in Kerala.
