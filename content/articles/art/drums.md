## The short answer

Drums are found almost everywhere because they are simple to make and powerful to hear. Stretch an animal skin over a hollow log, a clay pot or a gourd, and strike it. You have a drum. People in every continent worked this out on their own.

Drums also do jobs that matter to people. They keep a group moving together, whether soldiers marching, farmers working or dancers at a wedding. Their low, loud sound travels a long way, so they can send signals. And a steady beat seems to tug at the human body, making us want to tap our feet.

## How a drum makes sound

When you hit a drumskin, it bends in and springs back, over and over, very fast. Each push squeezes the air, sending out waves of sound, as you can read in [how sound travels](sound-travel). A big, loose skin vibrates slowly and booms. A small, tight one vibrates fast and sounds higher.

Most drums vibrate in a jumble of different patterns at once, so they sound like a thump rather than a clear note. Some drums, like the Indian [tabla](tabla), use clever tricks to make the vibrations line up into a ringing musical note.

## Drums that talk

In West Africa, especially among the [Yoruba people of Nigeria](nigeria), a talking drum is shaped like an hourglass, with cords running between two skins. The player holds it under one arm and squeezes. Tighter cords mean a higher note.

Yoruba is a tonal language: the same sounds mean different things when spoken high or low. By copying the tones of words, a skilled drummer can "say" names, [greetings](life-greetings) and praise poems that listeners understand. This is different from [Morse code](codes-morse), which turns each letter into dots and dashes.

## India's many drums

India has hundreds of kinds of drums. The *dhol*, a big barrel drum played with sticks, powers [bhangra](bhangra) dancing in Punjab and wedding processions everywhere. In Kerala, rows of *chenda* drummers play at [temple festivals](india-wonder-thrissur-pooram), loud enough to feel in your chest.

The *mridangam* keeps rhythm for South Indian classical music. The *damru*, a small hourglass drum, is held by Shiva in Hindu art. In the past, giant *nagara* kettledrums were played at royal palace gates to announce the hours or the arrival of a king.

## The drum kit is born

Before 1900, bands in the USA needed several drummers: one for the bass drum, one for the snare and another for cymbals. Around the early 1900s, drummers in New Orleans and in theatres began playing all of them at once. A foot pedal for the bass drum, made popular from about 1909, freed their hands.

The result was nicknamed the "trap set", short for "contraption". It became the heartbeat of [jazz](jazz), then of rock, pop and [hip-hop](hip-hop). Today, a drum kit sits at the back of almost every band, from school concerts to stadium shows.

## Why beats feel so good

Scientists have found that a steady beat activates parts of the [brain](brain-works) that plan movement, even when you are sitting still. That may be why crowds clap in time at cricket matches, and why marching bands play at [American football](american-football) games.

Drumming together also helps groups feel connected. Around the world, people drum at festivals, prayers, weddings and protests. In the language of drums, there is always a part for everyone, as you can read in [rhythm](rhythm-beat).
