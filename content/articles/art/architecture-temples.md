## The short answer

India's great stone temples were built by teams of architects, stone carvers, labourers and artists, often working for decades. They followed design rules written in old texts called the Shilpa Shastras, which set out how to plan a temple, how big each part should be and where the images should go.

Builders cut stone from quarries, moved it with ramps, rollers, levers, ropes and animals, and fitted blocks together, often without any mortar. Carvers then covered the walls with gods, dancers, animals and patterns. Many temples are still in daily use, so they are working places of worship as well as great works of art.

## A plan like a diagram

Temple builders often started with a square grid called a *mandala*, a plan that represents the order of the universe. The centre square is where the main deity sits.

That central room is the *garbhagriha*, meaning womb-chamber. It's usually small, dark and plain. Above it rises the tallest tower. In front are one or more halls, called mandapas, where people gather. Moving inward from the bright entrance to the dim shrine is meant to feel like a journey towards the sacred.

## North and south styles

Two main styles grew up. In the north, the Nagara style has a curved tower called a *shikhara*, which rises like a mountain peak and is often topped with a ribbed stone disc. The temples at [Khajuraho](madhya-pradesh) in Madhya Pradesh are famous examples.

In the south, the Dravida style has a stepped, pyramid-like tower called a *vimana* over the shrine. Later southern temples added [huge gateway towers](tamil-nadu-temples) called *gopurams*, covered in colourful figures, which can be taller than the main shrine. There were many regional blends in between, including in [Odisha](odisha), home of [Konark](konark).

## The Big Temple of Thanjavur

The [Brihadeeswarar temple](india-wonder-brihadeeswara) in Thanjavur, Tamil Nadu, was completed around 1010 CE by the Chola king Rajaraja I. Its vimana rises about 66 metres, as tall as a 20-storey building, and it was the tallest temple in India when it was built.

It is built of granite, a very hard stone. The blocks were cut to fit tightly and held in place largely by their own weight and by careful interlocking. At the very top sits a huge capstone, often said to weigh around 80 tonnes. Nobody wrote down exactly how it was raised. One popular idea is a long earth ramp; others suggest scaffolding and [levers](lever-lift) used in stages.

## Carving the stories

Once the structure was up, carvers brought the walls to life. They carved gods and goddesses, guardians at the doors, musicians, dancers, elephants and scenes from the epics. At Thanjavur, wall paintings from the Chola period were later found hidden under newer paintings.

Some temples were not built at all but carved downwards out of solid rock, like the Kailasa temple at Ellora, described in [How was the Kailasa temple carved from one rock?](ellora-kailasa).

## Temples as town centres

A big temple was more than a place of prayer. It employed priests, musicians, dancers, cooks, gardeners and accountants. It owned land, stored grain and hosted festivals. Towns often grew around temples, with streets circling them in rings.

Rulers built grand temples partly to show their power and devotion. The [Cholas](cholas) carved long inscriptions on their temple walls listing gifts, names and even the wages of temple staff, which tell historians a great deal about life a thousand years ago.
