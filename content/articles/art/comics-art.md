## The short answer

A comic tells a story in a sequence of pictures, usually arranged in boxes called panels, with words in speech balloons and captions. Making one is often a team job. A writer plans the story and the words. A penciller draws the pictures. An inker goes over the [pencil lines](pencils) in black ink. A colourist adds colour, and a letterer adds the words and sound effects.

Some artists do all of these jobs themselves. Today many comics are drawn on computers and tablets, but the steps are much the same as they were with pencils, brushes and paper.

## From script to page

A comic usually begins as a script. The writer describes each page: how many panels, what happens in each, and what everyone says. A page with lots of small panels feels busy and fast. A single huge panel makes a big moment, like a giant explosion or a hero's arrival, feel important.

Next, the artist makes rough sketches, called thumbnails, to plan where characters stand and where the balloons will go. Then come the careful pencils, the bold ink lines and the colours. Lettering comes last, so the words never cover anything important.

## The gap between panels

The white space between panels is called the gutter. Nothing is drawn there, yet that is where a lot of the story happens. Your mind fills in the action that links one picture to the next.

The American cartoonist Scott McCloud explained this in his book *Understanding Comics*, itself written as a comic. He called it closure. It's the same thing your brain does when it watches drawings flicker past in an [animated cartoon](animation-how), joining separate moments into movement.

## Words you can see

In comics, words are part of the picture. Speech balloons have a tail pointing to the speaker. A cloud-shaped bubble shows thoughts. Spiky balloons show shouting, and tiny letters show whispering.

Sound effects like CRASH, WHOOSH or DHISHOOM are drawn as big, shaped letters that look like the noise. Even the style of lettering can tell you who is speaking: a robot might talk in square letters, a ghost in wobbly ones. Long before speech balloons, people told stories in picture sequences on temple walls and in [scrolls](art-cheriyal), a bit like the way [writing began](writing-began) with pictures.

## Heroes from America and Japan

Newspaper comic strips became popular in the United States in the 1890s. In 1938, two young men from Ohio, Jerry Siegel and Joe Shuster, published the first Superman story, and comic books about [superheroes](superheroes-history) took off.

In Japan, manga grew hugely after the Second World War, led by artists like Osamu Tezuka, creator of *Astro Boy*. Manga are read right to left and are often printed in black and white, which lets artists draw hundreds of pages quickly. Many become [anime](studio-ghibli).

## Comics in India

In 1967 Anant Pai started [Amar Chitra Katha](amar-chitra-katha), comics that retold stories from the epics, history and [folk tales](indian-folk-tales). He reportedly got the idea after noticing that children on a TV quiz could answer questions about Greek myths but not about Indian ones. Generations of Indian children learned the [Ramayana](ramayana) and the lives of historical figures from these comics.

The cartoonist Pran created Chacha Chaudhary in 1971, a clever old man whose brain works faster than a computer, with his giant friend Sabu from Jupiter. *[Tinkle](tinkle)* magazine followed in 1980, with characters like Suppandi. Today Indian artists also publish [graphic novels](graphic-novels) and web comics on phones, and you can read about them in [India's favourite comic heroes](comics-india).
