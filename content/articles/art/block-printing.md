## The short answer

Block printing is a way of stamping patterns onto cloth or paper using carved blocks. A craftsman carves a design into a flat block of wood so that the pattern stands up from the surface. The printer presses the block into dye or paint, then onto the cloth, again and again, to make a repeating [motif](?motif).

Each colour usually needs its own block. To print a red and black flower, the printer first stamps the black outline all along the cloth. Then they take a second block and fill in the red, lining it up exactly. It sounds simple, but doing it neatly across many metres takes years of practice.

## Carving the blocks

Blocks are usually carved from hardwoods like teak or sheesham, which do not crack or swell easily when wet. The carver draws the design on the smooth face, then chips away everything else with tiny chisels, leaving the lines raised.

A detailed block can take days to carve. Small holes are often drilled through it so trapped air can escape, giving a cleaner print. A good block can be used for years. Some printing families keep old blocks for generations, like a library of patterns.

## On the printing table

The cloth is stretched on a long, padded table. The printer dips the block in a tray of dye, presses it onto the cloth and gives the back a firm thump with the side of the fist. Then they lift it, move along and stamp again. Small guide marks on the block help line up each print with the one beside it.

Patterns that repeat neatly across a surface are a kind of [tessellation](tessellations), and printers think carefully about how the edges of one stamp meet the next.

## Rajasthan and Kutch

Near [Jaipur](jaipur), the villages of Sanganer and Bagru have printed cloth for centuries. Sanganer is known for delicate flowers on white backgrounds. Bagru printers often use *dabu*, a sticky mud paste stamped on to block the dye. Where the mud sits, the cloth stays pale, making soft, cracked patterns.

In [Kutch](india-wonder-kutch-crafts), Gujarat, and across the border in Sindh, printers make *ajrakh*. It is printed on both sides in deep red and indigo blue, with star-like geometric patterns. Making ajrakh involves many stages of printing, dyeing, washing and drying, often over a couple of weeks. Running water is needed for the washing, and families have sometimes moved villages to find it.

## Cloth that travelled

Indian printed [cotton](clothes-cotton) was traded across the Indian Ocean for centuries. At Fustat, an old part of Cairo, archaeologists found many scraps of Indian block-printed cloth, some made centuries ago in Gujarat. They show that ordinary people in Egypt wore Indian prints, as you can read in [How did Indian cloth amaze the world?](textiles-india).

## Printing on paper

People in China used carved wooden blocks to print on paper long before Europe had printing. The oldest dated printed book in the world, the *Diamond Sutra*, was printed in China in 868 CE using woodblocks. It is now in the British Library in London.

In Japan, artists made colourful woodblock prints of landscapes and actors. The most famous, Katsushika Hokusai's *The Great Wave*, shows a [huge wave](waves-how) curling over boats with [Mount Fuji](mount-fuji) behind. It was printed in thousands of copies around 1831. Later, metal type and the [printing press](printing-press) made printing words much faster, but carved blocks never disappeared.
