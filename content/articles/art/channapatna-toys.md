## The short answer

Channapatna toys are smooth, brightly coloured wooden toys made in Channapatna, a town about 60 km from [Bengaluru](bengaluru) on the way to [Mysuru](mysuru). The town is so tied to the craft that it is called *Gombegala ooru*, Kannada for "town of toys".

Toymakers shape soft wood on a turning machine called a lathe. While the wood spins, they colour it with lac, a natural resin, and polish it with a dry leaf. The result is a glossy, rounded toy with no sharp edges: [tops](marbles-tops), rattles, stacking rings, little trains and [dolls](pop-barbie-dolls).

## Wood that turns

The traditional wood is *aale mara*, sometimes called ivory wood because it is pale and fine-grained. It is soft enough to carve easily but holds a shape well. Toymakers cut logs into blocks and leave them to dry before they start.

A block is fixed onto the lathe, which spins it very fast. The toymaker holds a chisel against it and peels away curls of wood, a bit like a pencil sharpener working from the outside. In a few minutes, a rough block becomes a perfect ball, cone or ring. Old lathes were turned by hand or foot. Today most are electric.

Because everything is turned in a circle, Channapatna toys are full of round shapes: balls, discs, rings and cones. Clever makers fit these together into more complicated toys. A set of stacking rings is just several turned discs on a turned pole. A little toy car might be a turned body with four turned wheels.

## Colour from an insect

The colour comes from lac. Lac is a resin made by tiny insects that suck sap from trees such as *palash* and *kusum*. Thousands of them crowd along a twig and coat it in a crust. People scrape off the crust, clean it and melt it.

Toymakers mix the melted lac with colours from plants and roll it into sticks, like thick crayons. Then they press a stick against a toy as it spins. [Friction](friction) heats the lac, which melts and spreads evenly. Finally they press a dried screwpine leaf against the toy to buff it to a shine. Without those small [insects](insects-importance), there would be no Channapatna gloss.

## A story of sultans and teachers

Many people in Channapatna say the craft goes back to Tipu Sultan, ruler of [Mysore](mysore-palace) in the late 1700s. According to this story, he invited woodworkers from Persia to teach local artisans. It is a popular tale, but there is little written evidence to confirm it.

The better-documented part comes later. In the early 20th century, a local craftsman named Bavas Miyan is credited with bringing in new techniques and helping many families learn the trade. Over the following decades, the town became famous across India for its lacquerware toys. Families passed the skill from parents to children, and whole streets filled with small workshops, with the hum of lathes and piles of wood shavings on the floor.

## Fighting plastic

By the 1990s and 2000s, cheap [plastic](plastic-made) toys flooded markets, and many Channapatna workshops struggled. Some toymakers left for other jobs.

Designers, craft groups and the government stepped in. They helped makers create new designs such as puzzles, jewellery and modern educational toys, and taught them to sell online and to shops abroad. Channapatna toys and dolls also received a GI tag, which protects the name for toys made in the region. Today you can find Channapatna [spinning tops](yo-yo-tops) in toy shops far from [Karnataka](karnataka), made the same slow way as before.
