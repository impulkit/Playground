## The short answer

Light adds up. Paint takes away. That one idea explains the puzzle.

Red, green and blue lights shone together on a white wall make white. Your [phone screen](made-phone-screens) builds every colour from tiny red, green and blue dots, called [pixels](pixels), switched on in different amounts.

Paint works the other way. A paint looks red because it soaks up most of the other colours in light and bounces red back to your eyes. Mix two paints and each one soaks up its share, so less light escapes. Mix them all and almost nothing bright is left: muddy brown or dark grey.

That's why printers use cyan, magenta and yellow ink, while screens use red, green and blue light.

## White light is a mixture

Around 1666, [Isaac Newton](newton-life) let a thin beam of sunlight pass through a glass prism in a darkened room. It spread into a band of colours, like a [rainbow](rainbow-how). Then he used a second prism to squeeze the colours back together, and they made white light again.

So white is not "no colour". It is all the colours of visible light arriving at once. A red apple looks red because its skin swallows most of those colours and sends back mainly red. In a room lit only by blue light, the same apple looks almost black, because there is no red light for it to bounce back.

## Three colours for your eyes

Why red, green and blue? The answer lies in your eyes, not in the light. The back of each eye has three kinds of colour-sensing cells, called cones. Roughly speaking, one kind responds most to reddish light, one to green and one to blue. Your brain compares their signals to work out every colour you [see](eyes-see).

Shine red and green light together and both the "red" and "green" cones fire. Your brain reads that mix as yellow, even though no yellow light is there. Screens use this trick millions of times a second. Look at a white patch of a phone screen through a drop of water and you can often see the separate red, green and blue dots.

## Paint takes colour away

Each [pigment](?pigment) is a powder that absorbs some colours and reflects the rest. Yellow paint swallows mostly blue light. Cyan, a greenish blue, swallows mostly red. Mix them, and between them they swallow both red and blue, so mainly green escapes. That's why yellow and blue make green in your paintbox.

Add more pigments and each one steals a bit more light. Stir red, yellow, blue and everything else together, and very little is reflected. You get a dull brown. Painters call this "mud", and art teachers spend years warning students to keep their brushes clean.

## Printers, painters and stage lights

[A colour printer](printers) holds cyan, magenta and yellow ink, plus black, often labelled CMYK. Tiny dots of the three colours overlap on white paper to make thousands of shades. Black is added because the three together make a murky dark brown, not a crisp black, and black ink is cheaper.

Artists learned these rules long before anyone could explain them. The painters of [Ajanta](ajanta-colours) mixed earth colours by eye and by experience. Stage lights in a school hall follow light's rules instead. Point red, green and blue spotlights at the same place and the overlap turns white, while the shadow of a person standing in front splits into a row of coloured silhouettes.
