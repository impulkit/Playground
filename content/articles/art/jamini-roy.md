## The short answer

Jamini Roy was one of India's best-known modern painters. He was born in 1887 in the village of Beliatore, in the Bankura district of Bengal, and died in [Kolkata](kolkata) in 1972.

He is famous for paintings that look simple at first: flat areas of colour, thick dark outlines, and figures with long, almond-shaped eyes. Mothers with children, dancers, drummers, Krishna, scenes from the Ramayana, and the Santhal people of Bengal fill his pictures. He borrowed this style from the folk painters of rural Bengal and turned it into something new. Today his paintings hang in the National Gallery of Modern Art in New Delhi and in museums and homes around the world.

## The art student who changed his mind

At about 16, Jamini Roy went to study at the Government School of Art in Calcutta, as Kolkata was then called. There he learned the European way of painting: oil paint, shading, perspective, and portraits that looked like photographs. He was good at it, and for a while he earned money painting portraits of wealthy people.

But he grew unhappy. These were other people's traditions, he felt, copied from Europe. At the same school, artists of the [Bengal School](bengal-school) were looking to old Indian paintings for ideas. Roy went further back still, to the art of ordinary villagers.

## Learning from scroll painters

In the 1920s he began studying the *patuas*, village painters who told stories on long scrolls, much like the [pattachitra](pattachitra) of Odisha. He also looked at Kalighat paintings, quick and bold pictures sold to pilgrims outside a famous temple in Kolkata.

Their pictures used only a few colours and strong, flowing lines. Roy copied their methods, then simplified even further. He gave up expensive oil paints and made colours from earth, chalk, lamp soot and plants. He painted on cloth, wooden boards and woven mats.

His figures changed too. Bodies became smooth curves, like shapes cut from paper. Faces turned sideways, with one huge eye looking out. Nobody in his pictures casts a shadow, and nothing fades into the distance. Everything sits flat on the surface, as it does on a village scroll or a temple wall. Critics in Calcutta were puzzled at first. Some thought he had forgotten how to paint properly. Many of his colours came from the same kinds of earth that ancient painters used, as at the caves of [Ajanta](ajanta-colours).

## Art for everyone

Jamini Roy believed art should belong to ordinary people as much as to rich collectors. So he painted a great many pictures, worked with helpers including his sons, and sold them cheaply. Some people criticised this, saying too many copies made each painting less special. He saw it differently: a village painter had always made many pictures, and nobody called that a problem.

Rabindranath [Tagore](tagore), himself a painter late in life, admired his work. His paintings were shown in London in 1946 and in New York in 1953, and visitors from abroad came to his home in Kolkata to buy them.

## Remembered today

In 1955 the Government of India gave him the Padma Bhushan, one of its highest awards. In 1976, after his death, the government declared his works national art treasures. By 1979 nine artists were on that list, including Raja Ravi Varma, Rabindranath Tagore and [Amrita Sher-Gil](amrita-sher-gil). Their original works cannot leave India without permission.

His influence can be seen on book covers, greeting cards and saris to this day. Many children in Bengal know his style before they know his name. It might be a mother with a baby on her hip, or a row of drummers, painted in a few sure lines on a warm, earthy background.
