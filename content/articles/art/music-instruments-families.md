## The short answer

Musical instruments are grouped by what vibrates to make the sound. Every sound is a shaking of the air, and something has to start that shaking. It might be a string, a tight skin, a column of air inside a tube, or the solid body of the instrument itself.

So a [sitar](sitar-strings) and a violin sit together, because both have strings. A [tabla](tabla) and a snare drum sit together, because both have a stretched skin. The same idea was written down in India about 2,000 years ago, and museums around the world still use a version of it today.

## Four groups from ancient India

The *Natya Shastra*, an old [Sanskrit](sanskrit) book on drama and music, puts instruments into four groups. *Tata* means stretched: instruments with strings, like the [veena](veena). *Sushira* means hollow: instruments you blow into, like the [bansuri flute](flutes). *Avanaddha* means covered: drums, where a skin is stretched over a frame or pot. *Ghana* means solid: things you strike that ring by themselves, like cymbals, bells and the clay ghatam pot.

Nobody knows exactly when the book was written. Scholars usually place it somewhere between about 200 BCE and 200 CE. What is clear is that the four groups were sensible enough to last. Teachers of Indian classical music still use the words today, and a student learning the mridangam will be told it is an avanaddha vadya, a covered instrument.

## The museum system

In 1914, two scholars in Germany, Erich von Hornbostel and Curt Sachs, published a system for sorting every instrument on Earth. It has four main groups, and they line up closely with the Indian ones. Chordophones have strings. Aerophones use air. Membranophones have a stretched skin. Idiophones vibrate as a whole, like a xylophone bar or a pair of manjira.

Each group splits again and again, like branches on a tree, so every instrument gets a number code. Later, a fifth group was added for electrophones: instruments such as [synthesizers](electronic-music), where the sound itself is made by [electricity](electricity-made).

## How an orchestra sees it

An [orchestra](orchestra) uses four everyday families instead. Strings include violins and cellos. Woodwinds include flutes, clarinets and oboes. Brass includes trumpets and tubas, where the player buzzes their lips. Percussion is everything struck or shaken, from timpani drums to the triangle. In a big symphony orchestra, the string players usually make up more than half of the people on stage.

These families are practical. They tell a conductor which players sit together and which sounds blend well. But they can be a little untidy. A flute is usually metal, yet it counts as a woodwind. A tuba and a trumpet sound very different, yet they share a family because both depend on buzzing lips.

## Instruments that break the rules

Some instruments refuse to fit neatly. A piano has strings, but hammers hit them, so orchestras often place it with percussion or in a group of its own called keyboards. The [harmonium](harmonium) has a keyboard too, yet its sound comes from small metal reeds shaken by air.

The ghatam is a clay pot, but players also press its mouth against their stomach to change the sound of the air inside. Your own voice is an aerophone of sorts: air from your [lungs](lungs-breathe) shakes two folds in your throat. Sorting instruments turns out to be a bit like sorting animals. The rules work well, and the oddballs are often the most interesting part.
