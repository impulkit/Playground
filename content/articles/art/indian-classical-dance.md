## The short answer

India's classical dances are old, highly trained styles that tell stories with the whole body. The Sangeet Natak Akademi, the national academy for music, dance and drama, recognises eight. They are Bharatanatyam from Tamil Nadu, Kathak from the north, [Kathakali](kathakali) and [Mohiniyattam](kuchipudi-mohiniyattam) from Kerala, Kuchipudi from Andhra Pradesh, [Odissi](odissi) from Odisha, [Manipuri](manipuri-dance) from Manipur and Sattriya from Assam.

Each looks and sounds different. But they share an idea from an ancient book, the *Natya Shastra*: dance should move the audience by showing emotions, not just steps. A dancer's eyes, eyebrows and fingers matter as much as the feet.

## A very old rulebook

The *Natya Shastra* is said to have been written by a sage called Bharata, somewhere between about 200 BCE and 200 CE. Nobody is sure of the date, and it may be the work of several authors. It covers acting, music, make-up, stage building and dance in great detail.

The book describes how a performance creates *rasa*, a kind of flavour of feeling in the audience, such as love, courage, laughter or fear. It lists 108 *karanas*, key movements of dance. Centuries later, sculptors carved these poses into temple walls in Tamil Nadu, and dancers today still study them.

## Hands that speak

Classical dances split into three parts. *Nritta* is pure dance: footwork and shapes that show [rhythm](rhythm-beat) and skill. *Nritya* adds expression, with the face and hands acting out the words of a song. *Natya* is full drama, with a dancer playing characters.

The hand gestures are called *mudras*. One shape can mean many things depending on how it moves. A flat hand with fingers together, called *pataka*, can become a flag, a forest, a wave or a door. Watching a skilled dancer is a bit like reading a book in a language you are slowly learning.

## Eight dances, many places

The styles grew in different places, often linked to temples, royal courts and village festivals. Kathakali, from [Kerala](kerala), uses huge skirts, [painted green faces](art-kathakali-makeup) and eye movements that can be seen from far away. Traditionally, its performances lasted all night.

Odissi, from Odisha, uses curved, [sculpture-like](sculpture) poses. You can see similar figures carved on the [Sun Temple at Konark](konark). Manipuri is soft and flowing, often telling stories of Krishna. Sattriya was created in Assam's monasteries more than 500 years ago, and joined the official list only in 2000.

## Nearly lost, then reborn

In the late 1800s and early 1900s, many of these traditions were in trouble. Royal patrons lost their wealth, and some reformers attacked temple dancing. Some states made laws against temple dancing in the decades around independence.

Dancers and teachers fought to keep the art alive. In 1930, the poet Vallathol Narayana Menon set up Kerala Kalamandalam to teach Kathakali. In 1936, Rukmini Devi Arundale founded Kalakshetra in Chennai to train [Bharatanatyam](bharatanatyam) dancers. People still debate how much the old traditions changed in this rebirth, and who was left out.

## Stories that never get old

Many dances take their stories from the *Ramayana* and the [Mahabharata](mahabharata), and from poems about gods such as Krishna and Shiva. Others tell of nature, seasons or love.

Today, children across India, the USA and the wider world learn these dances. In American cities, it is common to see a young dancer's *arangetram*, a first full solo performance, in a school hall on a Saturday. An art once taught only in temples and palaces now travels in suitcases full of ankle bells.
