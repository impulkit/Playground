## The short answer

English spread in three big waves. First, it grew up in England itself, soaking up words from Vikings, French-speaking Normans and Latin scholars. Then, from the 1600s, British ships, traders and armies carried it to North America, India, Africa, Australia and the Caribbean. Finally, in the 1900s, the power of the USA, with its films, music, science and computers, made English a language that people chose to learn.

Today roughly 1.5 billion people speak at least some English. Most of them learned it at school, not at home. India has one of the largest English-speaking populations in the world.

## A language of invaders

In the 400s and 500s CE, groups called the Angles, Saxons and Jutes sailed from what is now Germany and Denmark to Britain. Their speech became Old English, the language of the poem *Beowulf*. You would find it almost impossible to read today.

Then came the [Vikings](vikings), who left everyday words such as *sky*, *egg* and *knife*. In 1066, William of Normandy conquered England, and for about 300 years its rulers spoke French. That's why English has pairs like *cow*, the farmer's word, and *beef*, the French-speaking diner's word, or *pig* and *pork*. By the time of [Shakespeare](shakespeare), around 1600, English looked much more like it does now.

## Ships and empires

In 1607, English settlers founded Jamestown in Virginia, and English took root in North America. Over the next three centuries, the British Empire carried the language around the globe. In India, the [East India Company](east-india-company) began trading in 1600 and slowly became a ruling power. In 1835, the official Thomas Macaulay persuaded the government to spend money on English-language education, to train Indians who could work for the British.

That policy was controversial then and is still argued about now. But Indians made English their own. Leaders of the [freedom struggle](freedom-struggle) wrote and argued in it, and writers such as [R. K. Narayan](rk-narayan) and [Ruskin Bond](ruskin-bond) won readers all over the world.

## Indian English and its cousins

English never stays still when it moves. In India it gained words like *prepone*, the opposite of postpone, and *co-brother*, for the husband of your wife's sister. Indian English speakers say "do the needful" and "what is your good name?", phrases that sound odd in London but are perfectly clear in Lucknow.

American English changed too. Noah Webster's [dictionary](dictionaries) of 1828 dropped the u from *colour* and *honour*. Nigerian, Singaporean, Australian and Caribbean English each have their own words and rhythms. Many linguists now talk about "Englishes", in the plural, rather than one correct English.

## Why everyone learns it now

Since the middle of the 1900s, English has become the main shared language for science, business and travel. Pilots and air traffic controllers on international flights must be able to use English, so a [plane](airplanes-fly) from Delhi can land safely in Tokyo or New York. Most scientific papers today are published in English.

[The internet](internet-how) pushed it further. Much of the early internet was built in the USA, and English is still the most common language on websites. This brings opportunities, but also worries. Some people fear that smaller languages lose speakers when young people switch to English. Others point out that most English learners keep their home language too and simply add another, the way millions of Indian children grow up speaking two or three.
