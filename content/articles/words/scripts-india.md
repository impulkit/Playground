## The short answer

India has so many scripts because one ancient script, Brahmi, spread across a huge area over more than 2,000 years, and each region slowly shaped it in its own way. Different writing materials, different languages and different royal courts pulled the letters in different directions. It's a bit like one family whose children's [handwriting](words-handwriting) drifted further apart with every generation.

Today India uses more than a dozen major scripts, including Devanagari, Bengali, [Gurmukhi](punjabi-gurmukhi), Gujarati, Odia, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada and Malayalam. Urdu and Kashmiri use a [Perso-Arabic script](arabic-script), and Santali has its own script, Ol Chiki, created in 1925.

## One ancestor, many children

The oldest readable Indian script is Brahmi, carved on rocks and pillars by Emperor [Ashoka](ashoka) in the 3rd century BCE. Over the next thousand years, it split into northern and southern branches. The northern branch led, through scripts such as Siddham, Sharada and Nagari, to Devanagari, Bengali and Gurmukhi. The southern branch led to Tamil, Telugu, Kannada and Malayalam.

Brahmi travelled far beyond India too. Scripts in [Sri Lanka](sri-lanka), Myanmar, [Thailand](thailand), Cambodia and Tibet all grew from it, carried by traders, monks and Buddhist texts. A Thai or Tibetan letter can be a distant cousin of a Hindi one.

## Letters that carry a vowel

Indian scripts share a clever design. Each consonant letter carries a built-in "a" sound, so क is read "ka". To change the vowel, you add a small mark called a *matra*: कि (ki), कु (ku), के (ke). Two consonants can join into one combined letter, such as क्ष (ksha). Scripts that work this way are called [abugidas](?abugida).

They also [share an order](words-alphabet-order). Instead of A, B, C, letters are grouped by where the sound is made in the mouth. They start at the throat (ka, kha, ga), then move to the palate, the roof of the mouth and the teeth, and finish at the lips (pa, pha, ba, ma). Say them aloud and you can feel the sounds travelling forward. This order comes from ancient Indian scholars who studied speech sounds with great care.

## Why the shapes look different

If the letters share an ancestor, why do they look so unlike each other? [Writing tools](writing-tools) played a part. In the south, people wrote for centuries on dried palm leaves, scratching letters with an iron stylus. One popular explanation is that long straight lines would split the leaf along its veins, so southern scripts grew round and curly, as you can see in Malayalam, Telugu and Kannada.

In the north, many scribes wrote with ink and a reed pen. Devanagari gained its long top line, the *shirorekha*, from which letters seem to hang like clothes on a washing line. Bengali and Gurmukhi have top lines too. Telugu and Kannada scripts are so alike that a reader of one can often guess at words in the other.

## Forgotten and found again

By the 1700s, nobody could read Brahmi. The inscriptions on Ashoka's pillars were a mystery. In 1837, James Prinsep, an Englishman working at the mint in Kolkata, cracked the code. He noticed that many short inscriptions on a stupa at Sanchi ended with the same two letters. He guessed they recorded gifts and that the letters spelled *danam*, "gift". He was right, and the rest of the script followed.

The even older script of the Indus cities, used more than 4,000 years ago, has never been deciphered. Its inscriptions are very short, mostly on small seals, and no bilingual text like Egypt's [Rosetta Stone](hieroglyphs) has been found. Some scholars even doubt it wrote down a full language. The story of India's scripts still has a missing first chapter.
