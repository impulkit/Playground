## The short answer

Ruskin Bond is an Indian author who writes in English about the hills, small towns, trains and people of north India. He has written hundreds of short stories, essays and poems, and more than fifty books for children. Many Indian schoolchildren meet him in their textbooks.

His stories feel special because they are quiet and true to life. Nothing huge usually happens. A boy saves money for a bicycle. A girl is offered a trade for her blue umbrella. A tree outlives the people who planted it. Bond notices small things, writes simply, and lets readers feel them.

## A lonely boy in the hills

Bond was born in 1934 in Kasauli, a hill town in today's [Himachal Pradesh](himachal). His father worked as a tutor and later served in the Royal Air Force during the Second World War. His parents separated, and his father, whom he adored, died when Ruskin was ten. That loss runs quietly through many of his stories.

He went to boarding school in Shimla and spent holidays in Dehradun. Lonely and bookish, he read whatever he could find and began writing as a schoolboy.

## Homesick in England

After school, Bond went to Britain, hoping to become a writer. He took office jobs to pay the rent, and he missed India badly. Out of that homesickness came The Room on the Roof, written when he was about seventeen.

It tells of Rusty, an Anglo-Indian teenager who escapes his strict guardian and finds friends among Indian boys in Dehradun. It was published in 1956 and won the John Llewellyn Rhys Prize, a British award for writers under 35. With money from the book, Bond came home to India, and he has stayed.

## The view from Landour

Since the 1960s, Bond has lived in Landour, above the [hill station](india-wonder-hill-stations) of Mussoorie in [Uttarakhand](uttarakhand). For decades he has lived with an adopted family in a small flat, writing by hand at a desk by the window. Visitors climb the steep road hoping to meet him, and on many Saturdays he signs books at a bookshop on the Mall Road.

His landscape is the lower [Himalaya](himalaya-peoples): pine and deodar forests, leopards, langurs, mist, and the first [monsoon](monsoon) rains drumming on tin roofs. In The Night Train at Deoli, a young man passes a tiny station every summer on the [train](indian-railways) and glimpses a girl selling baskets. He never gets off. The story is a few pages long, and readers remember it for years.

## Big feelings, simple words

Bond writes in short sentences and avoids fancy words. That plain style is harder than it looks. Like [R.K. Narayan](rk-narayan), he writes about ordinary Indian life without explaining it for outsiders.

Trees matter in his stories as much as people. In Our Trees Still Grow in Dehra, the book that won him the Sahitya Akademi Award in 1992, he remembers planting trees with his grandfather. Old [banyans](banyan), cherry trees and [mango](mango-tree) orchards keep turning up, often outliving the humans around them.

## On screen

Several of Bond's stories have become films. Vishal Bhardwaj filmed The Blue Umbrella, about a village girl and a jealous shopkeeper. His story Susanna's Seven Husbands became the film 7 Khoon Maaf in 2011. Children have also enjoyed television versions of his [ghost stories](ghost-stories), which are more cosy than scary.

He received the Padma Shri in 1999 and the Padma Bhushan in 2014. He kept writing into his nineties, and his birthday in May is celebrated by readers across India.
