## The short answer

The Jataka tales are stories of the earlier lives of the [Buddha](buddha). Buddhists believe that before he was born as Prince Siddhartha, he lived many lives. Sometimes he was a human, and often an animal: a monkey, a deer, an elephant, a quail, a hare. In each life he practised a virtue, such as generosity, patience or courage, preparing for the life in which he would become enlightened.

The main collection, in the Pali language, has about 550 stories. Many are short, funny or moving [fables](?fable). Some are long adventures with kings, merchants and [magical beings](myths-indian-creatures).

## How each story works

Most Jataka tales follow a pattern. They begin in the present, with the Buddha hearing about something that happened among his followers. He then tells a story from long ago, which usually opens "Once upon a time, when Brahmadatta was reigning in Benares". At the end, he explains which of today's people played which part in the past.

The Buddha-to-be is called the Bodhisatta, the Pali form of Bodhisattva, "a being on the way to awakening". Many of the tales are older than Buddhism and were already folk stories across India. Some also appear in the [Panchatantra](panchatantra-tales), and a few resemble the fables of [Aesop](aesop), which shows how freely good stories travelled.

## The monkey king's bridge

In one well-known tale, the Bodhisatta is the leader of 80,000 monkeys living in a [mango](mango-tree) tree beside the Ganga. A king's archers surround the tree at night. The monkey king leaps across the river, ties a long vine to a tree on the far bank and to his own body, and stretches himself into a living bridge. His monkeys cross over his back to safety.

He is badly hurt, and the king, watching, is deeply moved. He learns what a real leader does for his people. This story, the Mahakapi Jataka, is carved on a stone medallion from Bharhut, now in the Indian Museum in Kolkata, and also appears in the paintings at [Ajanta](ajanta-colours).

## The hare in the Moon

In the Sasa Jataka, the Bodhisatta is a hare who lives with three friends: a monkey, a jackal and an otter. They agree to give food to anyone in need. One day, the god Sakka, disguised as a hungry traveller, comes to test them. The otter brings fish, the jackal brings food it has found and the monkey brings mangoes. The hare has only grass, which a person cannot eat. So he offers himself, leaping into a fire to become a meal.

The fire doesn't burn him. Sakka, deeply moved, draws the hare's image on the [Moon](moon-phases) so the world will remember his kindness. In many parts of Asia, people still look for a hare in the dark patches of the full Moon.

## Carved, painted and retold

Over 2,000 years ago, Buddhist sculptors carved Jataka scenes on the stone railings and gateways of stupas at Bharhut and Sanchi, in [Madhya Pradesh](madhya-pradesh). Pilgrims walking around the stupa could "read" the stories in stone. Later, painters at Ajanta filled cave walls with them.

The tales spread with Buddhism to Sri Lanka, Myanmar, [Thailand](thailand) and beyond. In Thailand and Laos, the Vessantara Jataka, the story of the Bodhisatta's last human life before becoming the Buddha, is recited at a festival every year. Between 1895 and 1907, scholars at Cambridge published the first complete English [translation](translation), in six volumes, so readers far from India could meet the generous hare too.
