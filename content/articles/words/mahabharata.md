## The short answer

The Mahabharata is an ancient Indian [epic](?epic) in [Sanskrit](sanskrit). At its heart is a family quarrel. Two groups of cousins, the five Pandava brothers and the hundred Kauravas, fight over the kingdom of Hastinapura. Tricked out of their share in a game of dice, the Pandavas spend 13 years in exile. Talks fail, and the cousins meet in a great war on the field of Kurukshetra, which lasts 18 days.

The epic is traditionally said to have been composed by the sage Vyasa. With about 100,000 verses in its longest versions, it is several times longer than the Greek *Iliad* and *Odyssey* put together.

## A story within stories

The Mahabharata is built like a set of nested boxes. Storytellers tell other storytellers what they heard. The main tale is also full of side stories. There is the love of Nala and Damayanti, the clever Savitri who argues with the god of death, and even a short version of the Ramayana.

Its characters are rarely simply good or bad. Arjuna is a great archer who doubts himself. Karna, a brave warrior raised by a charioteer, learns only shortly before the war that he is the Pandavas' elder brother. Draupadi, married to all five Pandavas, demands justice when she is insulted in the royal court. The epic asks hard questions about duty, loyalty and fairness, and it doesn't always give easy answers.

## The song on the battlefield

Just before the war begins, Arjuna looks across the field and sees teachers, cousins and uncles in the opposing army. He lays down his bow and refuses to fight. His charioteer, Krishna, whom Hindus regard as a form of the god Vishnu, answers him. Their conversation, 700 verses long, is the [Bhagavad Gita](bhagavad-gita), "the song of the Lord".

The Gita talks about duty, action and the nature of the self. It has been translated into many languages and read by people of many faiths. Mahatma [Gandhi](gandhi) read it often and wrote his own Gujarati [translation](translation) with a commentary.

## Written by an elephant god?

A much-loved legend says Vyasa needed a scribe fast enough to keep up. The elephant-headed god [Ganesha](myths-ganesha-stories) agreed, on one condition: Vyasa must never stop reciting. Vyasa added his own condition: Ganesha must understand every verse before writing it down. Whenever Vyasa needed a rest, he composed a tricky verse to keep Ganesha thinking.

Historians tell a different kind of story. The epic was probably passed on by storytellers for centuries, growing as it went, and written down in many versions. Between 1919 and 1966, scholars at the Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute in Pune compared more than a thousand manuscripts to produce a "critical edition", their best estimate of the oldest shared text.

## Told and retold

The Mahabharata is everywhere in Indian life. [Kathakali](indian-classical-dance) dancers in [Kerala](kerala) perform its battles with painted faces and huge skirts. People still call a trap you can enter but not escape a *chakravyuha*, after a battle formation in the epic. Comics such as [Amar Chitra Katha](amar-chitra-katha) introduced millions of children to its heroes.

In 1988, B. R. Chopra's TV series began on Doordarshan, and like the Ramayan series before it, it emptied streets on Sunday mornings. Abroad, the British director Peter Brook staged a nine-hour play of the epic in 1985. Its questions about family, power and right action still feel surprisingly modern.
