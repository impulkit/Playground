## The short answer

Indian stories are packed with creatures that never walked the Earth. There are serpent people called nagas, the eagle-like [Garuda](myths-garuda), a seven-headed flying horse, a two-headed bird strong enough to carry elephants, and water monsters with elephant trunks.

They come from old epics like the [Ramayana](ramayana) and Mahabharata, from the Puranas, from Buddhist and Jain tales, and from village folk stories. Many still look down from temple walls, sit on state emblems and appear in comics. Some are guardians and some are vehicles for gods and goddesses. Others are tricksters, ghosts or nature spirits. The same creature can look quite different in Tamil Nadu, Odisha and Bengal, because each region told the stories its own way.

## Guardians at the gate

Walk into an old South Indian temple and look at the pillars. You may see a yali, a creature with a lion's body and sometimes an elephant's trunk, rearing up on its back legs. Yalis were carved as protectors, and some are taller than a person.

Over doorways, you'll often find a makara: part crocodile, part fish, with a curling elephant trunk or tail. The river goddess Ganga rides one. Makaras gape over gateways from Sanchi to Cambodia and appear on jewellery too. Sculptors loved them because a makara could twist into any shape a doorway needed, one of the tricks of [temple sculpture](temple-sculpture).

## Snakes, birds and flying horses

Nagas are serpent beings who live in underwater palaces, guard treasures and control rain. Some are shown with human top halves and snake tails. People across India still honour snakes on Nag Panchami. The great serpent Shesha, with a thousand heads in some tellings, is the bed on which Vishnu rests.

Garuda, half man and half eagle, is the nagas' great rival. When the gods and demons churned the ocean, out came Airavata, a white elephant ridden by Indra, and Uchchaihshravas, a seven-headed flying horse.

## The two-headed bird

Gandaberunda is a giant bird with two heads facing opposite ways. In stories it is so strong it can carry an elephant in each claw. The kings of Vijayanagara and later the Wodeyar rulers of Mysore used it as a royal symbol. Today it sits on Karnataka's state emblem and appears in carvings at the [Mysore Palace](mysore-palace).

Odisha has its own wonder: the Navagunjara, made of nine animals. It has a rooster's head, a peacock's neck, a lion's waist, a snake's tail and more. In the Odia poet Sarala Das's Mahabharata, Arjuna meets it and realises it is Krishna in disguise. Pattachitra painters in Puri still paint it.

## Ghosts and tricksters

Not every creature is grand. Folk tales are full of bhoots (ghosts) whose feet point backwards, and of the chudail, a frightening spirit in North Indian stories. The vetala is a spirit who lives in corpses at cremation grounds. In [Vikram and Betal](vikram-betal), a clever vetala hangs from a tree and asks King Vikram a riddle every time he's carried off.

Yakshas are nature spirits who guard wealth and forests. In one famous Mahabharata story, a yaksha guarding a lake asks Yudhishthira a long string of questions before letting the brothers drink. Kinnaras, part human and part bird or horse, are heavenly musicians. Many cultures dreamed up similar mixtures, which is why scholars compare Indian creatures with Greek griffins and the [unicorns](myths-unicorns) of medieval Europe.
