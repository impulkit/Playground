## The short answer

Dr. Seuss was the pen name of Theodor Seuss Geisel, an American writer and cartoonist who lived from 1904 to 1991. He wrote and drew more than 60 children's books, including The Cat in the Hat, Green Eggs and Ham, How the Grinch Stole [Christmas](christmas) and The Lorax.

He made reading fun by turning it into a game. His books bounce along with strong rhythm and [silly rhymes](poems-for-kids). They use simple words that new readers can sound out, and wild pictures of creatures nobody has ever seen. Children can read them alone, which feels like a superpower when you are six.

## The problem with Dick and Jane

In the 1950s, most American children learned to read from school books about two children called Dick and Jane. The sentences went like "See Dick run. Run, Dick, run." Many children found them dull.

In 1954, the writer John Hersey wrote a magazine article asking why reading books for beginners were so boring. He suggested that someone like Dr. Seuss should write them. A publisher then gave Geisel a list of a few hundred words that young children should know, and challenged him to write a book using only those.

The result was The Cat in the Hat (1957), which uses about 236 different words. A tall cat in a striped hat turns up on a rainy day while their mother is out and wrecks the house, then tidies it all up just in time. It sold more than a million copies within a few years and changed beginner books for good.

## A beat you can bounce to

Read Seuss aloud and you can hear a steady pattern: da-da-DUM, da-da-DUM. Try "I do not like green eggs and ham. I do not like them, Sam-I-am." The [rhythm](rhythm-beat) makes lines easy to remember and fun to say, a little like [tongue twisters](tongue-twisters).

The rhymes help too. If a child can read "cat", they can guess "hat", "sat" and "that". Seuss also loved made-up words, like Sneetches, Lorax and the Grinch. Nonsense words can't be guessed from memory, so readers have to sound them out, which is exactly the practice they need.

## A cartoonist with a message

Before he wrote for children, Geisel drew cartoons and advertisements. His first children's book, And to Think That I Saw It on Mulberry Street (1937), was rejected by more than 20 publishers.

Many later books carry big ideas in silly packages. In The Lorax (1971), a small creature "speaks for the trees" against a businessman who chops them all down. It is still read in classrooms about [protecting nature](environment-heroes). The Sneetches is about creatures who look down on each other for not having stars on their bellies. Horton Hears a Who! says that "a person's a person, no matter how small".

## Criticism and change

Not everything Geisel drew has aged well. During the [Second World War](world-war-two), he drew political cartoons that included racist pictures of Japanese people. Some early books also contain hurtful pictures of people from Asia and Africa.

In 2021, the company that looks after his books stopped publishing six of them because of such images. Some people agreed with the decision. Others felt the books should stay in print with explanations. Either way, his most famous titles remain in shops and [classrooms](school-history).

In the United States, his birthday, 2 March, is used each year to celebrate reading, with "Read Across America" events in schools.
