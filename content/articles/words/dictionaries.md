## The short answer

People called [lexicographers](?lexicographer) decide what goes into a dictionary, but they don't decide which words are allowed. Their job is to watch the language. They collect millions of examples of words in books, [newspapers](newspapers), websites, song lyrics and transcripts of speech. They add a word when it is used widely, by many different people, over a period of time.

So, in a way, you help decide. If enough people use a new word for long enough, it will probably end up in the dictionary. Words that drop out of use stay in big dictionaries too, marked "old" or "archaic", so that readers of older books can still look them up.

## Word lists before dictionaries

People have made word lists for thousands of years. Scribes in ancient [Mesopotamia](mesopotamia) made clay tablets listing words in Sumerian alongside their meanings in Akkadian. In India, the *Amarakosha*, a [Sanskrit](sanskrit) word book written in verse more than a thousand years ago, grouped words by meaning: words for gods, plants, animals, people and much more. Students memorised it by chanting.

The first English dictionaries, in the early 1600s, mostly explained "hard words", difficult terms borrowed from [Latin](latin-roots) or French. They left out everyday words like "dog" or "bread", on the grounds that everyone knew them already.

## Johnson and Webster

In 1755, Samuel Johnson published *A Dictionary of the English Language*. With a handful of helpers, he spent about nine years defining some 42,000 words and backing them with quotations from famous writers. Some definitions were cheeky. He described a lexicographer as "a harmless drudge", someone doing dull, hard work.

In the USA, Noah Webster wanted [American English](english-spread) to have its own identity. His *American Dictionary of the English Language*, published in 1828, dropped the u from *colour* and *honour* and changed *centre* to *center*. That's one big reason American and British [spellings](words-spelling-bee) differ, and why this app changes colour to color for readers in the USA.

## Seventy years and millions of slips

In 1857, members of the Philological Society in London called for a new dictionary that would trace the full history of every English word. Volunteers across the English-speaking world read old books and posted in slips of paper with quotations showing how words had been used. Millions of slips arrived.

The editor, James Murray, worked in an iron shed in his garden in Oxford that he called the Scriptorium. One of the busiest volunteers was an American former army surgeon named William Chester Minor. He sent thousands of slips from a hospital in England for people with mental illness, where he lived for decades. The first full edition of the *Oxford English Dictionary* was finished in 1928, about 70 years after the project began.

## How a new word gets in

Today, lexicographers use computers to search huge collections of text, called corpora, holding billions of words. When a new word appears, they ask: is it used by lots of different people, in different places, over several years? Or is it a flash in the pan?

[New words](words-made-up) arrive all the time from technology, science and [slang](slang). "Selfie" was chosen as Oxford's [Word of the Year](words-emoji-language) in 2013. Indian English adds words too: *jugaad*, a clever, low-cost fix, joined the Oxford English Dictionary in 2017. Online dictionaries now update every few months.

A [search engine](search-engines) can show you a word's meaning in less than a second. Behind that answer is a team of people who read the evidence, argued over it and wrote the definition with care.
