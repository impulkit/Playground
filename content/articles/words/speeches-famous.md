## The short answer

A speech becomes famous when it says something important at exactly the right moment, in words simple enough to remember. The best speeches are often short. They use plain words, repeat a key line, tell a small story and end with a strong picture in the listener's mind.

The moment matters as much as the words. Jawaharlal Nehru's "Tryst with Destiny" speech was given at midnight as India became independent. Martin Luther King Jr.'s "I Have a Dream" was spoken to a quarter of a million people who had marched for equal rights. People remembered the words because they remembered the day.

## Two minutes at Gettysburg

In November 1863, in the middle of the [American Civil War](us-civil-war), a cemetery for soldiers opened at Gettysburg, Pennsylvania. The main speaker, Edward Everett, spoke for about two hours. Then [Abraham Lincoln](abraham-lincoln) stood up and spoke about 270 words.

He said the country had been "conceived in liberty", and that the living must make sure the dead had not died in vain. Some newspapers thought it was too short. Today it is carved in stone at the Lincoln Memorial in Washington DC, and American children learn it by heart.

## Sisters and brothers

In September 1893, a young Indian monk, [Swami Vivekananda](swami-vivekananda), stood up at the World's Parliament of Religions in Chicago. He began: "Sisters and brothers of America." Before he could go on, the hall broke into applause that lasted a couple of minutes.

Those five words felt warm and equal at a time when many speakers began with stiff formal greetings. He went on to speak about respect between religions. In India, his birthday, 12 January, is now celebrated as National Youth Day.

## Midnight in Delhi

Just before midnight on 14 August 1947, Nehru spoke to the [Constituent Assembly](constituent-assembly) in Delhi. "Long years ago we made a tryst with destiny," he said, and "at the stroke of the midnight hour, when the world sleeps, India will awake to life and freedom."

A tryst is a promise to meet. The idea that a whole nation had kept an appointment with its future was easy to picture. Five years earlier, Gandhi had given a very different kind of speech, launching the [Quit India movement](quit-india) with just three words for his followers: "Do or die."

## The dream on the steps

On 28 August 1963, [Martin Luther King Jr.](martin-luther-king) spoke from the steps of the Lincoln Memorial. Near the end, the gospel singer Mahalia Jackson called out, "Tell them about the dream, Martin." He put aside his notes. Again and again he repeated "I have a dream", each time painting a picture of a fairer America.

That repetition is an old trick. It gives listeners a beat to hold on to, like the chorus of a song. King was a preacher, and he had used parts of the dream passage in earlier sermons and speeches, so the words came easily when he needed them.

## Why some speeches last

Famous speeches usually do a few things well. They speak to a big moment. They use words a child could follow. They repeat a line until it sticks. And they are spoken by someone the audience trusts.

[Radio](radio-invented), television and now phones let a speech travel far beyond the room. In 2013 Malala Yousafzai, aged 16, spoke at the [United Nations](united-nations) about every child's right to go to school. Millions watched online, and her words were shared in classrooms on both sides of the world.
