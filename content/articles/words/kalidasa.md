## The short answer

Kalidasa was a poet and playwright who wrote in [Sanskrit](sanskrit), probably about 1,600 years ago. He is the most celebrated writer of classical Sanskrit, the polished form of the language used for literature in ancient India.

He left behind three plays, two long [epic](?epic) poems and at least one shorter poem. His play Shakuntala and his poem Meghaduta, "The Cloud Messenger", are read in classrooms from Pune to Paris.

## A poet with no biography

We know Kalidasa's words far better than his life. His name means "servant of Kali". Many scholars think he lived during the [Gupta age](gupta-golden-age), perhaps at the court of Chandragupta II, because his works describe a rich, settled kingdom much like that one. A popular legend makes him one of the "nine gems" at the court of a king called Vikramaditya.

Ujjain, in [Madhya Pradesh](madhya-pradesh), is often linked with him. He describes the city lovingly in his poems, and an annual festival there, the Kalidas Samaroh, celebrates his work with plays and music.

## Shakuntala, the lost ring

His most famous play tells of King Dushyanta, who falls in love with Shakuntala, a young woman raised in a forest hermitage. He marries her and gives her his ring, then returns to his palace. A curse makes him forget her, and only the sight of the ring can bring back his memory. Shakuntala loses it while bathing in a river.

The ring turns up inside a fish. The king remembers, and after much sorrow the family is reunited. Their son Bharata, the story goes, gives his name to India's ancient name, Bharat. Kalidasa took the plot from a much shorter version in the [Mahabharata](mahabharata) and turned it into a full drama.

## Letters by cloud

Meghaduta is a poem of about 110 verses. A yaksha, a kind of nature spirit, has been banished for a year to a hill in central India. When the first [monsoon clouds](clouds-types) arrive, he cannot bear being apart from his wife. So he asks a cloud to carry a message north to the Himalaya.

The poem follows the cloud past rivers, forests and cities, with careful details of birds, flowers and rain. Many poets later wrote their own "messenger poems" in imitation.

## Gods, kings and seasons

Kalidasa's two long poems are grand in scale. Kumarasambhava, "The Birth of Kumara", tells how the goddess Parvati wins the heart of Shiva through years of hard penance in the Himalaya. Their son Kumara, also called Kartikeya, is born to defeat a demon.

Raghuvamsha follows the family line of King Raghu over many generations, including Rama, the hero of the [Ramayana](ramayana). A shorter poem, Ritusamhara, describes India's six [seasons](seasons-why) one by one, from scorching summer to spring. Some scholars doubt it is really his.

## Famous far from home

In 1789 William Jones, a British judge in Calcutta who studied Sanskrit, published Shakuntala in English. A German version followed in 1791 and amazed readers in Europe, including the poet Goethe. It was one of the first pieces of Indian literature many Europeans had ever read.

In India, Kalidasa's plays are still performed in Sanskrit and in translation, and they shaped later Indian [theatre](theatre). His comparisons are what readers remember: a king's mind running back to his love like a silk flag fluttering backwards against the wind.
