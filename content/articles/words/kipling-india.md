## The short answer

For nearly 200 years, Britain controlled large parts of India, first through the [East India Company](east-india-company) and then directly from 1858 to 1947. Tens of thousands of British soldiers, officials, teachers and their families lived in India. Some were born there.

Writers among them wrote about what they saw, and readers in Britain were hungry for stories of a land that seemed distant and exotic to them. Adventure, jungles, palaces and [hill stations](india-wonder-hill-stations) all made exciting reading. India also gave these writers material that no one in London had: new landscapes, languages, foods and people.

The best known is Rudyard Kipling, born in Bombay in 1865. Others include E. M. Forster, George Orwell and the hunter-writer Jim Corbett.

## Kipling: love and empire

Kipling spent his early childhood in Bombay, speaking Hindustani with his servants before English. He later returned to India as a young [journalist](journalism) in Lahore. His stories, such as *Plain Tales from the Hills* and [The Jungle Book](jungle-book), made him famous. In 1907 he became the first English-language writer to win the Nobel Prize in Literature.

His novel *Kim*, published in 1901, follows an orphan boy who wanders the Grand Trunk Road with a Tibetan lama. Many readers find it full of affection for India's crowds, colours and languages.

But Kipling also strongly supported the British Empire. His poem "The White Man's Burden" (1899) argued that Western countries had a duty to rule other peoples. Many readers, then and now, have criticised these views. George Orwell, who admired some of his writing, called him an imperialist.

## Forster, Orwell and Corbett

E. M. Forster visited India in the 1910s and 1920s and wrote *A Passage to India* (1924). It shows friendships between Indians and British people breaking down under the unfairness of colonial rule, and many saw it as critical of the Raj.

George Orwell was born Eric Blair in 1903 in Motihari, Bihar, where his father worked for the colonial government. He left as a baby, but later served in the British police in Burma, and grew to dislike empire deeply.

Jim Corbett, born in Nainital in what is now [Uttarakhand](uttarakhand), wrote *Man-Eaters of Kumaon* (1944) about tracking tigers and leopards that attacked villagers. He later worked to protect wildlife, and India's first national park was renamed Corbett National Park after him in 1956.

## Seeing India from the outside

In 1978, the scholar Edward Said published *Orientalism*. He argued that many Western writers described Asia as mysterious and backward, partly to justify ruling it. His ideas changed how many people read Kipling and his circle.

Indian writers had been answering back in English for years. [R. K. Narayan](rk-narayan), Mulk Raj Anand and Raja Rao, all publishing in the 1930s, wrote about Indian life from the inside, with ordinary people as heroes instead of servants or backdrops.

## The ones who stayed

Some British families never left. Ruskin Bond was born in Kasauli in 1934 to a British family. After a few years in England as a young man, he came back and made India his home. He has spent his life writing about the hills of Mussoorie and the people who live there, and many Indian readers see him simply as one of their own.

That may be the clearest change since Kipling's day. Writing about India in English is no longer mainly done by visitors. Today, most of it is written by [Indian writers](indian-english-writers) themselves.
