## The short answer

When a language disappears, its words, songs, stories, jokes and ways of describing the world go with it. Many small languages hold knowledge found nowhere else, such as names for local plants and animals, or ways of reading the weather.

A language usually doesn't vanish suddenly. It fades when children stop learning it. Parents may switch to a bigger language for school, jobs or safety. The grandparents still speak the old language, then only a few elders, then nobody. Of the world's roughly 7,000 languages, a large share are now in danger.

## The last speaker of Bo

The Andaman Islands were once home to about ten Great Andamanese languages. After outsiders arrived in the 1800s, disease and violence greatly reduced the islanders' numbers.

Boa Sr was born around 1925 and was the last speaker of Bo. For years, she had nobody to speak her mother tongue with. Linguists recorded some of her words and songs. When she died in January 2010, [Bo fell silent](people-languages-dying). Some Great Andamanese languages still survive, spoken by a small community in the [Andaman Islands](andaman).

India has hundreds of languages, many spoken by small tribal communities. UNESCO's 2009 atlas listed about 197 endangered languages in India, more than any other country. Some have no written form, so if speakers stop using them, they leave almost no trace.

## Why languages fade

Languages are not lost because they are simple or weak. Every language is complex. They fade because of pressure.

Children may be taught only in a national or global language at school. Families move to cities for work. Radio, television and [the internet](internet-how) use bigger languages. In some countries, children were once punished for speaking their own language. In the US, Canada and Australia, Indigenous children were sent to boarding schools where their languages were often banned.

When a community feels its language is old-fashioned or useless, parents may stop passing it on, even out of love, hoping to give children a better start.

## What gets lost

Each language is a different way of seeing. Some languages describe directions using north, south, east and west instead of left and right, even for things on a table. Others have detailed words for kinds of snow, rain or soil.

Local languages often hold knowledge about medicinal plants, fishing grounds or seasonal changes, built up over generations. Linguists compare losing a language to losing a library. It is a little like [losing a species](extinction-today): something that took thousands of years to grow can disappear in a lifetime.

During the Second World War, the US military used the [Navajo language](navajo-nation) to send coded messages that enemies couldn't break. The [code talkers](code-talkers) showed how valuable a small language could be, even though many of them had been punished as children for speaking it.

## Bringing languages back

Revival is hard but possible. Hebrew had been used for prayer and writing for over 2,000 years but was rarely anyone's first language. From the late 1800s, people began raising children in it, and it now has millions of speakers.

In [Hawaii](hawaii-culture), fewer than 50 children could speak Hawaiian by the early 1980s. Families started "language nest" preschools in 1984, and now there are Hawaiian-medium schools from preschool to university. In Britain, Cornish and Manx have been revived after their last native speakers died.

In India, communities are recording songs, making [dictionaries](dictionaries), and teaching children in their mother tongue in early grades. The UN has named 2022 to 2032 the International Decade of Indigenous Languages. A language survives when children speak it at play, and every new speaker counts.
