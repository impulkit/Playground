## The short answer

Making a book takes many people and many months. A writer writes it. Editors help improve it and check it. Designers choose the size, fonts and cover. Then the files go to a printer, who prints the pages on huge sheets of paper, folds them into bundles, binds them together and trims the edges.

A typical paperback might take a year or more from finished manuscript to bookshop. The printing itself can take only a few hours, even for thousands of copies.

## From manuscript to pages

A book starts as a manuscript, the writer's version. An editor reads it and suggests changes: cut this chapter, explain that idea, make this character braver. The writer revises. Then a copy-editor checks spelling, [grammar](grammar-why), facts and consistency, like whether a character's eyes stay brown all the way through.

A designer lays out the pages. They choose a typeface, decide how wide the margins are and where pictures go. A [picture book](picture-books) may be designed spread by spread, with the illustrator and designer working together. The pages are checked again as "proofs" to catch any last mistakes.

Every published book also gets an ISBN, an International Standard Book Number. Since 2007 it has had 13 digits, and the last one is a check digit worked out from the others. It is usually printed under a [barcode](barcodes) on the back cover.

## Printing on giant sheets

Most books are printed by offset lithography. The page images are put on thin metal plates. Oily [ink](ink) sticks to the image areas, and water keeps the blank areas clean. The ink passes from the plate to a rubber blanket and then onto the paper.

The paper is much bigger than a single page. One sheet might carry 16 or 32 pages, arranged in a pattern called an imposition. Some pages are upside down and in a strange order, so they end up correct once the sheet is folded.

This idea goes back to the early days of the [printing press](printing-press). [Johannes Gutenberg](printing-revolution)'s workshop in Germany printed its famous Bible in the 1450s, with more than one page on each sheet.

## Folding, sewing and binding

A machine folds each printed sheet into a bundle called a signature. The signatures are stacked in order, which is called gathering. Look at the top or bottom edge of a thick hardback and you can often see the separate bundles.

For a sewn binding, threads stitch through the fold of each signature and hold them together. This lets the book open flat and last a long time. Many paperbacks are "perfect bound" instead: the folds are trimmed off and the loose pages are [glued](glue-sticky) at the spine.

Then a big blade trims three edges so the pages can be turned. Hardbacks get stiff boards covered in cloth or paper, and sometimes a separate paper jacket.

## The life of a book

The [paper](paper-made) for most books comes from wood pulp, and many publishers now use paper from forests certified as well managed. Some books are printed in India and the US for local readers. Others are printed in one country and shipped around the world.

Once printed, a book goes to warehouses, shops and [libraries](libraries). Many are also turned into e-books and audiobooks. But the folded sheet has survived since the 1400s, because it works. Hold a book up to the light at the spine and you can still see the trace of every fold.
