## The short answer

Most land animals can paddle across a river, and many dinosaurs probably could too. Footprints scratched into old riverbeds show dinosaurs touching the bottom with their toes while floating.

The famous "sea monsters" like [plesiosaurs](marine-reptiles-plesiosaur) were reptiles, but not dinosaurs.

Spinosaurus is the big puzzle. Scientists disagree about how much time it spent in water. And today, the champion swimming dinosaurs are [penguins](penguins-cold), which are birds.

## The sea monsters next door

During the [age of dinosaurs](dino-age-span), the seas were ruled by other reptiles. Ichthyosaurs looked like dolphins. Plesiosaurs had small heads, [long necks](sauropod-necks) and four big flippers. Mosasaurs were giant swimming lizards related to today's monitor lizards.

None of them were dinosaurs. Scientists define dinosaurs by features of the hips and legs that let them stand upright on land. Sea reptiles came from different branches of the reptile family tree, and they moved to the sea on their own.

## Footprints in the water

In 2007, scientists described an unusual trackway in La Rioja, Spain. Instead of full footprints, the rock showed long scratch marks in pairs, left by the claws of a two-legged meat-eater. The animal was floating in water and kicking off the bottom, the way you might "walk" in a swimming pool on tiptoe.

Similar swim tracks have been found at St. George, in Utah, and in China. They show that some meat-eating dinosaurs were happy to cross rivers and lakes. That does not make them true swimmers, but it means water was no barrier.

## The Spinosaurus argument

Spinosaurus was a huge meat-eater from North Africa, with a long crocodile-like snout and a tall sail on its back. Its first fossils were found in Egypt and described in 1915. They were destroyed when a museum in Munich was bombed during the [Second World War](world-war-two).

New fossils from [Morocco](morocco), described in 2014 and 2020, revived the debate. They showed short back legs, dense bones like a [penguin's](penguin-flight) and a tall, flat tail like a paddle. One team argued Spinosaurus chased fish underwater, using its tail like a newt. In 2022, another team built a 3D model and argued it would have been unstable and slow in deep water. They think it waded in shallows like a giant heron, snatching fish. Both sides are still publishing new evidence.

## A diving bird in the dinosaur age

Some dinosaurs really did become expert swimmers. Hesperornis was a flightless bird that lived about 80 million years ago in a shallow sea that then covered the middle of North America. Its fossils have been found in Kansas.

It had tiny useless wings, teeth in its beak and powerful legs set far back, like a modern diving bird. It probably chased fish underwater, kicking with its feet, rather like a loon or grebe today.

## Today's swimming dinosaurs

Penguins "fly" underwater using their stiff wings as flippers. [Emperor penguins](ocean-penguins-swim) can dive deeper than 500 m. Ducks paddle, cormorants dive after fish, and gannets plunge into the sea from high above. Even the ancestors of whales and [dolphins](dolphins-click) took to the water from land, though they were mammals, not dinosaurs. Swimming has evolved again and again, which is why many scientists expect a few more dinosaurs to turn out to have been at home in water.
