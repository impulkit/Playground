## The short answer

Everything we know comes from [fossils](?fossil), traces of life saved in rock.

Fossils aren't only bones. Footprints, eggs, nests, skin prints and even poo all tell stories.

Scientists work like detectives, putting many small clues together into one picture. The evidence now includes many thousands of fossils, from every continent on Earth, found and checked by scientists in many countries.

## Before anyone knew

People found dinosaur bones long before anyone knew what they were. In China, big fossil bones were called [dragon bones](myths-chinese). In 1677, an English museum keeper named Robert Plot drew part of a huge thigh bone and guessed it came from a giant human. Today we know it was from Megalosaurus.

Some historians think that [ancient legends of griffins](myths-giants) in Central Asia may have been inspired by skulls of beaked dinosaurs eroding out of the desert. It is an interesting idea, though hard to prove.

## Building the case

In the early 1800s, scientists began comparing fossil bones with the bones of living animals. The French scientist Georges Cuvier showed that some fossils came from animals that no longer existed anywhere. In 1824, William Buckland named Megalosaurus. A year later, the English doctor Gideon Mantell named Iguanodon, from teeth found in Sussex, some of them reportedly spotted by his wife, Mary Ann. They looked like an iguana's teeth, only many times bigger. In 1842, Richard Owen grouped these strange reptiles together and called them dinosaurs.

Since then, over 1,000 kinds of dinosaur have been named. Some are known from a single tooth. Others, like T. rex, are known from dozens of skeletons. Each new find can confirm or overturn earlier ideas, which is how science should work.

## Clue by clue

A single bone can tell you whether an animal walked on two legs or four. Teeth show what it ate: blade-like teeth for meat, leaf-shaped teeth for plants. Rough patches on bones show where muscles attached, and growth rings inside show how old the animal was.

Some fossils keep skin prints, showing scales like a stamp in mud. A few duck-billed dinosaurs have been found with large areas of skin preserved, sometimes called dinosaur [mummies](mummies).

## Tracks tell stories

Footprints show how animals moved. Long gaps between prints mean running. Many parallel tracks mean a herd walking together. In 1976, a zoologist worked out a way to estimate speed from the spacing of [footprints](dino-feet-tracks) and the size of the feet.

India alone has almost every kind of clue. There are bones from Jabalpur, found in 1828, nests of eggs in Madhya Pradesh and Gujarat, footprints in Rajasthan and fossil poo from Maharashtra. Each was found by different people, at different times, yet they fit together.

Other clues show behaviour. Tooth marks on bones show who tried to eat whom. Eggs and nests show how dinosaurs raised their young. [Fossil poo](dino-poop) shows what they had for lunch.

## Reading the whole site

Scientists also study the rock around the fossil to learn whether it was a river, a desert or a lake. See [how rocks are diaries](ancient-env). Layers of volcanic ash above and below a fossil can be dated, so scientists know [how old it is](fossil-age).

It is the same way [historians](history-sources) and [archaeologists](archaeology) work with the human past. No single clue proves everything. But when bones, tracks, eggs, rocks and dates all agree, the picture becomes very hard to doubt.
