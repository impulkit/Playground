## The short answer

In 1980, a father-and-son team, the physicist Luis Alvarez and the geologist Walter Alvarez, reported something strange about a thin layer of clay near Gubbio in Italy. It was full of [iridium](?iridium), a metal that is rare in [Earth's crust](earth-layers-crust) but common in asteroids.

Then the same layer turned up all over the world, in rocks of the same age, about 66 million years old. Every clue pointed to one thing: an enormous impact from space. A decade later, the [crater](?crater) itself was found, buried under Mexico.

## A clock that went wrong

Walter Alvarez wanted to know how long the thin clay took to form. Tiny bits of space dust fall on Earth all the time, at a fairly steady rate, so the team thought iridium could work like a clock.

The clock broke. There was roughly 30 times more iridium than expected. Either the clay had taken an impossibly long time to settle, or a huge amount of space rock had arrived all at once. The team chose the second idea and suggested an asteroid about 10 km across. That is roughly the height of Mount Everest above sea level, as a single rock.

## Other clues in the stripe

Iridium alone wasn't enough to convince everyone. Volcanoes can bring up a little iridium from deep [inside Earth](inside-earth), and India's [Deccan](deccan) volcanoes were erupting around the same time. So scientists looked for more clues.

They found shocked quartz: sand grains with criss-cross scars that form only under a sudden, violent squeeze, like the one from a giant impact. They found tiny glass beads called spherules, made when melted rock was thrown into the sky and rained back down. Near the Gulf of Mexico, they found jumbled rock that looked as if giant waves had dumped it.

## Finding the crater

The big question was simple: if a huge rock hit, where is the hole? Much of Earth is ocean, and old seafloor slowly sinks back into the planet, so the crater might have vanished.

It hadn't. Geologists working for Mexico's oil company had noticed a giant ring buried under the Yucatán Peninsula years earlier. In 1991, Alan Hildebrand and his colleagues showed it was an [impact crater](india-wonder-lonar) about 180 km wide, and the right age. It was named Chicxulub, after a village near its centre. Later dating has matched the crater and the clay layer very closely.

## Reading the layers

Below the clay, rocks contain the bones of non-bird dinosaurs, along with [ammonites](ammonites) and other animals that vanished. Above it, none appear. Rocks are laid down one layer on top of another, a bit like newspapers piling up in a corner, so the clay marks a single moment in time. Scientists use the same idea of layers in [archaeology](archaeology) and in working out the [age of fossils](fossil-age).

Not every scientist thinks [the asteroid](extinction) acted alone. Some argue the Deccan eruptions had already stressed life on Earth. But nearly all agree the impact was the main blow.

## A stripe you can visit

The boundary clay can be seen in many places: near Gubbio, and in the badlands of Montana and Colorado. In India, scientists found an iridium-rich layer between old lava flows at Anjar in Kutch, Gujarat. In some spots you can put your finger on it. It looks like nothing much, just a pale or dark line in a cliff. It marks the moment the age of the dinosaurs ended.
