## The short answer

When the asteroid hit about 66 million years ago, roughly three quarters of all species died out. The survivors had a few things in common. Most were small, ate many different foods, and could hide or wait out hard times.

Animals in rivers and lakes did better than those on land. Their food chain started with dead leaves and mud, not with fresh plants needing sunlight. Big animals that needed huge amounts of fresh food had the hardest time.

## A world in the dark

The impact threw dust, soot and droplets of sulphur high into the sky. For months, perhaps years, much less sunlight reached the ground. Plants struggled, and many animals that ate plants starved. Then the hunters that ate them starved too.

A [food chain](?foodchain) is only as strong as its first link. When green plants stopped growing, everything that depended on them was in trouble. That is why the giants, which needed tonnes of fresh food, were hit hardest.

## Small and not fussy

Small animals need less food, can shelter in burrows and holes, and breed quickly. Tiny mammals, many no bigger than a rat, could eat insects, worms, seeds and roots. If one food ran out, they switched.

These survivors became the ancestors of every mammal alive today, from mice to [elephants](elephants-talk) to you. What they did next is the story of the [rise of the mammals](mammals-rise).

## Life in rivers and lakes

[Crocodiles](living-fossils), turtles, frogs and salamanders did surprisingly well. In fresh water, food chains often start with fallen leaves, dead plants and mud, which stay around long after plants stop growing. Water also cools down and warms up slowly, protecting animals from sudden changes.

Crocodiles have another advantage: a big one can go months without eating. Their family has outlasted the dinosaurs by 66 million years so far.

Lizards and [snakes](lost-titanoboa) had a harder time. Studies of fossils from North America suggest most kinds there died out. The few that made it were mostly small.

## The birds that made it

Most birds of the time died too, including some toothed kinds. But a few small groups survived. One study suggests that forests were wiped out across much of the world, so birds that lived on the ground did best. Seeds lying in soil gave them food, since [seeds](seeds-fly) can wait for years before sprouting.

From those few survivors came every bird alive today. So the dinosaurs didn't all vanish. [A pigeon](lost-passenger-pigeon) on a balcony in Delhi or a turkey on a farm in Ohio is a living dinosaur.

## Life in the sea

In the oceans, the great [sea reptiles](ocean-reptiles), like mosasaurs, disappeared, and so did the [ammonites](ammonites), coiled shellfish that had lived for hundreds of millions of years. Many tiny floating creatures at the base of the sea's food chain also died out.

Yet many sharks, bony fish and [sea turtles](sea-turtles) survived. Sharks are older than the dinosaurs, and they have now made it through several mass extinctions.

## Luck and habits

Survival wasn't only about being clever or strong. Where an animal lived, what it ate and how big it was mattered most. Some scientists add a large helping of luck. A small furry creature in a burrow, in the right place, at the right time, became the starting point for a whole new age.
