## The short answer

Sabre-toothed cats like Smilodon probably used their long fangs for a precise killing bite, not for stabbing wildly or crushing bone. They first pinned their prey down with powerful front legs and a thick neck. Then they opened their jaws very wide and drove the fangs into the soft throat.

The fangs were long but fairly thin and could snap. So the strategy was "grab first, bite carefully". Computer models show Smilodon's jaw muscles were weaker than a lion's, but its neck and shoulders were very strong.

## A cat built like a wrestler

The best-known sabre-toothed cat is Smilodon, which lived in the Americas during the [ice ages](ice-ages). The largest species, from South America, may have weighed up to about 400 kg, heavier than most tigers. It had a short tail, long powerful front legs and a body built more for strength than speed.

Its upper fangs could reach about 28 cm long, including the part buried in the jaw. That is longer than a big banana. They were flattened from side to side, with fine serrations along the edge in young adults, like a steak knife. Like [your own teeth](teeth), they came in two sets. Young Smilodon had milk sabres first, and for a while the adult sabres grew in beside them, so a youngster could have four long fangs at once.

## The wide-open jaw

To use teeth that long, a cat needs to open its mouth very wide. Otherwise the lower jaw would be in the way. Smilodon could open its jaws to about 110 degrees. A lion manages about 65. Its skull had special joints and muscle attachments that made this possible.

In 2007, scientists built a detailed [computer model](computers-how) of a Smilodon skull and tested it like an engineer tests a bridge. The skull was strong when pushing down with the neck, but weaker than a lion's when pulling sideways. That suggested a quick, controlled bite into a held-down animal, rather than a long struggle.

## The tar pit cats

The best place in the world to study Smilodon is the [La Brea Tar Pits](la-brea) in [Los Angeles](los-angeles). Sticky tar oozed up through the ground there for thousands of years. Big plant-eaters got stuck, and meat-eaters that came to eat them got stuck too. Scientists have dug up the bones of thousands of Smilodon from the pits.

Some of these skeletons show old injuries that had healed, such as damaged hips or legs. A badly injured hunter could not easily feed itself. Some scientists think this means Smilodon lived in groups and shared food, like lions. Others think the injured cats simply scavenged. Smilodon is now the state fossil of California.

## Sabres again and again

Long sabre-like teeth evolved more than once. Several different groups of meat-eating mammals grew them, including a South American relative of marsupials that was not a cat at all. Sabre-toothed cats also lived in Africa, Europe and Asia. Their fossils have been found in the Siwalik Hills, along the foot of the Himalayas in India.

## Where did they go?

Smilodon died out about 10,000 years ago, at around the same time as [mammoths](mammoths), [giant sloths](giant-sloths) and many other [big ice age animals](ice-age-animals) in the Americas. The climate was warming, and humans had arrived and were hunting the same large prey. Scientists still debate how much each mattered. Without big, slow animals to hold down, a cat built like a wrestler had little to eat.
