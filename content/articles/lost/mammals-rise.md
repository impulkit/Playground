## The short answer

[When the big dinosaurs disappeared](what-if-dinos-lived), their "jobs" (big plant-eater, big hunter) were left empty. [Mammals](?mammal) spread into those jobs. Over millions of years they became horses, elephants, bats, whales, monkeys and, eventually, humans.

This kind of change, where living things slowly change over many generations, is called [evolution](?evolution). [The asteroid](extinction) didn't create mammals. It cleared the stage for them.

## Life in the shadows

Mammals are almost as old as dinosaurs. The first ones appeared more than 200 million years ago, and for a very long time most stayed small, from shrew-sized to about the size of a cat.

Many were probably [active at night](nocturnal-animals). Small eyes, sharp [hearing](ears-hear), a good sense of smell and warm, furry bodies helped them hunt insects in the dark, when big reptiles were less of a danger. Some scientists think this long night life is why many mammals today still [see colours less well](colour-blindness) than birds and reptiles do.

They were not all timid. Repenomamus, from China, was about the size of a badger. One fossil has the bones of a young dinosaur inside its stomach.

## The day everything changed

About 66 million years ago, an asteroid hit what is now Mexico. Dust and soot darkened the skies, and plants struggled to grow. Most big land animals starved.

Many mammals died too. But the [survivors](who-survived) had useful habits. They were small, could shelter in burrows and ate whatever they found: insects, seeds, roots. Small animals also need less food and breed quickly, so their numbers could bounce back.

## Growing into an empty world

For a long time, the fossil record from just after the disaster was patchy. Then, in 2019, a team working at Corral Bluffs near Colorado Springs described thousands of fossils from the first million years. They showed how fast things moved. Within a million years, some mammals weighed tens of kilograms, about as much as a big dog.

Much of this came from fossils hidden inside hard, rounded lumps of rock called concretions. For years, collectors had walked past them. Once the team learned what a lump with a skull inside looked like, they found them all over the hillsides. Teeth helped most of all. Mammal teeth are tough, so they fossilise well, and their bumps and ridges show whether an animal ate insects, seeds or leaves.

Plants changed alongside them. Early relatives of [beans](pulses-dal) and walnuts appeared, giving mammals rich new foods. Bigger bodies and new foods seem to have gone together.

## New shapes everywhere

Over the next 20 million years, mammals spread into almost every way of life. Bats took to the air, the only mammals that can really fly. Some hoofed land mammals slowly returned to the water and became [whales](whales-walked). Early relatives of [elephants](elephants-talk) grew in Africa, and India, then an island drifting north, had its own mammals.

Nothing did this on purpose. In each generation, the animals best suited to their surroundings left a few more young. Over enormous spans of time, small differences added up.

## Us

Primates, the group that includes [monkeys, apes and humans](human-evolution), appear in the fossil record soon after the asteroid. Our own species turned up only about 300,000 years ago, in Africa. If the 66 million years since the asteroid were squeezed into one day, modern humans would arrive in the last seven minutes.
