## The short answer

The Bone Wars were a bitter race between two American fossil hunters, Edward Drinker Cope and Othniel Charles Marsh, in the late 1800s. Each wanted to find and name more new animals than the other.

They hired teams to dig across the American West, bribed each other's workers, spread rumours and attacked each other in print. Sometimes their teams even destroyed fossils so the other side could not have them. Yet the race also produced some of the most famous dinosaurs ever named, including [Triceratops](triceratops-horns) and [Stegosaurus](stegosaurus).

## Friends turned rivals

Cope and Marsh met in Germany in 1864 and at first got on well. They even named fossils after each other. Marsh worked at Yale University and had a rich uncle, the banker George Peabody, who paid for Yale's natural history museum. Cope was a brilliant but hot-tempered scientist from a wealthy [Philadelphia](usa-wonder-philadelphia) family.

At that time, very few dinosaurs were known in North America. The best was Hadrosaurus, a duck-billed plant-eater dug up in New Jersey in 1858. It had been described by Joseph Leidy, a careful Philadelphia scientist who had helped train Cope. Leidy later grew tired of the fighting and turned to other kinds of science.

Things went sour in the late 1860s. Cope described a long-necked [sea reptile](ocean-reptiles) called Elasmosaurus, but put its head on the end of its tail. The mistake was pointed out publicly. Soon after, Marsh paid workers at a quarry Cope was using to send the best fossils to him instead.

## A race across the West

In the 1870s, [railways](steam-engine) were spreading across the American West, and diggers found huge bones in Colorado and Wyoming. At Como Bluff in Wyoming, two [railway workers](transcontinental-railroad) wrote to Marsh about bones sticking out of a ridge. It became one of the richest dinosaur sites in the world.

Both men hired collectors who worked in tough, lonely conditions. They spied on each other's camps and lured away each other's workers. Some teams smashed bones they could not carry, rather than leave them for rivals. Some digs took place on land that belonged to [Native American nations](native-nations). On a trip in 1874, Marsh met the Lakota leader Red Cloud, and later spoke up for him against corrupt government agents.

## Racing to print

Speed mattered most, because the first published name wins. Cope sometimes sent descriptions to journals by telegram. Both men named animals from just a few bones, and sometimes named the same animal twice. That rush caused muddles that took scientists decades to sort out, including the long confusion over [Brontosaurus](dino-names).

In 1890, the feud spilled into the New York Herald, a popular [newspaper](news-media). Cope accused Marsh of stealing ideas and mismanaging government money. Marsh fired back. Readers enjoyed the scandal, but it damaged the reputation of both men.

## What they left behind

By the end, Marsh had named about 80 new kinds of dinosaur and Cope about 56. Many are still accepted. Marsh named Triceratops, Stegosaurus, Allosaurus and Diplodocus. Cope named Camarasaurus and Coelophysis, among many other fossil animals.

The rivalry ruined them both. Cope lost much of his fortune in bad investments, and Marsh lost his government funding. Cope died in 1897 and Marsh in 1899. Their fossils still fill the great halls of museums in New York, New Haven, Washington and Philadelphia. Some crates of their bones sat unopened for many years after they died.
