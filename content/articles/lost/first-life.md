## The short answer

The first living things were single cells, far too small to see without a microscope. They lived in water, at least 3.5 billion years ago, when Earth was a very different planet, with no oxygen in the air and no plants on land. They were probably similar in some ways to today's bacteria and other [microbes](?microbe).

The oldest widely accepted fossils of life are stromatolites, layered rocks built by communities of microbes, found in Australia and about 3.5 billion years old. Some scientists think life began even earlier, over 4 billion years ago. Where and how life first appeared is one of the great open questions in science.

## A young, strange Earth

[Earth formed](earth-age) about 4.5 billion years ago. At first it was battered by asteroids and covered in hot, molten rock. Over time it cooled, oceans formed, and the air filled with gases from [volcanoes](volcano), including carbon dioxide, water vapour and nitrogen, but almost no oxygen.

Somewhere in this world, simple chemicals began joining into more complex ones. At some point, a few of these were able to copy themselves. Many scientists think an early form of RNA, a close cousin of [DNA](dna), could both carry information and do chemical work, and may have come first. This idea is called the "RNA world".

## Rocks built by microbes

Stromatolites form when sticky mats of microbes trap sand and mud. The microbes grow up through each layer to reach sunlight, and over years they build mounds, layer on layer, like a pile of pancakes.

Fossil stromatolites about 3.5 billion years old have been found in the Pilbara region of Western Australia. Older signs of life have been claimed from rocks in [Greenland](world-greenland) and Canada, but scientists still argue about them, because similar patterns can form without life.

India has ancient stromatolites too. Rocks near Udaipur and [Chittorgarh](chittorgarh) in Rajasthan contain stromatolites well over a billion years old. Living ones still grow in Shark Bay, Australia, where the water is too salty for snails that would graze them away.

## Where did life begin?

There are two leading ideas. Some scientists think life began at [hydrothermal vents](deep-sea-vents) on the sea floor, where hot, mineral-rich water provides energy and chemicals. Others favour shallow pools on land that dried out and filled again, concentrating chemicals each time. In 1871, Charles Darwin wondered in a letter whether life might have started in a "warm little pond".

In 1952, Stanley Miller, a student working with Harold Urey at the University of Chicago, tried to copy early Earth in a glass flask. He used water, gases and electric sparks to act like [lightning](lightning). Within days, the water contained amino acids, building blocks of proteins. It was a major step, but no living thing appeared.

## Microbes that changed the air

For billions of years, life stayed small. Some of those microbes, called cyanobacteria, learned to use sunlight to make food by [photosynthesis](photosynthesis), giving off oxygen as waste. Around 2.4 billion years ago, oxygen began building up in the air, an event called the Great Oxidation.

That oxygen later made larger, more active life possible, including animals. It also changed the rocks. Iron dissolved in the oceans reacted with oxygen and settled on the sea floor as red, rust-coloured layers. Many of the world's big iron ore deposits, including some in India, are ancient banded rocks like these. Every breath you take depends on what those tiny cells started.
