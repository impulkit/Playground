## The short answer

Yes. Birds belong to a group of dinosaurs called [theropods](?theropod), the same group as T. rex and [Velociraptor](velociraptor-real).

Scientists have matched dozens of features, from wishbones to the way some dinosaurs [sat on their nests](wild-penguin-dads). Feathered fossils from China, and studies of how animals are related, all point the same way.

So the dinosaurs never fully disappeared. One small, feathered branch made it through [the asteroid](extinction) strike 66 million years ago and is still here. There are about 11,000 kinds of bird alive today, which means there are more kinds of dinosaur now than there are kinds of mammal.

## The fossil that started it

In 1861, quarry workers in Germany found Archaeopteryx in fine limestone that had once been the mud of a lagoon. It was about the size of a crow, with feathers and wings like a bird, but teeth, clawed fingers and a long bony tail like a dinosaur. It looked like a perfect in-between.

Two years earlier, Charles Darwin had published his book on evolution. The timing was striking. A few years later, the English scientist Thomas Henry Huxley noticed how much Archaeopteryx resembled a small dinosaur called Compsognathus. He suggested that birds and dinosaurs were related. The idea then fell out of favour for most of the next hundred years.

## Feathered dinosaurs everywhere

In the 1970s, the American scientist John Ostrom compared Archaeopteryx with the small hunter Deinonychus and revived Huxley's idea. Then, in 1996, a farmer in Liaoning, China, found a small dinosaur called Sinosauropteryx with a coat of fuzzy filaments. It was the first non-bird dinosaur found with feather-like coverings.

Since then, hundreds of feathered dinosaurs have been found, especially in China. Some had simple fuzz, others full wings. Microraptor had long feathers on both its arms and its legs, like a biplane with four wings. [Feathers came long before flight](feathers).

## Bones that match

Birds and [theropods](?theropod) share wishbones, hollow bones, three main toes and a special wrist joint that folds. Even their [eggs](eggs) and nesting habits match. Oviraptor relatives have been found sitting on nests, arms spread over the eggs like a hen.

Theropods also breathed like birds. Their bones have holes where air sacs reached inside, part of a breathing system that keeps fresh air flowing through the [lungs](lungs-breathe). It helps birds fly high and fast today. [See how a bird's body flies](how-birds-fly).

## Surviving the asteroid

When [the asteroid hit](space-asteroid-defence), almost all dinosaurs died. Only some birds survived. Many scientists think the survivors were small, ground-living birds with beaks, able to eat seeds when the forests collapsed. Birds that lived in trees, and toothed birds, did not make it.

## So what is a dinosaur today?

Scientists call the ones that died out "non-avian dinosaurs". Birds are "avian dinosaurs". So crows, [pigeons](pigeons-homing) and peacocks are all dinosaurs, [some very clever ones](crow-smart). Their closest living relatives outside the dinosaurs are [crocodiles and alligators](croc-ancient), which also build nests and call to their young.

## Dinosaurs near you

India's national bird, the peacock, and the USA's national bird, the [bald eagle](bald-eagle), are both theropod dinosaurs. [An eagle's foot](eagles-hunt), with three forward toes and sharp curved claws, follows the same plan as the feet that left three-toed tracks across the dinosaur age. The [ostrich](penguin-flight), the biggest living bird, sprints at about 70 km/h on long, scaly legs that would not look out of place on a small dinosaur. Even a sparrow hopping on a windowsill is part of the family.
