## The short answer

[Amber](?amber) is tree resin that hardened into stone over millions of years. Resin is the thick, sticky liquid that oozes from a cut in some trees, such as pines. It seals wounds and traps insects that might attack the tree.

When a small creature got stuck in the resin, more resin often flowed over it and sealed it in. Buried in soil and squeezed over time, the resin slowly hardened. The animal inside was kept in its exact shape, down to tiny hairs and the hooks on its legs. Amber gives us a window into ancient forests that ordinary fossils cannot.

## A sticky trap

Most fossils form when bones or shells are buried in mud and turn to stone, as explained in [how a bone turns into stone](fossils-form). Amber works differently. The creature is not replaced by minerals. Its outline is sealed in a clear, golden case.

That is why amber fossils can show things that almost never survive, like the wings of gnats, spider webs and even a spider attacking its prey. Small animals are trapped most often: flies, beetles, [ants](ants-farm) and mites. Bigger animals usually managed to pull free. Lizards, frogs and small feathers have been found, but they are rare.

## A tail in amber

In 2015, a scientist named Lida Xing was looking at amber in a market in Myanmar. One piece, being sold for jewellery, held something odd: a small, feathered tail. Tests showed it came from a young dinosaur, about 99 million years old.

The tail had bones as well as feathers, so it could not be a bird's tail feather. The [feathers](feathers) were soft and fluffy, without the stiff central shaft of a flight feather. It was the first time anyone had seen dinosaur feathers in three dimensions, still attached to the body.

Amber from Myanmar raises hard questions, though. Some mines lie in conflict zones, and money from amber may fund fighting. In 2020, an international society of fossil scientists asked researchers to stop working on Myanmar amber bought after 2017.

## Amber from Gujarat

India has its own famous amber. In a lignite coal mine near Vastan, in Gujarat's Surat district, scientists found lumps of amber about 52 million years old. Inside were hundreds of kinds of [ancient insects](lost-giant-insects) and spiders.

This amber formed soon after India, which had been drifting north as an island, collided with Asia. Surprisingly, the creatures inside are close relatives of ones from other parts of the world. So animals may have crossed between India and Asia, or spread before India set off on [its long journey](india-drift). The resin came from trees in the same family as today's sal trees, still common in [Indian forests](forests-india).

## Could we clone a dinosaur?

In the [film](hollywood) Jurassic Park, scientists take dinosaur blood from a mosquito trapped in amber and use its [DNA](dna) to bring dinosaurs back. It is a clever idea, but it would not work.

DNA is a long, fragile molecule. After an animal dies, it starts to break into pieces. Studies of very old bones suggest that even in perfect conditions it is gone within a few million years at the most. The dinosaurs died out 66 million years ago. Amber seals the shape of a creature, but not its DNA.

## Gold of the sea

Amber is light, only a little heavier than seawater, so storms in the Baltic Sea in northern Europe wash lumps of it onto the beaches. People there have collected Baltic amber for thousands of years to carve into beads and jewellery. Today amber is still sold as a gem, and every now and then a jeweller spots something with legs inside.
