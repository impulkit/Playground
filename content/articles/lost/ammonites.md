## The short answer

Ammonites were sea animals that lived inside coiled shells. They were cephalopods, the group that includes squid, octopuses and cuttlefish. A squid-like body with tentacles poked out of the open end of the shell.

They appeared about 400 million years ago, survived the [Great Dying](great-dying) by a whisker, and became common in the seas of the dinosaur age. Then, 66 million years ago, they died out in the same disaster that ended the giant dinosaurs. Their shells are among the most common fossils on Earth.

## A shell like a submarine

An ammonite shell was divided into chambers by thin walls. As the animal grew, it moved forward and sealed off the old chamber behind it. The animal lived only in the last and biggest chamber.

The older chambers were filled with gas and liquid. A thin tube ran through all of them. By moving liquid in or out through this tube, the ammonite could make itself lighter or heavier, so it could float at the depth it wanted. A [submarine](submarines) uses the same idea with its tanks. The walls between chambers were often folded into frilly patterns, which may have helped the thin shell resist water pressure.

## Big, small and strange

Most ammonites were palm-sized or smaller. The largest known, found in Germany, had a shell about as wide as a grown man is tall. Some ammonites were not neatly coiled at all. Later kinds grew shells shaped like hooks, paper clips or loose corkscrews.

The name comes from Ammon, an Egyptian god often shown with curled ram's horns. The Roman writer Pliny the Elder called these fossils "horns of Ammon". In Alberta, Canada, some ammonite shells kept their shimmering mother-of-pearl layer for 70 million years. Polished, it becomes a rainbow-coloured gem called ammolite.

Their closest living relatives are squid and octopuses, not the nautilus, even though the nautilus has a similar chambered shell. An [octopus](octopus-smart) and an ammonite shared an ancestor long ago. The octopus lineage lost its outer shell and the ammonites kept theirs.

## Sacred stones and snakestones

In Nepal, the Kali Gandaki river carries black stones that break open to show ammonite fossils. Hindus call these stones shaligrams and worship them as a form of Lord Vishnu. Pilgrims travel to the river valley near Muktinath, and shaligrams are kept in homes and temples in India and [Nepal](nepal). The rocks formed in an ancient sea before the Himalayas rose.

In England, ammonites were once called snakestones. A legend in the town of Whitby said that a saint had turned snakes to stone. Fossil sellers sometimes carved snake heads onto them for tourists. [Mary Anning](mary-anning) sold ammonites on the beach at Lyme Regis.

## Clocks in the rock

Because ammonites changed fast and spread widely, geologists use them to [date rocks](fossil-age). Each species lasted only a short time. A geologist who finds a particular kind knows roughly when that layer formed. In [Kutch](gujarat-rann), in Gujarat, rocks packed with ammonites help geologists date the layers laid down when that area was a shallow sea in the Jurassic.

## Why they vanished

When [the asteroid](extinction) struck 66 million years ago, the seas changed fast. Ammonite babies were tiny and drifted near the surface, where the food chain collapsed. The nautilus, which lays larger eggs and lives in deeper water, came through. The ammonites, after more than 300 million years, did not.
