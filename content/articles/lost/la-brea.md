## The short answer

La Brea is a group of pools where sticky natural asphalt, a kind of tar, oozes up from underground. For tens of thousands of years, animals walked in and couldn't get out. The tar soaked their bones and kept them safe.

Scientists have dug out millions of [fossils](?fossil) there: Columbian mammoths, giant ground sloths, sabre-toothed cats, dire wolves, [camels](camels), birds, insects and even seeds and pollen. It is one of the richest Ice Age fossil sites anywhere, and it sits beside a busy road in the [second-largest city](cities-grow) in the United States.

## Where the tar comes from

Deep under Los Angeles there is oil, formed over millions of years from tiny sea creatures buried in mud. Some of it seeps up through cracks. Near the surface, the lighter parts evaporate and leave behind thick, black asphalt, the same kind of stuff used in [oil and coal](oil-coal) industries and on [roads](roads-built).

The name is a small joke. *La brea* means "the tar" in [Spanish](spanish-language), so "the La Brea Tar Pits" means "the the tar tar pits". People living nearby used the tar long before the city existed. The Tongva people used it to waterproof baskets and seal their canoes.

## A trap for hunters

When a horse or bison got stuck, it struggled and called out. Wolves and big cats came to eat it, and got stuck too. That is why La Brea has far more meat-eaters than plant-eaters. Scientists call this a predator trap.

Getting stuck didn't take much. A layer of asphalt only a few centimetres deep could hold a horse, especially on a warm day when the tar was soft. Leaves and dust often hid the surface, and rainwater sometimes pooled on top, so a thirsty animal might walk in to drink.

The count is lopsided. More than 4,000 dire wolves have been found, and more than 2,000 sabre-toothed cats. In most places, plant-eaters far outnumber hunters, so La Brea gives a rare, crowded look at the [sabre-toothed cat](sabre-tooth) and its rivals. The cat is now California's state fossil.

## Ice Age Los Angeles

These animals lived during the last [Ice Age](ice-ages), between about 50,000 and 10,000 years ago. Los Angeles was cooler and wetter then, with woodland, grassland and streams. Many of the big animals, like the [mammoths](mammoths), have since died out.

Because the pits trapped things over such a long time, scientists can compare bones from different layers. They can see how animals and plants changed as the [climate](?climate) warmed. Scientists still argue over how much of the great die-off was caused by a changing climate and how much by the arrival of human hunters.

## One human

Among the millions of bones, only one human skeleton has been found: a woman who lived about 10,000 years ago. Museum staff call her La Brea Woman. She is a reminder that people were already living in California while mammoths still walked there.

## Still working

The pits still bubble today, as gas rises through the tar and pops at the surface. In summer, fresh asphalt seeps up in lawns and car parks, and staff put up cones around it.

In 2006, builders digging an underground car park for a museum next door found 16 new fossil deposits. Rather than dig through them on the spot, workers boxed them up in wooden crates and moved them to the museum grounds. Scientists are still cleaning those crates, bone by bone, and visitors can watch through a window.
