## The short answer

Scientists put fossil feathers under super-powerful [microscopes](microscopes) and found tiny colour packets called [melanosomes](?melanosome).

Their shape is the clue. Round packets make ginger-brown. Long, thin packets make black or grey. No packets means white.

That's how we know little Sinosauropteryx had a ginger-and-white striped tail, a bit like a [red panda](red-panda). For most dinosaurs, though, no colour clues survive, so the colours in books are still an artist's best guess.

## Looking very closely

Melanosomes are far too small to see with an ordinary microscope. They are made of melanin, the same pigment that colours your hair and skin. In 2008, a scientist studying fossil feathers noticed that tiny shapes once thought to be bacteria matched melanosomes in living birds.

In 2010, two teams used this idea on dinosaurs for the first time. They compared the shapes in fossils with those in modern bird feathers, where the colours are known. Matching the shapes told them the colours. It is a bit like recognising [a friend's handwriting](words-handwriting). You don't need to see them write, because the shapes of the letters give them away. The fossils have to be special, though. They must come from very fine mud or volcanic ash, where feathers were pressed flat and sealed away from air.

## Colours we now know

Sinosauropteryx, found in China, had a ginger tail with pale stripes, a darker back and a bandit-like mask around its eyes. Anchiornis, a small feathered dinosaur, had grey and black body feathers, black-and-white wings and a reddish crest, like a woodpecker.

Microraptor, the four-winged glider, was glossy black with a shimmer, like a crow or starling. That shine comes from melanosomes lined up in neat layers. It probably helped with showing off, as [crow feathers](crow-smart) catch the light today.

## Patterns that hide

Colours can also tell us how dinosaurs lived. Psittacosaurus, a small horned dinosaur from China, was darker on top and paler underneath. Scientists even built a model and lit it to test the pattern. It matched animals that live in shady forests.

In 2017, a heavily armoured dinosaur from Alberta, Canada, called Borealopelta, was found with its skin preserved. It was reddish-brown on top and lighter below. An animal as big and armoured as a small tank still needed to [hide](camouflage), which tells us its predators must have been fearsome. A [tiger's stripes](tiger-stripes) work in a similar way, helping it vanish in long grass.

## What we still can't see

Melanosomes only tell part of the story. Many birds get their bright reds and yellows from pigments in their food, such as the pink of a flamingo. Those pigments break down much more easily and are rarely found in fossils. Blues and greens in birds often come from the structure of the feather rather than pigment, and are hard to detect.

So a dinosaur might have had bright patches we can't see. Some scientists also argue that a few of the tiny shapes could still be bacteria, so they check each case carefully.

## Why it matters

Colour is one of the hardest parts of an animal to rebuild from fossils. Bones give the shape, but the outside is usually guesswork. Every real colour clue helps artists and scientists make better pictures, and shows which parts of a [reconstruction](how-reconstruct) are evidence and which are imagination. Some living animals, like the [octopus](octopus-smart), can change colour in a blink. For dinosaurs, we are still finding out what colours they wore at all.
