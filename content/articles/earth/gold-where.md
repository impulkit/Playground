## The short answer

Gold atoms are not made on Earth. Ordinary stars, like the Sun, can build light elements such as carbon and oxygen, but not heavy ones like gold. That takes something far more violent.

Scientists think most gold was forged when two [neutron stars](space-weird-neutron-spoon), the super-dense leftovers of dead stars, crashed into each other. The crash sprayed gold and other heavy metals into space. Some of that dust ended up in the cloud that formed our Solar System. Inside Earth, hot water and moving rock later gathered the gold into veins where miners can find it.

## Made in a star crash

In August 2017, detectors in the USA and Italy picked up ripples in space itself, called gravitational waves. They came from two neutron stars colliding about 130 million [light-years](light-years) away. Telescopes around the world, including NASA spacecraft, then turned to look. They saw the glow of freshly made heavy elements.

That one crash was thought to have made gold weighing several times as much as Earth. Some scientists think certain rare exploding stars, a kind of [supernova](supernova), make gold too. The debate is about how much each kind of event contributes.

## Why gold isn't deep down

When Earth was young and partly molten, heavy metals sank towards the centre. Most of Earth's gold probably went with the iron into the core, far beyond reach. Many geologists think the gold we mine today arrived a little later, delivered by [meteorites](meteorites) that hit Earth after the core had formed.

Near the surface, hot water flowing through cracks dissolves tiny amounts of gold and leaves it behind in veins, often inside white quartz. When those rocks wear away, rivers carry the heavy gold flakes and drop them in gravel. That is where most gold rushes began.

## Rushes and mines

In January 1848, a carpenter named James Marshall spotted shiny flakes in a stream at Sutter's Mill, in California. Within a year, tens of thousands of people were heading west. They were called the [forty-niners](gold-rush), after the year 1849. The small port of [San Francisco](san-francisco) grew into a city almost overnight.

India has deep history with gold mining too. The Kolar Gold Fields in [Karnataka](karnataka), about 100 km from Bengaluru, had shafts more than 3 km deep, among the deepest in the world. It closed in 2001. The Hutti mine in Karnataka still produces gold today.

## Rare, by the numbers

Gold is about 19 times heavier than the same amount of water. So the 200,000 tonnes or so mined in all of history, divided by 19, fills only about 11,000 cubic metres. That makes a cube roughly 22 m on each side. It would stand about as tall as a seven-storey building.

Miners work hard for it. In a typical mine today, a tonne of rock may hold only a few grams of gold, about the weight of a single ring.

## Why people love it

Gold almost never rusts or tarnishes. A gold coin from [ancient Rome](ancient-rome) still shines. It is also very soft. Goldsmiths can beat it into sheets so thin that light shines through, and Indian sweet makers sometimes use edible gold and silver leaf, called vark.

Because gold is rare, heavy and long-lasting, people have used it as [money](money-worth) and savings for thousands of years. Indian families together hold a huge share of the world's gold, much of it as jewellery passed down at weddings and festivals.
