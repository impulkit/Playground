## The short answer

Soil is made from rock that has been broken down over a very long time, mixed with the remains of dead plants and animals, plus water and air. Living things do much of the work: roots, fungi, bacteria, worms and insects all help turn bare rock into soft, crumbly earth.

It is slow. In many places, nature takes hundreds of years to make a centimetre of soil, and sometimes up to 1,000. That thin layer feeds nearly every plant and animal on land, including us.

## Breaking the rock

Every soil starts with rock. Heat by day and cold by night make rock expand and shrink, and tiny cracks form. Water seeps in. In cold places it freezes, swells and splits the rock further. Rain is slightly acidic, so it slowly dissolves some [minerals](minerals-everyday) too. This breaking down is called [weathering](?weathering).

Plants join in. Lichens, tough partnerships of a fungus and a tiny plant-like partner, cling to bare rock and release acids. Roots push into cracks and widen them, like a wedge. Over centuries, solid rock crumbles into stones, then [sand](sand-where), silt and clay.

## Adding the life

Crushed rock alone is not soil. The next ingredient is dead and rotting material: fallen leaves, roots, dead insects and animal droppings. Fungi and bacteria break it down into a dark, spongy substance called [humus](?humus). Humus holds water like a sponge and is rich in the food plants need.

A single teaspoon of healthy soil holds more living things than there are people on Earth, mostly bacteria too small to see. Fungi spread through soil in long white threads, linking plant roots. The [mushrooms](mushrooms) you see above ground are only the fruit of these hidden networks.

## The worm's work

Charles Darwin spent decades watching [earthworms](earthworms). His last book, published in 1881, was about them. He showed that worms drag leaves underground and swallow soil, passing it out as fine, rich castings. Over years, they bury stones and slowly mix the soil from top to bottom.

Their tunnels let in air and rain, and give roots room to grow. Ants, termites and beetles do similar work, especially in dry places where worms are fewer.

## Soil in layers

Dig a deep hole and you'll see that soil has layers, which scientists call horizons. On top lies a dark layer of fallen leaves and humus. Below is topsoil, where most roots and life are found. Under that is subsoil, paler and harder, and then broken rock. At the bottom is solid bedrock.

Topsoil is often only a few tens of centimetres deep. A few centimetres of it can hold more life than all the metres below.

## India's soils

Different rocks and climates make different soils. In the Deccan, ancient [lava](deccan) has weathered into black [cotton](clothes-cotton) soil. It is sticky when wet, cracks when dry and holds water well, which suits cotton. Along [the Ganga](rivers-himalaya) and other big rivers, [floods](floods-why) have spread fine, [fertile alluvial soil](soil-types-india) over the plains for thousands of years.

In Kerala, heavy rain has washed many minerals out of the ground, leaving red laterite soil. Blocks of laterite can be cut and dried into building bricks, and many old houses there are built from it.

## When soil blows away

In the 1930s, [farmers on the Great Plains](usa-dust-bowl) of the United States ploughed up [grassland](grasslands) and planted wheat. When a long [drought](droughts) struck, the bare soil dried out. Strong winds lifted it into huge black dust storms, and the region became known as the Dust Bowl.

Soil that took centuries to form blew away in a few years. The US government set up a Soil Conservation Service in 1935 to teach farmers to protect the ground, with [trees as windbreaks](what-if-no-trees), crop rotation and ploughing along slopes. People who first [began farming](farming-began) thousands of years ago depended on soil, and farmers still do today.
