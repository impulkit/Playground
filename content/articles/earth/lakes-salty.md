## The short answer

A lake becomes salty when water flows in but can't flow out. Rivers and streams always carry small amounts of dissolved salts, picked up from rocks and soil. In most lakes, that water keeps moving, out through a river to the sea, so the salts are carried away.

Some lakes have no outlet. The only way water leaves is by evaporating into the air, and when water evaporates, the salt stays behind. Over thousands of years, the salt builds up and the lake becomes salty. Hot, dry places, where evaporation is strong, have the saltiest lakes.

## Salt without a sea

All river water has a little salt in it, though too little to taste. Rain falling on rock slowly dissolves tiny amounts of minerals, and rivers carry them along. The same process, over billions of years, made the [sea salty](why-sea-salty).

A lake with no outlet is like a smaller version of the ocean. It collects the salts from every stream that feeds it. The [water cycle](water-cycle) lifts pure water out of it as vapour and leaves the salts behind, year after year.

## The Dead Sea

The [Dead Sea](dead-sea) lies between Jordan, Israel and the West Bank. Its shore is [about 430 m below sea level](extreme-lowest-point), the lowest land on Earth. The Jordan River flows in, but nothing flows out, and the desert sun is fierce.

Its water is about ten times saltier than the ocean. That makes it so dense that people float with their heads, arms and feet sticking out. Salt crystals form white crusts along the shore. The lake is shrinking, though, by about a metre a year, as people take water from the Jordan River for farms and towns.

## The Great Salt Lake

In Utah, the Great Salt Lake is a leftover from a much bigger ancient lake called [Lake Bonneville](earth-salt-flats), which covered a huge area during the last [Ice Age](ice-ages). As the climate warmed and dried, it shrank, leaving the salt behind.

Today the lake's saltiness changes with the weather, and different parts have different amounts of salt. Brine shrimp, tiny animals about a centimetre long, thrive in it, and millions of birds come to eat them. A railway built across the lake in the 1950s split it in two, and the northern half became much saltier and turned [pink with microbes](earth-weird-pink-lakes).

## Sambhar Lake

Sambhar Lake, near [Jaipur](jaipur) in Rajasthan, is India's largest inland salt lake. In [the monsoon](monsoon), it fills with water from streams. In the dry months, it shrinks and leaves behind a shining white crust.

Salt has been collected from Sambhar for centuries, and the lake still produces salt today. The lake is a protected wetland, and in winter it is visited by thousands of [flamingos](flamingos-pink), which feed on tiny shrimp and algae.

## A crater lake turns pink

[Lonar Lake](india-wonder-lonar) in Maharashtra fills a round crater made when a [meteorite](meteorites) slammed into the ancient lava rock of the Deccan, many thousands of years ago. The lake has no outlet, and its water is both salty and strongly alkaline, a bit like water mixed with baking soda.

In June 2020, the lake turned bright pink. Scientists studied the water and suggested that a bloom of salt-loving microbes had multiplied as the water level dropped and the water became saltier. After the monsoon rains came, the colour faded.

## Salty lakes high up

[Pangong Tso](india-wonder-pangong), high in [Ladakh](ladakh) on the border between India and China, sits more than 4,000 m above sea level. Its eastern end is fresh, but the western part, in India, is slightly salty, because the lake has no outlet.

In winter, even this salty water freezes over. In summer, its colour changes through the day, from blue to green to grey, as the light shifts.
