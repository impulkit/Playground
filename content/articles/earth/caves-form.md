## The short answer

Most big caves form in limestone, a rock made mostly of the remains of ancient sea creatures. When rain falls, it picks up a little [carbon dioxide](carbon-cycle) from the air and soil, which turns it into a very weak acid, like a much gentler [fizzy drink](soft-drinks).

That water seeps into cracks in the limestone and slowly dissolves the rock. Over tens of thousands of years, tiny cracks widen into tunnels, and tunnels into caves. Some caves stretch for hundreds of kilometres underground.

## Rain that eats rock

[Limestone](rocks-types) is made of a [mineral called calcite](minerals-everyday). Pure water barely touches it. But slightly acidic water dissolves it, slowly, the way a sugar cube dissolves in tea, only millions of times slower.

Water follows the easiest path. It trickles down cracks and along the joins between rock layers, and dissolves the walls as it goes. Wider cracks carry more water, which dissolves more rock, so the biggest passages grow fastest. When the [water table](groundwater), the level of water underground, drops, the passages drain and fill with air. Now there is a cave.

## Stalactites and stalagmites

The same water that carves a cave also decorates it. When a drop of water carrying dissolved calcite seeps from a cave ceiling, it loses some of its carbon dioxide to the air. It can no longer hold all its calcite, so it leaves a tiny ring of it behind.

Drop by drop, the rings build down into a hanging stalactite. Where drops land on the floor, calcite builds up into a stalagmite. If the two meet, they form a column. One way to remember which is which: stalactites hold on *tight* to the ceiling, and stalagmites *might* reach it one day.

Most grow much more slowly than your fingernails, often by a fraction of a millimetre in a year. A metre-long stalactite may be thousands of years old.

## Caves in India

India has many limestone caves. The Borra Caves, in the Eastern Ghats of Andhra Pradesh, are full of tall stalactites. The Belum Caves, also in Andhra Pradesh, wind for more than 3 km underground.

The longest caves in India lie in [Meghalaya](meghalaya), in the rainy hills of [northeast India](northeast-india). Heavy rain and thick limestone have made hundreds of caves there. Krem Liat Prah, the longest, stretches for more than 30 km. Cavers from India and Britain have been mapping these caves together for decades.

## Record breakers

Mammoth Cave in Kentucky is the longest known cave system in the world, with more than 680 km of mapped passages. Layers of hard sandstone above the limestone acted like a roof, protecting the passages from collapse.

The biggest single cave passage known is Son Doong in [Vietnam](vietnam). Parts of it are big enough to fit a block of skyscrapers, and it has its own patch of jungle where the roof has fallen in. A local man, Ho Khanh, found its entrance in the early 1990s, and British cavers explored it in 2009.

## Other kinds of caves

Not every cave is dissolved limestone. Lava tubes form when the outside of a lava flow cools and hardens while hot lava keeps flowing inside, leaving a hollow tube. [Hawaii](hawaii-islands) has many. Sea caves are hollowed out by [waves pounding cliffs](cliffs-erosion). Ice caves form inside [glaciers](glaciers), where meltwater carves tunnels.

When the roof of a limestone cave grows too thin, it can collapse and leave a hole called a [sinkhole](sinkholes). People have also made their own caves. The temples of [Ellora](rashtrakutas) in Maharashtra were cut by hand into solid rock, including the [Kailasa temple](ellora-kailasa), carved from the top down out of a single cliff.
