## The short answer

Deep under the Pacific Ocean there is a *hot spot*, a place where hot rock rises from deep [inside Earth](inside-earth). Melted rock punches up through the sea floor and piles up as volcanoes. Over hundreds of thousands of years, they grow tall enough to break the surface as islands.

## A moving conveyor belt

The sea floor is part of the Pacific [plate](?plate), which slides north-west by about 7 to 10 cm a year, a little faster than your fingernails grow. The hot spot stays put. So each volcano is slowly carried away, and a new one forms over the hot spot.

That is why Hawaii is a chain of islands: the oldest in the north-west, the youngest (the Big Island) in the south-east. A brand-new island is growing underwater right now.

The chain doesn't stop at the islands you see on a map. A line of drowned volcanoes, called seamounts, stretches about 6,000 km across the Pacific, almost to Russia. The oldest are around 80 million years old. Halfway along, the line bends sharply. That bend shows the plate changed direction about 47 million years ago.

## Islands that grow, then shrink

Once a volcano drifts off the hot spot, it stops erupting. Then [rain, waves and wind](cliffs-erosion) start wearing it down. The heavy island also slowly sinks, pressing down on the plate beneath it.

So the islands tell a life story. The Big Island is young, tall and still growing. Maui is a bit older. Kauaʻi, far to the north-west, is older still, carved by rain into steep green cliffs. Beyond it, the old volcanoes have sunk until only [coral](coral-reefs) reefs and tiny atolls are left at the surface.

## Taller than Everest

Mauna Kea rises about 4,200 m above the sea, but its base is about 6,000 m below the waves. From bottom to top it is over 10,000 m, taller than [Everest](himalaya-form). Its neighbour, Mauna Loa, is [the largest active volcano on Earth](space-weird-tallest-mountain) by volume.

The air on top of Mauna Kea is so dry, still and clear that it is one of the best places on Earth to study the stars. Over a dozen [telescopes](telescopes-how) stand there. Many Native Hawaiians consider the summit sacred, and plans to build new telescopes there have led to long protests and debate.

## Kīlauea and Pele

Kīlauea, on the Big Island, is one of the most active volcanoes in the world. It erupted almost without stopping from 1983 to 2018. Its lava is runny, so it usually flows rather than explodes, and you can sometimes watch it from a safe distance. In 2018, lava flows destroyed hundreds of homes in the Puna district.

In Hawaiian tradition, Pele is the goddess of volcanoes and fire, said to live in the crater of Kīlauea. Many people on the island still leave offerings and speak of her with respect.

## The next island

About 35 km off the Big Island's south-east coast, a new volcano called Kamaʻehuakanaloa is growing on the sea floor. It used to be called Lōʻihi. Its top is still about 1,000 m below the surface. If it keeps growing, it may rise above the waves in tens of thousands of years.

India also rode a moving plate, all the way from near Antarctica to Asia.
