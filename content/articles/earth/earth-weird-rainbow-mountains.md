## The short answer

Rainbow mountains are made of layers of rock, each a different colour. Over millions of years, mud, sand and volcanic ash settled in lakes, rivers and plains, one layer on top of another. Different minerals and different climates gave each layer its own colour. Later, the rock was pressed into stone, squeezed and pushed up, so the stripes are now tilted and folded into hills.

The colours mostly come from tiny amounts of metals in the rock, especially iron. When iron meets oxygen, it turns red, like [rust](rust). Where there was little oxygen, the same iron can look green or grey. Wind and rain then wear away the surface, revealing the stripes.

## Pages in a book of rock

Each layer of [sediment](?sediment) is a bit like a page in a diary. It records what the land was like at the time. A red layer may mean a hot, dry period when iron rusted in the air. A grey or green layer may mean the mud lay under still water, where oxygen was scarce. A pale layer might be sandstone from an old desert.

Geologists read these stripes from the bottom up, oldest first. The [Grand Canyon](grand-canyon) in the USA has similar layers, but they are mostly flat. Rainbow mountains are special because their layers have been tilted, so many colours show side by side.

## China's painted hills

The Zhangye Danxia landform in Gansu province, China, is one of the most famous. Its layers of red sandstone and siltstone were laid down in an ancient basin many millions of years ago. Then the collision of India with Asia, the same crash that raised the [Himalayas](himalaya-form), squeezed and lifted them.

Today they look like waves of red, orange, yellow and white. In 2019, Zhangye became a UNESCO Global Geopark, a place protected for its rocks and landscapes. Boardwalks and viewing platforms let visitors watch the colours glow at sunset.

## Peru's mountain under the ice

Vinicunca, in the Andes of [Peru](peru), rises to about 5,200 metres, higher than any peak in the Alps. Its stripes are red, purple, yellow and green. For many years, much of it was hidden under snow. Local guides say that as the snow cover shrank, the colours became easier to see, and in the mid-2010s photos spread online.

Now thousands of visitors may climb up on a busy day, many on horseback. The air is thin, so it is a hard walk. The sudden fame has brought money to local villages, but also worries about erosion and crowds.

## America's Painted Hills

In Oregon, USA, the Painted Hills are low, rounded mounds striped red, gold and black. About 35 million years ago, volcanoes spread ash across the region. The ash turned into soft clay. As the climate changed between warm and wet and cooler and drier, each layer weathered into a different colour.

The black streaks are partly from old plant matter. The hills change colour with the weather too. After rain, the clay soaks up water and looks darker and brighter.

## Colours you can name

Each mineral gives its own colour. Iron oxide makes reds and oranges. A green mineral called chlorite, and iron without much oxygen, make greens. Some yellow minerals contain iron too, in a different form. Carbon from old plants and animals makes black.

It is a bit like a painter's palette, laid down by nature over millions of years. And the strangest part? Just a handful of minerals make all those shades, much as an artist can [mix many colours](colour-mix) from only a few tubes of paint.
