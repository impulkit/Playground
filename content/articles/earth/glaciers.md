## The short answer

A glacier is a huge body of ice that forms on land from snow piled up over many years. It moves in two main ways. First, the ice itself slowly bends and flows under its own enormous weight, the way thick honey spreads. Second, the whole glacier can slide over the ground on a thin layer of meltwater.

Most glaciers move a few centimetres to about a metre a day. That is too slow to see, but over a year it can add up to the length of several football fields.

## From snowflake to glacier

A [glacier](?glacier) begins in a place where more snow falls in winter than melts in summer. Year after year, new snow buries the old. The weight squeezes out the air, and the fluffy flakes turn into grainy, packed snow, then into solid ice.

This can take decades or even centuries. Old glacier ice is so dense that it often looks blue, because it soaks up red light and lets blue light through.

## Solid ice that flows

Ice seems hard and brittle, and on top, it is. That is why glaciers have deep cracks called crevasses, which open where the ice stretches or bends over bumps.

Deep inside, though, the ice is under huge pressure from the weight above. There, the ice crystals slowly slip and change shape, and the ice creeps downhill. The middle and top of a glacier usually move faster than the sides and bottom, which drag against rock.

Water helps too. Meltwater can collect under a glacier and act like a [slippery film](ice-skating). Some glaciers suddenly speed up for a few months, called a surge, moving many times faster than normal.

## Carving the land

As glaciers move, they scrape and pluck rock from the ground beneath them, like giant sheets of sandpaper. Over thousands of years, they turn V-shaped [river valleys](valleys) into wide U-shaped valleys.

They also carry rocks of every size, from dust to boulders as big as houses, and drop them when the ice melts. Huge boulders sitting alone in fields far from any cliff were a puzzle until scientists realised that ice had carried them there during the [Ice Ages](ice-ages).

## India's glaciers

The Himalaya holds thousands of glaciers, more than anywhere outside the polar regions. The Gangotri Glacier, about 30 km long, ends at an ice cave called Gaumukh, "the cow's mouth". The Bhagirathi river flows out of it, one of the [rivers that become the Ganga](rivers-himalaya).

The Siachen Glacier, in the Karakoram range, is about 76 km long, one of the longest outside the polar regions. Meltwater from Himalayan glaciers feeds rivers that hundreds of millions of people depend on, especially in the dry months before [the monsoon](monsoon).

## Shrinking ice

Most of the world's glaciers are getting smaller as the planet [warms](climate-change). In Glacier National Park in Montana, scientists think there were about 150 glaciers in 1850. Today fewer than 30 are big enough to count. Many Himalayan glaciers are also losing ice and retreating.

Ice caps and glaciers hold about two thirds of the world's fresh water, mostly in [Antarctica](antarctica) and Greenland. When ice on land melts, the water flows to the ocean and adds to [rising seas](sea-level-rise). Floating sea ice doesn't raise the sea much when it melts, but the great sheets on land do.

## The fastest glaciers

Some glaciers that flow into the sea move much faster than mountain glaciers. Sermeq Kujalleq in Greenland, also called Jakobshavn Glacier, is one of the fastest. In some summers, parts of it have moved more than 40 metres in a day. Icebergs that break off its end drift out to sea, and one of them may have sunk the [Titanic](titanic) in 1912.
