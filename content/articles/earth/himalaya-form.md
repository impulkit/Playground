## The short answer

When India crashed into Asia, neither side could sink easily. The land between them crumpled upward, like a rug pushed against a wall.

Rock from the bottom of an ancient sea got lifted up. That's why there are sea fossils near the top of Everest.

India still pushes north, so the Himalayas are still slowly growing, and still shaking.

## A crumpled sea floor

Between India and Asia once lay an ocean called the Tethys. As [India drifted north](india-drift), the floor of that ocean slid down beneath Asia. When India itself arrived, about 50 million years ago, its thick continental rock was too light to sink. The sea-floor rocks caught between were squeezed, folded and stacked up like a crumpled carpet.

The top of Everest is made of grey limestone that formed on the floor of the Tethys. It contains fossils of tiny sea creatures, such as [trilobites](trilobites) and sea lilies. In the [Spiti valley](spiti) of [Himachal Pradesh](himachal), villagers find coiled ammonite fossils in the fields, more than 4,000 m above the sea.

## Young mountains

At around 50 million years old, the Himalayas are [young for mountains](mountains-types). That's why they're so tall and sharp. Older mountains, like the Aravallis in Rajasthan, have been worn down for far longer. The [Appalachian Mountains](appalachians) in the eastern USA are hundreds of millions of years old, and their rounded tops show it.

The Himalayas are still rising, by a few millimetres a year in places. But rain, rivers, ice and [landslides](landslides) are wearing them down at the same time. The height of the peaks is a tug of war between the two.

## Measuring the top

In the 1800s, the British-run Great Trigonometrical Survey measured India's mountains using telescopes and careful [triangles](geometry-everyday). In 1852, a Bengali mathematician named Radhanath Sikdar worked through the calculations and found that a peak known only as Peak XV was the highest in the world. It was later named after George Everest, a former head of the survey. In [Nepal](nepal) it is called Sagarmatha, and in Tibet, Chomolungma.

In 2020, Nepal and China announced a new joint measurement: 8,848.86 m. People first stood on the summit in 1953, when [Tenzing Norgay and Edmund Hillary](everest-first) reached the top.

## A shaking range

India pushes into Asia by about 4 to 5 cm a year. That push builds up stress in the rocks, which is released in [earthquakes](earthquakes). In April 2015, a magnitude 7.8 earthquake struck Nepal, causing great damage in Kathmandu and the villages around it, and setting off avalanches on Everest.

Scientists think some parts of the range have not had a big earthquake in centuries, so stress is still building there. That's why strong buildings matter so much in Himalayan towns.

## Water tower of Asia

Snow and [glaciers](glaciers) in the Himalayas feed rivers like the Ganga, Indus and [Brahmaputra](brahmaputra). [Follow a drop of the Ganga](rivers-himalaya).

The mountains hold so much ice that the region is sometimes called the Third Pole, after the Arctic and Antarctic. More than a billion people live along rivers that start in these mountains. The range also acts as a wall. It blocks freezing winds from Central Asia in winter, and it forces the monsoon's wet winds to rise and drop their rain over India. Not everything is stopped, though. Every spring and autumn, [bar-headed geese](bar-headed-goose) fly right over the range between India and Central Asia.
