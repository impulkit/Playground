## The short answer

An ice age is a long stretch of time when Earth has big sheets of ice on land, far from the mountains. Earth has had several. The most recent one began about 2.6 million years ago and, strictly speaking, hasn't ended, because Antarctica and Greenland are still covered in ice.

Within this long ice age, the planet has swung back and forth between cold glacial periods, when [ice sheets](what-if-ice-melted) spread, and warmer interglacials, when they shrink. We live in a warm interglacial that began about 11,700 years ago. When people talk about "the Ice Age", they usually mean the last cold spell, which peaked about 20,000 years ago.

## A colder world

At the height of the last glacial period, a sheet of ice more than 3 km thick sat over Hudson Bay in Canada. It spread south over the places where Chicago and New York now stand. Another ice sheet covered Scandinavia and much of Britain.

Glaciers in the Himalaya grew longer and reached lower [valleys](valleys). Average temperatures around the world were several degrees colder than today. Many places were also drier, because cold air holds less moisture. The [Thar Desert](thar-formed) spread, and [the monsoon](monsoon) was weaker.

## When the sea went out

All that ice was water taken from the ocean. As the ice sheets grew, the sea fell by about 120 metres, roughly the height of a 40-storey building.

New land appeared. A wide plain called Beringia joined Siberia to [Alaska](alaska), and people and animals crossed it into the Americas. Britain was joined to Europe. A ridge of land linked southern India to [Sri Lanka](sri-lanka) in the shallow sea where the chain of islands and sandbanks called Adam's Bridge, or Ram Setu, lies today.

## Animals of the cold

The Ice Age had its own giants. [Woolly mammoths](mammoths) and woolly rhinos grazed the [cold grasslands](grasslands) of Europe, Asia and North America. [Sabre-toothed cats](sabre-tooth), giant ground sloths and dire wolves lived in the Americas. Many of these animals died out as the ice retreated, and as human hunters spread.

People lived through it too. In Europe, Stone Age artists painted horses, bison and mammoths on cave walls.

## Why the ice comes and goes

In the 1830s, the Swiss-American scientist Louis Agassiz argued that giant boulders scattered across Europe had been carried by ice. Many scientists doubted him at first. Today the evidence is overwhelming.

But why does the ice advance and retreat? In the early 1900s, the Serbian scientist Milutin Milanković worked out that small, regular changes in Earth's [orbit](?orbit) and tilt change how much summer sunlight reaches the far north. These cycles repeat roughly every 23,000, 41,000 and 100,000 years. When northern summers are cool, snow doesn't melt, and ice sheets grow.

Changes in the air add to the effect. In cold periods, the air held less carbon dioxide, a gas that traps heat. Bubbles of ancient air trapped in Antarctic ice show this clearly.

## A snowball Earth?

Much further back, about 700 million years ago, Earth may have gone through even harsher ice ages. Some scientists think ice reached almost to the equator, an idea called Snowball Earth. Others think a band of open water stayed near the equator. This is still being argued over.

## Today's warming

Without people, Earth would probably stay in this warm interglacial for tens of thousands of years. But burning coal, oil and gas has pushed carbon dioxide higher than it has been for hundreds of thousands of years. Today's [warming](climate-change) is happening much faster than the natural swings of the Ice Ages.
