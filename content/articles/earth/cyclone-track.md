## The short answer

Scientists cannot see the future, but they can measure a cyclone very closely and work out what it is likely to do. Satellites watch it from space. Buoys feel it at sea. Radars on the coast look inside it as it comes near.

All those measurements go into powerful computers that use the laws of physics to push the storm forward in time. Forecasters run these computer models many times, compare the answers and draw the most likely path, with a zone of doubt around it. The path is fairly easy to forecast. How strong the storm will be is much harder.

## How it works

A [cyclone](?cyclone) is pushed along by the huge winds around it, a bit like a paper boat carried by a stream. So forecasters need to know two things: where the storm is now, and how the winds around it will blow over the next few days.

1. **Watching from space.** [Weather satellites](weather-satellites) photograph the storm every few minutes, by day and, using [infrared](?infrared), by night. They show its spinning shape and how cold its cloud tops are.
2. **Feeling the sea.** Buoys anchored in the Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea measure wind, waves, [air pressure](?atmospheric-pressure) and water temperature, and send the readings by satellite. When a storm comes close, some switch to sending data more often.
3. **Looking inside.** Doppler radars send out short bursts of radio waves and listen for the [echoes](echoes) from raindrops. The echo's change shows whether the rain is moving towards the radar or away, the same way a siren sounds different as an ambulance passes. That reveals the winds inside the storm.
4. **Flying in.** In the Atlantic, US "hurricane hunter" planes fly straight through the storm into its calm eye. They drop instruments on little parachutes that measure wind, pressure, temperature and moisture all the way down to the sea.
5. **Running the models.** Supercomputers turn all of this into a forecast, the way [weather forecasts](weather-forecasts) are made every day, but focused on the storm.

## The cone and the many forecasts

The air is chaotic: a tiny error at the start can grow into a big one days later. So weather centres run the same model dozens of times, each starting from slightly different numbers. This bundle is called an ensemble.

If most runs send the storm to the same stretch of coast, forecasters are confident. If the runs spread out like the fingers of a hand, they are not. Counting how many runs end up in each place gives a [probability](?probability), just like the [chance of rain](chance-weather) on your weather app.

Forecasters often show the result as a "cone of uncertainty". The US National Hurricane Center draws circles along the forecast path, each sized so that about two-thirds of its past forecast errors fell inside. The cone gets wider with every day ahead, because the doubt grows with time.

## Why the path is easier than the strength

A storm's path depends mostly on giant wind patterns thousands of kilometres across, which computers capture quite well. Its strength depends on tiny things deep inside it: how warm the sea is right beneath it, how the clouds around the eye behave, and how winds high up tilt the storm.

Sometimes a cyclone suddenly blows up. Forecasters call it rapid intensification when the strongest winds rise by at least 55 km/h in a single day. It is one of the hardest things in weather to predict, because scientists still do not fully understand what sets it off. This is why a forecast can get the landfall point almost exactly right and still be surprised by how strong the storm is.

## Where it came from

For most of history, people knew a storm was coming only when the sky darkened and the sea rose.

Three inventions changed that. In 1950, scientists in the USA made the first weather forecast on an electronic computer. In 1960, TIROS-1 became the first successful weather satellite, and soon storms could not hide over empty ocean. In 1992, the European weather centre began running ensembles as a regular service, and in 1996 it raised the number of runs from 33 to 51.

India's own forecasts grew too. The [India Meteorological Department](imd), known as IMD, gave track forecasts only 24 hours ahead before 2009. It stretched them to 72 hours in 2009, and to 120 hours, five full days, in 2013.

## India's story

IMD's cyclone centre in New Delhi has a second job. Since 1988, as the Regional Specialised Meteorological Centre for the north Indian Ocean, it has sent cyclone advice to neighbours from Bangladesh to Oman. It also names the storms, using a list from 13 countries.

Once a storm becomes a cyclone, IMD sends out a new bulletin every three hours. Warnings come in four stages. A pre-cyclone watch goes out about 72 hours ahead. A yellow cyclone alert comes at least 48 hours before bad weather reaches the coast. An orange cyclone warning, at least 24 hours ahead, names the expected landfall point. Finally a red post-landfall outlook says where the storm will go once it is over land.

The difference better forecasts make shows in [Odisha](odisha). In 1999, the first warnings for the mainland came only about two days before a super cyclone struck, and its track was uncertain. In 2013, IMD predicted Cyclone Phailin's landfall point and time four to five days ahead, and close to 10 lakh people in Odisha moved to shelters. In 2019, IMD tracked Cyclone Fani to its landfall near Puri, and a reported 10 lakh people were moved in time. How the state gets families out is the story of [how India gets ready for a cyclone](cyclone-ready).

## The numbers that matter

- **About 97 km to about 72 km.** IMD's average one-day error in a cyclone's position, in 2011–2015 and then in 2020–2024. That is roughly the distance from one town to the next.
- **About 183 km to about 154 km.** Over the same years, the three-day error also fell. A cone that is narrower means fewer people need to leave home for nothing.
- **6 km.** How far off IMD's three-day forecast of Phailin's landfall point was in 2013. Its one-day forecast was off by 13 km.
- **Two out of three.** How often, in past forecasts, the storm's centre stayed inside the cone. One time in three, it did not.
- **A little over 7 seconds an hour.** How long a typical US weather radar actually spends sending out pulses. The rest of the hour it is listening for echoes.

## What people argue about

**How wide to warn?** Warning a wide stretch of coast keeps more people safe if the storm wobbles. But moving people costs money, shuts schools and markets, and if storms often miss, some people may stop listening. Officials have to weigh both risks each time.

**Lines or cones?** Some experts say the single line on a map makes people think the storm will follow it exactly. Others say the cone is confusing too, because people take it as the size of the storm.

**Physics or AI?** [Artificial intelligence](ai-what) models, trained on decades of past weather, can produce storm tracks in minutes. Some scientists think they will beat traditional physics models. Others point out that they learn from the past and may stumble on unusual storms.

## Myths and mix-ups

**"Forecasters just guess."** Every forecast rests on millions of measurements and the laws of physics, and it is checked after every storm.

**"If the forecast changes, it was wrong."** Forecasts are updated as new data comes in. A path that shifts by a few kilometres is the system working.

**"Once the eye crosses the coast, the danger is over."** Rain and floods can continue far inland, which is why IMD's last warning stage is about where the storm goes after landfall.

## What it means for you

When a cyclone is coming, the forecast tells your family how much time there is and which areas need to move. Days of warning mean time to reach a shelter calmly, without a last-minute rush.

The forecast also shapes everyday plans far from the sea. Trains and flights may be rescheduled, exams moved and fishing boats called home, because a storm's path is now known days in advance.
