## The short answer

A gemstone is usually a [mineral](?mineral) crystal that is beautiful, hard and rare. A crystal forms when atoms line up in the same neat pattern again and again, like bricks in a perfectly built wall. That pattern gives crystals their flat faces and sharp edges.

Crystals grow in three main ways inside Earth. Some grow as hot melted rock slowly cools. Some grow from hot, mineral-rich water trickling through cracks. Others form when buried rock is squeezed and heated until its atoms rearrange. Slow growth with room to spare makes the biggest, clearest crystals.

## Born in fire and water

When [magma](?magma) cools slowly deep underground, crystals have time to grow large. Some granite has crystals bigger than your thumb, and certain cooling magmas produce gems such as topaz and tourmaline.

Water does a lot of the work too. Hot water deep underground carries dissolved minerals into cracks and hollows. As it cools, crystals build up on the walls. A hollow rock lined with purple amethyst points, called a geode, formed this way, one layer of atoms at a time. Emeralds often grow in hot-water veins like these.

## Diamonds from the deep

[Diamonds are pure carbon](space-weird-diamonds), squeezed into crystals about 150–200 km down, in the [mantle](?mantle). The heat and pressure there are enormous. Most diamonds are between one and three and a half billion years old.

They reach us thanks to rare, fast volcanic eruptions from very deep down. These blasted up narrow pipes of rock called kimberlite, carrying diamonds with them. Many pipes were later worn down, and rivers washed the diamonds into gravel beds. That is where most early diamonds were found.

## India's diamonds

For about two thousand years, India was the world's main source of diamonds. Many of the famous ones came from river gravels in the region around Golconda, near today's [Hyderabad](hyderabad). Traders carried them to [Persia](ancient-persia), Europe and China. India's supply only lost its lead in the 1700s, after diamonds were found in Brazil.

The Hope Diamond, a deep blue stone of about 45 carats, is thought to have come from these Indian mines. Today it sits in the [Smithsonian](smithsonian)'s National Museum of Natural History in Washington, DC, and is one of the most visited objects in any [museum](museums). Its blue comes from tiny amounts of the element boron.

The Koh-i-Noor, whose name means "mountain of light" in Persian, is also believed to have come from the Golconda region. It passed through the hands of Mughal emperors, Persian and Afghan rulers and the Sikh Maharaja Ranjit Singh. The British took it in 1849. It is now part of the British Crown Jewels in the [Tower of London](london-tower), and India, Pakistan and Afghanistan have each asked for it back.

## How hard is hard?

In 1812, a German scientist named Friedrich Mohs made a simple scale. He ranked ten minerals from softest to hardest, based on which could scratch which. Talc is 1, and you can scratch it with a fingernail. Quartz is 7. Corundum, the mineral of rubies and sapphires, is 9. Diamond is 10.

Hardness is why gems last. A diamond scratches glass easily, which is how people once tested them. Not every gem is a crystal, though. [Pearls](pearls) grow inside oysters, and [amber](amber-fossils) is hardened tree resin, yet both are counted among the gems.
