## The short answer

A hurricane, a typhoon and a [cyclone](cyclones-india) are all the same kind of storm: a huge, spinning [system of thunderstorms](thunderstorms) that grows over warm seas. Scientists call it a *tropical cyclone*.

In the Atlantic it is a **hurricane**. In the north-western Pacific it is a **typhoon**. In the Indian Ocean, near India, it is a **cyclone**.

## How it grows

The sea must be warmer than about 26.5°C. Warm water evaporates, rises, and releases heat as it forms clouds. That heat powers the storm. Because [Earth spins](what-if-earth-stopped), the storm starts turning, anticlockwise in the northern half of the world and clockwise in the southern half.

Earth's spin has almost no twisting effect right at the equator, so tropical cyclones hardly ever form there. They usually start a few hundred kilometres north or south of it. Once a cyclone moves over land or cold water, it loses its fuel and weakens, though it can still bring huge amounts of rain far inland.

Around India, cyclones are most common in May and from October to December, on either side of the summer [monsoon](monsoon). The Bay of Bengal gets more of them than the Arabian Sea, because its waters are warmer.

## The calm eye

In the middle is the *eye*: a circle of calm, often clear sky. Around it is the eyewall, with the strongest winds.

The eye is usually tens of kilometres across. Air sinks gently inside it, which is why the sky can look blue while a wall of black cloud towers all around. From space, satellite pictures show it as a neat hole in a giant pinwheel of cloud.

## The sea comes ashore

Strong winds are dangerous, but the biggest threat is usually water. The storm's winds push the sea towards the coast, piling it up into a dome several metres high, called a storm surge. If it arrives at high [tide](tides), it rises even higher and can sweep far inland.

In 2005, Hurricane Katrina's surge broke through the flood walls of [New Orleans](new-orleans) in the USA. In the Bay of Bengal, where the coast is low and flat, storm surges have caused some of the worst disasters in history. In 1970, the Bhola cyclone struck what is now [Bangladesh](bangladesh), and hundreds of thousands of people died, most of them in the surge.

## Warnings save lives

In 1999, a super cyclone hit [Odisha](odisha) with little warning, and around 10,000 people died. Odisha decided it would not happen again. It built hundreds of cyclone shelters and trained volunteers in villages.

In 2013, Cyclone Phailin, nearly as strong, struck the same coast. This time, the India Meteorological Department tracked it for days using [satellites](satellites-help) and radar, and about a million people were moved to safety. Fewer than 50 people lost their lives. The United Nations praised the effort. [Weather forecasts](weather-forecasts) had turned into a life-saving tool.

## Who picks the names?

Lists of names are agreed in advance. For the Indian Ocean, India, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka and other nearby countries each suggest names, and the next name on the list is used. Each Indian Ocean name is used only once. In the Atlantic, names of very deadly storms are retired.

The current Indian Ocean list was released in 2020. Thirteen countries suggested 13 names each, 169 in all. Names must be short, easy to say, and not hurtful to anyone. Hurricanes in the USA are also rated from Category 1 to 5 by wind speed, while India uses its own scale, from "cyclonic storm" up to "super cyclonic storm".
