## The short answer

Mountains form when the rocky outer shell of the Earth gets pushed, pulled, cracked or melted. That shell is broken into huge plates that move a few centimetres a year. When they collide, rocks crumple upward into fold mountains. When they crack, blocks of land can lift or drop to make block mountains. When melted rock bursts through, it piles up into volcanic mountains.

There are also dome mountains, where rising rock bulges the surface without breaking through, and worn-down old ranges that are the stumps of ancient giants. Most big ranges are a mix of these. Every mountain is in a slow tug-of-war between forces lifting it and weather tearing it down.

## Fold mountains

The world's biggest ranges are fold mountains. They form when two plates push together and the rock layers between them buckle, like a rug shoved against a wall. The [Himalaya](himalaya-form) began rising about 50 million years ago, when the Indian plate crashed into Asia. India is still pushing north at about 5 centimetres a year, and the range is still growing.

That is why climbers find fossils of sea creatures high in the Himalaya. The rocks were once mud on the floor of an ancient sea. Black stones containing [ammonite](ammonites) fossils, called shaligram, are found along the Kali Gandaki river in Nepal, and many Hindus treat them as sacred. The Alps in Europe and the Appalachians in the USA are also fold mountains.

## Block mountains

Sometimes the crust cracks along long breaks called faults. Huge blocks of rock can then be pushed up or slide down. Raised blocks form block mountains, often with one steep side and one gentle side.

The Sierra Nevada in California is a famous example. Its steep eastern face rises sharply above the desert. In India, the Satpura range in central India is often described as a block mountain, lifted between faults. The Narmada river flows west through a long, low valley that dropped between faults to the north of it.

## Volcanic mountains

Volcanic mountains are built from melted rock, called magma, that rises through cracks and erupts as [lava](?lava) and ash. Each eruption adds a new layer. [Mount Fuji](mount-fuji) in Japan and Mount [Kilimanjaro](kilimanjaro) in Tanzania are cone-shaped volcanoes built this way.

Some of the biggest volcanoes are under the sea. Mauna Kea in [Hawaii](hawaii-islands) rises over 4,200 metres above sea level, but its base sits deep on the ocean floor. From bottom to top, it is over 10,000 metres tall. India's only active volcano, on Barren Island in the Andaman Sea, is also mostly underwater.

## Dome mountains

Sometimes magma pushes up from below but doesn't erupt. It cools underground, bulging the rock above it into a dome. Over time, erosion strips away the softer top layers and leaves the hard core standing as hills.

The Black Hills of South Dakota formed partly this way. Their hard granite was later carved into the four presidents' faces of [Mount Rushmore](mount-rushmore). The Black Hills are also sacred to the Lakota people, who know them as Paha Sapa.

## Old, tired mountains

Mountains don't last forever. Rain, frost, [glaciers](glaciers) and rivers wear them down. The Aravalli hills in Rajasthan, Haryana and Delhi are among the oldest mountains on Earth. They were raised long before the dinosaurs and were probably once very tall. Now their highest point, Guru Shikhar at Mount Abu, is about 1,722 metres.

The [Appalachians](appalachians) tell a similar story. Hundreds of millions of years ago they may have rivalled the Himalaya. Today their rounded, forested ridges are gentle enough for long family hikes. The Appalachian Trail runs along them for about 3,500 kilometres, from Georgia to Maine.
