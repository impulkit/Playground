## The short answer

Rock layers are like pages in Earth's diary, with the oldest at the bottom. At the Grand Canyon, very old rock sits right under much younger rock, and about 1.2 billion years of pages are missing.

The rock was not lost by magic. It was probably worn away by [erosion](?erosion) over a very long time. The big question is what lifted the land up so it could be worn down.

## Reading the canyon walls

The [Grand Canyon](grand-canyon) is about 1.8 km deep at its deepest. The Colorado River cut it, exposing layer after layer like a sliced cake.

Near the bottom lie dark, twisted rocks about 1.7 billion years old, some of the [oldest rocks](rocks-oldest) you can see in North America. Directly above them are flat layers of sandstone only about 500 million years old. The line where they meet is called the Great Unconformity. "Unconformity" is a [geologist's](?geologist) word for a break in the record.

The timing is interesting. Just after the gap ends, life on Earth suddenly became far more varied in the [Cambrian](cambrian) period. Some scientists think minerals washed off the eroding land into the sea may have helped.

## Three big ideas

**Rising from below.** In 2026, two geologists at the University of Wollongong in Australia used a computer model of how Earth's insides move. It suggests slow, hot currents in the [mantle](?mantle), the thick layer [under the crust](inside-earth), pushed parts of ancient continents upwards. Wind, rain and rivers then scraped those high lands down. Around 540 million years ago, the same regions began to sink, and new layers started piling up on top.

**Giant glaciers.** Others think enormous [glaciers](ice-ages), during times when much of Earth was covered in ice, ground the rock away like sandpaper.

**A retreating cliff.** In 2026, a team at the University of Southampton suggested a giant cliff once stretched across ancient North America and slowly ate its way backwards as it eroded.

## Why scientists still argue

The same gap appears in Scandinavia, China and elsewhere, but not always with the same age. That makes one single cause hard to prove. Many geologists think more than one idea may be right: land had to be lifted first, and ice may have helped scrape it clean.
