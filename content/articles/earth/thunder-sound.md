## The short answer

Thunder is the sound made by lightning. A lightning bolt heats the air around it to about 30,000 °C in a tiny fraction of a second. The air expands so fast that it pushes outward in a shock wave, like an explosion. That wave reaches your ears as thunder.

It rumbles because a lightning bolt is long, often several kilometres. Sound from the near end reaches you first, and sound from the far end arrives a few seconds later. [Echoes](echoes) off hills, buildings and clouds stretch the noise out even more.

## Air that explodes

[Lightning](lightning) is a giant spark of electricity, a much larger version of the small shock you sometimes feel from [static electricity](static-electricity). The channel it travels through is only a few centimetres wide, but the electric current in it is enormous.

In about a millionth of a second, the air in the channel becomes several times hotter than the surface of [the Sun](sun-what). Hot air takes up more room than cold air, and this air has no time to move politely out of the way. It bursts outward, squashing the air around it. That pressure wave spreads out in all directions as [sound](sound-travel).

## Crack or rumble?

If lightning strikes very close, you hear a sharp crack or a loud bang, because the sound from the nearest part of the bolt arrives all at once. From farther away, the sound has travelled a long way and bounced off many things. The high-pitched parts fade, leaving a low, rolling rumble.

The shape of the bolt matters too. Lightning zigzags and branches. Each twist sends sound towards you from a slightly different distance, so the thunder arrives as a long series of booms that blur together into one rumble.

## Counting the distance

Light travels about a million times faster than sound. You see a lightning flash almost instantly, but its thunder takes time to arrive. Sound in air travels about 340 metres a second, so it covers about a kilometre every 3 seconds, or a mile every 5.

Count the seconds between flash and thunder and divide by 3 to get the distance in kilometres. It is simple [speed and distance](speed-distance) maths. If the gap is 30 seconds, the storm is about 10 km away.

## When thunder roars

Thunder can usually be heard up to about 15 km away, and lightning can strike that far from the storm too. The US National Weather Service has a simple rule: "When thunder roars, go indoors." If you can hear thunder, you are close enough to be struck.

The safest places are inside a proper building or a car with a metal roof. Trees, open fields and water are the most dangerous places in a storm. In India, lightning is especially common in the weeks before and during the [monsoon](monsoon), and the [India Meteorological Department](imd) sends out warnings when thunderstorms are expected.

## Gods of thunder

For most of history, nobody knew what caused thunder, and people explained it through stories. In the Vedas, Indra, king of the gods, wields the vajra, a thunderbolt weapon, to release the rains. In [Norse myths](norse-myths), Thor's hammer was said to cause thunder. Zeus in Greek stories threw lightning bolts.

About 2,000 years ago, the Roman poet Lucretius suggested that thunder might come from clouds crashing together, an idea that lasted a long time. Scientists showed in the 1700s and 1800s that lightning is electricity, and that thunder follows from it.

## Thunder without rain

Sometimes you can see lightning flicker on the horizon on a summer night without hearing any thunder. People call it heat lightning, but it is ordinary lightning from a storm too far away for its thunder to reach you. Sound fades and bends in the air over long distances, so the light arrives and the sound doesn't. Your [ears](ears-hear) simply can't catch it.
