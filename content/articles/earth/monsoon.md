## The short answer

In summer, land heats up much faster than the sea. Hot air over the land rises.

Cooler, wet air from the ocean rushes in to take its place, bringing clouds and rain. That's the monsoon.

In winter, it flips: the land is cooler, so dry winds blow from land to sea.

## A giant sea breeze

If you've been to a beach on a sunny afternoon, you may have felt a cool breeze blowing in from the water. The monsoon is the same idea, on the scale of a whole continent and a whole season.

By May, the plains of north India and the high Tibetan Plateau are baking. Air over them rises, leaving low pressure behind. Over the Indian Ocean, the air is cooler and heavier. So moist winds start flowing north-east across the Arabian Sea and the Bay of Bengal. They carry enormous amounts of water evaporated from warm seas. The Himalayas act as a wall, holding the wet air over India.

## The rain's journey

The [India Meteorological Department](imd) declares the monsoon's arrival in [Kerala](kerala), usually around 1 June. From there, one branch sweeps up the west coast, and another curls in from the Bay of Bengal over the Northeast. Normally they cover the whole country by about 8 July. The rains usually reach [Mumbai](mumbai) around 11 June and Delhi near the end of June, and people in both cities follow the monsoon's progress like the score in a cricket match.

The [Western Ghats](western-ghats) force the wet winds upward, so the coast gets drenched while places just inland stay much drier. In Meghalaya, winds from the Bay of Bengal funnel into the Khasi Hills, and villages like Mawsynram get more than 11 metres of rain in a typical year.

## Why it matters so much

Most of India's rain falls in the four monsoon months. Farmers plant rice, cotton and other crops by it, and reservoirs fill for the rest of the year.

About half of India's farmland depends directly on rain. A good monsoon can lift the whole economy, while a weak one can mean drought, higher food prices and worried governments. Rivers swell, wells refill, and in the Western Ghats the [frogs](frogs-monsoon) start their loud evening chorus.

## Predicting it

The India Meteorological Department forecasts the monsoon using [ocean temperatures](ocean-and-climate), winds and [computer models](supercomputers). Forecasts use [probability](chance-weather).

Monsoon forecasting led to a big discovery. In the 1920s, Gilbert Walker, the head of the department, searched old weather records for clues. He found a giant see-saw in air pressure between the Indian and Pacific Oceans, which he called the [Southern Oscillation](el-nino). Today we know it is linked to El Niño, a warming of the Pacific that often weakens India's rains.

## Monsoon and history

Sailors used these seasonal winds for thousands of years to cross the Arabian Sea.

Ships from Arabia, Egypt and Rome sailed to [Kerala's ports](kochi) with the summer winds and went home when the winds reversed in winter. The word "monsoon" itself came to English from the Arabic word for season, by way of Portuguese sailors. India isn't the only place with a monsoon. [Arizona](grand-canyon-state) in the USA has a summer monsoon too, with sudden storms rolling over the desert.
