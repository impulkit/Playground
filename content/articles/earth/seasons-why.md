## The short answer

We have seasons because Earth is tilted. Its [axis](?axis), the imaginary line it spins around, leans over by about 23.4°. As Earth travels around the Sun over a year, the axis keeps pointing the same way in space.

So for part of the year, the northern half of Earth leans towards the Sun. Sunlight there is more direct, days are longer, and it is summer. Six months later, the northern half leans away. Sunlight is slanted, days are shorter, and it is winter. The southern half has the opposite seasons.

## Not about distance

Many people think summer comes when Earth is closest to the Sun. But Earth's [orbit](?orbit) is almost a perfect circle, and Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January, in the middle of winter in India and the United States.

The tilt matters far more than the small change in distance. And if distance were the cause, the whole planet would have summer at the same time. Instead, when it's winter in Chicago, it's summer in Sydney.

## Direct and slanted light

When the Sun is high in the sky, its light hits the ground straight on, concentrating its energy on a small area. When the Sun is low, the same amount of light is spread over a larger area, so each patch of ground is warmed less. Slanted sunlight also passes through more air, which absorbs and scatters some of it.

Long days add to the effect. In summer, the Sun is up for more hours, so the ground has more time to warm. In winter, long nights give it more time to cool.

## Solstices and equinoxes

Around 21 June, the northern half of Earth leans most towards the Sun. This is the June solstice, the longest day of the year in the north. Around 21 December comes the December solstice, the shortest day. In between, around 20 March and 22 September, are the [equinoxes](india-wonder-sun-temple-modhera), when day and night are roughly equal everywhere.

Many festivals mark these turning points. Makar Sankranti, in mid-January, celebrates the Sun's journey northward and the lengthening of days. In Gujarat it is famous for [kite flying](kites-festival). It once fell close to the winter solstice, but because Earth's axis slowly wobbles over thousands of years, the dates have drifted apart.

## Seasons in India

India sits close to the tropics. The Tropic of Cancer, the line where the Sun is directly overhead at noon on the June solstice, passes through [eight Indian states](india-states-tour), from Gujarat to Mizoram. Near the tropics, temperatures change less between summer and winter than in places farther north.

So in much of India, the seasons people notice most are about rain. Many Indian [calendars](calendars) traditionally divide the year into six seasons, or ritus, including Vasant (spring), Grishma (summer) and Varsha (the rains). The [monsoon](monsoon) shapes life in India as much as heat and cold do.

## Seasons at the poles

Near the poles, the tilt makes seasons extreme. In Utqiagvik, the [northernmost town](extreme-northernmost-town) in [Alaska](alaska), the Sun doesn't set for about 80 days in summer. In winter, it doesn't rise for about two months.

At the South Pole, the Sun rises once a year, around the September equinox, and sets once, around the March equinox. That's six months of daylight followed by six months of night. Scientists who spend winter at research stations in Antarctica don't see the Sun for months.

## When the hottest days come

The longest day is around 21 June, but in Delhi the hottest days come in May and June, before the monsoon rains cool things down. In much of the USA, the hottest weeks are usually in July. The land and sea take time to warm up, like a pot on a stove, so the hottest days often come a few weeks after the strongest sunshine.
