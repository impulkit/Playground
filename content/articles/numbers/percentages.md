## The short answer

Per cent means "out of a hundred". The words come from Latin, per centum, meaning "for each hundred". So 25 per cent, written 25%, means 25 out of every 100, which is the same as a quarter.

Percentages make it easy to compare things of different sizes. Scoring 45 out of 50 in one test and 72 out of 80 in another is hard to compare at a glance. Turn both into percentages and you get 90% each. That is why percentages appear on exam results, phone [batteries](batteries), [sale signs](discounts), [weather forecasts](weather-forecasts) and food packets.

## Out of a hundred

A percentage is really a fraction with 100 on the bottom. 50% is 50/100, which is a half. 10% is 10/100, or a tenth. 1% is one hundredth.

To find a percentage of something, change it to a [fraction](fractions) or a [decimal](decimals) and multiply. Ten per cent is easiest: just divide by ten. So 10% of 350 rupees is 35 rupees. Twenty per cent is double that, and 5% is half of it. With these tricks, you can work out most discounts in your head.

## Old taxes and new symbols

The idea is very old. The Roman emperor Augustus put a tax of one hundredth on goods sold at auction. Merchants in medieval Italy worked out interest on loans "per cento", for every hundred.

When writing by hand, they shortened "per cento" in different ways. Historians have traced how one shorthand, a "p" followed by a small looped mark, slowly changed over a couple of centuries into the sign % that we use now. [Taxes](taxes) and trade have been driving maths for a long time.

## Percentages all around

Your phone battery shows a percentage so you can tell at a glance how much charge is left. A weather forecast that says a 70% [chance of rain](chance-weather) means that on days with conditions like these, it rained about 7 times out of 10.

Percentages describe our planet and our bodies too. About 71% of the Earth's surface is covered by [ocean](ocean-zones). Roughly 60% of an adult's body weight is water, which is one reason you need to [drink water](water-drink) every day. In India, board exam results are given as percentages. Many American schools turn percentages into letter grades, so 90% and above often earns an A.

## Tricky percentages

Percentages can fool people. Suppose a shirt costs 1,000 rupees. The price rises by 10%, to 1,100. Then it falls by 10%. Ten per cent of 1,100 is 110, so the new price is 990 rupees. The rise and fall were the same percentage, but they were taken from different amounts.

Advertisers know that big percentages sound impressive. "Now with 50% more!" might mean a [chocolate](chocolate-making) bar went from 20 grams to 30 grams. Newspapers sometimes report that a risk has "doubled", a 100% increase, when it went from 1 in a million to 2 in a million. Both are true, but the second way sounds much scarier.

## More than 100 per cent

Can you have more than 100%? Yes, when you compare with a starting amount. If a tree grows from 2 metres to 5 metres, its height has increased by 150%. But you cannot score 110% in a test marked out of 100, and nobody can really give "110 per cent effort". There is only so much of you to give.
