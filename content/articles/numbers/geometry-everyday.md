## The short answer

Geometry is the maths of shapes, sizes, angles and space. You use it every time you judge whether a sofa will fit through a door, cut a sandwich into triangles or aim a ball into a goal. Builders, tailors, gardeners, game designers and pilots all use it at work.

The word comes from Greek and means "earth measurement". The ancient Greek historian Herodotus wrote that Egyptian officials had to re-measure farmers' fields after the yearly flood of the [Nile](egypt-nile) washed away the boundaries. Measuring land, buildings and the sky has driven geometry ever since.

## Shapes that keep things standing

Look closely at a railway bridge or a mobile phone tower and you will see triangles everywhere. A triangle is rigid. If its three sides are fixed, it cannot be squashed into a different shape. A square, in contrast, easily leans over into a diamond.

So engineers brace structures with triangles, in frames called trusses. Roofs, cranes, electricity pylons and many [bridges](bridges) depend on them. Even a simple folding chair has a triangle hidden in it when it is open.

[Circles](circles) have their own tricks. A manhole cover is round partly because a circle is the same width in every direction, so it cannot drop through its own hole. A square cover could be turned on its diagonal and fall in.

## Nature's geometry

Bees build their [honeycombs](honeycomb) from hexagons. Hexagons fit together with no gaps, like tiles on a floor, and they enclose a lot of space for the amount of wax used. In 1999, the mathematician Thomas Hales proved that hexagons are the most efficient way to divide a flat surface into equal areas.

You can find geometry in a sunflower's spirals, in the six arms of a snowflake and in the smooth curve of a thrown ball. Planets move in shapes called ellipses, a kind of stretched circle.

Spiders are geometers too. Many build orb webs with threads running out from the centre like spokes, and a spiral laid across them at fairly even spacing. Nobody teaches a young spider this. The pattern is built into its behaviour, and it gives a strong, light net that catches insects from any direction.

## Geometry on the field

[Sport is full of geometry](maths-sport). A cricket pitch is 22 yards long, about 20.12 m, and the creases are painted at exact distances. A fielder who wants to cut off a run quickly takes the shortest path, a straight line, to where the ball will be.

In [carrom](carrom), snooker and football, a ball bouncing off a wall leaves at about the same angle it arrived at. Good players learn to "see" these angles. A carrom player banking the striker off the edge to hit a coin is doing geometry by instinct.

## Old geometry, new phones

Indian priests more than 2,500 years ago wrote down rules for building fire altars with ropes and pegs. These texts, the Sulba Sutras, include ways to make right angles and squares of a given area. The oldest, by [Baudhayana](baudhayana), also states the rule now known as Pythagoras's theorem.

Today, the [GPS](gps-how) in a phone works out where you are by measuring your distance from several [satellites](satellites-help) at once and finding the one point that fits all those distances. Tailors, architects and video game designers rely on geometry too. A tailor turns body measurements into flat paper shapes that become a curved shirt, and a game designer builds whole worlds out of millions of tiny triangles. Even a [pizza](pizza) cut into eight equal slices is a lesson in angles: each slice is 45 degrees.
