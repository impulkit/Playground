## The short answer

Place value means that a digit's worth depends on where it sits in a number. In 444, all three digits are the same, but they mean different things. The one on the right is 4 ones. The middle one is 4 tens, or 40. The left one is 4 hundreds, or 400. Together they make 400 + 40 + 4.

Each place is worth ten times the place to its right. That's why we call it a base-10 system. With just ten digits, 0 to 9, and the rule of places, you can write any number at all, from your age to the number of stars in the galaxy.

## Life without place value

Not every number system works this way. In Roman numerals, X always means 10, wherever it appears. To write 2026 the Romans wrote MMXXVI: two thousands, two tens, a five and a one. Big numbers need long strings of letters, and doing a sum like 3,847 × 26 is very difficult.

Many ancient peoples counted with marks, knots or pebbles. Pebbles in Latin are *calculi*, which is where the word "calculate" comes from. To do sums, merchants used counting boards or an [abacus](abacus), moving beads up and down in rows. Each row stood for ones, tens or hundreds. In a way, the abacus used place value long before people wrote it down.

## India's big idea

Indian mathematicians put three ideas together: ten digits, place value and a symbol for zero to mark an empty place. Without zero, 25, 205 and 250 would look alike. By the time of [Aryabhata](aryabhata), around 499 CE, Indian astronomers were working with place value for huge calculations.

The idea spread west. In the 800s, the Persian scholar al-Khwarizmi wrote a book about calculating with [Indian numerals](indian-numerals). In 1202, the Italian mathematician Fibonacci, who had learned the system from traders in North Africa, explained it to Europeans in his book *Liber Abaci*. Merchants found it far faster than Roman numerals, though some cities at first banned it in official accounts, worried that the new digits were easy to forge.

## Base 60 on your wrist

Around 4,000 years ago, people in Mesopotamia used place value too, but in base 60. Each place was worth sixty times the place to its right. Sixty can be split evenly into halves, thirds, quarters, fifths and sixths, which made dividing easy.

We still live with their system. There are 60 seconds in a minute and 60 minutes in an hour, which is how [clocks](clocks) count. A circle has 360 degrees. When a clock reads 2:45, it's really a base-60 number: 2 hours and 45 sixtieths of an hour.

## How India groups its digits

Place value works the same everywhere, but people group the digits differently when they write big numbers. In India, after the first three digits, commas come every two places: 1,00,000 is one lakh and 1,00,00,000 is one crore. In the USA and much of the world, commas come every three places: 100,000 and 10,000,000.

Both are correct. One [lakh](lakh-crore) and a hundred thousand are exactly the same amount, with the commas in different places. The digits and their places don't change at all.

## The same trick, other bases

Place value doesn't have to use ten. The Maya of Central America used base 20, possibly from counting on fingers and toes, and also had a symbol for zero. Computers use base 2, called [binary](binary-code), with only 0 and 1. In binary, each place is worth double the one to its right: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16. So 101 in binary means 4 + 0 + 1, which is 5.

A car's odometer is a good picture of place value. When the ones wheel rolls past 9 back to 0, it nudges the tens wheel forward by one. Every number system with places works this way, whatever its base.
