## The short answer

A grid is a set of lines crossing at right angles, like the squares on graph paper. Put a grid over a map, label the columns with letters and the rows with numbers, and every square gets a short name, like B7. Instead of saying "somewhere near the top, a bit left of the river", you can say "look in B7".

Grids turn a messy question, "where is it?", into a pair of simple labels. The same idea locates a seat in a cinema, a square on a chessboard or a cell in a spreadsheet. On a global scale, lines of latitude and longitude make a grid over the whole planet, and your phone's [GPS](gps-how) uses it to pin you to within a few metres.

## Along the corridor, up the stairs

Most grids use two labels: one for how far across and one for how far up or down. Teachers often say "along the corridor, then up the stairs" to remember which comes first.

A [chessboard](chess-india) is a perfect grid. Its columns are labelled a to h and its rows 1 to 8, so every square has a name, such as e4. Players can write down whole games this way, and anyone can replay them years later. The board game Battleship works the same way: call out "C5" and hope you hit something.

## A grid over the Earth

Over 2,000 years ago, Greek scholars such as Hipparchus imagined lines wrapped around the globe. Lines of [latitude](?latitude) run east to west and tell you how far north or south of the equator you are. Lines of longitude, or [meridians](?meridian), run from pole to pole and tell you how far east or west you are.

Together they make [coordinates](coordinates). Delhi is at roughly 28.6° north and 77.2° east, and New York at about 40.7° north and 74.0° west. Latitude has a natural starting line, the equator. Longitude doesn't, so in 1884 a conference in Washington chose the meridian through Greenwich, in London, as zero.

## Cities built on grids

Some cities are grids themselves. More than 4,000 years ago, the [Indus cities](indus-cities) of Mohenjo-daro and Harappa had main streets crossing at right angles, with blocks of houses between them.

In 1811, planners laid out a grid of numbered streets and avenues across most of Manhattan in [New York](new-york). That is why a New Yorker can say "meet me at 42nd and 5th" and you know exactly where to go.

India's [Chandigarh](chandigarh), planned in the 1950s by the architect Le Corbusier, is divided into rectangular sectors, mostly about 800 metres by 1,200 metres. Each sector is numbered and has its own shops, schools and parks. The number 13 was skipped, apparently because some considered it unlucky.

## From PIN codes to DIGIPIN

In 1972, India Post introduced six-digit PIN codes. The first digit stands for a region, and later digits narrow down to a single [post office](post-office) area. A PIN code covers many streets, though, and many Indian homes have no clear address at all.

In 2025, India Post launched DIGIPIN. It lays an invisible grid over the whole country, dividing it into squares about 4 metres by 4 metres. Each square gets a 10-character code worked out from its latitude and longitude. A house in a village with no street name can now share a precise code for deliveries or ambulances.

## Scale and squares

On a map, each grid square stands for a real area on the ground. If the map's [scale](maps-scale) says each square is 1 kilometre across, you can count squares to estimate distances. Hikers, soldiers and rescue teams use grid references to tell each other exactly where to go, often down to a few metres.

It all comes back to one simple trick. Draw lines, label them, and a whole world of places becomes something you can name, share and find.
