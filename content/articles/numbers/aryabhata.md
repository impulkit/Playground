## The short answer

Aryabhata was an Indian mathematician and astronomer who lived about 1,500 years ago. In 499 CE, at the age of 23, he wrote the Aryabhatiya, a short book of verses on maths and astronomy that was studied in India for centuries.

He gave a very accurate value for pi, worked out rules for arithmetic and geometry, and made tables that helped calculate the positions of planets. He also said the Earth spins on its axis, which explains [day and night](day-night) and the apparent movement of the stars. Eclipses, he explained, happen when the shadow of the Earth or Moon falls on the other.

## A young author

Aryabhata tells us his own age. In his book, he says he was 23 years old when 3,600 years of the current age, the Kali Yuga, had passed. That works out to 499 CE, so he was born in 476 CE.

He wrote that he worked in Kusumapura, which most historians identify with [Pataliputra](bihar-history), near modern Patna in Bihar. It was a great centre of learning during the [Gupta age](gupta-golden-age). Some scholars think he came from elsewhere, possibly the south, partly because his work was most closely studied in Kerala.

## A whole universe in 121 verses

The Aryabhatiya is short, just 121 verses in [Sanskrit](sanskrit), written to be memorised. The verses are so compact that later scholars wrote long commentaries explaining them. It has four sections: one on a system for writing numbers with letters, one on mathematics, one on time and one on the movements of the planets.

The maths section covers square and cube roots, [areas of triangles](area-volume) and [circles](circles), and ways to solve certain [equations](equations). It also includes a table of half-chords of a circle, which became the sine function in trigonometry. The Sanskrit word jya was carried into Arabic as jiba. It was later misread as jaib, meaning a fold or pocket, and translated into Latin as sinus, which became the English "sine".

## Pi and place value

Aryabhata gave a rule that works out to pi being about 3.1416, very close to the true value of 3.14159. He described this value as approximate. Some historians think he understood that [pi](pi) could not be written exactly.

His calculations used a place-value system, in which a digit's position gives its value. That system depends on the idea of an empty place, which later became [zero](zero-invent). But Aryabhata did not write rules for zero as a number. That came over a century later with Brahmagupta.

## A spinning Earth

Most ancient astronomers thought the stars were fixed on a great sphere that turned around a still Earth once a day. Aryabhata disagreed. He said the Earth spins, and he used the image of a person on a boat seeing the trees on the shore move backwards.

He also explained [eclipses](eclipses) using shadows. A lunar eclipse happens when the Earth's shadow falls on the Moon, and a solar eclipse when the Moon blocks the Sun. Many people in his time believed the demon Rahu swallowed the Sun or Moon. Aryabhata's explanation did not involve Rahu at all. He still thought the Sun and planets went around the Earth, as almost everyone did then.

## A name in orbit

Later astronomers like Brahmagupta argued with some of his ideas, including the spinning Earth. But Aryabhata's methods spread across India and, through Arabic translations, influenced astronomers in Baghdad.

On 19 April 1975, [India's first satellite](space-aryabhata-satellite) was launched on a Soviet rocket. It was named Aryabhata, a nod to India's long tradition in astronomy. An observatory in Nainital, the Aryabhatta Research Institute of Observational Sciences, also carries his name.
