## The short answer

Artists use maths all the time, sometimes on purpose and sometimes by instinct. Perspective, which makes a flat painting look deep, is geometry. Symmetry, which gives the Taj Mahal its calm balance, is geometry too. Tile patterns, kolams and quilts are built from shapes that fit together by mathematical rules.

Some artists love maths openly. The Dutch artist [M. C. Escher](maths-art-escher) drew birds that turn into fish and staircases that climb forever. Others use simple rules of thumb, such as dividing a picture into thirds. Today, digital artists use maths in every line of code, and some create art by turning [equations](equations) into pictures.

## Making flat look deep

Before the 1400s, many European paintings looked rather flat. Distant people were not always smaller, and buildings tilted oddly. Then, in Florence, the architect Filippo Brunelleschi worked out the rules of [perspective](perspective). In 1435, Leon Battista Alberti wrote them down in a book called *On Painting*.

The key idea is the vanishing point. Lines that run straight away from the viewer, like the edges of a road or railway, appear to meet at a single point on the horizon. Objects shrink in size by a regular amount as they get further away. Leonardo da Vinci's *Last Supper* uses perspective so that all the lines of the room lead to Jesus's head at the centre.

## Kolam and rangoli

In South India, especially Tamil Nadu, many women draw a [kolam](art-kolam) every morning at the doorstep, using rice flour. Many kolams start with a grid of dots called *pulli*. The artist then draws lines that loop around the dots, often in one continuous line that ends where it started.

Mathematicians and computer scientists have studied kolams, because they follow precise rules of symmetry and repetition. Some patterns can be grown step by step, like the instructions in a computer program. Similar patterns turn up in [rangoli](rangoli-symmetry) across India.

## Tiles and impossible worlds

Shapes that cover a surface without gaps or overlaps form a tessellation. Islamic artists became masters of this, covering walls with stars and polygons that repeat in complex ways. The [Alhambra palace in Spain](spain) is one of the most famous examples.

The Dutch artist M. C. Escher visited the [Alhambra](alhambra) and was inspired. He went on to make [tessellations](tessellations) of lizards, birds and fish that lock together like jigsaw pieces. He also drew [impossible buildings](puzzle-impossible-shapes), such as staircases that seem to go up forever in a loop.

## Symmetry and balance

Many great buildings use symmetry. The [Taj Mahal](taj-mahal) is almost perfectly mirror-symmetrical, with matching minarets and gardens on either side. Its reflection in the long pool doubles the effect. Indian temples, [Gothic cathedrals](gothic-cathedrals) and the US Capitol in Washington all use symmetry to feel calm and grand.

Mathematicians describe several kinds. Mirror symmetry means one half reflects the other, like a butterfly. Rotational symmetry means a design looks the same after a turn, like a [rangoli](rangoli-art), a wheel or the round rose window of a cathedral. Translation symmetry means a pattern repeats as it slides along, like the border of a saree or a row of block-printed flowers. Artists often mix all three, then break the pattern in one small place to catch your eye.

## A ratio with a reputation

The golden ratio, about 1.618, is often said to be hidden in great art. Some modern artists and designers have used it on purpose. But many claims are doubtful. Historians have found little evidence that the builders of the Parthenon or Leonardo planned their work with it. Measure a building in enough places and you can find almost any number you want.

What is certain is that artists today have more maths at hand than ever. Computer graphics use geometry to draw every frame of an animated film. [Fractals](fractals), shapes that repeat at smaller and smaller sizes, can be turned into pictures of coastlines, clouds and ferns that look strikingly real.
