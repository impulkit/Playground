## The short answer

A prime number is a whole number greater than 1 that can only be divided exactly by 1 and by itself. The first few are 2, 3, 5, 7, 11 and 13. A number like 12 is not prime, because it can be made by multiplying 3 × 4 or 2 × 6. Numbers like that are called composite.

Primes are the building blocks of all whole numbers. Every whole number above 1 can be made by multiplying primes together, in exactly one way. For example, 60 is 2 × 2 × 3 × 5. Primes look simple, but they hold some of the [oldest unsolved puzzles](unsolved-problems) in maths, and they keep your messages safe online.

## Building blocks

Think of primes like atoms in chemistry. Just as water is made of hydrogen and oxygen, 30 is made of 2, 3 and 5. No matter how you start splitting a number, you always end with the same primes. This rule is so important that it is called the fundamental theorem of arithmetic.

Two is the only even prime, because every other [even number](even-odd) can be divided by 2. After that, primes become rarer as numbers get bigger, but they never stop. Around 300 BCE, the Greek mathematician [Euclid](euclid) proved there are infinitely many. His proof is short and clever, and mathematicians still admire it.

## The sieve and the search

Over 2,000 years ago, Eratosthenes, a Greek scholar in Egypt, found a simple way to list primes. Write out numbers, then cross out multiples of 2, then of 3, then of 5, and so on. The ones left standing are prime. Computers still use versions of his sieve.

Today, volunteers around the world run a project called the Great Internet Mersenne Prime Search, using spare computer time to hunt for giant primes. In October 2024, it confirmed the largest prime known so far, with more than 41 million digits. It was found by Luke Durant, a former engineer, who used thousands of graphics processors in [cloud data centres](data-centres).

## Secrets made of primes

Multiplying two primes is easy. Try 13 × 17, and you get 221. But going backwards is much harder. If someone gives you a huge number and says it is two primes multiplied, finding them could take the fastest computers longer than the age of the universe.

Many systems that keep data safe online use this idea. Your browser, banking apps and payment systems rely on maths built on primes and other hard problems. That is part of what protects your [passwords](passwords) and card details as they travel across the [internet](internet-how). Learn how [secret codes](cipher-codes) have changed over time.

## A message to the stars

In 1974, astronomers used the giant Arecibo radio telescope in [Puerto Rico](puerto-rico) to beam a message towards a cluster of stars about 25,000 light years away. They chose a length of 1,679 signals because it is 23 × 73, the product of two primes. A clever listener would realise the signal can only be arranged as a grid of 23 by 73, and see a picture appear. Nobody expects a reply soon, but scientists hope primes would be a sign of intelligence anywhere, as you can read in [alien life](alien-life).

## Cicadas and unsolved puzzles

In the eastern United States, some kinds of [cicadas](maths-prime-cicadas) spend 13 or 17 years underground, then emerge in their billions at once. Both numbers are prime. One theory says prime cycles make it harder for predators with shorter cycles to time their own numbers to match. Biologists still debate whether that is the real reason.

Some prime puzzles remain unsolved after centuries. In 1742 Christian Goldbach guessed that every even number above 2 is the sum of two primes, like 10 = 3 + 7. Computers have checked it for enormous numbers, but nobody has proved it is always true.
