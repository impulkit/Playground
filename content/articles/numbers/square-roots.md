## The short answer

A square root answers the question: which number, multiplied by itself, gives this number? The square root of 25 is 5, because 5 × 5 = 25. The square root of 100 is 10. We write it with a special sign, √, so √25 = 5.

The name comes from squares. If a square has [an area of 49 tiles](area-volume), its sides are √49 = 7 tiles long. Finding a square root is like working backwards from the area of a square to the length of its side. Some numbers, like 2, have square roots whose digits go on forever.

## Perfect and not so perfect

Numbers like 1, 4, 9, 16, 25 and 36 are called perfect squares. Their square roots are whole numbers. Most numbers are not like that. √2 is about 1.414. √10 is about 3.162. Their [decimals](decimals) never end.

In ancient Greece, followers of Pythagoras proved that √2 cannot be written as a fraction at all. Numbers like this are called irrational, meaning they can't be written as a ratio of two whole numbers. A legend says a follower named Hippasus was drowned for revealing it, but there is no reliable evidence for the story.

## A tablet from Babylon

At Yale University in the USA there is a small, round clay tablet called YBC 7289. It comes from ancient Babylon, in [Mesopotamia](mesopotamia), and is thought to date from around 1800 to 1600 BCE. It may have been a student's exercise.

On it is a square with its diagonals drawn in. Next to the diagonal is a number, written in the Babylonian base-60 system, that equals about 1.41421296. That is the square root of 2, correct to about six decimal places. The scribe also used it to work out the diagonal of a square with sides of 30.

In India, the Sulba Sutras gave √2 as about 1.4142156, and in 499 CE [Aryabhata](aryabhata) described a step-by-step method for finding square roots of large numbers.

## Guess, check, improve

How did people find square roots before calculators? One good method is very old, and sometimes called the Babylonian method. Make a guess. Divide the number by your guess. Then take the average of your guess and the answer. That average is a better guess. Repeat.

Try it for √10. Guess 3. 10 ÷ 3 is about 3.33. The average of 3 and 3.33 is about 3.17. Try again with 3.17 and you get about 3.1623, which is almost exactly right. A modern [calculator](calculators) uses clever methods like this, repeated very fast.

## Where square roots show up

Square roots appear whenever squares and distances do. The [Pythagorean theorem](pythagoras) says a² + b² = c², so finding the long side of a triangle means taking a square root. A builder checking a diagonal, a game working out how far apart two characters are, and a phone's GPS all use them.

They also show up in nature and science. [A pendulum's swing time](how-swings) depends on the square root of its length, so a pendulum four times as long swings only twice as slowly. And what about the square root of a negative number, like −1? No ordinary number works, because any number times itself is positive. So mathematicians invented a new one, called *i*. It sounds like make-believe, but engineers use it every day to design electrical circuits.
