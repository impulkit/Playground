## The short answer

A 3D shape is a solid shape that takes up space. It has three dimensions: length, width and height. A drawing of a square is flat, or 2D. A dice, a brick or a cricket ball is 3D, because you can hold it and turn it around.

Some 3D shapes have flat sides and straight edges, like cubes, pyramids and prisms. Mathematicians call these [polyhedra](?polyhedron), from Greek words meaning "many seats". Others are curved, like spheres, cylinders and cones. Almost everything you can pick up is a 3D shape, or a mixture of several.

## Faces, edges and corners

A polyhedron has three kinds of parts. **Faces** are the flat surfaces. **Edges** are the lines where two faces meet. **Vertices**, or corners, are the points where edges meet.

A cube has 6 square faces, 12 edges and 8 corners. A square-based pyramid, like the [pyramids](pyramids) of Egypt, has 5 faces, 8 edges and 5 corners. A triangular prism, shaped like a tent, has 5 faces, 9 edges and 6 corners.

Curved shapes need different words. A cylinder has two flat circular faces and one curved surface. A cone has one flat circle and a point at the top, called the apex. A sphere has no flat faces, edges or corners at all.

## A rule that always works

In the 1750s, the Swiss mathematician Leonhard Euler noticed something. For a cube, take the corners and subtract the edges, then add the faces: 8 − 12 + 6 = 2. For a square pyramid: 5 − 8 + 5 = 2. For a prism: 6 − 9 + 5 = 2.

It works for every solid without holes through it, however lumpy or strange. A shape with a tunnel through it, like a picture frame or a bead, gives a different answer. Mathematicians use this to tell shapes apart in a field called topology.

## The five perfect solids

Some solids are especially neat. Every face is the same regular shape, and the same number of faces meet at every corner. There are only five: the tetrahedron, with 4 triangles; the cube, with 6 squares; the octahedron, with 8 triangles; the dodecahedron, with 12 pentagons; and the icosahedron, with 20 triangles.

The Greek philosopher Plato linked them to fire, earth, air, water and the heavens, so they are called Platonic solids. [Euclid](euclid) proved that there can be only five. Today they are the dice of [board games](board-games): four-sided, six-sided, eight-sided, twelve-sided and twenty-sided.

## Shapes in the world

Nature is full of 3D shapes. Salt crystals grow as tiny cubes. Soap [bubbles](bubbles-round) form spheres, because a sphere holds the most air inside the smallest skin. Bees build [honeycomb](honeycomb) from six-sided prisms that fit together without gaps.

People build them too. The [Great Stupa at Sanchi](sanchi) is a hemisphere, half a sphere. Water tanks are often cylinders, ice-cream cones are cones, and a [Rubik's Cube](rubiks-cube) is a cube made of 26 smaller visible cubes. The classic black-and-white football, first used at the 1970 World Cup, is made of 12 pentagons and 20 hexagons stitched together, which puffs up into something close to a sphere.

## Flattening a solid

If you unfold a cardboard box carefully, you get a flat shape called a [net](nets-boxes). Every polyhedron has at least one net. A cube has 11 different ones, and some look nothing like a cross. Packaging designers think about nets all the time, trying to waste as little card as possible.

Going the other way, from flat to solid, is how origami artists and engineers fold flat sheets into boxes, domes and even solar panels for satellites.
