## The short answer

Months come from the Moon. Its shape goes through a full cycle, from new Moon to full Moon and back, in about 29.5 days. People everywhere noticed this, and many early calendars counted time in Moons. The word "month" itself comes from "Moon".

About twelve of these cycles fit into a year of seasons. Twelve Moons make about 354 days, while the Earth takes about 365¼ days to go round the Sun. So twelve months became the standard. Over time, many calendars stretched their months to 30 or 31 days so that twelve of them would fill the solar year exactly.

## The Moon and the Sun disagree

The trouble is that the Moon and the Sun don't fit together neatly. Twelve Moon cycles fall about 11 days short of a year. Thirteen go about 18 days over.

Different cultures solved this in different ways. The Islamic calendar follows the Moon only, with years of 354 or 355 days. So festivals like [Eid](eid) move about 11 days earlier each year in the solar calendar. Many Hindu, Jewish and Chinese calendars follow the Moon but add an extra month every two or three years to stay in step with the seasons.

The calendar used in most of the world today goes the other way. It ignores the Moon and follows the Sun.

## A Roman calendar with gaps

Our calendar comes from [ancient Rome](ancient-rome). According to Roman writers, the very first Roman calendar had just ten months and began in March, when farming and fighting started again after winter. The last month was the tenth, *December*, from the Latin *decem*, "ten". September, October and November meant seven, eight and nine.

The cold weeks of winter were simply left out, without a month. Later, [January and February](calendar-maths) were added to fill the gap. Roman tradition credited the ten-month calendar to Romulus, the legendary founder of the city, and the two new months to the next king, Numa. Eventually the new year moved to January, and the old [number names](numbers-names) ended up two months out of place.

## Julius Caesar fixes the year

By the time of Julius Caesar, the Roman calendar was a mess. Officials sometimes added extra days to keep friends in power. The months had drifted so far that harvest festivals fell in the wrong season.

In 46 BCE, Caesar added extra days to bring the calendar back in line. That year had about 445 days and was later nicknamed the "year of confusion". From 45 BCE, the new Julian calendar had 365 days, with an extra day every fourth year.

After his death, the month Quintilis was renamed *July* in his honour. Later, Sextilis became *August*, after the first emperor, Augustus.

## Twelve months, many calendars

In 1582, Pope Gregory XIII made a small change to the leap year rule. His Gregorian calendar is the one on most walls today, and it has a [leap year](leap-years) system that keeps it in step with the [seasons](seasons-why) for thousands of years.

But plenty of other calendars are still used side by side. [Ethiopia](ethiopia)'s calendar has 13 months: twelve of 30 days each, and a short thirteenth month of five or six days. India has an official national calendar, along with many regional and religious calendars. Chinese New Year falls on a different date each year, in January or February, because it follows the Moon. That is why people around the world celebrate [the new year](new-year) on so many different days.
