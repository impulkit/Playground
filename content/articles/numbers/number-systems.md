## The short answer

When you count to ten, you run out of single digits and start again, putting a 1 in the tens place. This is called base ten, or the decimal system. Almost every culture on Earth counts in tens, and the simplest explanation is right at the end of your arms. People counted on their [fingers](maths-binary-fingers) long before they wrote numbers down.

But tens are not the only choice. Some peoples counted in twenties, using fingers and toes. Others used fives, eights, [twelves](clocks-maths) or sixties. Computers count in twos. Traces of these other systems hide in our clocks, our languages and even in [famous speeches](speeches-famous).

## Ten digits and a clever trick

The digits we use, 0 to 9, plus the idea that a digit's place shows its value, were developed in India. In 222, the first 2 means two hundreds, the second two tens and the last just two. With [zero](zero-invent) as a placeholder, ten symbols can write any number at all.

Arab scholars learned this system and carried it west. Europeans called the digits Arabic numerals, but Arab writers called them Indian numbers. Today they are often called Hindu-Arabic numerals. They replaced clumsy systems like [Roman numerals](roman-numerals), because they make sums much faster.

The digits did not always look the way they do now. Early Indian forms of the numbers, found in inscriptions and old manuscripts, changed shape as they travelled. The 2 and 3 you write probably began as two and three short strokes, joined together by fast-moving pens. Even today, the Devanagari digits used in Hindi look quite different from the ones in this sentence.

## Fingers and toes

The Maya of Central America counted in twenties, perhaps using fingers and toes. They had a symbol for zero and wrote numbers with dots, bars and a shell shape. The [Maya](aztecs-maya) used this system to run detailed [calendars](calendars).

Twenty also left marks in Europe. In French, 80 is quatre-vingts, "four twenties". Old English counted in scores, or twenties. When [Abraham Lincoln](abraham-lincoln) began his Gettysburg Address in 1863 with "Four score and seven years ago", he meant 87 years.

Some peoples counted in eights. The Yuki of California are said to have counted the spaces between their fingers instead of the fingers themselves. Number words around the world show huge variety, as you can see in the many [languages of the world](languages-world).

## Sixties and twelves

The Babylonians, about 4,000 years ago, counted in sixties. We still use their system for time and angles: 60 seconds, 60 minutes and 360 degrees. Sixty can be split evenly in many ways, which helps when dividing things up.

Twelve shows up a lot too. There are 12 months, 12 hours on a clock face, 12 inches in a foot and 12 items in a dozen. A few enthusiasts even argue the world should switch to base 12, because 12 divides more neatly than 10. They call it dozenal counting.

## Twos for computers

Computers use base two, called [binary](binary-code). A tiny switch is either off or on, so it can show only 0 or 1. In binary, 2 is written 10, 4 is 100 and 8 is 1000. Programmers also use base 16, called hexadecimal, using the letters A to F for the numbers 10 to 15.

Even within base ten, people group digits differently. Indians write one hundred thousand as 1,00,000, a lakh, and ten million as 1,00,00,000, a crore. Americans write 100,000 and 10,000,000. The digits are the same; only the commas move.
