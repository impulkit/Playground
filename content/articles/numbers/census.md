## The short answer

You count a billion people the slow way: one home at a time. In a census, the government trains an army of counters, called enumerators. Each one is given a small patch of streets or villages, and visits every household in it. They ask the same questions everywhere: who lives here, how old they are, what work they do.

All the counting is tied to one exact moment. India's 2011 census counted people as they were at midnight on 1 March 2011. Babies born after that moment were left out, so the totals don't keep shifting while the counting goes on. Then millions of forms are added up, and a country finds out how many of its people there are, and where.

## A plan before the knocking

A census really happens in two steps. First, counters walk every lane to list every building and home, so nothing gets missed later. Months afterwards, they return to count the people inside.

In India in 2011, about 27 lakh enumerators and supervisors did the work, many of them schoolteachers. They reached high mountain villages, island homes and [city slums](slums-housing) alike. Special teams counted homeless people on a chosen night, at railway platforms and under flyovers.

The result: about 121 crore people, roughly a sixth of the world's [population](population). India's next census, delayed by the COVID-19 pandemic, began with house listing in April 2026 and is due to count people in February 2027. It is the first to collect answers mainly on mobile phones rather than paper.

## The machine that sped it up

In the USA, counting has been the law since the very start. The [US Constitution](us-constitution) says the people must be counted every ten years, and the first census, in 1790, found about 3.9 million people.

By 1880, the country had grown so much that adding up the answers by hand took about eight years. An engineer named Herman Hollerith had an idea. Each person's answers were punched as holes in a card. A machine pushed metal pins against the card, and wherever a pin passed through a hole, it closed an electric circuit and moved a counter. The 1890 census was tallied far faster. Hollerith's company later joined others to become IBM, and his punched cards fed some of the [first computers](first-computers).

## Why bother counting?

A census decides real things. In the USA, the totals set how many seats each state gets in the House of Representatives, part of [Congress](congress-usa). In India, census numbers help plan where to build schools, hospitals and roads, and how to share money between states.

The questions also show how a country changes. Censuses record how many people can read, which languages they speak at home, and how many have [moved from villages to cities](migration-india). A huge pile of [big numbers](big-numbers) slowly turns into a picture of everyday life.

## Nobody counts perfectly

Every census misses some people and counts a few twice. Families move. Some people are away, or worried about strangers asking questions. In the USA, studies after recent censuses found that young children were among the people most often missed.

To catch errors, statisticians run a second, smaller survey soon after the main count. They visit a sample of areas again and compare the two lists. The gap tells them roughly how many people slipped through. A census is less like a perfect photograph and more like a very careful sketch, checked twice.
