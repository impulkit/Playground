## The short answer

A number pattern, or sequence, is a list of numbers that follows a rule. Spot the rule and you can predict every number that comes next, even the millionth one.

Some rules are simple. In 5, 10, 15, 20 you keep adding 5. In 1, 3, 9, 27 you keep multiplying by 3. Others are sneakier, like 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, which are the [square numbers](triangular-numbers). Mathematicians love patterns because finding one usually means something deeper is going on underneath.

## Poets who counted

[Sanskrit](sanskrit) poetry is built from short and long syllables, a bit like short and long beats in music. Around 2,000 years ago, the scholar Pingala studied how many different rhythms you could make from them.

Later writers, including Virahanka (around the 7th century) and Hemachandra (12th century), asked a neat question. If a short beat lasts one unit and a long beat lasts two, how many rhythms fill a line of a given length? The answers ran 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13: each number is the sum of the two before.

In 1202, Leonardo of Pisa, nicknamed Fibonacci, used the same numbers in a puzzle about breeding rabbits. Europe named them after him. The pattern also turns up in pine cones and sunflower heads, as [Fibonacci in nature](fibonacci-nature) explains.

## A triangle that keeps giving

Write a 1 at the top. Below, write 1 1. Each new row starts and ends with 1, and every other number is the sum of the two numbers just above it. You get 1 2 1, then 1 3 3 1, then 1 4 6 4 1.

This triangle was studied in India, where a 10th-century commentary on Pingala describes it as the Meru Prastara, named after a mythical mountain. Chinese mathematicians such as Yang Hui wrote about it in the 1200s, and French thinker Blaise Pascal studied it in the 1650s. Hidden inside are the counting numbers, the [triangular numbers](triangular-numbers), powers of two and even the Fibonacci numbers.

## Doubling and the chessboard

A well-known legend links this to [chess](chess-india), whose earliest known form, chaturanga, comes from India. The inventor, it says, asked a king for one grain of rice on the first square of the board. He wanted two on the second, four on the third, and so on, [doubling each time](exponents). The king laughed at such a small reward. By the 64th square, the total would be more rice than the whole world grows in centuries.

The story is a legend, but the maths is real. Doubling patterns start slowly and then explode. Scientists see the same shape when bacteria multiply or when a video spreads online. Fold a sheet of paper in half again and again, and its thickness doubles each time too, which is why you can rarely fold it more than seven or eight times.

## How to hunt for a rule

When you meet a new sequence, try a few tests. Look at the gaps between numbers. If the gaps are equal, you're adding. If they grow, look at the gaps between the gaps. Then try dividing each number by the one before. A steady answer means you're multiplying.

Some patterns resist every test. The [prime numbers](prime-numbers) (2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13) follow no simple rule that anyone has found, even though mathematicians have chased one for over 2,000 years. That stubbornness is part of what keeps them interested.
