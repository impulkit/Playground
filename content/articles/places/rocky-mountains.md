## The short answer

The Rocky Mountains, or Rockies, are the great mountain range of western North America. They run for more than 4,800 kilometres, from northern British Columbia in Canada down through Montana, Wyoming and Colorado to New Mexico in the USA. Their highest peak is Mount Elbert in Colorado, at 4,401 metres.

Compared with the old, rounded [Appalachians](appalachians) in the east, the Rockies are young and jagged. Most of their rise happened between about 80 and 55 million years ago, around the time [the last dinosaurs lived](extinction). Later, during the ice ages, [glaciers](glaciers) sharpened the peaks and carved deep U-shaped valleys and lakes.

Colorado alone has more than 50 peaks above 14,000 feet (about 4,270 metres). Locals call them "fourteeners", and some people spend years trying to climb every one.

## The great divide

Along the highest ridges of the Rockies runs an invisible line called the Continental Divide. Rain or snow falling to the west of it flows towards the Pacific. Rain falling to the east flows to the Atlantic, mostly by way of the [Mississippi](mississippi) and the Gulf of Mexico.

Some of North America's big rivers start in the Rockies, including the Colorado, the Rio Grande and the Missouri. Snow piles up on the peaks in winter, then melts slowly through spring and summer. Cities and farms far away, in dry states such as [Arizona](grand-canyon-state) and California, depend on that slow melt for their water.

## Bighorns, elk and grizzlies

As you climb the Rockies, the land changes in bands. Dry grassland gives way to pine and spruce forest, and above about 3,500 metres in Colorado the trees stop. Up there is alpine tundra, a land of low flowers, lichens and wind.

Bighorn sheep, with huge curled horns, leap across ledges. Mountain goats, with white shaggy coats, live on even steeper cliffs. Elk gather in herds, and in autumn the males make a high, whistling call called bugling. Grizzly bears still roam the northern Rockies, in [Yellowstone](usa-wonder-yellowstone), Glacier National Park and Canada. In Yellowstone, [wolves](yellowstone-wolves) were brought back in 1995 after being wiped out decades earlier.

## Crossing the wall

For travellers heading west, the Rockies were a wall. In 1805, the explorers [Meriwether Lewis and William Clark](lewis-clark), guided in part by a young Shoshone woman named [Sacagawea](usa-sacagawea), struggled across the Bitterroot Mountains in snow and nearly starved.

Railways came later. Canada's first railway across the country was finished in 1885, climbing through the Rockies at Kicking Horse Pass. The same year, Canada set aside land near [hot springs](geysers-hot-springs) at Banff, which grew into the country's first national park. Today trains, highways and tunnels cross the range, but storms still close mountain passes in winter.

## Life high up

Denver, the capital of Colorado, is nicknamed the "Mile High City" because it sits about 1,600 metres above sea level. One step on the state Capitol building is marked as one mile high.

Air is thinner at that height, so there is less air pressure pushing down on the water in a pot. Water [boils](boiling-water) at a lower temperature, about 95°C, and food takes longer to cook. Visitors from the coast often feel short of breath for a day or two. Athletes sometimes train in Colorado on purpose, because their bodies make extra red blood cells to cope with the thin air. In Ladakh, India's soldiers and [climbers](mountaineering) know the same trick.
