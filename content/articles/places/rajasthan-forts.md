## The short answer

For hundreds of years, Rajasthan was split into many kingdoms ruled by [Rajput](rajputs) clans, such as the rulers of Mewar, Marwar and Amber. Neighbouring kingdoms often fought each other, and armies from outside the region invaded too. Every ruler needed a stronghold, and a stronghold showed off his power.

The land helped. The Aravalli hills, some of the oldest mountains on Earth, give steep, rocky ridges that are hard to climb. In the west, the Thar Desert made attacks difficult for any army without enough water. Builders placed forts on hilltops, ridges and desert rises, and surrounded them with thick walls. Many still stand, from [Chittorgarh](chittorgarh) to Jaisalmer.

## A wall you can see from far away

Kumbhalgarh, built mostly in the 1400s by Rana Kumbha of Mewar, has a wall that runs for about 36 km over the hills. In places it is wide enough for several horses to ride side by side. Inside are dozens of temples, wells and palaces.

Chittorgarh, also in Mewar, is one of the largest forts in India, spread over a long hilltop about as big as several hundred football fields put together. It was besieged three times in its long history, and its stories of courage are still told in songs.

## Built to survive a siege

An attacking army often didn't try to climb the walls. It surrounded the fort and waited for the people inside to run out of food or water. So builders planned for long sieges.

Forts stored grain in deep stores and collected every drop of rain. Amber Fort near [Jaipur](jaipur), and many others, used channels, step wells and large tanks to keep water through the dry months. Gates came in a series, one after another, often on a winding uphill path. Iron spikes on the doors stopped elephants from ramming them, and sharp bends meant no enemy could charge straight in. Narrow slits in the walls let [archers](archery), and later gunners, shoot out while staying hidden.

## The golden fort in the desert

Jaisalmer Fort rises out of the [Thar Desert](thar-formed) like a giant sandcastle. It was founded in 1156 by Rawal Jaisal, and is built from yellow sandstone that glows gold in the evening. It sat on trade routes where camel caravans carried goods between India and lands to the west.

What makes it unusual today is that people never left. Thousands still live inside, with homes, shops, temples and small hotels in its narrow lanes. That brings problems: modern water pipes and drains have leaked into the old walls, and parts have crumbled. Conservation groups are working to protect it.

## From war to world heritage

Forts lost their military purpose in the 1800s, when much of Rajasthan came under British influence and cannons got stronger. Many royal families turned parts of their forts into museums or hotels. Mehrangarh in Jodhpur, founded in 1459, now holds collections of palanquins, armour and paintings.

In 2013, UNESCO listed six of the state's hill forts as a World Heritage Site: Chittorgarh, Kumbhalgarh, [Ranthambore](ranthambore), Gagron, Amber and Jaisalmer. They joined India's long list of [World Heritage Sites](unesco-sites), chosen because they show how Rajput builders used hills, rivers and desert for defence. Gagron is unusual: rivers surround it on three sides, so it is a hill fort and a water fort at once. Ranthambore's fort now stands inside a tiger reserve. Visitors climbing to its gates sometimes spot a tiger resting near the old walls, as if it has taken over guard duty.
