## The short answer

Paris has been called the City of Light, or *la Ville Lumière*, for two reasons. In the 1660s, the city's police chief ordered lanterns to be hung in the streets, making Paris one of the first big cities lit at night. Later came gas lamps and then electric lights.

In the 1700s, Paris was also a centre of the Enlightenment, a time when thinkers argued for science, reason and [human rights](human-rights). The "light" can mean ideas as well as lamps. Today Paris is the capital of France and one of the most visited cities in the world.

## From an island to a capital

More than 2,000 years ago, a tribe called the Parisii lived on an island in the River Seine. The Romans conquered them and built a town called Lutetia. The island, now the Île de la Cité, is still the heart of Paris.

On it stands Notre-Dame, a [Gothic cathedral](gothic-cathedrals) begun in 1163. In April 2019, a fire destroyed its roof and spire. Craftspeople spent five years rebuilding them, using oak beams and traditional tools, and the cathedral reopened in December 2024.

## The tower nobody wanted

In 1889, France held a giant world fair to mark 100 years since the [French Revolution](french-revolution). The engineer Gustave Eiffel's company built an iron tower about 300 m tall as its entrance. It was the tallest structure in the world until 1930.

Many artists hated it. It was supposed to come down after 20 years, but scientists used it for radio experiments, and the army found it useful for sending signals. So it stayed. Now with its antennas it is about 330 m tall, and the [Eiffel Tower](eiffel-tower) is repainted by hand about every seven years.

## Art and museums

The [Louvre](famous-paintings) was once a royal palace. It opened as a public museum in 1793 and is now the most visited museum in the world. Its most famous painting, the [Mona Lisa](mona-lisa) by [Leonardo da Vinci](leonardo), hangs behind thick glass.

In the late 1800s, painters such as [Claude Monet](impressionists-monet) worked in and around Paris, painting outdoors and catching changing light. Their style, called [Impressionism](impressionism), changed art. Many of their works hang in the Musée d'Orsay, a museum in an old railway station.

## Wide streets and the Métro

In the 1850s and 1860s, the city's planner Baron Haussmann knocked down many crowded medieval streets and built wide avenues called boulevards. They were lined with trees and cream-coloured stone buildings of the same height. Much of Paris still looks this way. The Métro, the [underground railway](metro-trains), opened in 1900, and its old station entrances have curving green ironwork.

## Ideas that spread

During the French Revolution, scientists in Paris helped create the metre and the kilogram, so that everyone could measure the same way. The [metric system](metric-system) is now used by almost every country.

In 2024, Paris hosted the [Olympics](olympics) for the third time, with the opening ceremony held on boats along the Seine instead of in a stadium. Athletes sailed past the Louvre and Notre-Dame, and the Olympic cauldron rose into the sky beneath a giant balloon.

## A city of cafés and bread

Paris is famous for its cafés, where people sit at small round tables on the pavement, watching the street. Many Parisians buy a fresh baguette, a long thin loaf, every day. Bakers compete each year for the prize of best baguette in Paris, and the winner supplies bread to the French president's palace for a year.
