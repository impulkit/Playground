## The short answer

Living next to a volcano sounds like a terrible idea. Yet hundreds of millions of people do it, and many have good reasons.

Volcanic ash and lava break down into some of the most fertile soil on Earth, so farms near volcanoes can grow rich crops. Volcanoes bring hot water and heat that can warm homes and make electricity. They give useful stone and minerals, and they attract tourists. Many volcanoes erupt only rarely, perhaps once in hundreds of years, so families can live there for generations without seeing an eruption. And for many people, it is simply home, where their families have always lived.

## Soil worth the risk

When volcanic rock and ash break down, they release nutrients such as potassium and phosphorus that plants need. That is why the island of Java, in Indonesia, is crowded with rice fields climbing up the slopes of its volcanoes.

Around Mount Vesuvius in Italy, farmers grow tomatoes, grapes and lemons on the dark volcanic soil. The coffee of Central America and some of the tea of Java also grow on volcanic slopes. Even in India, the black cotton soil of the Deccan Plateau formed from the lava of ancient [Deccan](deccan) eruptions.

## Cities in the shadow

Naples, one of Italy's biggest cities, lies just a few kilometres from Vesuvius. In 79 CE, Vesuvius buried the Roman towns of [Pompeii](pompeii) and Herculaneum in ash. Today about 6 lakh people live in the "red zone" nearest the volcano, with detailed plans to evacuate if it stirs.

[Mexico](mexico) City lives within sight of Popocatépetl, which often puffs ash. [Seattle](seattle), in the US, has Mount Rainier on its skyline. In 1980, nearby Mount St Helens erupted, blowing away the top of the mountain. Auckland, [New Zealand](new-zealand)'s largest city, is built on a field of about 50 small volcanoes.

## Living with ash

In Kagoshima, in southern Japan, the volcano Sakurajima sits across a bay from the city and erupts small amounts of ash many times a year. Residents check ash forecasts, much as others check for rain.

The city gives families yellow bags to fill with ash swept from gardens and roofs, then collects them from special points. Schoolchildren wear helmets on some walks to school. People there have grown used to it, as you can read in [Japan](japan-culture).

## Free heat from below

Volcanoes are also a power source. In [Iceland](iceland), heat from underground warms about nine in ten homes. Kenya, [the Philippines](world-philippines), Indonesia and New Zealand produce electricity from geothermal power plants that use steam from deep underground.

Hot springs near volcanoes have drawn bathers for thousands of years. In Japan, soaking in [onsen](geysers-hot-springs), natural hot spring baths, is a much-loved tradition.

## Reading the warning signs

Scientists watch active volcanoes closely. They use seismometers to feel tiny [earthquakes](earthquakes), GPS to measure if the ground is swelling, and instruments to sniff gases coming out of cracks. A rising number of small earthquakes and puffing gas can be signs that magma is moving up.

In 1991, Philippine and US scientists saw these signs at Mount Pinatubo. They warned authorities, and tens of thousands of people were moved away before one of the largest eruptions of the 20th century. Thousands of lives were saved. India's only active volcano, Barren Island in the Andaman Sea, has no people living on it, but scientists still keep an eye on its eruptions.
