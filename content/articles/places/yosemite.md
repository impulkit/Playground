## The short answer

Yosemite (say yoh-SEM-it-ee) is a national park in the mountains of California. Its heart is Yosemite Valley, a deep, flat-floored valley about 11 km long, walled in by grey [granite](?granite) cliffs and waterfalls. Above it are high meadows, lakes, forests and peaks.

The park covers about 3,000 square kilometres, roughly twice the size of Delhi. Most of the roughly 4 million visitors a year crowd into the valley, which is only a small slice of it. Black bears, mule deer and peregrine falcons live there, and in the south of the park stand groves of giant sequoias, some of the biggest trees on Earth.

The bears are clever at breaking into cars that smell of food, so visitors must lock snacks and cool boxes in metal bear-proof lockers. In 1984 UNESCO named Yosemite a World Heritage Site.

## Carved by ice

The cliffs began as molten rock that cooled slowly deep underground millions of years ago. Over time the land rose and the softer rock on top wore away, leaving the hard granite exposed.

Rivers cut a narrow canyon through it. Then, during the ice ages, [glaciers](glaciers) crawled down the canyon and ground its sides smooth and steep. When the ice melted, it left the U-shaped valley we see today, with streams pouring over the edges as waterfalls. Half Dome, the rounded rock that looks sliced in two, got its flat face partly from rock peeling away in giant sheets.

## Walls of rock

El Capitan is a single block of granite rising about 900 m (3,000 ft) above the valley floor. [Climbers](sport-climbing) from around the world spend days clinging to it, sleeping on ledges or in tents hung from the cliff. In 2017 the American climber Alex Honnold climbed it with no rope at all, in under four hours.

Yosemite Falls drops about 740 m in three steps. In spring, the spray soaks people standing on the bridge below. By September it may be bone dry. In winter, snow closes the high road across the park, and a cone of ice can build up at the foot of the upper fall. Each February, if the sky is clear and the snowmelt is just right, the setting sun lights up thin Horsetail Fall so it glows like lava.

## The first people of the valley

Long before the park existed, the Ahwahneechee people lived in the valley they called Ahwahnee. They gathered acorns from the oak trees and traded with neighbouring groups across the mountains, much like other [Native nations](native-nations) of the West.

In 1851, during the California Gold Rush, armed men entered the valley and forced most of them out. Some families later came back and worked in the park. Their descendants still live in the region, and the park now works with them to tell their history.

## Saving a valley

Visitors began arriving soon after, and some worried the valley would be fenced off and sold. In 1864, in the middle of the Civil War, President [Abraham Lincoln](abraham-lincoln) signed a law handing Yosemite Valley and a grove of sequoias to California to protect "for public use". It was one of the first times any government set aside wild land for everyone.

A Scottish-born writer named John Muir spent years walking the mountains and writing about them. His campaigning helped Yosemite become a national park in 1890. Today the biggest problem is crowds. On busy summer days the valley road can fill with cars like a city street, and the park has sometimes asked visitors to book ahead. The sequoias, some of them close relatives of the [redwoods](redwoods) on the coast, have seen more than a thousand summers and do not seem to mind.
