## The short answer

Singapore became a garden city on purpose. In the 1960s it was a newly independent island with crowded [slums](slums-housing), polluted rivers and few parks. Its first prime minister, Lee Kuan Yew, believed that a clean, green city would attract business and make people proud of their new country. In 1963 he planted a tree to start a tree-planting campaign. In 1967 the government announced a plan to make Singapore a "Garden City".

Millions of trees and shrubs were planted along roads, over flyovers and between tall apartment blocks. Today about half of Singapore's land is covered in greenery. It now calls itself a "City in Nature".

## A tiny island country

Singapore is an island at the tip of the Malay Peninsula, about 140 km north of the equator. Its land area is only about 735 km², smaller than Mumbai's metropolitan area, yet about 6 million people live there.

Its name comes from the Sanskrit word Simhapura, meaning "lion city". In 1819, Stamford Raffles of the British [East India Company](east-india-company) set up a trading post there. Singapore became a busy port. In 1963 it joined Malaysia, but in 1965 it separated and became an independent country.

## Trees everywhere

Green rules are written into how Singapore is built. Developers often have to replace any greenery they remove, on rooftops, walls or balconies. Some [skyscrapers](skyscrapers) have gardens growing up their sides. A network of park connectors lets people walk and cycle between parks.

Gardens by the Bay, opened in 2012, has "Supertrees", tall metal structures 25 to 50 m high covered in living plants. Some collect rainwater and some carry [solar panels](solar-panels). Nearby, two huge glass domes hold plants from cool mountains and dry lands. Patches of real [rainforest](rainforests) still survive on the island, too, along with [mangroves](mangroves) on the coast.

## The water problem

Singapore has no large rivers or lakes, so water is a big worry. The country speaks of "Four National Taps". It collects rainwater in reservoirs, buys water from Malaysia, removes salt from seawater, and cleans used water so well that it can be drunk again. That [purified](water-purify) water is called NEWater. Much of it goes to factories, which need very pure water.

## Many cultures, one city

Singapore's people have Chinese, Malay, Indian and other roots. Its four official languages are English, Malay, [Mandarin](chinese-characters) and Tamil, so Tamil appears on signs and banknotes. Little India is full of temples, spice shops and flower-garland stalls, especially at Deepavali.

Food brings everyone together at [hawker centres](people-street-vendors), open-air food courts with dozens of small stalls. You can eat chicken rice, roti prata, laksa and dosa side by side. In 2020, UNESCO recognised Singapore's hawker culture as part of the world's intangible heritage. It is the city's version of [street food](street-food).

## Strict and tidy

Singapore is known for strict rules. There are fines for littering, and the sale of most [chewing gum](chewing-gum) has been banned since 1992. Supporters say this keeps the city clean and safe. Critics say some laws, including limits on protests and the press, go too far. Either way, visitors usually notice two things first: the heat, and how green everything is.

## A city that keeps growing

Because land is scarce, Singapore has filled in parts of the sea to make more. Its area has grown by about a quarter since the 1960s. Changi Airport, built partly on reclaimed land, holds the Jewel, a glass dome with an indoor waterfall about 40 m high, the tallest in the world.
