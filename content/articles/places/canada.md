## The short answer

Canada is a huge country in the north of North America, stretching from the Atlantic to the Pacific and up into the Arctic. It has endless forests, cold blue lakes, prairies of wheat, towering mountains and frozen islands near the North Pole.

For all that space, only about 4 crore people live there. Most of them crowd into a narrow band in the south, close to the border with the United States, where the weather is gentlest. Toronto, Montreal and Vancouver are the biggest cities. The capital is Ottawa, not Toronto, which surprises many visitors.

## Lakes, forests and a lot of cold

Canada has more lakes than any other country. Some, like [Lake Superior](great-lakes), which it shares with the US, are so big that they have waves and shipwrecks. Forests cover over a third of the land, and much of the far north sits on [permafrost](?permafrost), ground that stays frozen all year.

Winters can be fierce. In 1947 a village called Snag, in the Yukon, recorded about minus 63 °C, [the coldest temperature](weather-records) ever measured in North America outside Greenland. Up north, people often see the [northern lights](aurora) ripple green and purple across the night sky. Yet in summer, the same country has sunny beaches and fruit farms.

The wild animals match the landscape. Moose wade into lakes to eat water plants, black bears raid berry bushes, and polar bears wait on the shores of Hudson Bay for the sea to freeze. In the town of Churchill, Manitoba, so many polar bears pass through each autumn that many people don't lock their car doors, so anyone surprised by a bear can jump inside.

## Two languages, many peoples

Canada has two official languages, English and French. In the province of Quebec, French is the main language, and road signs, shops and schools mostly use it. Food packets all over Canada carry both languages, so a cereal box gives you a free French lesson at breakfast.

Long before either language arrived, [indigenous](?indigenous) peoples lived here, and they still do: First Nations, Inuit and Métis. In 1999 Canada created a new territory in the far north, Nunavut, where most people are Inuit and Inuktitut is an official language. Canada has also faced up to painful history, such as residential schools where indigenous children were taken from their families.

## A country of newcomers

Nearly one in four people in Canada was born in another country. Large numbers come from India, China and the Philippines. [Punjabi](punjabi-gurmukhi) is one of the most spoken languages in Canada after English and French, and cities like Brampton and Surrey have big Indian communities, with gurdwaras, sweet shops and [Diwali](diwali-lights) fireworks.

Families move for jobs, study and to join relatives. Their stories are part of a wider pattern you can read about in [immigration stories](immigration-stories). Many Indian students now study at Canadian universities too.

## Hockey, basketball and a maple leaf

Ice hockey is the sport Canadians love most, played on frozen ponds and rinks from childhood. Less well known is that a Canadian, James Naismith, invented [basketball](basketball) in 1891, while teaching at a college in Massachusetts. His first hoops were peach baskets nailed to a balcony.

The red maple leaf on Canada's flag has been there since 1965. Maple trees give maple syrup, made by boiling down the sweet sap that flows in early spring. It takes roughly 40 litres of sap to make 1 litre of syrup, so a bottle on a pancake table represents a lot of patient boiling.
