## The short answer

Alaska is the biggest US state, more than twice the size of Texas, but very few people live there, about 740,000. It sits in the far north-west of North America, next to [Canada](canada), with Russia just across the sea. Much of it is wilderness: mountains, glaciers, forests and [tundra](tundra), which is cold, treeless land with frozen soil underneath.

Life in Alaska follows the seasons. Summer days are very long, and people fish, hike and work outdoors late into the bright evening. Winters are long, dark and cold. In many villages there are no roads at all, and people travel by small plane, boat or snowmobile.

## Bought from Russia

Alaska Native peoples have lived there for thousands of years. They include the Iñupiat, Yupik, Unangan (Aleut), Athabascan, Tlingit and Haida. Russian fur traders arrived in the 1700s. In 1867, Russia sold Alaska to the United States for 7.2 million dollars, about two cents an acre. Some newspapers called it "Seward's Folly", after the US official who arranged the deal.

It turned out to be a bargain. Gold was found in the 1890s, and later oil. Alaska became the 49th state in 1959.

## Light and dark

Alaska is so far north that the Sun behaves strangely. In Utqiaġvik, the [northernmost town](extreme-northernmost-town) in the USA, the Sun does not set for about 80 days in summer. In winter, it stays below the horizon for about two months. People use blackout curtains in summer and bright lamps in winter.

The long nights have a bonus. Alaska is one of the best places on Earth to see the [aurora](aurora), the [northern lights](solar-wind), glowing green and purple across the sky.

## Mountains, ice and fire

Denali, about 6,190 m tall, is the highest mountain in North America. Huge [glaciers](glaciers) flow down from the mountains, and some reach the sea, where chunks of ice crash into the water. Alaska also has dozens of active [volcanoes](volcano).

Alaska gets more [earthquakes](earthquakes) than any other state. On 27 March 1964, a magnitude 9.2 earthquake struck, the most powerful ever recorded in North America. It shook the ground for about four and a half minutes and caused a tsunami.

## Dogs and the race to Nome

In 1925, a disease called diphtheria broke out in the town of Nome. Medicine was needed fast, but no plane could fly in the winter storms. Teams of sled [dogs](dogs-domestic) carried it about 1,000 km in relay in under six days. The dogs Balto and Togo became famous.

Today the Iditarod sled dog race, about 1,600 km long, follows parts of that old trail every March. Mushers, the people who drive the sleds, race through blizzards with teams of 12 to 16 dogs.

## Oil and wildlife

In 1968, a huge oilfield was found at Prudhoe Bay on the Arctic coast. A pipeline about 1,290 km long was built to carry the [oil](oil-coal) south to a port. It was finished in 1977. Oil money helps pay for schools and roads, and each year most Alaskans receive a share in a payment from an oil fund.

Alaska is also full of wildlife. Brown bears catch salmon in rivers, moose wander into towns, and whales feed off the coast. Many Alaskans hunt and fish for much of their food, as their families have for generations.
