## The short answer

Kolkata sits on the Hooghly River in West Bengal. For more than a century it was the capital of British India and one of the biggest cities in the British Empire. That past left grand buildings, India's first metro and trams that rattled through the streets for 150 years.

But what people love most about Kolkata is its spirit. It is a city of books, poetry, films, football, sweets and long arguments over tea. During Durga Puja, the whole city turns into an open-air art gallery. Today around 1.5 crore people live in its wider metropolitan area.

## Three villages and a trading post

In 1690, Job Charnock of the [East India Company](east-india-company) set up a trading post beside three villages: Sutanuti, Gobindapur and Kalikata. The last probably gave the city its name. The British built Fort William and the town grew around it. Calcutta became the capital of British India in 1772.

Merchants, clerks and labourers poured in from across Bengal and beyond. In 1911 the British moved the capital to [Delhi](delhi), but Calcutta stayed India's largest city for decades more. Its name was officially changed to Kolkata in 2001.

## A bridge with no bolts

The Hooghly is wide and busy with boats, so the city needed a bridge that would not block them. [Howrah Bridge](howrah-bridge), opened in 1943, is a cantilever bridge: two huge steel arms reach out from the banks and meet in the middle, with no pillars in the river at all. It is about 705 m long.

The steel is held together by rivets, not nuts and bolts. Today the bridge carries a huge stream of cars, buses, handcarts and people on foot every day.

## City of ideas

In the 1800s, Calcutta became a centre of new thinking, a period later called the [Bengali Renaissance](bengali-renaissance). Reformers such as Raja [Ram Mohan Roy](raja-ram-mohan-roy) and Ishwar Chandra [Vidyasagar](ishwar-chandra) campaigned for women's education and against cruel customs. Colleges, [newspapers](hist-first-newspaper-india) and printing presses spread new ideas fast.

[Rabindranath Tagore](tagore), born in Kolkata in 1861, became the first Asian to win the Nobel Prize in Literature, in 1913. He wrote the national anthems of both India and [Bangladesh](bangladesh). Later, the film-maker [Satyajit Ray](satyajit-ray-stories) made films here that are studied around the world, and [Mother Teresa](mother-teresa) ran her mission for the poor in the city.

## The festival city

Every autumn, during [Durga Puja](durga-puja), Kolkata's neighbourhoods build pandals, temporary halls made of bamboo and cloth. Some are shaped like temples, ships or castles. Inside stand clay idols of the goddess Durga, made by craftspeople in the area called Kumartuli. Millions of people walk from pandal to pandal late into the night. In 2021, UNESCO added Durga Puja in Kolkata to its list of the world's intangible cultural heritage.

## Trams, metro and sweets

Kolkata's horse-drawn trams began in 1873 and electric trams in 1902. Only a few routes are left now, but it is the only city in India that still runs them. In 1984, the city opened India's first underground metro. Its yellow Ambassador taxis became a symbol of the city.

Kolkata is also famous for sweets like [rosogolla](bengali-food) and mishti doi, sweet curd set in clay pots. South of the city, the river meets the sea in the Sundarbans, a mangrove forest where tigers swim between islands.
