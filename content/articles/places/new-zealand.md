## The short answer

New Zealand is an island country in the south-western Pacific Ocean. It has two main islands, the North Island and the South Island, and hundreds of smaller ones. In Māori, the language of its first people, it is called Aotearoa, often translated as "land of the long white cloud".

It is a land of contrasts packed close together: volcanoes and geysers in the north, glaciers, fjords and the snowy Southern Alps in the south, and beaches everywhere in between. About 53 lakh people live there, and more than four times as many sheep (2024 figures). New Zealand is also famous for its strange birds, its rugby team and the films of [*The Lord of the Rings*](tolkien), which were shot among its mountains.

## The land of birds

New Zealand broke away from the old [supercontinent](pangaea) of Gondwana about 80 million years ago, before most mammals had spread around the world. Apart from a few bats, no land mammals reached it. Birds filled their roles instead.

There were [moa](lost-moa), giant flightless birds, the biggest taller than a man with its neck stretched up. The Haast's eagle, the largest eagle known, hunted them. Today the [kiwi](penguin-flight), the country's national bird, is a shy, flightless night bird that sniffs for worms with nostrils at the tip of its beak. It lays one of the biggest eggs for its size of any bird.

The kākāpō is the only flightless parrot in the world, a heavy, green, owl-faced bird that waddles and climbs. Rats, cats and stoats brought by people nearly wiped it out. After decades of rescue work on predator-free islands, there are still fewer than 300. Unlike the [dodo](dodo), it was saved in time, just.

## Polynesian voyagers

The first people arrived about 1250 to 1300 CE. They sailed thousands of kilometres from eastern Polynesia in large double-hulled canoes, steering by stars, waves and the flight of birds. Their descendants are the Māori. Within a couple of centuries, the moa had been hunted to extinction.

In 1840, many Māori chiefs and the British Crown signed the Treaty of Waitangi. The English and Māori versions differ in important ways, especially about who would hold power and land, and arguments over it continue today. Māori language and culture, including the haka, a powerful group dance, are now part of national life. Te reo Māori became an official language in 1987.

## Fire and ice

New Zealand sits where two of Earth's plates grind together, so it has earthquakes and active [volcanoes](volcano). Around Rotorua, the ground steams and bubbles, mud pools plop and [geysers](geysers-hot-springs) shoot boiling water into the air. Many homes and hotels use the underground heat.

In the South Island, the Southern Alps rise to Aoraki / Mount Cook, 3,724 metres high. Glaciers slide down the valleys, and in the south-west, Milford Sound is a fjord with walls rising straight out of dark water, just like the [fjords of Norway](norway-fjords).

## Firsts and a famous climber

In 1893, New Zealand became the first self-governing country to give all adult women the [right to vote](womens-vote) in national elections, after a campaign led by Kate Sheppard. Her face is on the ten-dollar note.

On the five-dollar note is [Sir Edmund Hillary](mountaineering), a beekeeper from Auckland. In 1953, he and Tenzing Norgay, a [Sherpa climber](people-sherpas) from Nepal and India, became the first people known to reach the top of Mount Everest. Hillary spent much of his later life building schools and hospitals for Sherpa communities in Nepal.
