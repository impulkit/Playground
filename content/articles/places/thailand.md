## The short answer

Thailand is a country in mainland Southeast Asia, shaped a little like an elephant's head with its trunk running south down the Malay Peninsula. It has about 7 crore people. Bangkok, the capital, sits on the Chao Phraya River near the sea. Thais call it Krung Thep, short for a ceremonial name so long, 168 letters in the Roman alphabet, that Guinness World Records lists it as the [longest place name](words-longest) anywhere.

Thailand is known for golden Buddhist temples, spicy and sour food, beaches and islands, elephants, and kickboxing called Muay Thai. It is also the only country in Southeast Asia that was never ruled by a European power. It is a kingdom, with a king as head of state, though since 1932 the king has not ruled alone.

## Never a colony

In the 1800s, Britain ruled Burma and Malaya to the west and south, and France ruled Laos, Cambodia and [Vietnam](vietnam) to the east. Siam, as Thailand was then called, sat in between.

Its kings, especially King Mongkut and his son King Chulalongkorn, kept the country independent with careful diplomacy. They modernised the army, schools and railways, and signed treaties with both powers. Siam did have to give up some border lands to Britain and France. But it kept its independence while almost all its neighbours became colonies, and stayed independent through the long age of empires that ended with [decolonisation](decolonisation) after 1945. In 1939 the country changed its name to Thailand, meaning "land of the free".

## Rama on the palace walls

Indian ideas reached Thailand over many centuries through traders, monks and the [Khmer empire](khmer-empire) of Cambodia. The Thai script developed from Khmer writing, which itself grew from the [scripts of southern India](scripts-india).

The Thai version of the Ramayana is called the Ramakien. Its scenes cover the walls of a long gallery around the Temple of the Emerald Buddha in Bangkok. Thailand's kings have taken the name Rama for over two centuries: the current king is Rama X. And the great city of Ayutthaya, capital from 1351 until it was destroyed by a Burmese army in 1767, was named after Ayodhya. Its ruins are now a World Heritage Site.

## Monks, temples and elephants

Most Thais are Buddhists. Saffron-robed monks walk the streets at dawn collecting food from families, who see giving as a way of making merit. Many Thai boys spend a short time as novice monks, sometimes just a few weeks, as a rite of passage. The [Buddha](buddha) appears everywhere, from giant reclining statues to tiny shrines in shops.

The Asian elephant is Thailand's national animal. Elephants once hauled teak logs out of forests and carried kings into battle. Logging with elephants was banned in 1989. Many of the country's few thousand captive [elephants](elephants-talk) now live in camps and sanctuaries, and a few thousand more still live wild in national parks.

## Sour, sweet, salty, spicy

Thai cooks aim to balance four tastes in one meal: sour from lime and tamarind, sweet from palm sugar, salty from [fish sauce](food-science-umami) and spicy from chillies. Tom yum is a hot and sour soup with lemongrass and prawns. Green curry gets its colour from fresh green chillies and herbs.

Chillies only arrived in Asia from the Americas in the 1500s, carried by Portuguese traders, yet Thai food is now famous for them. Thailand is also one of the world's biggest rice exporters, and its fragrant jasmine rice smells faintly of popcorn when it cooks.
