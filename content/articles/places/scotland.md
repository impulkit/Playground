## The short answer

Scotland is one of the four countries that make up the United Kingdom, along with England, Wales and Northern Ireland. It covers the northern third of the island of Great Britain, plus nearly 800 islands. Its capital is Edinburgh and its largest city is Glasgow.

Scotland is known for wild mountains and deep lakes, called lochs, castles, bagpipes, kilts and tartan. It is also known for inventors, writers and scientists far out of proportion to its size of about 55 lakh people, fewer than live in Bengaluru. Many Scots speak with a strong accent, and some still speak Scottish Gaelic or Scots.

## Highlands and lochs

The north and west of Scotland are the Highlands, a land of bare mountains, moors and long, narrow lochs. Ben Nevis, at 1,345 metres, is the highest mountain in the UK. These mountains are very old. Hundreds of millions of years ago they were part of the same range as the [Appalachians](appalachians) in North America, before the Atlantic Ocean opened and split them apart.

Loch Ness is about 37 kilometres long and over 200 metres deep. It holds more fresh water than all the lakes of England and Wales combined. It is most famous, of course, for Nessie, the monster many visitors hope to glimpse. The best-known "photo" of her, taken in 1934, turned out decades later to be a toy submarine with a model head stuck on top. Searches with sonar and even DNA sampling of the water have found plenty of eels, but no monster.

## A city on a volcano

Edinburgh Castle sits high on Castle Rock, the plug of an ancient [volcano](volcano) that erupted about 350 million years ago. Across the city rises Arthur's Seat, another old volcano, where people walk up for the view. Neither has erupted for hundreds of millions of years.

Every August the city hosts the Edinburgh Festival Fringe, one of the largest arts festivals in the world, with thousands of shows in theatres, pubs and even on buses. J.K. Rowling wrote parts of the first Harry Potter books in Edinburgh cafés.

## Inventors and ideas

For a small country, Scotland has produced a surprising number of world-changing ideas. Alexander Graham Bell, born in Edinburgh in 1847, was one of the inventors of the [telephone](telephone-history), which he patented in the USA in 1876. John Logie Baird, from near Glasgow, showed the first working television pictures in London in 1926, part of the story of [television](television-invented).

In 1928, the Scottish scientist Alexander Fleming noticed that mould growing on a dish in his London lab had killed the bacteria around it. That mould gave penicillin, the first widely used [antibiotic](antibiotics). In 1996, scientists at the Roslin Institute near Edinburgh created Dolly the sheep, the first mammal cloned from an adult cell.

## Kilts, pipes and unicorns

Highland clans wove woollen cloth in checked patterns called tartans, and many families today have their own tartan. Bagpipes, with a bag of air squeezed under the arm, make a sound loud enough to carry across a glen. Golf grew up on the windy links of the east coast, and St Andrews is called the home of golf.

Scotland's national animal is the [unicorn](myths-unicorns), a legend chosen for its strength and pride. In 2014, Scots voted in a referendum on whether to become independent from the UK. About 55 percent voted to stay, and the question is still debated today.
