## The short answer

Sikkim, a small state in the eastern Himalaya, decided in 2003 to stop using chemical fertilisers and pesticides on its farms. Over the next 12 years, the government cut and then banned their sale, trained farmers in natural methods, and paid for farmland to be checked and certified. In January 2016, Sikkim was declared fully organic.

Organic farming means growing food without factory-made chemical fertilisers or pesticides. Instead, farmers feed the [soil](soil-made) with [compost](?compost) and manure, and keep pests down with natural sprays, crop rotation and helpful insects. Sikkim's leaders wanted to protect its mountain soils, rivers and forests, and to earn more from food sold as pure and healthy.

## Why a mountain state?

Sikkim is tiny, with only about six lakh people at the 2011 census. Its farms are mostly small plots on steep terraces. Many farmers there had never used much chemical fertiliser in the first place, especially compared with the plains of Punjab during the [Green Revolution](green-revolution). That made the switch easier than it would be in a big farming state.

The land is also fragile. Heavy chemical use can wash off slopes into streams and lakes. Sikkim is home to forests of rhododendrons and orchids, the [red panda](red-panda), which is its state animal, and snow leopards. Keeping chemicals out was seen as a way of protecting all of them.

## How the switch happened

The government set up training centres, gave farmers organic seeds and helped them build compost pits and vermicompost beds, where [earthworms](earthworms) turn waste into rich fertiliser. Farmers learned to make sprays from neem, chillies and cow urine to keep insects away, and to plant different crops in turn so pests can't settle. Step by step, it reduced the chemicals it supplied. Then it banned their sale and use, with fines for breaking the rule.

About 75,000 hectares of farmland were certified organic. In 2018, Sikkim won the Future Policy Gold Award, given by the World Future Council together with the UN's Food and Agriculture Organization. It beat 51 other policies from around the world.

## Cardamom, ginger and oranges

Sikkim is one of the world's biggest growers of large [cardamom](cardamom-pepper), a smoky, dark spice used in curries and biryanis. Ginger, turmeric, buckwheat and Sikkim mandarin oranges also grow well there. Many of these now sell under an organic label for higher prices.

Not everything has gone smoothly. Some farmers say their harvests fell after the switch, and that they struggled with pests. Organic food is often more expensive, and Sikkim still brings in a lot of vegetables from neighbouring states, where chemicals are allowed. Researchers continue to study how well the policy works over time. Other Indian states, and countries such as [Bhutan](bhutan), have watched closely, because Sikkim is one of the few places to have tried this across a whole region.

## A kingdom that joined India

Sikkim was once an independent kingdom, ruled by kings called the Chogyals. In 1975, after a vote, it became India's 22nd state. Its people include Lepcha, Bhutia and Nepali communities, and many follow Buddhism or Hinduism.

Kangchenjunga, the third-highest mountain in the world at about 8,586 m, rises on the border with Nepal. Many Sikkimese consider it a guardian deity. Out of respect, [climbers](mountaineering) who reached its top from the Nepal side in 1955 deliberately stopped a few steps short of the very summit, and many climbers since have done the same.
