## The short answer

The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow channel of sea between Iran and Oman. It is the only way for ships to sail out of the Persian Gulf to the open ocean.

Some of the world's biggest oil and gas sellers sit around that Gulf. So when ships cannot pass through this [strait](?strait), the whole world feels it, from petrol pumps in Europe to gas stoves in India.

## How it works

Imagine a big housing society with only one gate. Every milk van, school bus and delivery bike must use it. If that gate is blocked, the whole society is stuck, even if every road inside is fine. Hormuz is that gate for the Persian Gulf.

Here is how oil normally reaches you through it:

1. Countries such as Saudi Arabia, Iraq, Kuwait, the UAE, Qatar and Iran pump oil and gas from under the ground.
2. Giant ships called [tankers](?tanker) load it at ports along the Gulf.
3. The tankers sail through Hormuz in two marked lanes, one in and one out, each about 3.2 km wide.
4. They cross the Arabian Sea to ports such as Mundra, Kandla and Mumbai.
5. [Refineries](oil-refinery) turn the crude oil into petrol and diesel, and cylinders are filled with cooking gas.

Because so much passes through one spot, experts call Hormuz a [chokepoint](?chokepoint). The [Suez Canal](suez-canal) is another famous one. A ship does not need to be hit to stop. If owners think a trip is too dangerous, or [insurance](insurance) for the trip costs too much, many ships simply wait at anchor.

Some oil can dodge the strait. Saudi Arabia has a pipeline about 1,200 km long to the Red Sea that can carry up to 5 million barrels a day. The UAE has one to the port of Fujairah. Together they carry far less than the strait normally does.

## Where it came from

People have sailed here for thousands of years. A Greek guidebook for sailors, written about 2,000 years ago, already described this passage. Nobody is sure where the name comes from. One idea links it to Hormoz, an old Persian name for the god Ahura Mazda. Another links it to a Greek word for a bay.

From about the 10th to the 17th century, a rich trading kingdom called Ormus stood on an island in the strait. Ships carried pearls, horses, spices and cloth between Persia, Arabia and India. In 1515, the [Portuguese](portuguese-india) seized Hormuz and made passing ships pay them. In 1622, the Persian Safavid rulers and the English East India Company drove them out.

In the 1980s, during a long war between Iran and Iraq, both sides attacked tankers. People called it the Tanker War. In 1988, after Iranian sea mines damaged a US warship, the USA struck Iranian targets in the Gulf.

## India's story

India buys most of its crude oil from abroad, so [where that oil comes from](india-oil) is a big question. Before 2026, about 40 to 45% of India's crude came through Hormuz. Even more of its [cooking gas](cooking-gas) did. India imports about 60% of the LPG it uses, and about 90% of those imports normally sail through the strait.

On 28 February 2026, the USA and Israel began strikes on Iran, and Iran hit back. Iran then put limits on ships using the strait, and traffic almost stopped. In March, prices of crude oil jumped, and on 7 March a home LPG cylinder went up by 60 rupees. The government told families not to panic-book cylinders. Under the booking rules, city homes could book a new one 25 days after the last delivery, and village homes after 45 days.

India bought oil from other places to fill the gap. By 11 March, the Petroleum Ministry said about 70% of India's crude imports were coming from outside the strait, from about 40 countries. On 14 March, two Indian ships, Shivalik and Nanda Devi, crossed Hormuz carrying 92,700 tonnes of LPG.

In early November 2026, a senior Iranian lawmaker said ships from India, China, Iraq and Qatar could pass. He said India's exemption would help Iran buy medicines from India, and that Indian ships would pay a fee. Earlier in the year, Iran's ambassador said Indian ships had not been charged, and India has said it does not pay tolls.

## The numbers that matter

- **About 20 million barrels a day**: the oil that passed through Hormuz in 2025. That is nearly one in every five barrels the world uses.
- **About 3,000 ships a month**: normal traffic, or about 100 trading ships a day. On some days in September 2026, ship-tracking data counted just 3 crossings.
- **About one-third**: the share of the world's trade in nitrogen fertiliser, such as urea, that normally passes through, by some estimates. Farmers far away depend on it too.
- **About 73 dollars to about 96 dollars**: the price of a barrel of Brent crude just before the war, and in mid-November 2026.
- **13%**: India's share of the Gulf crude that crossed the strait in October 2026, according to the ship-tracking firm Kpler.

## What people argue about

Iran says the strait runs past its coast and that ships need its permission during [the war](us-iran-conflict). It says it is defending itself against attacks by the USA and Israel. The USA and many other countries say the strait is an international waterway, and that ships of every country have the right to pass freely. In July 2026, the USA put back a naval [blockade](?blockade) of Iran's southern ports, which Iran opposes.

There is also a debate inside India. Some say India should keep friendly ties with every side, so its ships can keep sailing, as the November exemption suggests. Others say India must depend less on any one route, by buying from more countries, storing more oil and using more [clean energy](electric-cars). Many experts think India needs a bit of both.

## Myths and mix-ups

**"Hormuz is a canal."** It is a natural strait. The [Suez Canal](suez-canal) was dug by people. Hormuz was made by nature.

**"If Hormuz shuts, India's oil stops completely."** India buys oil from dozens of countries, and much of it never goes near Hormuz. But prices rise for everyone, so India still feels it.

**"Only rich car owners care about oil."** Oil and gas move buses, trucks, tractors and fishing boats, and cook food in crores of homes. When they cost more, [prices of many things rise](inflation).

## What it means for you

You may never see the Strait of Hormuz, but it reaches your kitchen and your school bus. When tankers stop, cooking gas can run short and cylinders can cost more. Diesel prices push up the cost of moving vegetables, milk and books by truck.

This is why India keeps emergency oil in underground caves, and why it is building more solar power and electric buses. That is why a strip of sea thousands of kilometres away matters at home. To see how ships use ports at both ends, read [how giant ships use ports](sea-ports).
