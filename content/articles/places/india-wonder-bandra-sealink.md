## The short answer

The Bandra-Worli Sea Link is a 5.6 km, eight-lane road bridge that curves across the Arabian Sea, joining two parts of [Mumbai](mumbai). It took about nine years to build and opened on 30 June 2009. A trip that used to take 20 to 30 minutes in peak traffic now takes about 10.

Engineers built it in three parts. First they drilled 604 deep holes in the seabed and filled them with steel and [concrete](cement-concrete). Then they placed hundreds of ready-made concrete pieces on top to form the road. Over the shipping channels, they hung the road from steel cables strung from tall towers. That cable-hung section is what makes it look like a giant harp.

## Building on the seabed

Mumbai's sea floor is mud on top and hard [basalt](?basalt) rock below. That rock came from enormous lava flows in the [Deccan](deccan) about 66 million years ago. The engineers needed to reach it, because mud can't hold up a bridge.

Workers on barges and temporary platforms drilled holes up to 2 metres wide and pushed them between 6 and 34 metres into the seabed. Each hole was lined, filled with a cage of steel bars and poured full of concrete. These piles act like the roots of a tree. The sea rises and falls about 4 to 5 metres with the [tides](tides) here, and the monsoon brings rough water, so work stopped for weeks at a time.

## Building the road like Lego

The road was not poured in place over the sea. It was made on land at a casting yard at Worli, in giant concrete blocks called segments. Each one weighed many tonnes.

Barges carried the segments out. A huge steel machine called a launching gantry stood on the piles, lifted each block and held it in position. Steel cables were threaded through all the blocks and pulled tight, squeezing them into one solid road. It works like a row of books that you can lift off a shelf by pressing hard on both ends.

## The harp in the sea

Where ships and fishing boats pass, the road needed long gaps without piles. Here the engineers built [cable-stayed](bridges) sections. The main tower near Bandra rises about 126 metres. Steel cables fan out from it and hold up a road span of 250 metres on each side, about two and a half football fields with nothing underneath.

The builders said that the steel wire inside all the cables, laid end to end, would stretch about as far as the distance around the Earth. Each cable is a bundle of many thin wires, so the total adds up fast.

## Why it took so long

Work began in 2000 and was meant to take about five years. It took nine. Court cases, design changes and worries from the Koli fishing families of Worli, whose boats use these waters, all slowed it down. The cost rose from about ₹660 crore to around ₹1,600 crore. Once it opened, cars paid a toll to cross, and tens of thousands of vehicles now use it on an average day.

## A city that keeps building into the sea

Mumbai was once seven islands, and people have been filling in and bridging its sea for 300 years. Since the Sea Link opened, the city has added a new coastal road that joins its southern end. In January 2024, the 21.8 km Atal Setu across Mumbai harbour opened, becoming India's longest sea bridge. Yet the first time India crossed the sea by bridge was more than a century earlier, on a railway line built in 1914 to an island in the south called [Pamban](india-wonder-pamban).
