## The short answer

The Amazon is the largest tropical rainforest on Earth. It covers about 5.5 million square kilometres of South America, an area bigger than India and Pakistan put together. Most of it is in Brazil, but it spreads into eight other countries.

Through the middle runs the Amazon River and more than a thousand streams that feed it. The forest is warm and wet all year. Those two things together let an enormous number of living things grow, from trees taller than a ten-storey building to frogs smaller than your fingernail.

## A river that rules everything

The Amazon River carries more water than any other river. Roughly a fifth of all the fresh water that rivers pour into the world's oceans comes out of its mouth. Whether it is also the longest river is still argued over, and you can read why in the article on the [longest rivers](rivers-longest).

At Manaus, the dark, tea-coloured Rio Negro meets the pale, muddy Solimões. Their water differs in speed, temperature and how much it carries, so they run side by side for kilometres, black and brown, before blending. Every rainy season the rivers rise many metres and flood the forest. Fish swim among tree trunks and eat fallen fruit, and some help plant new trees by dropping the seeds.

## Rivers in the sky

A big tree in the Amazon can pull hundreds of litres of water out of the soil in a day and breathe it out through its leaves. Multiply that by hundreds of billions of trees, and the forest releases a vast cloud of water vapour. Scientists call these moving streams of moist air "flying rivers".

The wind carries them west and south, where they fall as rain on farms and cities far away. Some of that water falls on the forest again, so the same water can be recycled many times as it crosses the continent.

## Crowded with life

About one in every ten known species lives in the Amazon. There are jaguars, [sloths](power-sloth), pink river dolphins, poison dart frogs and the giant Victoria water lily, whose floating leaves can grow wider than a dining table. New kinds of plants and animals are still being found every year.

Hundreds of [indigenous](?indigenous) groups live in the Amazon, some in villages that have barely changed for generations and a few who avoid contact with outsiders altogether. Their knowledge of plants has led to [medicines](medicine-how) and foods used far beyond the forest. Brazil nuts, cocoa and the açaí berry all come from Amazon trees. Studies of satellite maps show that forest inside indigenous lands is often cleared far less than forest outside them.

## Under threat

Large parts of the Amazon have been cut down for cattle ranches, soya farms, roads and mining. Scientists estimate that close to a fifth of the original forest has already been cleared. When trees are burned, the carbon stored in them goes into the air, which links the forest to [climate change](climate-change).

Some researchers worry that if too much forest disappears, the flying rivers will weaken and parts of the Amazon could dry into grassland. Brazil's [deforestation](what-if-no-trees) rate has risen and fallen over the years with changes in laws and how strictly they are enforced. The forest also gets help from far away. Every year, dust blown across the Atlantic from the [Sahara](sahara) settles on the Amazon and adds nutrients to its thin soil.
