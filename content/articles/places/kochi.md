## The short answer

Kochi, on the coast of [Kerala](kerala), has been a trading port for over 600 years. Its harbour sits where backwater lagoons meet the Arabian Sea, and its hinterland grows [black pepper](food-pepper-black-gold), cardamom and other [spices](spices) that the world wanted. Traders came from Arabia, China and, later, Portugal, the Netherlands and Britain.

Many stayed. Jewish, Christian, Muslim, Hindu, Gujarati and Konkani communities built homes, temples, churches, mosques and a famous synagogue side by side. That's why a short walk in old Kochi can take you past buildings from several continents. The city has long been one of India's great meeting places.

## A harbour made by a flood

For centuries, the main port in this area was Muziris, a little to the north, where Roman ships once came for pepper. According to tradition, a great flood in 1341 silted up Muziris and broke open a natural harbour at Kochi.

Whatever exactly happened, Kochi became the new trading hub. The local rulers, the Rajas of Kochi, welcomed traders and made money from taxes. Kerala's pepper was so valuable that it was sometimes called "black gold", and it drew ships along the [trade routes](trade-routes) of the Indian Ocean.

## Nets from China?

Along the waterfront at Fort Kochi stand the famous Chinese fishing nets, called cheena vala in [Malayalam](malayalam). Each is a giant wooden frame, up to about 10 m high, with a net hanging from it. A team of fishers lowers it into the water using big stones as counterweights, then hauls it up with the catch.

Many people believe the nets were brought by Chinese traders, perhaps around the time of the admiral [Zheng He](zheng-he), whose fleets visited the Kerala coast in the early 1400s. Others think the Portuguese brought them from their colony at Macau. Nobody knows for certain. Fewer nets work today, because catches near the shore have fallen and repairing the big wooden frames is costly, but the ones still standing are among Kochi's best-known sights.

## Portuguese, Dutch and British

In 1498, the Portuguese explorer Vasco da Gama reached Kerala by sea. The Portuguese soon set up a fort in Kochi. Da Gama died in Kochi in 1524 and was buried in St Francis Church, one of the oldest European-built churches in India. His remains were later taken back to Portugal, but his first gravestone is still there.

In 1663, [the Dutch took over](dutch-french-india). They rebuilt the Mattancherry Palace, first built by the Portuguese as a gift for the Raja, so it became known as the Dutch Palace. Its walls carry beautiful murals from the Ramayana. The British took control in the late 1700s, and Kochi later grew into a major naval base and shipbuilding port. India's first home-built [aircraft carrier](aircraft-carriers), INS Vikrant, was built at Cochin Shipyard.

## The synagogue and the Biennale

Jewish traders lived in Kerala for many centuries. In 1568, a community known as the Paradesi Jews built a synagogue in Mattancherry. Its floor is covered with hand-painted blue and white tiles brought from China. Most of Kochi's Jews moved to Israel after 1948, and only a handful remain, but the synagogue is still cared for.

Modern Kochi looks forward as well as back. In 2012, the city launched the Kochi-Muziris Biennale, a big contemporary art festival held in old warehouses. In 2015, its airport became the world's first to run fully on [solar power](solar-panels). In 2023, it opened a Water Metro, with electric boats linking the islands of its backwaters.
