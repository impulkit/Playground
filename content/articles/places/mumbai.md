## The short answer

Long ago, Mumbai was seven low islands off the Konkan coast: Colaba, Old Woman's Island, Bombay Island, Mazagaon, Parel, Worli and Mahim. Koli fishing families lived there, sailing out from small villages. At high tide the sea flooded the gaps between the islands. At low tide some of them could almost be crossed on foot.

From the 1700s, the British began building sea walls and filling in the shallow water. Over about 150 years the islands joined into one long strip of land. Today a city of more than 2 crore people, counting its suburbs, sits on that land.

## A wedding present

Portugal took the islands in 1534. In 1661, King Charles II of England married the Portuguese princess Catherine of Braganza, and the islands were part of her dowry. The king had little use for them. In 1668 he rented them to the [East India Company](east-india-company) for £10 a year.

The Company saw what the king had missed: a deep, sheltered harbour, perfect for big ships. Merchants, shipbuilders and workers arrived from Gujarat, the Konkan and much further away.

## Pushing back the sea

The biggest early project was the Hornby Vellard, a sea wall finished in the 1780s. It stopped the tide flooding the low land around Worli, so it could be drained and built on. More causeways and fillings followed. Hills were cut down and the rock and soil dumped into creeks.

By the late 1800s the seven islands were one. Work never fully stopped. Areas such as Nariman Point and Cuffe Parade were built on reclaimed land in the 1900s. Along the edges, [mangrove](mangroves) forests still grow in the salty mud, as they did before the city came.

## Trains, cotton and films

On 16 April 1853 the first passenger train in India ran from Bombay to Thane, about 34 km. The [railways](indian-railways) and the port turned the city into a giant trading hub. [Cotton](clothes-cotton) mills grew up in Parel and attracted thousands of workers. The grand railway station now called Chhatrapati [Shivaji](shivaji) Terminus, finished in 1888, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Today Mumbai's [suburban trains](india-wonder-mumbai-local) carry about 75 lakh people a day. A train every few minutes moves office workers, schoolchildren and even home-cooked lunches.

The city is also home to Hindi cinema, often called [Bollywood](bollywood), which makes hundreds of films a year.

## A city of festivals

Mumbai's people come from every corner of India, and its calendar shows it. During [Ganesh Chaturthi](ganesh-chaturthi), huge idols of [Ganesha](myths-ganesha-stories) are carried through the streets to the sea, with drums and crowds that fill whole roads. Eid, Christmas, Diwali and the Parsi New Year are all celebrated in the city. The Parsis, whose ancestors came from [Persia](ancient-persia), helped build much of old Bombay's industry, shipyards and schools.

## Living with the monsoon

Because so much of Mumbai is low, filled-in land, heavy rain can flood it fast. On 26 July 2005, nearly a metre of rain fell on parts of the city in a single day. Since then, drains and pumping stations have been improved, but every [monsoon](monsoon) still tests the city.

## What's in a name

The name Mumbai comes from Mumbadevi, a goddess worshipped by the Kolis. The Portuguese and British called the place Bombay, and some say that name came from Portuguese words for "good bay". In 1995 the city's name was officially changed to Mumbai, the name [Marathi](marathi-gujarati) and Gujarati speakers had always used.
