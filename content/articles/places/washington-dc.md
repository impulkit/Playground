## The short answer

Washington, D.C. became the capital because of a deal. After the [American Revolution](american-revolution), the new government owed huge war debts. Alexander Hamilton, from the North, wanted the national government to take over states' debts. Southern leaders such as [Thomas Jefferson](thomas-jefferson) and James Madison didn't like the plan. In 1790, at a dinner arranged by Jefferson, they reached a compromise: the South would support Hamilton's debt plan, and the capital would be built in the South, on the Potomac River.

Congress passed the Residence Act in 1790. President [George Washington](george-washington) chose the exact site, and the city was named after him. The government moved in in 1800.

## A district, not a state

The [US Constitution](us-constitution) called for a special federal district that belonged to no state, so no single state could control the national government. Maryland and Virginia gave land for a square of about 260 km², tilted like a diamond. In 1846 the Virginia part was given back.

D.C. stands for District of Columbia. Its residents can vote for president, but they have no voting member in [Congress](congress-usa). Some people want D.C. to become a state, while others argue it should stay a federal district. Its car number plates carry the words "End Taxation Without Representation". That echoes a slogan from the American Revolution, when colonists complained about paying taxes to a British parliament they had no vote in.

## A city drawn by design

In 1791, a French-born engineer, Pierre Charles L'Enfant, drew a plan with wide avenues spreading out from the Capitol and the President's House like spokes on a wheel. Surveyor Andrew Ellicott and the astronomer Benjamin Banneker, a free Black man, helped mark out the boundaries.

During the War of 1812, British troops burned the Capitol and the [White House](white-house) in 1814. Both were rebuilt. The President's House was painted white, and by the early 1900s everyone called it the White House, which became its official name in 1901. The Capitol's great cast-iron dome was added in the 1850s and 1860s. Work on it carried on even during the [Civil War](us-civil-war), because President Abraham Lincoln said it was a sign that the nation would go on.

## The National Mall

At the heart of the city is the National Mall, a long lawn running about 3 km from the Capitol to the Lincoln Memorial. The Washington Monument stands in the middle. When it was finished in 1884, it was the tallest structure in the world, until the [Eiffel Tower](eiffel-tower) passed it in 1889.

On 28 August 1963, about 250,000 people gathered on the Mall for the March on Washington. From the steps of the Lincoln Memorial, [Martin Luther King Jr](martin-luther-king) gave his ["I Have a Dream" speech](speeches-famous).

## Free museums

Along the Mall stand the museums of the [Smithsonian](smithsonian) Institution, most of them free. It began with money left by James Smithson, a British scientist who had never visited America. The National Air and Space Museum holds the [Wright brothers](wright-brothers)' first plane and an Apollo spacecraft. The National Museum of Natural History holds the Hope Diamond. These [museums](museums) welcome millions of visitors a year.

## Cherry blossoms and a low skyline

In 1912, the city of Tokyo gave Washington about 3,000 cherry trees. Every spring they bloom around a lake called the Tidal Basin, and a festival celebrates them. Because of a 1910 law limiting building heights, the city has no skyscrapers. The Washington Monument still rises above almost everything.
