## The short answer

The Appalachians are a long chain of mountains running down the eastern side of North America, from Newfoundland in Canada to Alabama in the southern USA. They are rounded and covered with forests, and the highest peak, Mount Mitchell, is just over 2,000 metres. That's lower than [Shimla](himachal).

They look gentle because they are very old. The Appalachians were pushed up in stages between about 480 and 270 million years ago, long before [the first dinosaurs](dino-age-span). They were probably once as tall as the Alps, and perhaps taller. Since then, hundreds of millions of years of rain, frost and rivers have worn them down to their roots.

## Born in a crash of continents

The Appalachians formed as continents collided while the supercontinent [Pangaea](pangaea) was coming together. When the land that would become Africa pushed into North America, layers of rock crumpled up like a rug pushed against a wall. This is how the [Himalaya](himalaya-form) are being built today, as India pushes into Asia.

Later, Pangaea split. The Atlantic Ocean opened in the middle of the old mountain range and carried pieces away. Some of the same rocks and folds turn up today in Ireland, the Highlands of [Scotland](scotland) and the Anti-Atlas mountains in [Morocco](morocco).

## A footpath 3,500 kilometres long

In 1921, a forester named Benton MacKaye proposed a walking trail along the whole ridge, so that city people could escape into the wild. Volunteers cleared paths and built shelters, and the Appalachian Trail was finished in 1937. It runs about 3,500 kilometres from Springer Mountain in Georgia to Mount Katahdin in Maine.

Each year, a few thousand people try to walk all of it in one go. It takes most of them five to seven months, and many give up along the way. Hikers follow white paint marks on trees and rocks, about the size of a dollar bill.

## Salamanders and synchronised fireflies

Because the southern Appalachians escaped the ice sheets of the last ice age, plants and animals had a long, steady time to evolve. The region has more kinds of salamander than anywhere else on Earth. Some breathe through their skin and have no lungs at all.

In early summer, at Elkmont in Great Smoky Mountains National Park, thousands of male [fireflies](fireflies) of one species flash at the same moment, then go dark together. The effect is so popular that the park runs a lottery for places to watch. The Great Smoky Mountains is the most visited national park in the USA, with over 12 million visits in recent years.

## Coal, fiddles and the Cherokee

The Cherokee and other Native nations lived in the southern Appalachians for thousands of years. In 1838 and 1839 the US government forced most of the Cherokee to leave for land far to the west. Thousands died on the journey, now called the Trail of Tears.

Settlers from Scotland, Ireland, England and Germany brought fiddles and ballads to the mountain valleys. African Americans brought the banjo, which has roots in West African instruments. Their music blended into old-time and bluegrass, two of the roots of [country music](country-music).

The mountains also hold thick seams of [coal](oil-coal), formed from swamps of giant plants about 300 million years ago. Coal mining gave the region jobs for more than a century, but also dangerous work and damaged hills.
