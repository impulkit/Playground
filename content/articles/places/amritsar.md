## The short answer

The Golden Temple, in Amritsar in Punjab, is the holiest place for Sikhs. Its kitchen is famous because it runs one of the largest free community meals in the world. Every day, tens of thousands of people of every background sit down together on the floor and are served dal, roti, a vegetable dish and kheer, all free.

The meal is called [langar](maths-recipes-scale). It comes from a teaching that goes back to [Guru Nanak](guru-nanak), the founder of Sikhism: that all people are equal and that food should be shared. Most of the cooking, serving and washing is done by volunteers. Doing this kind of service for others is called seva.

## A temple on the water

The city grew around a sacred pool that the fourth Sikh Guru, Guru Ram Das, had dug around 1577. The name Amritsar means "pool of nectar". His son, Guru Arjan, built the temple, the Harmandir Sahib, in the middle of the pool. It was completed in 1604, and the Sikh holy book was placed inside.

The temple has four doors, one on each side, to show that people from every direction and every background are welcome. In the early 1800s, Maharaja [Ranjit Singh](ranjit-singh) covered its upper floors with gilded copper, and people began calling it the Golden Temple. At night, it glows on the dark water.

## How the langar works

The kitchens run nearly all day and night. Huge iron cauldrons, some big enough to sit inside, bubble with dal and kheer. Machines help with some jobs, such as flipping [rotis](roti-chapati), but a great deal is still done by hand. Volunteers sit together chopping onions, rolling dough and stirring pots with paddles like oars.

Guests sit in long rows on mats in a big hall. Servers walk along with buckets and ladles, filling metal plates and topping them up. When one sitting finishes, the hall is cleared and washed, and the next group comes in. Another team scrubs thousands of plates and passes them back. On festival days, the number of meals can pass 1 lakh.

## Why everyone sits on the floor

The langar was started by Guru Nanak in the 1500s, at a time when the [caste system](caste-history) often stopped people from eating together. In the langar, everyone sits side by side in a row, called pangat, and eats the same food. The third Guru, Guru Amar Das, is said to have asked all visitors to eat in the langar before meeting him.

Sikh tradition says even the Mughal emperor [Akbar](akbar) sat on the floor with ordinary people to eat when he visited. Today, gurdwaras around the world, from Delhi to California, run langars. During floods, earthquakes and the COVID-19 pandemic, many Sikh groups cooked meals for people in need.

## More of Amritsar

A short walk from the temple is Jallianwala Bagh, a walled garden. In 1919, British troops fired on a large, peaceful gathering there, killing hundreds of people. The [massacre](jallianwala) shocked India and strengthened the freedom movement. Bullet marks can still be seen on the walls.

About 30 km west of the city is the Attari–Wagah border with Pakistan. Every evening, soldiers from both countries perform a loud, high-stepping ceremony as the flags come down, watched by cheering crowds. Then visitors return to Amritsar, where many end the day with a plate of langar or a buttery Amritsari kulcha, and the temple shining over its pool.
