## The short answer

Bhutan is a small kingdom in the eastern Himalaya, between India and China, with fewer than 8 lakh people. Most countries judge their progress by GDP, the total value of everything they produce. In the 1970s, Bhutan's young fourth king, Jigme Singye Wangchuck, said that Gross National [Happiness](happiness) was more important than Gross National Product.

The idea is that a good country helps people live good lives, which is more than simply richer ones. So Bhutan regularly asks its citizens about their health, sleep, education, community, culture, environment and how they feel. Government plans are supposed to be checked against how they affect this happiness. It doesn't make Bhutan a paradise, but it does make the country think differently about what progress means.

## Measuring a feeling

Happiness sounds impossible to count. Bhutan's answer is a long questionnaire. Trained [surveyors](surveys) visit thousands of households and ask people about nine areas of life, including psychological wellbeing, health, time use, good governance and how connected they feel to their community.

The answers are combined into a GNH Index. It shows which groups are doing well and which are struggling, so that money can be aimed where it is most needed. Other countries, and even the United Nations, have borrowed parts of the idea when they measure wellbeing.

## A forest kingdom

About 70 percent of Bhutan is covered in forest, and its constitution says that at least 60 percent must stay forested forever. All those trees absorb more carbon dioxide than the country releases, so Bhutan is often described as carbon negative, a rare thing in a world worried about [climate change](climate-change).

Fast rivers rush down from the mountains, and Bhutan builds [dams](dams-power) to turn them into electricity. Much of that power is sold to India, and it is the country's biggest export. Indian visitors can enter with a voter ID card instead of a passport, and the Indian rupee is accepted alongside Bhutan's own currency, the ngultrum.

## Change, slowly

Bhutan kept the outside world at arm's length for a long time. [Television](television-invented) and the internet were only allowed in 1999. Climbing mountains above 6,000 metres is banned, out of respect for the gods believed to live on them. So Gangkhar Puensum, at about 7,570 metres, may be the highest mountain on Earth that nobody has climbed.

Thimphu is one of the very few capital cities with no [traffic lights](road-safety). A set was put up at a busy crossing in the 1990s, but people found it impersonal and it was soon taken down. A police officer in white gloves still waves the cars through by hand from a little painted booth.

High above the Paro valley, the Tiger's Nest monastery clings to a sheer cliff. Legend says the Buddhist teacher Guru Rinpoche flew there on the back of a tigress in the 8th century to [meditate](mindfulness) in a cave.

In 2008, Bhutan held its first national elections for a parliament. The push came from the kings themselves, even though many Bhutanese wanted them to keep their power. The country's national sport is [archery](archery), and village matches come with singing, dancing and cheeky teasing of the other team.

## Hard questions

Bhutan has critics too. In the early 1990s, more than a lakh Nepali-speaking people, called Lhotshampa, left or were forced out of southern Bhutan after new rules on citizenship and dress. Many lived for years in refugee camps in Nepal, and most were later resettled in countries such as the USA. Others point out that many Bhutanese, especially in villages, are still poor, and that in recent years thousands of young people have moved abroad, many to Australia, to study and work.
