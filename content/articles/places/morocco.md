## The short answer

Morocco is a country in the north-west corner of Africa, facing both the Atlantic Ocean and the Mediterranean Sea. At its northern tip it is only about 14 kilometres from Spain, across the Strait of Gibraltar. It is a kingdom, ruled by a king together with an elected parliament.

Inside its borders you can find almost every kind of landscape. The Atlas Mountains run through the middle, with snow on their peaks in winter. To the south and east, they drop down to rocky plains and the dunes of the [Sahara](sahara). Along the coasts are busy ports and beaches. Morocco's old walled cities, called medinas, are mazes of narrow lanes, markets and workshops.

## Amazigh, Arab and more

The first known people of North Africa were the Amazigh, often called Berbers. Many Moroccans are Amazigh, and in 2011 their language, Tamazight, became an official language alongside [Arabic](arabic-script). It has its own alphabet, Tifinagh, which you can see on road signs.

Arab armies brought Islam to the region in the 7th century. Later, Moroccan dynasties ruled parts of Spain, and Jews and Muslims expelled from Spain after 1492 settled in Moroccan cities. France and Spain controlled most of Morocco in the early 1900s, and it became independent in 1956. That is why many people speak French as well as Arabic, and why Moroccan food, music and buildings mix so many traditions.

## A university from the year 859

In the old city of Fez, a woman named Fatima al-Fihri used her inheritance to found a mosque called al-Qarawiyyin in 859. It grew into a centre of learning, teaching religion, law, astronomy and mathematics. UNESCO and Guinness World Records describe it as the [oldest university in the world](hist-first-university) still operating. Its library, among the [great libraries](great-libraries) of the Islamic world, still holds very old handwritten books.

Fez itself has one of the largest car-free old towns anywhere. Goods are carried through its thousands of lanes by donkeys and handcarts. In the Chouara tannery, workers dye leather in honeycomb rows of stone pits full of colour.

## The traveller from Tangier

In 1304, a boy named [Ibn Battuta](ibn-battuta) was born in Tangier, on Morocco's northern coast. At 21 he set off for [Mecca](mecca-hajj) and kept going for nearly 30 years. He travelled through the Middle East, Central Asia and India, where he worked as a judge in Delhi, then on to China. On returning home he dictated the story of his travels, one of the great travel books of history.

## Tiles, tea and tagines

Moroccan builders cover walls and fountains with zellige, mosaics of small hand-cut clay tiles. The pieces are cut into stars, crosses and polygons, then fitted together into patterns with no gaps, a beautiful kind of [tessellation](tessellations). Many patterns are built around eight-pointed and twelve-pointed stars.

Guests are welcomed with sweet green mint [tea](tea), poured from high above the glass to make it froth. Families cook in a tagine, a clay pot with a tall cone-shaped lid that sends steam back down onto the food. Couscous, tiny steamed balls of wheat, is a Friday favourite.

In the south-west, argan trees grow in dry, rocky land, almost nowhere else on Earth. Goats climb into them to eat the leaves and fruit, and local women crack the hard nuts to press argan oil.
