## The short answer

After 1994, when South Africa held its first election in which people of every race could vote, Archbishop Desmond Tutu began calling it the "Rainbow Nation". [Nelson Mandela](apartheid), the new president, used the phrase too.

The idea was simple. A rainbow has many colours, and it is beautiful because they sit side by side. South Africa has people whose families are Black African, white, Indian and of mixed heritage, speaking a dozen official languages. The nickname was a hope that all of them could share one country after decades of being kept apart by law.

## A country kept apart

From 1948, South Africa's government ran a system called [apartheid](?apartheid), which means "apartness" in Afrikaans. Laws decided where people could live, which schools they could attend, which beaches they could visit and even whom they could marry, all by race. Black South Africans, who were most of the population, could not vote for the national government.

Many people fought this. Some were jailed for years. The most famous was [Nelson Mandela](nelson-mandela), who spent 27 years in prison. Countries around the world refused to play sport with South Africa, and India was one of the first to cut trade ties, in 1946, even before apartheid became law.

## The Indian connection

In 1893 a young lawyer named [Mohandas Gandhi](gandhi-south-africa) arrived in South Africa to work on a court case. That year he was thrown off a train at Pietermaritzburg for sitting in a carriage reserved for white passengers. He stayed for over twenty years, and it was here that he first organised peaceful mass protest. You can follow what happened next in the story of [Gandhi](gandhi).

Today about one and a half million South Africans have Indian roots, many of them in and around Durban. Their families mostly arrived in the late 1800s to work on sugar plantations. Durban's bunny chow, a hollowed-out loaf filled with curry, grew from that mix.

## Twelve languages

South Africa has twelve official languages. They include isiZulu, isiXhosa, Afrikaans, English, Sesotho and Setswana. In 2023, South African [Sign Language](sign-language) became the twelfth. The national anthem itself switches between five languages in a single song.

Many South Africans switch languages every day: one at home, another at school, a third with friends. That is ordinary in India too, where plenty of children speak two or three languages before they learn to read. You can see how common this is in the article on [languages around the world](languages-world).

## From mountains to two oceans

The land is as mixed as the people. [Cape Town](world-cape-town) sits beneath Table Mountain, whose flat top is often covered by a white sheet of cloud locals call the "tablecloth". To the north-east, Kruger National Park is one of Africa's largest wildlife reserves, home to lions, elephants and rhinos.

South Africa's coast touches two oceans. The cold Atlantic washes the west, and the warm Indian Ocean the east. The water on a beach near Durban can feel bath-warm, while Cape Town's is chilly enough to make you gasp.

The cold western current brings up food from the deep, so the sea there is full of fish, seals and seabirds. Near Cape Town, a colony of African [penguins](penguin-flight) waddles across a public beach called Boulders, a few minutes' walk from people's houses. [Penguins](ocean-penguins-swim) on an African beach sound odd, but the chilly water suits them. Out at sea, great white sharks hunt the seals, and in the warmer east, whales pass close enough to shore to be watched from cliff tops.
