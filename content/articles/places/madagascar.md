## The short answer

Madagascar is a huge island in the Indian Ocean, off the south-east coast of Africa. It has been cut off from every other landmass for tens of millions of years. Its plants and animals have been evolving alone all that time, with no fresh arrivals walking in from outside.

The result is a land of [species](?species) seen nowhere else. About 90 percent of its plants and animals are found only in Madagascar. It has more than a hundred kinds of lemur, about half the world's chameleons, strange spiny forests, and six kinds of baobab tree that grow wild nowhere else. Scientists sometimes call it the world's eighth continent.

## An island adrift

About 165 million years ago, Madagascar and India, still joined together, broke away from Africa as the old [supercontinent](pangaea) of [Gondwana](?gondwana) split apart. Then, around 88 million years ago, India separated from Madagascar and began [drifting north](india-drift) towards Asia, where it later crashed in and pushed up the Himalaya.

Madagascar stayed behind, sitting about 400 kilometres off Africa. Any new animals had to cross the sea. Only a few kinds of land mammal ever made it, probably by floating over on rafts of fallen trees after storms. Each lucky group then spread out and evolved into many new species, filling jobs that monkeys, cats or [woodpeckers](woodpeckers) do elsewhere.

## Lemurs, fossas and a finger like a twig

Lemurs are primates, related to monkeys, apes and us, but from a much older branch. They range from the mouse lemur, which fits in your palm, to the indri, which sings loud wailing songs through the rainforest. Ring-tailed lemurs sunbathe in the morning with their arms spread wide.

The aye-aye taps on tree bark with a long, thin middle finger, listens for grubs moving inside, then hooks them out. The fossa, which looks like a cross between a cat and a mongoose, is the island's top hunter and climbs trees after lemurs.

Chameleons here come in every size, from one of the smallest reptiles on Earth, about as long as a fingernail, to giants longer than a ruler. Their colour changes and [camouflage](camouflage) help them signal to each other and hide from birds.

## Late-arriving people

Humans reached Madagascar surprisingly late, probably in the last few thousand years, though the exact timing is debated. Some of the first settlers sailed all the way from Southeast Asia, across the Indian Ocean. Others came from Africa. That is why the Malagasy language is related to languages spoken in Borneo, thousands of kilometres away, while many people also have African ancestry.

People found giant animals that are now gone. Elephant birds, taller than a man and flightless, laid eggs that could hold the contents of more than a hundred hen's eggs. They and giant lemurs the size of gorillas died out within the last thousand years or so, much like the [dodo](dodo) on nearby Mauritius.

## Forests under pressure

Much of Madagascar's original forest has been cut down, for farming, firewood and charcoal. Many lemurs are now endangered, and some kinds live in only a single patch of forest. Scientists warn that the island is one of the places on Earth where [extinction](extinction-today) is happening fastest. When forest is cleared on steep hills, rain washes the red soil into the rivers, which run rusty red into the sea. Astronauts have described seeing that red stain from orbit. National parks and local groups are working to protect what remains.
