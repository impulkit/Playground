## The short answer

Deep in your brain sits the hypothalamus, a part about the size of an almond. It works like the thermostat on an air conditioner, keeping your body at about 37 °C.

When your [immune system](immune-system) is fighting germs, it releases chemical signals that reach the hypothalamus. These signals tell it to raise the setting by a degree or two. Your body then works to heat itself up, and that rise is a fever. Many germs grow more slowly when it's hotter, and some of your immune cells work faster. So a fever is usually a sign that your body is fighting, not that something has gone wrong with the fight.

## Shivers, then sweat

A fever often comes in stages. First, [the brain](brain-works)'s new setting is higher than your body's temperature, so you feel chilly. Blood vessels in your skin narrow to keep heat in, which can make you look pale. Your muscles shake in quick bursts, which is shivering. Shivering makes heat, the way rubbing your hands together does.

Once your body reaches the new setting, you may feel hot and flushed. When the illness eases, the setting drops back to normal. Now your body is too hot for the setting, so you [sweat](sweat) and throw off the covers. People call this the fever breaking.

## Where does 37 come from?

In the 1860s, a German doctor named Carl Wunderlich collected a huge number of temperature readings from his patients. He decided that healthy people [average](averages) 37 °C, and that number stuck in textbooks around the world.

But normal isn't one fixed number. Your temperature is lower in the early morning and higher in the late afternoon. It's a little different in the mouth, the armpit and the ear. In 2020, a team at Stanford University looked at American records going back to the 1860s. They found that average temperatures seem to have dropped a little. One possible reason is that people today have fewer long-lasting infections, so their bodies run slightly cooler. Other scientists are still checking the idea.

## Measuring the heat

For most of history, doctors judged a fever by touching a forehead. In the early 1600s, an Italian doctor named Santorio Santorio made one of the first instruments to measure body heat. Early medical [thermometers](thermometer) were about 30 centimetres long and took up to 20 minutes to give a reading.

Today, in India and most of the world, body temperature is measured in Celsius. In the USA it is usually in Fahrenheit, where normal is about 98.6 °F and a fever starts at around 100.4 °F. The two [scales](temperature-scales) measure the same heat in different steps.

## When to tell an adult

Most fevers in children come from common infections like [colds](cold-flu) and go away in a few days. Resting and drinking plenty of water helps. Some medicines can bring a fever down and make you feel more comfortable, but only an adult should decide on these.

Doctors want to know about a fever in a young baby, a very high fever, or one that lasts more than a few days. They also want to know if someone with a fever has a stiff neck, a rash that doesn't fade when pressed, or trouble breathing. If you feel unwell, telling a parent, teacher or doctor is always the right first step.
