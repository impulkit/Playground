## The short answer

Disease has shaped history as much as kings and wars. Plagues have emptied cities, toppled empires, decided battles and changed how people live, work and build their towns.

A few stand out. The Black Death swept across Asia and Europe in the 1300s. Smallpox and other diseases carried by Europeans devastated the peoples of the Americas after 1492. Cholera spread from Bengal around the world in the 1800s and pushed cities to build sewers. Mosquito-borne diseases decided who could build the [Panama Canal](panama-canal). And the 1918 flu killed more people than the First World War.

## The Black Death

In the 1340s, a plague spread along trade routes from Central Asia to the Black Sea and into Europe. It was caused by bacteria carried by fleas on rats, and it could also pass between people. Between 1347 and 1351, the [Black Death](black-death) killed perhaps a third or more of Europe's population, and it also struck the Middle East and North Africa.

With so many workers dead, survivors could demand higher wages. Some historians think this helped weaken the old feudal system in parts of Europe. In 1377, the port of Ragusa, now Dubrovnik, made arriving ships wait in isolation for 30 days. Venice and other ports later stretched the wait to 40 days, quaranta giorni in Italian, which gave us the word quarantine.

## Smallpox in the Americas

When Europeans arrived in the Americas after 1492, they brought diseases such as smallpox and measles. The Indigenous peoples had never met them and had no resistance. Epidemics killed a very large share of the population over the following century, though exact numbers are hotly debated.

Disease helped [small groups of Spanish soldiers](spanish-conquest) conquer the Aztec and Inca empires. It emptied villages ahead of settlers. Few events in history have been shaped so heavily by germs.

## Cholera and clean water

In 1817, cholera began to spread beyond its old home in Bengal. Over the next decades, it travelled along trade and pilgrim routes to Asia, Europe and the Americas in a series of pandemics. Cholera spreads through water dirtied with sewage.

Cities like London, New York and later Mumbai and Kolkata built proper sewers and clean water supplies. [Toilets and sewers](toilets-sewers) did as much to lengthen lives in the 1800s and 1900s as almost any medicine.

## Mosquitoes and canals

In the 1880s, a French company tried to dig a canal across Panama. Thousands of workers died, many from yellow fever and malaria, and the project collapsed. Scientists then discovered that [mosquitoes](mosquito-diseases) spread both diseases.

When the USA took over in 1904, the army doctor William Gorgas led a campaign to drain standing water and screen windows. Yellow fever disappeared from the canal zone, and the canal opened in 1914. A century earlier, in 1802, yellow fever had wrecked a French army sent by Napoleon to Haiti, helping the Haitian Revolution succeed.

## The flu of 1918

In 1918 and 1919, a new flu spread around the world as soldiers came home from the First World War. It infected perhaps a third of the world's people and killed tens of millions. India was among the worst hit, losing perhaps 12 million people or more.

## Fighting back

In 1897, Waldemar Haffkine made an early plague vaccine in Bombay, and his laboratory became today's Haffkine Institute. Vaccines later wiped out [smallpox](smallpox) entirely. Health workers in India carried out the final hunt for cases in the 1970s, and the WHO declared it eradicated in 1980. Polio may be next. COVID-19, which spread around the world in 2020, showed that new diseases can still change the world.
