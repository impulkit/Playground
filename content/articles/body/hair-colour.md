## The short answer

Hair gets its colour from [melanin](?melanin), the same family of pigments that colours skin and eyes. There are two main kinds. Eumelanin is brown to black. Pheomelanin is red to yellow. Every shade of human hair comes from how much of each one you have.

Lots of eumelanin makes hair black or dark brown. Less makes it light brown. Very little makes it blond. Plenty of pheomelanin with little eumelanin makes it red. How much of each your body makes is controlled by your [genes](genes-family), passed down from your parents, which is why hair colour runs in families.

## Tiny pigment factories

At the root of each hair, under the skin, is a little pocket called a follicle. Inside it, special cells called melanocytes make melanin and pass it into the new hair as it grows. Once the hair pushes out of the skin, it is no longer alive, and its colour is locked in.

The pigment comes in tiny grains packed inside the hair. Black hair is crowded with dark grains. Blond hair has only a few, so light passes through and scatters, making it look golden. In red hair, pheomelanin gives a warm orange glow.

## Mostly dark

Around the world, black and dark brown hair are by far the most common. They are the usual colours across Asia, Africa, the Americas and much of Europe. Dark hair holds a lot of eumelanin, which helps protect the scalp from the sun's ultraviolet rays.

Blond and red hair are common mainly in northern and western Europe, and in people whose families came from there. Red hair, found in about 1 or 2 per cent of people worldwide, is most common in [Scotland](scotland) and Ireland. It is usually caused by changes in one gene, called MC1R. Usually a person needs two copies, one from each parent, to have red hair.

## Blond in the Pacific

In the Solomon Islands, northeast of Australia, many children have dark skin and bright blond hair. For years people guessed it might come from European sailors or traders. In 2012, scientists tested the islanders' DNA and found the blond hair comes from a change in a different gene, one that is almost never found in Europeans.

That means blond hair evolved at least twice, in different places and in different ways. Hair colour is controlled by many genes, more than a hundred, so there are many routes to the same shade.

## Changing over a lifetime

Many children are born fair and get darker as they grow, as their bodies make more eumelanin. Some babies are born with lots of dark hair that falls out and grows back lighter. Summer sun can bleach the ends of hair, because ultraviolet light slowly breaks down melanin. Those faded ends are dead hair, so they stay light until they are cut off.

Later in life, the melanocytes slowly stop working. New hairs grow with less and less pigment until they are white. A mix of white and dark hairs looks grey. Most people start getting [grey hair](grey-hair) somewhere in their 30s or 40s, and when depends mostly on genes.

## Colours we choose

People have been changing their hair colour for thousands of years. In India, henna, the same plant used for [mehendi](mehendi), has long been used to dye hair a reddish-brown. Today, shops sell dyes in every colour from black to bright blue.

Your natural hair colour says very little about who you are, but it holds a small record of your family history. Some genes affect both hair and [skin colour](skin-colour), but many work separately. That is how a child in the Solomon Islands can have dark skin and golden hair at the same time.
