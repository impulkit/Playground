## The short answer

Your teeth are covered in a thin, sticky film called [plaque](?plaque). It's made of millions of [bacteria](?bacteria). When you eat sugar, they eat it too. As they digest it, they make acid.

That acid sits against your teeth and slowly dissolves minerals out of the enamel, the hard, shiny outer layer. One sweet won't do much, because your [saliva](gross-saliva) washes away and calms the acid, and your teeth can take back some lost minerals. But if acid attacks come again and again, faster than teeth can repair, a soft spot forms. In time, it becomes a hole, called a cavity or [tooth decay](tooth-decay). If it reaches the inner part of the tooth, it can hurt.

## The hardest thing in your body

Enamel is harder than any [bone](bones). It's made almost entirely of a crystal mineral containing calcium. But it has one weakness: acid. Lemon juice, [fizzy drinks](soft-drinks) and the acid from plaque bacteria can all soften it.

Unlike bone, enamel can't grow back once a hole forms. The cells that made it disappear before the tooth even appears in your mouth. That's why a cavity has to be cleaned out and filled by a dentist. Your [teeth](teeth) can, though, repair very early damage using minerals from saliva, especially with the help of fluoride.

## It's how often, not how much

The worst thing for teeth isn't the amount of sugar, but how often you have it. After each sugary snack, the mouth stays acidic for a while, often half an hour or more. Then saliva brings it back to normal.

Sipping a sweet drink slowly, or sucking [sweets](sweets-world) one after another, keeps the acid coming all day. Eating sweets as part of a meal, then rinsing with water, is much kinder to teeth. Sticky foods like toffee and dried fruit cling on and feed bacteria longer. So do starchy snacks like chips and biscuits, which break down into sugar in the mouth.

## Fluoride and a city in Michigan

In the early 1900s, a dentist in Colorado named Frederick McKay noticed that many local people had brown-stained teeth, but surprisingly few cavities. Years of detective work traced both to fluoride, a mineral naturally present in their water.

In 1945, Grand Rapids, in Michigan, became the first city in the world to add a small, careful amount of fluoride to its tap water. Cavities among its children dropped sharply. Today, most [toothpastes](made-toothpaste) contain fluoride, which helps enamel rebuild and resist acid. Too much fluoride is a problem too, and in parts of India where groundwater has very high levels, it can stain teeth and harm bones.

## Very old teeth

People have suffered from tooth decay for thousands of years. It became much more common when [farming began](farming-began) and people ate more starchy grains, and again when cheap [sugar](sugar) spread around the world a few hundred years ago.

At Mehrgarh, an ancient farming village in what is now Pakistan, archaeologists found teeth between about 7,500 and 9,000 years old with neat holes drilled into them, probably with flint-tipped tools. Across India, people have long cleaned their teeth with twigs of [neem](neem) or babul. Today's nylon [toothbrush](toothbrush) dates from the 1930s. Brushing twice a day with fluoride toothpaste and seeing a dentist regularly are the best ways to keep the acid-makers in check.
