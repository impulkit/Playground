## The short answer

When you run, your leg muscles suddenly need much more oxygen and fuel, and they produce more waste, such as carbon dioxide. [Blood](blood) carries oxygen to muscles and takes the waste away. So the faster the muscles work, the faster blood has to flow.

Your heart does two things to keep up. It beats more often, and it squeezes harder, pushing out more blood with each beat. At the same time you breathe faster and deeper, so [your lungs](lungs-breathe) can load more oxygen into that blood.

None of this needs you to think. Your brain and body handle it automatically.

## A pump that changes gear

At rest, an adult's heart pumps about 5 litres of blood a minute, roughly a bucketful. During hard [exercise](exercise), a fit adult's heart can pump 20 litres or more. Children's hearts are smaller and beat faster, but they speed up in the same way.

Your heart also has a top speed. A rough rule of thumb says your maximum heart rate is about 220 minus your age, so around 208 beats a minute for a 12-year-old. It's only a guide, since people differ a lot, but it shows how much room for speeding up a young heart has.

It is a bit like a cricket team in the final overs of a chase. The batters run harder between wickets and hit bigger shots at the same time, because the target is getting closer.

## Who gives the order

Deep in your brain, an area called the brainstem keeps watch on your body. When you start moving, nerves send signals to the heart's [natural pacemaker](accident-pacemaker), a small patch of cells that sets its rhythm.

Sensors in your blood vessels notice rising carbon dioxide, and your brain responds by raising both heart rate and breathing. A hormone called adrenaline, released when you're excited or scared, makes the heart beat faster too. That is why your heart can race before a race has even started.

## Help from your muscles

Pumping blood out is only half the job. It also has to come back up from your feet, against gravity. Here your leg [muscles](muscles) help. Each time they squeeze, they press on veins, which have little one-way valves inside, and push blood back towards the heart.

This is why soldiers standing still for a long time sometimes faint, while people walking around rarely do. Moving muscles act like a second pump.

## A stronger heart

Your heart is a muscle, and like other muscles it gets stronger with regular exercise. A trained heart pumps more blood per beat, so it can beat more slowly at rest.

Elite [marathon runners](running-marathon) and cyclists often have resting heart rates of 40 or even lower. Doctors generally say a normal adult resting rate is about 60 to 100 beats a minute. Children's rates are higher, and babies' are higher still.

## Cooling down

When you stop running, your heart doesn't drop straight back to normal. It slows over a few minutes as your muscles clear out their waste and your breathing settles. The quicker it recovers, the fitter you usually are.

Over a lifetime, a human heart beats around two and a half to three billion times. A mouse's heart can beat over 500 times a minute, while a diving [blue whale](blue-whale)'s can slow to just two or three beats a minute.
