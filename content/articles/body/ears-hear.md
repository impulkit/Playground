## The short answer

[Sound](sound-travel) is air wobbling back and forth. The outer part of your ear, the bit you can see, catches these wobbles and funnels them down a short tube to your eardrum, a thin skin stretched tight like a drum.

The sound shakes the eardrum. Behind it, three tiny [bones](bones) pass the shaking along to a snail-shaped tube called the [cochlea](?cochlea), which is full of fluid. Inside are thousands of hair cells. As the fluid ripples, their tiny hairs bend, and the cells send electrical signals along a nerve to your brain.

Your brain turns those signals into everything you hear, from a whisper to a thunderclap.

## Hammer, anvil and stirrup

The three bones in your middle ear are the smallest in your body. Their common names describe their shapes: the hammer, the anvil and the stirrup. The stirrup, or stapes, is only about 3 mm long, smaller than a grain of rice.

Together they work like a set of [levers](lever-lift). They take the large, gentle movements of the eardrum and turn them into smaller, stronger pushes on the cochlea. Without them, most sound would bounce off the fluid instead of going into it.

## A piano in a snail shell

Uncoiled, the cochlea would be about 3.5 cm long. Different parts of it respond to different pitches. Hair cells near the start pick up high sounds, like a whistle. Those deep in the curl pick up low sounds, like a truck's rumble. It's a little like the keys of a [piano](piano), laid out in order inside a shell the size of a pea.

Young people can hear from about 20 to 20,000 vibrations a second. As people age, the highest sounds usually fade first. Bats and dogs can hear much higher sounds than we can, and [bats](echolocation) use them to find insects in the dark.

## Hearing cells don't grow back

Each cochlea has only about 15,000 hair cells, and in humans they don't grow back once they're damaged. Very loud sounds, or loud music for long periods, can harm them. Hearing loss from noise is permanent but preventable.

That's why the World Health Organization advises keeping the volume on [headphones](sound-recording) moderate, taking breaks, and using [ear protection](how-noise-cancel-ears) at very loud events. If your ears ring after something loud, that's a warning sign.

## When hearing changes

The composer Ludwig van [Beethoven](beethoven) began losing his hearing in his twenties. By the end of his life he was almost completely deaf, yet he kept writing music, hearing it in his head.

Today, [hearing aids](hearing-aids) make sounds louder, and cochlear implants can send signals straight to the hearing nerve. Many deaf people use sign language, a full language with its own grammar. Indian Sign Language and American Sign Language are as different from each other as Hindi and English.

## The balance organ

Next to the cochlea are three curved tubes, set at right angles to each other, also filled with fluid. They sense when your head turns, tilts or nods. Two small sacs nearby sense gravity and whether you're speeding up.

Your brain combines these signals with what your eyes and muscles tell it. When they disagree, for example when you read in a moving car, some people feel sick.

## Popping ears

A thin tube connects each middle ear to the back of your throat. It keeps the air pressure on both sides of the eardrum the same. When a plane climbs or a car drives up a mountain road to a hill station, the pressure changes quickly. Swallowing or yawning opens the tube, and your ears pop as they even out.
