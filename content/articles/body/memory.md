## The short answer

A memory isn't stored in one spot like a file in a drawer. It's a pattern of connections between [neurons](?neuron) spread across your brain. When you learn something, certain connections get stronger. When you remember it, that pattern lights up again.

A small curled part deep inside the brain, the hippocampus, helps turn [new experiences](mystery-deja-vu) into lasting memories. Over time, the memory is shared out to other areas, which is why very old memories can survive even if the hippocampus is damaged.

Remembering is less like playing a recording and more like rebuilding a model from a few saved pieces.

## Short and long

Your short-term memory is like a small whiteboard. It holds only about four to seven things at once, such as a phone number you've just been told, and wipes clean in seconds unless you keep repeating it.

That's why long numbers are split into chunks. A ten-digit mobile number is easier to hold as two groups of five than as ten separate digits. Psychologists call this *chunking*.

Long-term memory is more like a huge library with no clear limit. Some of it holds facts and events, such as your last birthday. Some holds skills, like [swimming](swimming) or tying shoelaces, which you can do without remembering how you learned.

## The man who couldn't make new memories

In 1953, an American man known to science as H.M. had surgery to stop serious seizures. Surgeons removed parts of his brain, including large parts of the hippocampus on both sides. The seizures improved, but H.M. could no longer form new long-term memories. He could chat with someone, then forget meeting them minutes later.

The psychologist Brenda Milner tested him for years. She found he could still learn new skills, like drawing while looking in a mirror, even though he never remembered practising. His case showed that skills and facts are stored in different ways.

## Easily changed

In 1974, the American psychologist Elizabeth Loftus showed people a film of a car crash. People asked how fast the cars were going when they "smashed" guessed higher speeds than people asked about cars that "hit" each other. A week later, some of the "smashed" group remembered broken glass that was never in the film.

## Brains that memorise a city

To drive a black taxi in [London](london), drivers must learn about 25,000 streets, a test called "The Knowledge". In 2000, scientists scanned their brains and found that the back part of [the hippocampus was larger](body-sense-direction) than in other people. The longer they had driven, the bigger it tended to be. Brains change with use.

## Memory without books

For thousands of years, the Vedas were passed down in India mostly by memory. Students learned to chant long [Sanskrit](sanskrit) texts in several patterns, including forwards and in tricky back-and-forth orders, so that any mistake would stand out. UNESCO added the tradition of Vedic chanting to its list of the world's intangible heritage.

Ancient Greek speakers had their own trick, the "memory palace". You picture a building you know well and place each thing you want to remember in a different room. Memory champions still use it today.

## Why forgetting is useful

In 1885, Hermann Ebbinghaus showed that we forget most new things fast, unless we review them. Spreading practice over several days works far better than one long session. It's why teachers go back over [times tables](multiplication-tables) again and again.

Forgetting isn't always a failure. Your brain drops details it doesn't need, like what you ate for lunch three Tuesdays ago, so the important things are easier to find. [Sleep](sleep-why) helps decide what stays.
