## The short answer

When you cut yourself, your body runs a repair job in four overlapping stages.

First it stops the bleeding. Blood vessels narrow, and tiny cell pieces called [platelets](?platelet) stick together over the gap, held by a mesh of protein threads. This forms a clot. When the clot dries on the surface, it becomes a scab.

Next, white blood cells clean out germs and dirt, which is why a cut can look red and puffy. Then new skin cells and fresh tissue grow in to fill the gap. Finally, over months, the repair is strengthened and tidied. A small cut is closed within days. A deeper one may [leave a scar](power-axolotl).

## Stopping the leak

Your [blood](blood) carries billions of platelets. Normally they float along smoothly. But when a vessel tears, they meet the rough tissue underneath, turn sticky and pile up. They also send out chemical signals calling for help.

Those signals start a chain of reactions that turns a protein in the blood into long threads called fibrin. The fibrin wraps around platelets and red blood cells like a net, and the plug becomes firm. Most small cuts stop bleeding within a few minutes.

## The clean-up

Over the next few days, your [immune system](immune-system) gets to work. Extra blood flows to the area, bringing white blood cells that swallow bacteria and dead cells. The area may feel warm, look pink and be a little sore.

This is normal. But sometimes a cut gets more painful, swells a lot, oozes pus or has red streaks spreading from it. Then it may be infected, and a trusted adult should get a doctor to look at it. People with a condition called haemophilia have blood that doesn't clot properly, so even small cuts need extra care.

## Building new skin

Under the scab, cells at the edges of the wound start to multiply and crawl across the gap. Other cells lay down collagen, a tough protein that acts like scaffolding. Tiny new blood vessels grow into the area to feed it.

When the gap is covered with new [skin](skin), the scab has nothing left to do and falls off on its own. Picking it off early tears away new cells and can make a scar more likely.

## Why moist is better

For a long time, many people believed cuts should be left open to dry. In 1962, a British scientist, George Winter, tested this on young pigs, whose skin is a lot like ours. Wounds that were covered healed about twice as fast as wounds left open to the air.

This led to the modern plasters and dressings that keep a wound clean and slightly moist. Good [first aid](first-aid) for a small cut is simple: wash it with [clean water](water-purify), press to stop bleeding, and cover it.

## Scars and stitches

A deep cut may need stitches, [glue](glue-sticky) or strips that hold the edges together, so there is less gap to fill. Surgeons have been closing wounds this way for thousands of years. The ancient Indian surgeon [Sushruta](sushruta) described stitching with thread.

Scar tissue is made of collagen laid down in a hurry, so it is less stretchy than normal skin. It has no hair or sweat glands. Scars often fade over a year or two, but rarely vanish completely. Some people's scars grow large and raised, which doctors can help with.
