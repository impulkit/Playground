## The short answer

Screens affect your body in two main ways: through your eyes and through your sleep.

When you look at a screen up close for a long time, the tiny [muscles](muscles) inside your eyes work hard to keep it in focus, and you blink much less. Your eyes can get tired, dry, itchy or blurry for a while. This is called digital eye strain. It goes away with rest. Screens also give off bright light, and bright light in the evening tells your brain it's still daytime. That can delay the hormone that makes you sleepy. Exciting games and videos keep your brain alert too. So screen use late at night can push bedtime later and make sleep shorter.

## Tired eyes

To see something close, a ring of muscle inside each eye squeezes to change the shape of the lens. Reading a book needs it too, but people tend to stare at screens for longer without a break. Your eyes also blink less when you concentrate, sometimes half as often as normal. Each blink spreads [a fresh layer of tears](gross-eye-boogers), so fewer [blinks](blinking) mean drier eyes.

Eye doctors in the USA suggest the 20-20-20 rule. Every 20 minutes, look at something about 6 metres away for 20 seconds. That relaxes the focusing muscles. Keeping the screen at arm's length helps too.

## The short-sight puzzle

Short sight, or myopia, means faraway things look blurry. It's becoming much more common. In some cities in East Asia, a large majority of young adults are short-sighted. Scientists have estimated that by 2050, about half the people in the world could be.

Close-up work, including screens and books, seems to play a part. But the strongest finding is about daylight. Children who spend more time outdoors are less likely to become short-sighted, whatever else they do. Bright daylight seems to help the eye grow to the right length. When Taiwan began sending schoolchildren outdoors for two hours a day, rates fell. Short-sighted children see clearly again with [glasses](lenses-glasses).

## Screens and sleep

Your body has an inner clock that follows light and dark. As evening comes, your brain releases melatonin, a hormone that helps you feel sleepy. Bright light, especially bluish light, can delay it. Phones, tablets and laptops give off quite a lot of blue light, and we hold them close to our faces.

Yet scientists think blue light is only part of the story. A gripping game, a funny video or messages from friends keep your brain switched on, whatever the colour of the light. Many sleep experts suggest putting screens away about an hour before bed and keeping phones out of the bedroom at night. [Sleep](sleep-why) matters a lot for growing children, for memory and for mood.

## Do blue-light glasses help?

Glasses that filter blue light are sold widely in India and the USA. In 2023, a large review of studies found little evidence that they reduce eye strain or improve sleep. Eye doctors generally don't recommend them for this.

Screens can be great for learning, staying in touch and having fun. The trick is balance: breaks for your eyes, time outside, screens off before bed. [Smartphones](smartphones) and computers have made screens part of daily life, so families often agree simple rules that suit them. Knowing how to stay [safe online](online-safety) matters as much as caring for your eyes.
