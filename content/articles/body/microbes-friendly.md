## The short answer

No. Most germs, or microbes as scientists call them, are harmless, and many are helpful. Microbes are living things too small to see without a microscope. They include [bacteria](?bacteria), yeasts and other tiny organisms.

Only a small share of microbes make people ill. Many more live quietly in your gut, on your skin and in your mouth. Together they are called your microbiome. They help you digest food, make some [vitamins](vitamins) and crowd out harmful germs.

Outside your body, microbes make [soil](soil-made) fertile, put oxygen in the air and turn milk into [curd](curd-yogurt).

## A crowd inside you

Most of your microbes live in your large intestine, the last part of your gut. An adult carries about 38 trillion bacteria there and elsewhere, weighing roughly 200 grams, about as much as a mango.

These [gut bacteria](digestion-gut) eat parts of your food that [your stomach](stomach-digest) and small intestine can't break down, such as some kinds of fibre. In return, they make useful substances, including vitamin K, which helps your blood clot. A rich mix of gut bacteria also seems to help train your immune system, so it learns not to attack everything it meets.

## Your first microbes

A baby in the womb has very few microbes. During and after birth, it picks up bacteria from its mother, from milk, from people who hold it and from everything it puts in its mouth. Within a few years, a child's microbiome looks much like an adult's.

Your microbiome is a bit like a fingerprint. No two people have exactly the same mix, and it shifts with what you eat and where you live.

## Microbes in the kitchen

Indian kitchens have used microbes for thousands of years, without anyone knowing they were there. Curd is milk thickened by bacteria. [Idli and dosa batter](dosa-idli) rises overnight because microbes make gas inside it. [Bread rises](food-science-bread-rise) because of yeast, which is a tiny fungus. [Cheese](food-why-cheese-holes), vinegar, [pickles](pickles-achar) and soy sauce all depend on microbes too.

## Helpers in the wider world

In fields, bacteria living in the roots of peas, beans and [lentils](lentils-world) take nitrogen from the air and turn it into plant food. Other microbes rot fallen leaves and dead animals, returning their goodness to the soil.

In the oceans, tiny microbes make a large share of the oxygen we breathe. Inside the guts of cows, microbes digest tough grass. Termites rely on microbes in their guts to break down wood, which is why they can eat a wooden door.

## Microbes as medicine makers

People now put microbes to work on purpose. Many [antibiotics](antibiotics) are made by microbes that live in soil, which use them to fight off rival bacteria. Since the 1980s, people with diabetes have used human insulin made by bacteria that scientists gave the human insulin gene. Before that, insulin had to be collected from the bodies of pigs and cattle.

## The first person to see them

In the 1670s, Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, a cloth seller in Delft in the Netherlands, made tiny but powerful lenses. Looking at drops of water through one of [his microscopes](microscopes), and later at scrapings from his own teeth, he saw "little animals" wriggling about. He was the first person known to see bacteria.

It took another two centuries before scientists proved that some microbes cause disease. That discovery led to soap campaigns, vaccines and new medicines. But it also gave germs a bad name that most of them don't deserve.
