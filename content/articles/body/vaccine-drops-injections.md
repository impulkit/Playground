## The short answer

A vaccine trains your [immune system](immune-system) to recognise a germ. Where it is given decides where that training happens.

Germs like polio enter through the mouth and grow in the gut. A vaccine swallowed as drops builds defences, such as [antibodies](?antibody), right there in the gut lining. Germs that travel in the blood, or enter through cuts or the lungs, are often better handled by an injection.

## Two polio vaccines

In 1955, an American scientist named Jonas Salk released the first polio [vaccine](?vaccine). It used killed virus and was given by injection. It protects the person well, but it does less to stop the virus growing in the gut.

A few years later, Albert Sabin made a vaccine from weakened live virus, given as drops. It copies how real polio enters, so it builds strong protection in the gut. It is also cheap, needs no needles and can be given by volunteers, which made it perfect for giant campaigns like [India's Pulse Polio](polio-india) drive.

## Pros and cons

Drops are quick, painless and easy to give to crores of children in a few days. But the weakened virus in them can, very rarely, change and cause illness, especially where too few children are vaccinated. Injected vaccines with killed virus cannot do this.

So many countries now use both. India added injected polio vaccine to its routine schedule in 2015, alongside the drops. Using both gives strong protection in the blood and in the gut.

## Other swallowed vaccines

Polio is not the only one. Rotavirus, which causes severe tummy upsets in babies, is fought with drops too. Cholera, a germ spread by dirty water, has an oral vaccine. Some flu vaccines are sprayed into the nose, because that is where flu gets in.

Every vaccine is a clever shortcut for your body's defences. The world has already wiped out [smallpox](smallpox), one of the deadliest [diseases in history](disease-history), using vaccines. Polio may be next: wild polio now survives in only a very few places on Earth.
