## The short answer

Laughter is one of the oldest ways humans communicate. Babies laugh before they can talk. Children born unable to see or hear laugh too, so nobody has to learn it by copying.

Scientists think laughter grew out of play. When young apes wrestle and chase, they make a panting, breathy sound that tells each other "this is only a game, I'm not really attacking". Over millions of years, that play-pant may have turned into the "ha-ha-ha" of human laughter. Today, we laugh mostly to show friendliness and to share a feeling with others. Jokes are only a small part of it.

## Mostly not jokes

The American scientist Robert Provine spent years studying laughter. He and his students sat in shopping centres and on college campuses, quietly noting what people said just before they laughed. They recorded more than a thousand moments of laughter.

Only a small share came after anything like a joke. Most came after ordinary remarks, such as "I'll see you guys later". People laughed far more when they were with others. Speakers laughed more than listeners. Laughter, Provine concluded, is mainly about relationships, not humour. You laugh with [friends](friendship) because they're your friends.

## Apes, rats and an old sound

In 2009, a team led by Marina Davila-Ross recorded the sounds that young orangutans, gorillas, chimpanzees and bonobos make when they're tickled. They compared them with human babies. The more closely related a species was to humans, the more its tickle-laugh sounded like ours.

Ape laughter is breathy and goes in and out with each breath. Humans laugh mostly on the breath out, in short bursts, which lets us laugh in strings of "ha-ha-ha". Rats also chirp when [tickled](tickles), at a pitch too high for us to hear.

When you laugh, your diaphragm and chest [muscles](muscles) squeeze in quick, regular bursts. Provine measured the notes in a laugh and found they come about five times a second, each "ha" very short. Try laughing slowly, one "ha" every two seconds, and it sounds odd at once. People are also quite good at telling a real laugh from a polite, put-on one.

## Catching laughter

Like [yawns](yawns), laughter is catching. Hearing someone laugh makes you more likely to laugh too. That's why old TV comedies added recorded laughter to their shows.

Sometimes it spreads much further. In 1962, at a girls' boarding school in what is now Tanzania, a few students started laughing and couldn't stop. It spread to other pupils and nearby villages over several months, and some schools closed for a while. Nobody was badly harmed. Doctors think stress and the catching nature of laughter played a part.

## Laughing on purpose

In 1995, a doctor named Madan Kataria started a laughter club in a park in [Mumbai](mumbai). People gathered in the morning and laughed together on purpose, without jokes. Fake laughter soon turned real, because laughter is so catching. Laughter yoga clubs have since spread to many countries.

Does laughter make you healthier? It does seem to lower stress for a while and make people feel closer, and some small studies suggest other benefits. Bigger claims, like curing illness, don't have good evidence. Still, a good laugh with friends over a story about [Akbar and Birbal](akbar-birbal), or a cartoon, is one of the cheapest pleasures there is. Your [brain](brain-works) rewards it by releasing chemicals that make you feel good.
