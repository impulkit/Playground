## The short answer

When your brain decides something is a threat, it switches on an alarm system. Within seconds, two small glands sitting on top of your kidneys release [adrenaline](?adrenaline) into your blood. Your heart beats faster and harder. You breathe more quickly. Your muscles tense, your palms sweat and your mouth goes dry. Your stomach may feel full of butterflies.

All of this gets your body ready to fight or to run. It's called the fight-or-flight response. For our ancestors, the threat might have been a leopard or a rival. For you, it might be an exam, a stage show or a first day at a new school. Your body reacts in much the same way, even though you don't need to run.

## The alarm, step by step

The alarm starts in the [brain](brain-works). A part that watches for danger signals the hypothalamus, which acts like a control room. It sends messages down nerves to the adrenal glands, and adrenaline floods out.

Adrenaline makes your [heart](heartbeat-exercise) pump more blood to your muscles. Your airways open wider to take in more oxygen. Your [liver](liver) releases stored sugar for quick energy. Your pupils widen to let in more light. [Digestion](stomach-digest) slows down, because there's no point digesting lunch while escaping a leopard. Blood moves away from the stomach, which is part of why it feels strange. A slower [hormone](?hormone), cortisol, keeps the body's energy up if the threat lasts.

## A useful kind of nervous

A little stress can help. In 1908, two American psychologists, Robert Yerkes and John Dodson, found that mice learned a task best with a moderate level of pressure, not too little and not too much. The idea has been stretched since then, but many people do notice they focus better with a little nervousness.

Athletes, musicians and actors feel butterflies before big moments too. Many say the trick isn't to get rid of the feeling, but to see it as their body getting ready. Some actors in the [theatre](theatre) say that if they stopped feeling nervous before a show, they'd worry something was wrong.

## When the alarm stays on

The alarm system is built for short emergencies. The trouble comes when it stays switched on for weeks, because of worries at home, at school or with friends. Then people may sleep badly, get headaches or tummy aches, feel tired, or catch colds more easily.

That doesn't mean something is wrong with you. It means your body is working hard and needs rest and support. Good [sleep](sleep-why), exercise, time outdoors and time with people you trust all help bring the system back to calm. If worries feel too big or go on for a long time, telling a parent, teacher, school counsellor or doctor is a smart and brave step.

## The off switch

The body has a calming system too, which slows the heart and restarts digestion. One of the quickest ways to switch it on is to breathe slowly, making each breath out longer than the breath in. Breathing practices taught in yoga, found across India for many centuries, use the same idea.

Other things help as well: a walk, a good laugh, a hug, music or writing worries down on paper. The biologist Robert Sapolsky called one of his books Why Zebras Don't Get Ulcers. A zebra's alarm switches off soon after the lion has gone, but people can keep theirs running for weeks just by worrying. With practice, people can learn to switch theirs off more like the zebra.
