## The short answer

Your nervous system is your body's message network. The brain and the spinal cord, which runs down inside your backbone, form the control centre. Nerves branch out from them to every part of the body, like the wires of a giant telephone exchange.

Messages travel as tiny electrical pulses along nerve cells, called [neurons](?neuron). Where one neuron meets the next, there's a tiny gap called a synapse. The pulse can't jump it, so the neuron releases chemicals that cross the gap and set off a new pulse in the next cell. All of this happens in a few thousandths of a second.

## Two-way traffic

Some nerves carry messages in. Sensors in your skin, eyes, ears, nose and tongue send news of touch, light, sound, smell and taste towards the brain. Other nerves carry messages out, telling [muscles](muscles) to move and glands to release substances.

Most of the time, your brain decides what to do. But some urgent jobs are handled in the spinal cord, in what are called [reflexes](reflexes). Touch something hot, and your spinal cord tells your arm to jerk back before your brain has even registered the heat.

## The electric frog

In the 1780s, the Italian scientist Luigi Galvani noticed that dead frogs' legs twitched when touched with metal instruments. He argued that animals carried their own "[animal electricity](power-electric-eel)". His experiments helped spark huge interest in [electricity](electricity-history) and led to the first batteries.

Galvani was partly right. Nerve signals are electrical, but they work differently from current in a wire. A neuron pumps charged particles, such as sodium and potassium, in and out across its skin. A signal is a wave of these particles rushing through tiny gates, one patch after another, down the length of the cell.

## Squid, speed and a Nobel Prize

The neurons in squid include some unusually thick nerve fibres, up to about a millimetre across. That made them easy to study with the tools of the 1930s and 1940s. Using them, the British scientists Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley worked out exactly how a nerve signal travels. They shared the 1963 Nobel Prize for it.

In 1850, the German scientist Hermann von Helmholtz had already measured nerve speed in a frog's leg. It was far slower than many expected, only about 30 metres per second.

## Fast lanes and slow lanes

Not all nerves are equally quick. Many fibres are wrapped in a fatty coating called myelin, which works a bit like the plastic insulation on a wire. Signals hop from gap to gap along it, reaching about 120 m/s.

Thin fibres without myelin are much slower, sometimes under 2 m/s. That explains the stubbed toe. A fast nerve brings the first sharp jab of [pain](pain) almost at once. A slower one delivers the dull ache a moment later.

## The longest wires

Some neurons are very long. A single cell can stretch from the bottom of your spine to your big toe, about a metre in a tall adult. The sciatic nerve, which carries many such fibres, is the longest and widest nerve in the body, about as thick as your thumb near its top.

Nerves in the brain and spinal cord don't regrow well if they are badly damaged, which is why [helmets](helmets) and seatbelts matter. Nerves in the arms and legs can sometimes slowly heal, at a rate of roughly a millimetre a day.
