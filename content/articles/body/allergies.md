## The short answer

Your [immune system](immune-system) is meant to attack dangerous germs. In people with an allergy, it also mistakes something harmless for a threat. That might be pollen, [house dust](gross-dust-mites), a cat, peanuts or a bee sting.

The first time, the body quietly makes antibodies against it. These stick to special cells in your skin, nose, [lungs](lungs-breathe) and gut. The next time the harmless thing turns up, those cells burst open and release a chemical called [histamine](?histamine). Histamine makes blood vessels leak, skin itch and noses run. That is why an allergy can feel like a cold that comes on in minutes.

## Sneezing season

The most common allergy is hay fever. Many trees, grasses and weeds send clouds of [pollen](?pollen) into the wind to reach other plants, the way some plants send [seeds flying](seeds-fly). Breathe in enough, and an allergic nose starts to stream.

In 1819, an English doctor, John Bostock, described his own summer sneezing and itchy eyes. In 1873, Charles Blackley, who also had it, showed that pollen was to blame by rubbing pollen on his skin and watching it swell. In India, the pollen of a fast-spreading weed called parthenium, or congress grass, is a well-known cause of allergies. In the USA, a weed called ragweed is the big late-summer trigger, and a single plant can release up to a billion grains of pollen. Pollen counts are usually highest on warm, dry, windy days and drop after rain washes the air.

## From an itch to an emergency

Most allergies are a nuisance: sneezes, itchy eyes, a rash. A few people have reactions that spread through the whole body, called anaphylaxis. Their throat or lips may swell, breathing gets hard and they can faint. This needs emergency help at once.

People who know they have a serious allergy often carry an adrenaline pen, a small injector that a person can use in seconds. Many schools in India and the USA now train teachers to spot the signs. [Food allergies](food-allergies) to peanuts, tree nuts, milk, eggs and shellfish are among the most common serious ones.

## Why are there more allergies now?

In many countries, allergies seem to have become more common over the last hundred years. Scientists don't fully agree on why.

One idea, often called the hygiene hypothesis, says that children who grow up with fewer germs, animals and outdoor dirt may have immune systems that are less well trained. Children who grow up on farms do seem to have fewer allergies. Many scientists now think it's less about dirt and more about meeting a wide range of [friendly microbes](microbes-friendly). Pollution, diet and genes probably play a part too.

## Changing advice on peanuts

For years, parents in the USA and Britain were told to keep peanuts away from babies who might be allergic. Then, in 2015, a large study in London found that giving peanut foods early, under medical guidance, made a peanut allergy far less likely in at-risk babies.

Advice has since changed in several countries. Doctors can also test for allergies with tiny skin pricks, and sometimes slowly train the body to accept a trigger. Allergies often run in families, and some children grow out of theirs. If you think you might be allergic to something, tell a parent and a doctor.
