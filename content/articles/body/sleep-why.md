## The short answer

Sleep isn't the body simply switching off. While you sleep, your brain is busy sorting the day's memories, your body releases a [hormone](?hormone) that helps you grow, and tired tissues repair themselves.

[Without enough sleep](what-if-no-sleep), people find it hard to concentrate, remember things or control their mood. Reaction times slow down. Over weeks, lack of sleep makes it easier to fall ill.

Every animal studied so far seems to need some kind of sleep or rest. That suggests sleep does something living things can't easily do while awake.

## A night in waves

Sleep comes in cycles of about 90 minutes. First you drift into light sleep, then into [deep sleep](body-sleepwalking), when your breathing slows and it's hard to wake you. Deep sleep is when the body releases most of its growth hormone.

Then comes REM sleep, short for "rapid eye movement". Your eyes flick about under closed lids, your brain becomes very active, and most vivid [dreams](dreams) happen. A normal night has four to six of these cycles, with more deep sleep early on and more REM towards morning.

## Sorting the day

During sleep, the brain seems to replay and strengthen things you learned. In experiments, people who sleep after learning a list of words or a new skill usually remember it better than people who stay awake. Sleep helps turn fresh experiences into lasting [memories](memory).

Studies in mice suggest sleep may also help clear waste chemicals out of the brain, like a cleaning crew working the night shift. Scientists are still checking how much this happens in people.

Sleep helps the body fight germs too. In one American experiment, volunteers were given a cold virus on purpose. Those who usually slept less than six hours a night were more likely to catch a cold than those who slept seven hours or more.

Waking briefly between cycles is normal. Most people do it a few times a night and never remember it.

## Your body clock

Deep in your brain sits a tiny cluster of cells that acts as a [clock](clocks), roughly 24 hours long. Light reaching your eyes keeps it in step with [day and night](day-night). When it gets dark, your body makes more melatonin, a chemical that makes you feel sleepy.

Bright screens late at night can confuse this clock and push sleepiness later. Flying across the world confuses it too. Fly from Delhi to New York and your body still thinks it's Indian time for days. That groggy feeling is [jet lag](jet-lag).

## The boy who stayed awake

In 1964, a 17-year-old in San Diego named Randy Gardner stayed awake for about 11 days for a school science project. As the days passed, he became moody, struggled to focus and had trouble remembering things. After the experiment, he slept for about 14 hours and recovered. Record books later stopped accepting attempts like his, because going without sleep is dangerous.

## How much is enough

Sleep experts recommend 9 to 12 hours a night for children aged 6 to 12, and 8 to 10 hours for teenagers. Adults need about 7 or more. Babies sleep most of the day.

Animals vary widely. Some [bats](bats-fly) sleep around 20 hours a day, while giraffes may manage only a few hours. [Dolphins](dolphins-click) never fully let go at all. For people, a regular bedtime, a dark room and no screens just before sleep are the simplest ways to get a good night.
