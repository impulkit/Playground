## The short answer

Your lungs are two soft, spongy bags inside your ribs, but they can't move on their own. Underneath them sits the [diaphragm](?diaphragm), a dome-shaped sheet of muscle. When it tightens, it flattens and pulls down. Your ribs swing up and out a little at the same time. The space inside your chest grows, and air rushes in through your nose or mouth to fill it.

When the diaphragm relaxes, it springs back up, the space shrinks, and air is pushed out. You do this about 15 to 20 times a minute when you're resting, mostly without thinking about it at all.

## A tree turned upside down

Air goes down your windpipe, which splits into two tubes, one for each lung. These split again and again into thinner and thinner branches, like an upside-down tree. At the tip of every twig is a bunch of tiny bubbles called [alveoli](?alveoli).

There are hundreds of millions of them. Each one is wrapped in a net of very thin blood vessels. The wall between the air and the [blood](blood) is far thinner than a sheet of paper. Oxygen slips across into the blood, and carbon dioxide, the waste gas your body makes, slips back out into the air. Spread flat, all those bubble walls would cover about 70 square metres, roughly a [badminton](badminton) court.

## Who tells you to breathe?

You can [hold your breath](body-breath-hold), but not for long, and you never have to remember to breathe while you sleep. A part of your brainstem, at the base of the brain, keeps the rhythm going.

It mostly watches carbon dioxide, not oxygen. When carbon dioxide builds up in your blood, the brainstem sends a stronger signal to the diaphragm. That burning urge you feel when you hold your breath is really your body noticing too much carbon dioxide. When you run, your muscles make more of it, so you breathe faster and deeper without deciding to.

## Breathing where the air is thin

Higher up, each breath holds less oxygen because the air is thinner. On the top of [Mount Everest](mountaineering), a lungful carries only about a third of the oxygen it would at sea level. When Tenzing Norgay and Edmund Hillary first reached the top in 1953, they carried bottled oxygen. For years many doctors thought climbing [Everest](everest-first) without it was impossible. In 1978, Reinhold Messner and Peter Habeler did it anyway.

People who live high up, such as in [Ladakh](ladakh) or the Andes, have bodies that have adjusted over a lifetime or over many generations. Some of them have bigger chests or blood that carries oxygen differently.

## Keeping the pipes clean

Every day your lungs take in dust, pollen and germs. The tubes are lined with sticky mucus and millions of tiny hairs called cilia. The hairs beat together like oars, sweeping the mucus up towards your throat, where you swallow it or cough it out.

This system works well, but it can be overwhelmed. Smoke and [air pollution](air-pollution) carry tiny particles that slip deep into the alveoli, where they are hard to clear. That is why cities like Delhi in winter and Los Angeles on smoggy days warn people to stay indoors when the air is bad. A resting adult moves more than 8,000 litres of air in a day, enough to fill dozens of bathtubs.
