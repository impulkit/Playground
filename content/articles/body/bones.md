## The short answer

An adult usually has 206 bones. A newborn baby has many more, around 270 to 300, because lots of them start out as separate pieces.

As a child grows, some of those pieces join together. The skull begins as several plates with soft gaps between them, which lets a baby's head squeeze through at birth. These plates slowly knit into one. The same happens at the base of your spine and in your hips.

The exact number varies a little. Some people have an extra rib or tiny extra bones in their hands and feet.

## Hands and feet hold most of them

Each hand has 27 bones and each foot has 26. That's more than 100 bones in your hands and feet together, over half of the whole skeleton. All those small bones let your fingers grip a pencil and your feet adjust to a bumpy road.

The longest bone is the femur, in your thigh. It's roughly a quarter of your height. The smallest is the stapes, deep inside your ear, smaller than a grain of rice. It passes sound vibrations along to your inner ear.

## Alive inside

Bones look solid, but they are living tissue. Their hard outer layer is mostly calcium phosphate, a mineral, woven together with a tough, slightly bendy protein called collagen. The mineral makes bone hard. The collagen stops it snapping like glass.

Inside many bones is a lighter honeycomb of spongy bone. In some, the hollow middle holds soft marrow. Red marrow makes your [blood](blood) cells, millions of them every second.

Bone is constantly being dissolved and rebuilt by special cells. When you break a bone, those cells form new bone across the gap, which is why a cast only has to hold the pieces still.

## A photo of a hand

In 1895, the German scientist Wilhelm Röntgen discovered invisible rays that passed through skin but were blocked by bone. He called them [X-rays](x-rays), because X stood for something unknown. One of his first pictures showed the bones of his wife Anna Bertha's hand, with her wedding ring floating around one finger. Within months, doctors around the world were using X-rays to look at broken bones.

## Bones need work

Bones get stronger when they're used. Running, jumping and carrying things make them build more mineral. Calcium from foods like [milk](milk), curd, ragi and green leafy vegetables helps, and so does vitamin D, which your skin makes in sunlight.

In space, bones get lazy. Floating astronauts don't put weight on their legs, so their bones can lose mineral. NASA has found astronauts on long missions can lose roughly 1% of bone mass in certain bones each month, unless they [exercise](exercise) hard every day.

## Not quite bone

Teeth look like bones but aren't. Your nose and the top of your ears are shaped by cartilage, which is firm but bendy. Babies have more cartilage, and much of it slowly turns into bone as they grow. The growth plates near the ends of long bones are made of cartilage too. When they finally turn to bone, usually in the late teens, you stop [growing taller](growing-taller).

## A record written in bone

Bones last long after a body is gone. [Ancient skeletons](mystery-roopkund) tell scientists what people ate, how tall they were and what injuries they had. Over millions of years, buried bones can even turn to stone, which is how we know about dinosaurs.
