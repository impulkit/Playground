## The short answer

A muscle is a bundle of long, thin cells called fibres. When your brain sends a signal down a nerve, the fibres get shorter. As the muscle shortens, it pulls on whatever it's attached to.

Most of the muscles you use to move are joined to [bones](bones) by tough cords called tendons. When a muscle pulls on a bone, the bone swings at a joint, like a door turning on its hinge. Because muscles can only pull, they come in pairs. One pulls a bone one way, and its partner pulls it back.

You have more than 600 of these muscles, and you use dozens of them just to stand up.

## Three kinds

*Skeletal muscles* move your bones. You control them when you want to, which is why you can wave or kick a [football](football-world).

*Smooth muscles* line your stomach, intestines and blood vessels. They squeeze food along and narrow or widen blood vessels without you ever thinking about it.

*Heart muscle* is found only in your [heart](heart-beat). It contracts about 1 lakh times a day for your whole life and never needs a rest.

## Tiny sliding ropes

Inside each fibre are millions of even thinner threads made of two proteins, actin and myosin. In 1954, scientists in Britain found that these threads don't shrink. Instead, they slide past each other. Myosin has tiny heads that grab the actin, pull, let go and grab again, like a team pulling a rope hand over hand in a tug of war.

Each grab uses a little energy from your food. That's why muscles need a steady supply of oxygen and sugar from your blood, and why your heart beats faster when you [exercise](heartbeat-exercise).

## Your arm is a lever

Your elbow works like a [lever](lever-lift). The biceps is attached just below the elbow, close to the joint. That means it has to pull hard, but a small shortening swings your hand a long way, fast. It trades strength for speed, which is handy for throwing a ball.

## Big, small and busy

The biggest muscle is the gluteus maximus in your bottom. It keeps you upright and powers climbing and running. The smallest, the stapedius, is in your ear and is only about a millimetre long. It tightens when loud sounds arrive, to protect your [hearing](ears-hear).

The muscles that move your eyes are among the busiest. Six small muscles hold each eyeball and swivel it in every direction. They make tiny flicks many times a second as you read, and you hardly notice them.

## Getting stronger

When you [lift heavy things](body-strength-limit), your muscle fibres get thicker over time. They don't grow in number much. In 2021, the weightlifter Mirabai Chanu won a silver medal for India at the Tokyo [Olympics](olympics). She lifted a total of 202 kg in two lifts, more than four times her own body weight. Years of training had built muscles that could pull that hard.

Muscles also make heat. When you're cold, they twitch quickly without moving you anywhere. That's shivering, and it's your body's way of warming up.

## Machines that copy muscles

Engineers have tried to copy muscles for years. Most [robots](robots) move with electric motors, which spin rather than pull. Some new robot "muscles" are made from special plastics or tubes that shorten when air is pumped in, getting closer to the way a real muscle works.
