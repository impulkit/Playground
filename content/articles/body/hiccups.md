## The short answer

Under your lungs lies the [diaphragm](?diaphragm), the dome-shaped [muscle](muscles) that does most of your breathing. Normally it tightens and relaxes in a smooth rhythm. During a hiccup, it suddenly jerks, as if it has been startled. That pulls in a quick gulp of air.

Less than a tenth of a second later, the vocal cords in your throat snap shut and stop the air. That sudden stop makes the "hic" sound. The whole thing is a [reflex](reflexes) controlled by nerves running between your brainstem and the diaphragm, which is why you can't simply decide to stop.

## What sets them off

Hiccups often start after eating or drinking too fast, gulping [fizzy drinks](soft-drinks) or swallowing air. A very full [stomach](stomach-digest) sits right under the diaphragm and may irritate it or the nerves nearby. Spicy food, sudden excitement and laughing hard can trigger them too.

Most bouts last a few minutes and then fade. Hiccups that last for days are rare, and a doctor should check them, because they can be a sign of another problem.

Hiccups are also very regular. In most people they come every few seconds, at a steady beat, and the gap stays much the same throughout a bout. Depending on the person, the rate can be anywhere from about 4 to 60 hiccups a minute.

## Leftovers from tadpoles?

Nobody knows for sure why we hiccup. One idea, put forward by French scientists in 2003, links it to our distant ancestors. [Tadpoles](frogs-monsoon) and some fish breathe by gulping water over their gills while closing a flap to keep it out of their lungs. That gulp-and-close action looks a lot like a hiccup. The idea is that the brain circuit for it may have been passed down, long after our ancestors left the water.

Another idea is that hiccups help babies. Babies hiccup a lot, even before birth. Some scientists think hiccups may help them push swallowed air out of the stomach so there's more room for milk. Both ideas are still argued about, and neither has been proven.

## Sixty-eight years of hics

The longest bout of hiccups on record belongs to Charles Osborne, a farmer from Iowa in the USA. His hiccups began in 1922, and they didn't stop until about 1990, roughly 68 years later. He was listed in [Guinness World Records](world-records), and newspapers and radio shows came to talk to him.

Early on, he hiccupped dozens of times a minute, and over time it slowed down. Even so, he lived a full life, marrying twice and raising eight children. His case is very unusual, but it showed doctors how hard the reflex can be to switch off once something keeps setting it off.

## Cures, real and imagined

Every family has a cure. [Hold your breath](body-breath-hold). Sip water from the far side of a glass. Eat a spoon of sugar. Get a fright. Some of these may work by interrupting the nerve signals or by letting carbon dioxide build up in the blood, which calms the diaphragm. None has been proven in careful scientific tests.

The honest answer is that waiting usually works best. Eating slowly, not gulping fizzy drinks and taking calm breaths can make hiccups less likely in the first place. Animals hiccup too. Cats, dogs and rats have been seen doing it, a sign that this odd [brain](brain-works) circuit is very old.
