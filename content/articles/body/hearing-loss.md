## The short answer

Your ears can be damaged by sounds that are too loud, or by fairly loud sounds that go on for too long. The damage happens in the [cochlea](?cochlea), a snail-shaped tube deep inside each ear. It's lined with rows of tiny hair cells that bend when sound waves pass, sending signals to the brain.

Very loud noise can bend these hair cells so hard that they are injured or die. In humans they don't grow back, so the hearing loss is permanent. The good news is that noise damage is almost completely preventable by turning volume down, taking breaks and protecting your ears around very loud sounds.

## Measuring loudness

Loudness is measured in [decibels](?decibel), or dB. A whisper is about 30 dB, normal talking about 60 dB, and heavy city traffic about 85 dB. A rock concert can reach 110 dB, and a big firecracker exploding nearby can be louder still.

The scale is tricky. Each 10 dB step means ten times more sound energy. So 100 dB isn't just a bit louder than 90 dB. It carries ten times as much energy, and it can damage ears in a fraction of the time.

The World Health Organization suggests children keep their weekly listening at around 75 dB or lower. The louder a sound, the shorter the safe time.

## What happens inside the ear

After a very loud event, such as a concert or a festival night full of [fireworks](fireworks), many people notice their hearing seems muffled, or they hear ringing. The ringing is called tinnitus. It often fades within a day as stressed hair cells recover.

But repeated damage adds up. Each time, a few more hair cells may die. Over years, people slowly lose the ability to hear high sounds, like birdsong or some consonants in speech. Conversations in noisy rooms become hard to follow.

## Earphones and safe listening

Earphones send sound straight into the ear canal, so it's easy to turn them up too high without realising, especially on a noisy bus or Mumbai local train. The WHO estimated in 2022 that over a billion young people worldwide are at risk of hearing loss from unsafe listening habits.

Many phones now warn you when the volume has been high for a long time. A simple rule of thumb many audiologists suggest is to keep the volume below about 60% and take regular breaks. [Headphones](how-headphones) with [noise cancelling](noise-cancelling) can help, because blocking outside noise means you don't need to turn the music up as much.

## Festivals, stadiums and factories

Festivals can be loud in both India and the USA, from Diwali and wedding processions to Fourth of July fireworks and packed football stadiums. Indian rules set noise limits for firecrackers and for [loudspeakers](loudspeakers) at night, though they are not always followed.

Standing further from loudspeakers, covering your ears during big bangs, and wearing soft foam [earplugs](how-noise-cancel-ears) at concerts all protect your hearing. Workers in noisy factories and on airport runways wear ear defenders for the same reason.

## Help for hearing loss

If someone has trouble hearing, a hearing test is quick and painless. [Hearing aids](hearing-aids) make sounds louder, and cochlear implants can send signals directly to the hearing nerve for people with severe loss. Many deaf people also communicate in [sign language](sign-language), a full language with its own grammar. Tell an adult if your ears ring often or sounds seem muffled for more than a day.
