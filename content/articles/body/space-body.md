## The short answer

On Earth, [gravity](?gravity) pulls on your body every second of your life. Your muscles, bones and heart are built to work against it. In [orbit](spacecraft-move), astronauts are in constant free fall, so they float, and the body behaves as if gravity has almost vanished.

The body quickly starts to change. Fluids drift up towards the head. Muscles and bones get weaker because they [aren't needed to hold the body up](what-if-no-bones). The spine stretches, eyesight can change, and the sense of balance gets confused.

Most of these changes reverse after astronauts return to Earth, but some take months, and a few may last.

## Puffy faces and bird legs

On Earth, gravity keeps much of your blood and other body fluids in your legs. In space, those fluids spread upwards. Astronauts' faces look round and puffy, their noses feel blocked, and their legs get thinner. Astronauts jokingly call this "puffy face, bird legs".

The blocked nose dulls their [sense of smell](space-weird-smell), which also makes food taste bland. Many astronauts start asking for spicy sauces they wouldn't normally want.

The extra fluid around the head can also press on the back of the eyes. Some astronauts on long missions have found their eyesight changes, and NASA studies this closely.

## Bones and muscles in danger

Your [bones](bones) are alive. They are constantly being broken down and rebuilt, and they grow stronger where they carry weight. In space, bones in the hips, legs and spine carry almost nothing. Astronauts can lose about 1 percent of the bone in these places every month.

Muscles shrink too, especially in the legs and back. To fight this, astronauts on the [space station](space-station) exercise for about two hours a day. They run on a treadmill strapped down with bungee cords, cycle and use a machine that lets them lift "weights" using air pressure.

## Which way is up

Inside your ear is a balance organ that senses movement and gravity, a bit like a spirit level. In space, it sends confusing signals that don't match what the eyes see. In their first few days, many astronauts feel [space sick](motion-sickness), with headaches and nausea.

The brain adapts within days. But back on Earth, it has to readjust. Astronauts often walk unsteadily at first, and some say that, after landing, they let go of a cup in mid-air, expecting it to float.

## Twins and an Indian yogi in orbit

In 2015 and 2016, NASA astronaut Scott Kelly spent 340 days on the space station. His identical [twin](twins) brother Mark, also an astronaut, stayed on Earth. Scientists compared them before, during and after. They found many changes in Scott's body, from his [gut bacteria](digestion-gut) to how his genes were switched on. Most went back to normal within months.

India has its own space story. In 1984, Rakesh Sharma flew to the Soviet Salyut 7 space station. Among his experiments, he tested whether yoga exercises could help the body cope with weightlessness. In 2025, Shubhanshu Shukla became the first Indian to visit the International Space Station. India's [Gaganyaan](gaganyaan) programme aims to launch Indian astronauts on an Indian rocket.

## Radiation and the road to Mars

Earth's thick air and magnetic field shield us from most radiation from the Sun and deep space. Astronauts above the air get much more, which raises their risk of illness later in life. On the space station, they are still partly protected by Earth's magnetic field.

A trip to Mars could take two to three years there and back. Doctors and engineers are still working out how to keep astronauts' bones, muscles, eyes and minds healthy for that long, far from any hospital.
