## The short answer

Inside almost every cell of your body is a long molecule called [DNA](dna). It holds the instructions for building and running you, written in a chemical code. The instructions are divided into sections called [genes](?gene). Some genes help decide your height, the curl of your [hair](hair-grow), the colour of your eyes and the shape of your nose.

You get two copies of each gene, one from your mother and one from your father. So you're a mix of both. But each parent passes on only half of their own genes, chosen by chance. That's why brothers and sisters look alike but not the same, and why you might have your grandmother's nose or your uncle's laugh.

## Packed into chromosomes

Your DNA is very long. Stretched out, the DNA in a single cell would be [about 2 metres long](body-weird-facts). To fit, it's coiled up tightly into 46 bundles called [chromosomes](?chromosome), arranged in 23 pairs. In each pair, one came from your mother and one from your father.

When a parent's body makes an egg or a sperm cell, each pair is split up and shuffled, so only one of each pair goes in. Pieces of the two chromosomes can even swap places first. The result is that each egg and sperm carries a slightly different mix. The number of possible combinations is so huge that, apart from identical [twins](twins), no two people have ever had the same set.

## A monk and his peas

The rules of inheritance were first worked out by Gregor Mendel, a monk in what is now the Czech Republic. Between 1856 and 1863, he grew thousands of pea plants in his monastery garden, carefully crossing tall plants with short ones and yellow peas with green ones.

He noticed that some traits disappeared in one generation and came back in the next. He reasoned that each plant carried two factors for each trait, one of which could hide the other. He published his work in 1866, and almost nobody noticed. Only in 1900 did other scientists rediscover it. Those factors are what we now call genes. Peas, like the [dal](pulses-dal) on your plate, are pulses.

## Strong and hidden

Some versions of a gene are dominant, which means they show up even if you have only one copy. Others are recessive, and they only show if you get two copies, one from each parent. A recessive version can hide for generations, then reappear when two carriers have a child.

Most traits, though, aren't controlled by a single gene. Height is shaped by hundreds. [Eye colour](eyes-colour) depends on several, which is why [brown-eyed](body-eye-colour-babies) parents can have a blue-eyed child, and occasionally the other way round. [Blood groups](blood-groups) are one of the few traits that follow simple rules quite closely.

## Genes aren't everything

Genes give you a starting plan, but life shapes how it unfolds. Food, sleep, exercise, illness and the world around you all affect how tall you grow and how healthy you are. Identical twins share all their genes, yet they can turn out quite different.

In 1953, James Watson and Francis Crick, using [X-ray](x-rays) pictures made in Rosalind Franklin's lab, worked out that DNA is shaped like a twisted ladder. In 2003, scientists finished reading almost the whole human code for the first time. They found about 20,000 genes, far fewer than many expected. A water flea has more.
