## The short answer

Anaemia means your blood does not have enough [haemoglobin](?haemoglobin), the red protein that carries oxygen. People with anaemia often feel tired, dizzy or out of breath.

India has a lot of it because several causes pile up at once. Many diets are low in iron the body can easily absorb. Worms and infections steal blood. Girls and women lose blood every month in their periods. And some families carry inherited blood conditions. How we measure anaemia matters too, and scientists still argue over how big India's number really is.

## How it works

Your [blood](blood) is packed with red blood cells, tiny discs made in the soft marrow inside your [bones](bones). Each one is stuffed with haemoglobin. Every haemoglobin molecule has four atoms of iron at its heart, and each iron atom can hold one molecule of [oxygen](?oxygen).

Think of it like Mumbai's dabbawalas. The red cells are the delivery workers. Haemoglobin is the tiffin box. Iron is the clip that holds the lid shut. In the [lungs](lungs-breathe), each box is filled with oxygen. In your muscles and brain, the box is opened and the oxygen delivered.

Now take away the clips. Workers still go out, but with fewer full boxes. Your brain and muscles get less oxygen than they want. That is why anaemia can make you tired, pale, cold in the hands and feet, and breathless when you run.

A red cell lives about 120 days, so your body must keep making new ones. That needs a steady supply of iron, plus [vitamins](vitamins) like folate and B12, and [protein](?protein).

## Where it came from

People noticed tired, pale patients long before they knew why. In the 1700s, an Italian doctor in Bologna, Vincenzo Menghini, showed that blood contains a good amount of iron, mostly in its red part. He also found that iron people swallowed could later be detected in the blood.

In the 1900s, scientists worked out the shape of haemoglobin itself. Max Perutz, working at Cambridge in England, spent many years mapping it using X-rays. In 1962 he shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry with his colleague John Kendrew.

Doctors also needed a line to decide who counts as anaemic. In 1959, experts at the World Health Organization (WHO) suggested a limit. In 1968, a WHO group set the cut-offs used ever since: below 13 grams of haemoglobin per decilitre of blood for men, and below 12 for women who are not pregnant. In 2024, after reviewing newer studies, WHO kept those adult numbers.

## India's story

India counts anaemia mainly through the National Family Health Survey, or NFHS, which visits lakhs of homes. In the round done in 2019–21, called NFHS-5, about 57% of women aged 15 to 49 and 67% of children aged 6 months to 5 years were found anaemic. Child anaemia had gone up since the round before, in 2015–16.

In 2018, the government launched Anaemia Mukt Bharat (Anaemia-free India). Children get iron syrup. Schoolchildren aged 5 to 10 get a pink iron-folic acid tablet each week, teenagers a blue one, and women a red one. Pregnant women are asked to take a red tablet every day. Twice a year, on 10 February and 10 August, children aged 1 to 19 are given medicine to clear out worms.

India also adds iron to food. Since a pilot began in 2019, rice given through ration shops and school meals like the [mid-day meal](mid-day-meal) has been fortified, meaning tiny iron-rich grains are mixed in. About 65% of Indians eat [rice](rice) every day, so it reaches a great many plates.

## The numbers that matter

- **4 iron atoms** sit in every haemoglobin molecule. Without iron, the body cannot build working haemoglobin, however much food you eat.
- **120 days** is how long a red blood cell lives. Your body needs iron all the time to replace the old ones.
- **About 4 in 10** young children around the world, and **about 3 in 10** women aged 15 to 49, are anaemic, says the WHO. South-East Asia, which includes India, has more anaemic women than any other region.
- **Just over 1 in 4** pregnant women in India took the full 180 iron tablets in NFHS-5, even though nearly 9 in 10 were given them. Handing out tablets is easier than getting people to take every one.
- **Only about half** of Indian women ate dark green leafy vegetables daily and animal protein at least once a week in 2019–21, UNICEF found. Iron-rich food is not on every plate.

## What people argue about

**Finger or vein?** The NFHS tested a drop of blood from a pricked finger. Some scientists say finger blood often reads too low, because it can mix with watery fluid from around the cells. A study led from St. John's Medical College in Bengaluru, using blood from a vein, suggested earlier figures were too high. Because of these doubts, the government stopped haemoglobin testing in NFHS-6 (2023–24) and planned to use vein-blood surveys instead. Others warn that changing the method makes it hard to compare new numbers with old ones.

**Do WHO's cut-offs fit Indians?** The 1968 lines rest on a handful of studies, mostly of white people in Europe and North America. A 2021 study in The Lancet Global Health, led by Harshpal Singh Sachdev, worked out lower cut-offs from healthy Indian children. With them, anaemia among children and teens fell from about 30% to about 11%. Other experts replied that these lines were only statistical, not tied to how healthy children actually were. In 2024, WHO decided not to set different cut-offs for different groups of people, saying there was not yet enough evidence.

**Iron in everyone's rice?** The government says fortified rice is safe for everyone and follows WHO advice for countries where rice is a staple. Some health experts worry about people with thalassaemia or sickle cell disease, inherited conditions where extra iron can build up and harm the body. In 2023 the Supreme Court asked the government to respond to these worries. In 2024 the warning label for these groups was removed after an expert review. Some experts still say people with these conditions should be checked by a doctor first.

## Myths and mix-ups

**"Spinach is the best source of iron."** Spinach has iron, but it also has natural chemicals called oxalates that make some of that iron harder to absorb. Iron from meat, fish and eggs is taken in more easily than iron from plants. Vegetarians can still get plenty from [dal](pulses-dal), chana, rajma and greens, especially eaten with lemon or amla, because vitamin C helps.

**"If I feel tired, I should take iron tablets."** Tiredness has many causes, from poor sleep to [stress](stress). Too much iron can be harmful, so iron supplements should be taken only when a doctor or health worker advises.

**"Anaemia is only about food."** Worms, [malaria](mosquito-diseases) and other infections can cause it too. So can heavy blood loss and inherited blood conditions.

## What it means for you

Anaemia is common, but it is usually easy to find and treat. A simple blood test shows your haemoglobin level. If you often feel very tired, get breathless quickly, or look unusually pale, tell a parent or a doctor.

For most families, the everyday habits are simple. Eat a mix of foods: dal, greens, fruit, and eggs, fish or meat if you eat them. Have a squeeze of lemon with iron-rich meals, and keep [tea](tea) for later, since it blocks some iron. Wash your hands and wear slippers outside, to keep worms away. And if your school gives out the weekly iron tablet, it is there to help.

As girls go through [puberty](puberty-basics) and start their periods, they need more iron than before. That is one reason the blue tablet is meant for teenagers. A little iron each week keeps those dabbawala tiffins full, and your brain and muscles well supplied.
