## The short answer

When you start to run, your [muscles](muscles) need much more energy. They make it by burning sugar and fat, and for that they need oxygen. So your whole body works to deliver more.

Your heart beats faster and harder, pumping more blood. You breathe faster and deeper to pull in oxygen and push out carbon dioxide. Blood vessels in your muscles open wider, while those in your stomach narrow a little. All that burning makes heat, so you get hot and start to [sweat](sweat). Your brain releases chemicals that can lift your mood. Do this often, and your body adapts: your heart, lungs, muscles and bones all get stronger.

## The first minute

In the first few seconds of a sprint, muscles use a small store of ready energy that lasts only moments. Then they start breaking down sugar stored inside them. Your [heart rate](heartbeat-exercise) climbs from around 80 beats a minute to well over 150.

Your [lungs](lungs-breathe) can move more than ten times as much air during hard exercise as at rest. If you're [running as fast as you can](body-speed-limit), the muscles still can't get oxygen quickly enough. They switch to a faster backup method that leaves acidic leftovers. That's the burning feeling in your legs, and the reason you can't sprint flat out for long.

## Long and steady

Slow, steady exercise is different. When you jog, cycle or swim at an easy pace, your heart and lungs can keep up with what your muscles need. Muscles burn more fat as fuel, and you can go on for much longer.

Long-distance runners train for this. Their hearts get bigger and stronger, so each beat pumps more blood. That's why a fit athlete can have a resting heart rate below 50, while most adults sit around 60 to 100. Their muscles also grow more tiny blood vessels and more of the little power stations inside cells that burn fuel with oxygen.

## Stronger after rest

Hard exercise causes tiny tears in muscle fibres. You don't feel them at first. A day or two later, your muscles may feel stiff and sore as your body repairs them. Each repair makes them a little stronger.

[Bones](bones) get stronger too. When you jump, run and climb, the pull and push on your bones tell them to add more mineral. Children who are active build stronger bones that last into old age. Rest matters as much as exercise, since that's when the rebuilding happens, mostly during [sleep](sleep-why).

## Good for the brain as well

Exercise sends more blood to the [brain](brain-works). Many studies have found that active children tend to concentrate better and sleep more soundly. Exercise also releases chemicals that can make you feel calmer and happier.

The World Health Organization suggests children and teenagers get about an hour of activity a day, mostly the kind that makes you breathe hard. It doesn't need to be a sport. A game of [kho-kho](kho-kho) in the school yard, a long bike ride, dancing at a wedding, [a game of tag](life-games-playground) or a [basketball](basketball) game in a park all count. So does walking to school.

Sitting still for hours has the opposite effect. Muscles get weaker and the heart gets less practice. Health experts suggest breaking up long stretches of sitting, even just by standing up and moving around for a few minutes every hour.
