## The short answer

All over your body are special nerve endings that sense damage. Scientists call them nociceptors, from a Latin word for harm. Some react to sharp pressure, some to great heat or cold, and some to chemicals released by injured cells.

When they fire, they send signals up your nerves to your spinal cord and on to your brain. Your brain then decides what's going on and creates the feeling of pain. It's meant to be unpleasant, because pain's job is to make you stop what you're doing and protect the injured part. A stubbed toe makes you hop and hold it. A sore ankle makes you limp, giving it time to heal.

## Two waves of pain

Stub your toe and you may notice two pains. First comes a quick, sharp jab. A moment later comes a dull, throbbing ache. They travel on different kinds of nerve fibres. The fast ones are wrapped in a fatty coating that speeds the signal along. The slow ones aren't.

The sharp pain tells you exactly where and what happened. The slow ache keeps reminding you to look after the injury. Even before the pain arrives, a [reflex](reflexes) may already have jerked your foot away.

## Made in the brain

Pain isn't a simple measure of damage. A football player may not notice a cut until the match ends. A small worry can make a sore throat feel worse. The brain turns the pain signal up or down depending on what else is happening, what you expect and how you feel.

The [brain](brain-works) itself has no pain sensors. That's why surgeons can sometimes operate on the brain while a patient is awake, which helps them avoid harming areas that control speech or movement. Some people who have lost an arm or leg still feel pain where it used to be. This phantom pain shows how much of pain is built by the brain.

## A life without pain

A very small number of people are born unable to feel pain. It sounds like a gift, but it's dangerous. Children with this condition can break bones, bite their tongues or burn themselves without noticing. They have to learn to check their bodies carefully every day.

That shows why pain matters. It's the body's alarm. In 2021, the American scientist David Julius shared a Nobel Prize for finding the sensor that detects heat. He found it by studying capsaicin, the chemical that makes [chillies](chillies) burn. It sets off the very same sensor as real heat, which is why [spicy food feels hot](spicy-hot) even when it's cold.

## Easing pain

People have always [looked for ways to ease pain](oddity-old-medicine). Ancient Egyptians and Greeks chewed willow bark. In the 1800s, chemists found the active substance in it, and in 1899 the company Bayer began selling a version called aspirin.

Surgery used to be done with patients wide awake. In ancient India, the [Sushruta](sushruta) Samhita describes giving wine to patients before operations. In 1846, in Boston, a dentist named William Morton showed in public that breathing ether could put a patient to sleep during an operation. Today, anaesthetics and pain medicines make surgery safe and painless. Long-lasting pain is still hard to treat, so if something hurts for a long time, it's important to tell an adult or a doctor.
