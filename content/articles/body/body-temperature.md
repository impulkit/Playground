## The short answer

Your body works best at about 37°C. Chemical reactions inside your cells, run by [enzymes](?enzyme), are tuned to that temperature, and if it changes a lot, they stop working properly.

A small region at the base of your brain, called the hypothalamus, acts like a thermostat. It gets reports from temperature sensors in your skin and in your blood. When you get too hot, it switches on cooling: sweating and sending more blood to the skin. When you get [too cold](cold-body), it switches on warming: shivering, pulling blood away from the skin and raising [goosebumps](goosebumps).

## The heat you make

You are always making heat. Every cell burns food for energy, and some of that energy comes out as warmth. Even sitting still, an adult gives off about as much heat as a 100-watt bulb. Muscles make much more when you run or play.

That's why a crowded classroom heats up so fast, and why you can feel warm air coming off people after a football match. The hard part isn't making heat. It's getting rid of the extra.

## Cooling down

[On a hot day](heat-body), or when you exercise, [your skin flushes](body-belly-sounds) as tiny blood vessels near the surface widen. Warm blood flows close to the skin and gives off heat to the air.

Then you [sweat](sweat). Each drop takes heat with it as it evaporates. In dry heat, like a Rajasthan summer or an Arizona afternoon, sweat evaporates fast and cools you well. In humid weather, like Mumbai or Florida in the monsoon season, sweat lingers on the skin and you feel sticky and hot.

## Warming up

[When you're cold](body-cold-swimmers), blood vessels in your skin narrow, keeping warm blood deeper in your body. Your fingers and toes may go pale and chilly. That protects your heart, brain and other organs.

If that's not enough, you start shivering. Shivering is your muscles tightening and relaxing very quickly, which makes heat. Goosebumps are a leftover from furry ancestors. In animals with thick fur, raising the hairs traps a layer of warm air. In humans, with little body hair, they don't help much.

## When the thermostat changes

During an infection, your immune system tells the hypothalamus to raise its setting. That's a [fever](fever). You may feel cold and shiver even as your temperature climbs, because your body is trying to reach the new, higher setting. Many scientists think fever helps the body fight germs.

Too much outside heat can overwhelm the system. During severe [heatwaves](heatwaves) in India and the USA, people working outside, the elderly and babies are most at risk of heatstroke, when body temperature rises dangerously high. Drinking water, resting in shade and avoiding the hottest hours all help. Anyone who feels dizzy, confused or stops sweating in the heat needs help from an adult right away.

## Where 37 came from

In 1868, the German doctor Carl Wunderlich published body temperatures from many thousands of patients and settled on 37°C as normal. Converted to Fahrenheit, that became 98.6°F. In 2020, researchers at Stanford University compared old and new records and found that the average American's temperature has dropped slightly since the 1800s. They suggest less illness and more comfortable homes may be the reasons. Doctors now treat 37°C as a handy average rather than an exact rule, since healthy people differ a little from each other.
