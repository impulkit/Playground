## The short answer

Blood looks like one red liquid, but it's really a mixture. Just over half of it is *plasma*, a pale yellow liquid that is mostly water. Floating in the plasma are three kinds of cells.

Red blood cells carry oxygen from your [lungs](lungs-breathe) to every part of your body. White blood cells fight germs. Platelets, tiny cell fragments, plug leaks when you cut yourself.

An adult has about 5 litres of blood, roughly the amount in a big can of cooking oil. A child has less, depending on size. Your [heart](heart-beat) pumps the whole lot around your body about once a minute when you're resting.

## Red cells: the delivery team

Red blood cells are the most common cells in your body. They are tiny discs, thinner in the middle, like a doughnut that forgot to make its hole. This shape gives them lots of surface for picking up oxygen and lets them fold to squeeze through blood vessels narrower than themselves.

Each is packed with [haemoglobin](?haemoglobin), a protein built around iron. Haemoglobin grabs oxygen in the lungs and lets it go in your muscles and brain. It's also what makes blood red. Iron in foods like spinach, dal and meat helps your body make it.

A red cell lives about 120 days. Then it's broken down and recycled. Your bone marrow, the soft middle of [bones](bones) such as your hips and ribs, makes about 2 million new ones every second.

## White cells and platelets

White blood cells are your body's defenders. There are several kinds. Some swallow bacteria whole. Others make antibodies, which stick to germs and mark them for destruction. When you're ill, your body makes more of them.

Platelets are smaller still. When a blood vessel is damaged, they rush to the spot, stick together and help form a clot. Fibres form a net that traps more cells. On the skin, this dries into a [scab](scabs).

## Plasma: the river

Plasma carries more than cells. Sugar from your food, salts, vitamins and [hormones](hormones) all travel in it. So does waste, like carbon dioxide, which goes back to the lungs to be breathed out. Plasma also spreads heat around, which helps keep your whole body at the same temperature.

## Going round in circles

People once thought the [liver](liver) made new blood all the time and the body used it up. In 1628, the English doctor William Harvey showed that the heart pumps the same blood round and round in a loop. He measured how much blood the heart pushes out with each beat and realised the body couldn't possibly make that much every hour. It had to be recycled.

Centuries earlier, the Arab physician Ibn al-Nafis had already described blood passing through the lungs. Harvey put the whole circuit together.

## Blue and green blood

Not every animal has red blood. An [octopus](octopus-smart) has blue blood, because it uses copper instead of iron to carry oxygen. Horseshoe crabs have blue blood too, and it's used to test medicines and injections for harmful bacteria. Some lizards in New Guinea even have green blood.

## Giving blood

Blood can't yet be made in a factory, so hospitals depend on donors. In India, healthy adults from 18 to 65 can usually [donate](blood-donation). In the USA, the minimum age is 16 or 17 in most states. One donation can help save several lives, because the blood is often split into red cells, plasma and platelets for different patients. Before it's given, it must be matched by [blood group](blood-groups).
