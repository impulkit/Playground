## The short answer

In space there's no air or road to push against. So a rocket pushes on something it carries with it: its own exhaust. It throws hot gas out of the back at great speed, and the gas pushes the rocket forward.

Once a spacecraft is moving, it keeps going. Nothing in empty space slows it down. Earth's [gravity](?gravity) is always pulling on it, though. Go fast enough sideways, and you keep falling around Earth instead of into it. That's an [orbit](?orbit).

## Newton's cannon

More than 300 years ago, [Isaac Newton](newton-life) described an experiment that couldn't be done. He imagined a cannon on top of a very tall mountain, above the air.

Fire the ball slowly and it falls to the ground nearby. Fire it faster and it lands farther away. Fire it fast enough and something odd happens. Earth's surface curves away beneath the ball as fast as the ball falls. It never lands. It just keeps falling, all the way around the world.

For a spacecraft just above the air, that speed is about 7.8 km a second. At that speed you could cross India from Kashmir to Kanyakumari in about seven minutes.

## Falling around Earth

The International [Space Station](space-station) orbits about 400 km up, roughly as far as Mumbai is from Goa. It goes around Earth roughly every 90 minutes, so the crew sees about 16 sunrises and sunsets every day.

Astronauts on board still feel about 90% of the gravity we feel on the ground. They float because the station and everything inside it are falling together. If you were in a lift whose cable snapped, you would float inside it for a moment too, for the same reason.

## Steering in space

To change direction, a spacecraft fires small thrusters. Every push must be planned, because fuel is heavy and limited. Most of a rocket's weight at launch is fuel, burned in the first few minutes.

So engineers look for clever paths. India's [Mangalyaan](isro-missions) circled Earth several times, firing its engine each time it swung close, to stretch its orbit before heading for Mars. Chandrayaan-3 did the same, looping around Earth and then around the Moon in carefully changed orbits before it landed. Spacecraft heading to the Moon or planets are aimed not at where their target is, but at where it will be when they arrive.

## A free push from a planet

Spacecraft can also borrow speed from planets. When a probe swoops close behind a moving planet, the planet's gravity flings it forward, a bit like a ball bouncing off the front of a moving train. This is called a gravity assist, or slingshot.

NASA's [Voyager probes](voyager), launched in 1977, used Jupiter and Saturn this way. Voyager 2 also visited Uranus and Neptune. Both are now beyond the planets, more than 20 billion km away. They are still coasting at about 15 to 17 km a second, with no engine needed.

## Gentle engines

Not every engine has to roar. Some spacecraft use ion engines, which push out a thin stream of charged gas. The push is tiny, about the weight of a sheet of paper resting on your hand. But it can run for years. Little by little, the spacecraft speeds up until it is moving faster than a chemical [rocket](rockets-how) alone could make it go.
