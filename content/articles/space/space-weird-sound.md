## The short answer

For human ears, yes. Sound is a wave of squashing and stretching that travels through stuff, like air, water or rock. Space is nearly a [vacuum](?vacuum), almost empty of anything to squash. So a sound has nothing to travel through. If two astronauts on a spacewalk shouted at each other without radios, they would hear nothing.

But space isn't quite empty everywhere. Giant clouds of thin gas fill galaxies and galaxy clusters, and planets like Mars have air. In those places, sound-like waves do travel, sometimes in very strange ways.

## Why the Moon was quiet

On the Moon, there's no air at all. When Apollo astronauts dropped tools, the tools hit the ground silently. The astronauts could only talk to each other by radio, which uses light-like waves that need nothing to travel through.

Their own voices still worked inside the spacesuit, because the suit was full of air. And if a hammer hit the ground right beside an astronaut's boot, the shake could travel up through the suit and be felt more than heard. The way [sound travels](sound-travel) through solids explains that.

## A black hole that hums

In 2003, NASA's Chandra X-ray telescope found ripples in the hot gas around a giant black hole in the Perseus galaxy cluster, about 250 million light-years away. The ripples are real pressure waves, sound waves, pushed out by the black hole.

The note is so low that humans could never hear it, about 57 octaves below middle C on a piano. A single wave takes millions of years to pass. In 2022, NASA scientists raised the pitch by about 57 octaves so people could listen. It sounds like a deep, ghostly groan. An octave is a doubling of frequency, so 57 octaves is a doubling 57 times over, part of the [maths of music](maths-music).

## The sounds of Mars

Mars has air, thin but real, about one percent as thick as Earth's. In 2021, NASA's Perseverance rover landed with microphones and recorded the first sounds from the surface: wind, the crack of its laser zapping rocks, and the whirr of the Ingenuity helicopter.

Scientists found that sound on Mars travels at about 240 m/s, slower than on Earth. Even stranger, high-pitched sounds travel a little faster than low ones. Mars air is mostly carbon dioxide, which behaves oddly with sound at that low pressure. Sounds also fade faster, so you would need to stand close to hear a friend. The story of [the Mars rovers](mars-rovers) has more of their discoveries.

## A noisy space station

Inside the International Space Station, it is far from quiet. Fans and pumps run day and night to move air around. Without fans, the carbon dioxide astronauts breathe out would gather round their faces, because warm air doesn't rise in weightlessness. Astronauts often wear earplugs to sleep.

## Turning space into sound

Spacecraft can also pick up waves in thin charged gas, called [plasma](?plasma), around planets. Scientists turn these readings into sound so people can hear them. Recordings from the Voyager probes, sent out in 1977, include eerie whistles from Jupiter's magnetic field.

Space films usually ignore all this and add roaring engines and booming explosions. That isn't the science, it's the storytelling, a habit that took hold when the first [Star Wars](star-wars-effect) film filled the screen with roaring spaceships.
