## The short answer

A black hole is a place where a huge amount of matter is squeezed into a tiny space. Its [gravity](?gravity) is so strong that nothing close to it can escape, not even light. The edge of no return is called the *[event horizon](what-if-fall-black-hole)*.

Many black holes form when a very big [star](?star) runs out of fuel. Its core collapses under its own weight and keeps on collapsing. So a black hole is not really a hole. It is one of the heaviest things in the universe, packed into one of the smallest spaces.

## How a star becomes a black hole

A star spends its whole life in a tug of war. Gravity pulls its gas inward, and the heat from its core pushes outward. When the fuel runs out, the push fades and gravity wins.

A star like the Sun ends as a [white dwarf](star-life-cycle), roughly the size of Earth. A much heavier star blows itself apart as a [supernova](supernova). If the core left behind is heavy enough, nothing can stop it shrinking. To turn the Sun into a black hole, you would have to crush it into a ball only about 6 km across, smaller than many towns. You can follow the whole story in [how stars live and die](stars-born).

## An Indian connection

In 1930, a 19-year-old student named Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar sailed from India to England. On the long voyage he worked out sums about dying stars. He found a limit: a dead star's core heavier than about 1.4 Suns cannot settle down quietly. It must keep collapsing.

The famous astronomer Arthur Eddington mocked the idea in public. Chandrasekhar kept working anyway. In 1983 he won the Nobel Prize in Physics, and NASA later named its Chandra X-ray Observatory after him. It still studies black holes today.

## Seeing the invisible

You can't see a black hole itself. But gas swirling around one crashes together and gets extremely hot, so it glows.

In 2017, scientists joined radio [telescopes](telescopes-how) in places as far apart as Hawaii, Spain, Chile and Mexico. Working together, they acted like a single dish the size of Earth. In 2019 they released the result: a photo of a glowing orange ring around the black hole at the centre of the galaxy M87, about 55 million [light-years](?lightyear) away. That one weighs about 6.5 billion Suns.

## The giant in our galaxy

At the centre of our own [Milky Way](milky-way) sits a black hole called Sagittarius A*, about 4 million times the mass of the Sun. For decades, astronomers tracked stars racing around an invisible point there. Andrea Ghez in the USA and Reinhard Genzel in Germany shared a Nobel Prize in 2020 for showing that something enormous was hiding in the middle. Its first picture came out in 2022.

It is about 26,000 light-years away, far too distant to harm us.

## Stretched and slowed

Near a small black hole, gravity pulls much harder on your feet than on your head. Anything falling in gets stretched long and thin. Scientists really do call this "spaghettification".

Time behaves oddly too. Albert [Einstein](einstein) predicted that clocks near a black hole tick more slowly than clocks far away. In 2015, two detectors in the USA called LIGO felt tiny ripples in space from two black holes crashing together over a billion light-years away. India has approved its own detector, LIGO-India, to join the search.
