## The short answer

Astronauts eat food quite a lot like ours, packed in ways that work without gravity. On the International Space Station there are more than 100 different dishes to choose from, including scrambled eggs, curries, [noodles](maggi-noodles), soups, chicken, fruit and puddings.

Most of it is either freeze-dried, which means the water has been removed so it is light and keeps for years, or heat-treated and sealed in pouches, like a ready meal. Astronauts add hot or cold water to the dried food, warm the pouches in a small oven, and eat straight from the packet with a spoon. Every few months a cargo ship brings fresh fruit and vegetables, which disappear fast.

## Tubes, cubes and a sandwich

The first space meals were not much fun. In 1962, John Glenn became the first American to eat in orbit, squeezing applesauce from a tube like toothpaste. Early astronauts also nibbled bite-sized cubes coated in gelatine to stop them crumbling.

In 1965, astronaut John Young secretly took a corned beef [sandwich](food-sandwich-name) on the Gemini 3 mission and offered it to his crewmate. Crumbs began floating around the capsule, and they had to put it away. Back on Earth, Young was told off. Loose crumbs can clog air vents or get into delicate instruments, so space food is still designed to stay in one piece.

## Why everything tastes bland

In space, the body's fluids drift upwards towards the head instead of being pulled down to the legs. Astronauts get puffy faces and blocked noses, as if they have a mild cold. Since most of what we call flavour actually comes from [smell](nose-smell), food tastes duller.

That is why astronauts often ask for spicy dishes and bring hot sauce. The way your [tongue and nose work together](taste-why) explains a lot about life in orbit.

## Indian food in orbit

When Rakesh Sharma flew to a Soviet space station in 1984, he took Indian food with him. The Defence Food Research Laboratory in Mysuru prepared the dishes, so he could share an Indian meal with his crewmates. The same laboratory has developed meals for India's [Gaganyaan](gaganyaan) astronauts, Indian dishes packed so they keep for months and can be eaten safely without gravity.

When Shubhanshu Shukla visited the space station in 2025, he took [Indian sweets](sweets-india) and other foods to share with astronauts from other countries. [Sunita Williams](women-in-space), whose father was from Gujarat, has talked about taking samosas into orbit.

## Growing food in space

For long trips to Mars, carrying everything from Earth would be very heavy. So astronauts are learning to grow food. In August 2015, crew members on the [space station](space-station) ate red romaine lettuce they had grown on board in a small plant chamber called Veggie. They added a little oil and vinegar first.

Since then they have grown cabbage, mustard greens, radishes and chilli peppers. In 2021 they made [tacos](tacos) with the first [chillies](chillies) grown in orbit. Plants also give astronauts something green to look after, which many say makes the station feel more like home.

## Eating without gravity

Drinks come in sealed pouches with straws that can be clipped shut, because an open cup of water would wobble out as floating blobs. Sticky foods are popular because they stay on the spoon. Astronauts often eat together, strapping food packets to trays with [Velcro](velcro), and the shared meal is one of the best parts of the day.

Water for drinks and rehydrating food is mostly recycled, including water from the air and from urine. After cleaning, it is as clean as good drinking water on Earth, and astronauts joke that yesterday's coffee becomes today's coffee.
