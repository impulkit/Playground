## The short answer

Space itself has no smell, because it is nearly empty. A smell is made of tiny bits of a substance floating in air, and your nose catches them. Out in space there is almost no air and almost nothing to catch.

But astronauts keep noticing something odd. When they come back inside the [International Space Station](space-station) after a spacewalk and take off their helmets, their suits and tools give off a strong scent. They describe it as hot metal, seared steak or welding fumes. Nobody is fully sure why. Out in the rest of the universe, telescopes have found clouds and planets that, in theory, would smell of rum, raspberries and rotten eggs.

## The smell on the spacesuit

The station has an airlock: a small room where air is pumped out before a spacewalk and pumped back in afterwards. Once the air returns, the crew smell it. Former NASA astronaut Don Pettit wrote that it reminded him of a sweet, metallic smell, like welding. Others have said it is like the smell of a hot engine.

One popular idea is that the outside of a [spacesuit](spacesuits) picks up loose oxygen atoms in orbit. Up there, sunlight splits oxygen into single, very jumpy atoms. When they meet the cabin air, they may form [ozone](?ozone), which has a sharp, electric smell. Another idea is that surfaces simply get very hot in sunlight and very cold in shadow, which can make metals release odd smells. Both ideas are still guesses.

## Gunpowder on the Moon

The Apollo astronauts got a stronger whiff. Moon dust is fine and sticky, and it clung to their suits as they climbed back into the lunar lander. Gene Cernan, on Apollo 17 in 1972, said it smelled like spent gunpowder. Others on the crews agreed.

Moon dust is mostly glassy bits of broken rock. It has never been worn smooth by wind or water, so its grains are sharp, like crushed glass. When those fresh edges met the damp air of the cabin, they may have reacted and released a smell. Back on Earth, the samples no longer smell, because they have long since met our air.

## A galaxy that smells of rum

In 2009, astronomers using a radio telescope studied Sagittarius B2, a huge cloud of gas and dust near the middle of our [Milky Way](milky-way). They found ethyl formate there. On Earth, this chemical is part of what makes [raspberries](berries) taste like raspberries, and it smells a little like rum.

You would never get to sniff it. The cloud is about 26,000 light years away, and it also contains poisonous chemicals. Still, it is a strange thought: some of the same [molecules](?molecule) in your fruit bowl drift between the stars.

## Rotten eggs at the edge of the solar system

Not every space smell is nice. In 2018, scientists using the Gemini North telescope in Hawaii found hydrogen sulfide in the clouds of [Uranus](uranus-sideways). That is the gas that makes rotten eggs stink. A visitor would not have time to complain, though. Uranus is colder than minus 200 °C and its air is mostly hydrogen and helium.

Comets can be smelly too. The Rosetta spacecraft followed comet 67P for two years from 2014. Its sensors found hydrogen sulfide, ammonia and hydrogen cyanide around the [comet](comets). Scientists joked that 67P smelled of rotten eggs, a horse stable and bitter almonds.

## Bottling the smell for training

Astronauts in training have sometimes been given samples of a "space smell" made on Earth by a chemist who copies scents. The idea is to stop the real thing from surprising them up there. And the oddest part? Astronauts often find their food tastes dull in orbit, because floating body fluids stuff up their noses. Without a working [nose](nose-smell), even a spicy curry starts to taste like cardboard.
