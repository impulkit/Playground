## The short answer

Dark matter is the name scientists give to something they cannot see but whose pull they can measure. Galaxies spin too fast. Clusters of galaxies hold together too tightly. Light from distant galaxies bends too much as it passes by. In every case, there is far more [gravity](gravity-fall) than the visible stars and gas can explain.

So astronomers think the universe is full of an invisible kind of matter, about five times more than ordinary matter. It does not shine, reflect light or block it. It only pulls. What it is made of remains one of the biggest unsolved questions in science.

## The missing mass

In 1933, a Swiss astronomer in California, Fritz Zwicky, studied a huge group of galaxies called the Coma Cluster. The galaxies were moving so fast that the cluster should have flown apart long ago. Something unseen had to be holding it together. He called it dunkle Materie, German for dark matter.

Few people took him seriously at first. Zwicky was famous for wild ideas and a prickly temper, and it took about 40 years for the evidence to catch up.

## Spinning too fast

In the 1970s, the American astronomer Vera Rubin and her colleague Kent Ford measured how fast stars move around the centres of [galaxies](galaxies). Planets far from the Sun move slowly. Neptune crawls compared with Mercury. Rubin expected the same for stars at a galaxy's edge.

Instead, the outer stars raced around almost as fast as the inner ones. The only explanation was a huge halo of invisible matter surrounding each galaxy, many times heavier than the stars. Our own [Milky Way](milky-way) is wrapped in one too. Most astronomers think its gravity helped gather the gas that formed the galaxy in the first place. A giant new telescope in Chile, the Vera C. Rubin Observatory, released its first images in 2025 and is named in her honour.

## Bending light

[Einstein](einstein) showed that mass bends space, so light passing a heavy object curves, like a ball rolling past a dip in a trampoline. This is called gravitational lensing. Galaxy clusters act as giant lenses, stretching the light of galaxies behind them into arcs.

By measuring the bending, astronomers can map where the mass is. In a pair of colliding galaxy clusters known as the Bullet Cluster, the hot gas, which holds most of the ordinary matter, piled up in the middle. But the lensing showed most of the mass had passed straight through, on either side. Dark matter, it seems, hardly bumps into anything, not even itself.

## Hunting for it

Most physicists think dark matter is some kind of particle not yet discovered. If so, huge numbers of them may be passing through your body right now, without a trace. To catch one, scientists have built detectors filled with tonnes of liquid xenon, a heavy gas cooled into a liquid, deep underground, where rock blocks other particles. One sits about 1.5 km below the ground in an old gold mine in South Dakota, USA.

So far, none has found a clear signal, even after years of watching. Some scientists suspect the particles are even lighter or stranger than expected. A smaller group argues there is no dark matter at all, and that gravity behaves differently over enormous distances.

## The universe's recipe

Measurements from the European Planck space telescope suggest the universe is about 5% ordinary matter, about 27% dark matter, and about 68% something even stranger. That last part, called [dark energy](mystery-dark-energy), seems to be pushing the universe apart faster and faster. Put together, the planets, stars and everything you have ever touched are a small sprinkle on top of a universe we barely understand.
