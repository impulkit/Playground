## The short answer

Winds on Neptune have been clocked at more than 2,000 km/h. That is faster than the [speed of sound](sky-sonic-boom) on Earth, and about six times the top speed of Japan's [bullet trains](bullet-trains). No other planet's winds have been measured faster, though Saturn's come closest.

The strange part is that Neptune is the planet farthest from the Sun. It gets about 900 times less sunlight than Earth, and on Earth it is sunlight that drives the wind. Scientists think two things help. Neptune gives off more heat from deep inside than it receives from the Sun, which keeps its gases churning. And with no land or mountains to rub against, once the winds get going, very little slows them down.

## Found with a pencil

Neptune is the only planet found by maths before anyone saw it. In the early 1800s, astronomers noticed that [Uranus](uranus-sideways) kept drifting slightly off its predicted path. Something unseen seemed to be tugging it.

Using [Newton's laws of gravity](newton-life), the French mathematician Urbain Le Verrier calculated where a hidden planet should be. In England, John Couch Adams had been working on the same puzzle. On 23 September 1846, Johann Galle at the Berlin Observatory looked where Le Verrier said, and found Neptune within an hour. For years afterwards, British and French astronomers argued over whether Adams or Le Verrier deserved the credit. Today both are usually named.

[Galileo](galileo) had actually seen Neptune more than 200 years earlier, in 1612 and 1613. He drew it in his notes as a faint star.

## Storms as big as Earth

When [Voyager 2](voyager) flew past in August 1989, it photographed a dark oval storm about as wide as Earth. Scientists called it the Great Dark Spot. It looked a bit like Jupiter's Great Red Spot, which has lasted for centuries.

But when the [Hubble Space Telescope](hubble) looked in 1994, the Great Dark Spot had vanished. Since then Hubble has watched other dark storms appear and fade over a few years. Bright white clouds of frozen methane race around the planet, some of them circling it in about 16 hours.

## Faster than a hurricane

The strongest [hurricanes](hurricanes) and cyclones on Earth have winds of around 300 km/h. Neptune's fastest winds are roughly seven times stronger. They blow mostly in bands around the planet, some heading the opposite way to Neptune's spin.

Neptune is about four times as wide as Earth and is an [ice giant](space-weird-hot-ice), like Uranus. Below its clouds lies a deep, hot layer of water, ammonia and methane. Heat leaking out from that interior may be what keeps the weather so wild, even in the cold and dark of the outer solar system.

## A backwards moon

Neptune has at least 16 moons. The largest, Triton, travels around the planet in the opposite direction to Neptune's spin. No other big moon in the solar system does that.

The best explanation is that Triton did not form there. It was probably a small icy world, a bit like [Pluto](pluto), that wandered too close and was captured by Neptune's gravity. Voyager 2 spotted dark plumes on Triton, possibly [geysers of nitrogen gas](geysers-hot-springs), rising about 8 km above its frozen surface. Triton is one of the coldest places ever measured, at about −235°C.

Neptune's gravity is slowly dragging Triton closer. In a few billion years it may be pulled apart, and its pieces could spread out into a ring around the planet.
