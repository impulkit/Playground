## The short answer

A rocket [burns fuel](rocket-fuel) to make a huge amount of hot gas, then forces that gas out of a nozzle at the bottom at enormous speed. As the gas shoots down, the rocket is pushed up. This push is called [thrust](?thrust).

It works because of a rule discovered by [Isaac Newton](newton-life): for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. Stand on a skateboard and throw a heavy ball forward, and you roll backward. A rocket does the same thing, throwing out tonnes of gas every second.

Unlike a jet engine, a rocket carries its own oxygen, or another chemical that does the same job. That means it can keep burning fuel in space, where there is no air.

## Mostly fuel

Getting into orbit is hard. To circle Earth without falling back, a spacecraft needs to travel sideways at about 28,000 km/h. To reach that speed, a rocket needs vast amounts of fuel, so most of a rocket's weight on the launch pad is fuel. Often it is around 90 per cent.

To save weight, big rockets are built in stages, stacked on top of each other. When the bottom stage runs out of fuel, it drops away and the next stage fires. The rocket keeps getting lighter, so it can go faster. India's [LVM3](isro-launch), which carried [Chandrayaan-3](chandrayaan) towards the Moon in 2023, has three stages.

## From fire arrows to the Moon

Rockets are old. People in China were using gunpowder rockets by the 1200s, related to the [fireworks](fireworks) we still enjoy. In the late 1700s, the armies of Hyder Ali and [Tipu Sultan](tipu-sultan) of Mysore fired rockets with iron casings at British troops. The British studied captured ones and used them to design their own war rockets.

Modern rockets began with the American scientist Robert Goddard. On 16 March 1926, in a snowy field in Massachusetts, he launched the first rocket powered by liquid fuel. It flew for about 2.5 seconds and reached about 12 m high, roughly the height of a four-storey building. Less than 45 years later, a far bigger liquid-fuel rocket, the Saturn V, sent astronauts to the Moon.

## A church, a bicycle and a rocket

India's space story began in a fishing village. In the early 1960s, the scientist [Vikram Sarabhai](vikram-sarabhai) chose [Thumba](space-rocket-bicycle), near Thiruvananthapuram in [Kerala](kerala), as a launch site. It lies close to the magnetic equator, a good place to study the upper atmosphere.

{{story:vikram-sarabhai}}

A local church, St Mary Magdalene, was handed over for the project, and the bishop's house became an office. Rocket parts were carried to the pad on a bicycle and a bullock cart. On 21 November 1963, a small American-made Nike-Apache rocket rose from Thumba. Among the young engineers on the team was A. P. J. [Abdul Kalam](apj-kalam), who later led India's own rocket programme.

## Steering in space

A rocket on the launch pad is steered by swivelling its engine nozzles, a bit like pointing a hose. Once in space, spacecraft use small thrusters to turn and adjust their path.

Rockets are also getting [reusable](reusable-rockets). Since 2015, SpaceX has landed the first stages of its Falcon 9 rockets upright, then flown them again, sometimes more than 20 times. ISRO is testing a reusable winged vehicle called Pushpak. For most of history a rocket was used once and thrown away. That is starting to change.
