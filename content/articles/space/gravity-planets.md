## The short answer

Your weight depends on where you are standing. Weight is the pull of [gravity](?gravity) on your body, and different planets and moons pull with different strengths. On a small world with weak gravity, you would weigh much less. On a giant like Jupiter, you would weigh much more.

Your [mass](weights-mass), the amount of stuff you are made of, stays the same everywhere. A child who weighs 40 kilograms on Earth would still be the same child on the Moon, but a scale there would read only about 6.6 kilograms. Strictly speaking, scientists measure weight in newtons, a unit of force, but bathroom scales show kilograms, so this article uses those.

## What sets a planet's pull

Two things decide how hard a planet pulls on you at its surface. One is its mass: more mass means stronger gravity. The other is its size: the farther you are from the planet's centre, the weaker the pull.

That's why some results surprise people. Saturn has 95 times Earth's mass, but it is so wide that at its cloud tops you'd weigh only about 7% more than at home. Mars has about twice Mercury's mass, yet Mercury's heavy iron core gives it the same surface pull, about 38% of Earth's.

## A trip around the solar system

Here is what a 40 kg child would weigh, using surface gravity numbers from NASA. The giant planets have no solid ground, so their numbers are for the cloud tops.

- **Moon:** about 6.6 kg
- **Mercury and Mars:** about 15 kg
- **Venus:** about 36 kg
- **Uranus:** about 35 kg
- **Saturn:** about 43 kg
- **Neptune:** about 45 kg
- **Jupiter:** about 101 kg
- **Pluto:** about 2.5 kg

To work out your own weight, multiply your Earth weight by 0.17 for the Moon, 0.38 for Mars or 2.5 for [Jupiter](jupiter-giant).

The extremes are even stranger. If you could somehow stand on the Sun's glowing surface, that 40 kg child would weigh more than a tonne, about as much as a small car. On a tiny asteroid only a few hundred metres across, the pull is so weak that a hard jump could launch you off into space for good.

## Bouncing on the Moon

Weaker gravity changes how you move. On the Moon, a jump that lifts you 30 centimetres on Earth would carry you about 1.8 metres high, and you'd float down slowly. [Apollo](apollo-moon) astronauts found that walking normally felt awkward, so many of them bounced along like kangaroos.

Heavy spacesuits that would crush you on Earth felt light on the Moon. The Apollo suits with their backpacks weighed around 80 kilograms on Earth, but only about 13 on the Moon. On Jupiter, by contrast, even standing up would feel like carrying another person on your back.

## Gravity and your body

Our bodies are built for Earth's pull. Bones and muscles stay strong because they work against gravity every day. Astronauts who spend months on the space station, where they float, lose bone and muscle unless they exercise for about two hours a day. Doctors study what happens to [astronauts' bones](space-body) to plan longer trips.

Mars's gravity is about 38% of Earth's. Nobody yet knows whether that is enough to keep a human body healthy over years. Scientists planning future [Mars bases](mars-colonies) are trying to find out.

## The idea behind it all

In 1687, Isaac [Newton](newton-life) published his law of gravity, which says every object pulls on every other, with a strength that depends on their masses and the distance between them. The same rule explains why an apple falls, why the Moon orbits Earth and why you'd weigh more on Jupiter. You can test it, in a small way, every time you step on a scale.
