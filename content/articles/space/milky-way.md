## The short answer

The Milky Way is our galaxy: a huge, flat, spinning disc of stars, gas and dust, held together by gravity. The Sun is one of somewhere between 100 billion and 400 billion stars in it.

On a dark night, away from city lights, you can see part of the disc as a faint, glowing band across the sky. That band is the light of countless distant stars, too far away to see one by one. Ancient Greeks thought it looked like spilled milk. In [Sanskrit](sanskrit) and Hindi it is called Akash Ganga, the river of the sky.

## Our place in the disc

Seen from outside, the Milky Way would look like a giant pinwheel, with a bright bulge in the middle and curving arms of stars. A straight bar of stars crosses the centre, which is why astronomers call it a barred spiral.

The galaxy is about 100,000 [light-years](light-years) across. Light, the fastest thing there is, would take 100,000 years to cross it. If the whole Milky Way were shrunk to the width of India, the paths of [all eight planets](planets-order) around the Sun would fit inside a bottle cap, about 3 cm wide.

We live in a quieter area about 26,000 light-years from the centre. When you look at the Milky Way's band in the sky, you are looking along the flat disc from the inside.

That makes mapping it hard, like trying to draw a map of a forest while standing among the trees. Thick dust hides much of the disc. Astronomers use radio [telescopes](telescopes-how) and infrared light, which pass through dust, to trace the spiral arms. The European Space Agency's Gaia spacecraft, launched in 2013, measured the positions of more than a billion stars to build the best map yet.

## A giant in the middle

At the very centre sits a supermassive [black hole](black-holes) called Sagittarius A*. It has about 4 million times the mass of the Sun. Two teams, led by Reinhard Genzel in Germany and Andrea Ghez in the USA, tracked stars whipping around it for decades. Their work won the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics.

In 2022, the Event Horizon Telescope, a team using radio dishes across the world as one giant telescope, released the first picture of the glowing ring around it.

## Seeing it for yourself

In 1610, [Galileo](galileo) pointed one of the first telescopes at the Milky Way. He discovered it was made of huge numbers of faint stars packed together. Before that, nobody knew what the glow was.

Today most people can't see it at all. Streetlights and buildings light up the sky so much that the faint band disappears. In 2022, India set up its first [dark-sky reserve](stargazing) at Hanle in [Ladakh](ladakh), high in the mountains, where the Milky Way still stretches clearly from one horizon to the other.

## Neighbours and the future

The Milky Way is not alone. Two small galaxies, the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds, can be seen from the southern half of the world. The nearest big spiral galaxy, Andromeda, is about 2.5 million light-years away and moving towards us.

Astronomers have long expected the two to [collide](galaxy-collisions) in about 4 to 5 billion years. Newer studies suggest it is far from certain. Even if they do meet, the stars are so far apart that they would almost never hit each other.
