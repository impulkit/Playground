## The short answer

On the International [Space Station](space-station), everything floats, because the station and everyone inside are falling around Earth together.

Astronauts [strap themselves in to sleep](space-sleep) and use special toilets that work with air suction.

Exercise keeps [bones](bones-growth) and [muscles](muscles) strong, because nothing pushes against them in orbit.

Living like this takes practice. Every task, from cutting hair to washing hands, has to be redesigned so that nothing drifts away. Most astronauts say the first few days are confusing, and that after a week or two floating feels natural and even fun.

## Sleeping

Each astronaut has a small private cabin about the size of a phone booth, with a laptop, a few photos and a sleeping bag tied to the wall. In space there is no up or down, so it doesn't matter which way the bag faces. Arms tend to float up in front of the body while asleep, unless tucked inside.

Because the station goes round Earth every 90 minutes, the Sun rises and sets about 16 times a day. Astronauts keep to a fixed schedule set to the time in London, with about eight hours set aside for [sleep](sleep-why). Many wear eye masks and earplugs, because fans hum all the time. They have to: in still air, the carbon dioxide you breathe out can collect in a bubble around your head.

## Washing and the toilet

There are no showers. Astronauts wash with damp towels and use special shampoo that doesn't need rinsing. They brush their teeth normally, then swallow the toothpaste or spit into a towel.

The toilet uses fans to suck waste away, because nothing falls. Urine is collected and cleaned, along with sweat and moisture from the air, into fresh drinking water. In 2023 the station's system reached about 98 per cent water recovery. Dirty clothes can't be washed, so they are worn for days, then packed into a cargo ship that burns up when it falls back into the atmosphere.

## Staying fit

Without gravity, the body stops working hard to hold itself up. Muscles shrink, and bones can lose roughly 1 per cent of their strength each month in places like the hips and spine. Fluid shifts up towards the head, making faces puffy and legs thin.

So astronauts [exercise](exercise) for about two hours every day. They run on a treadmill held down by elastic straps, pedal an exercise bike with no seat, and lift "weights" on a machine that uses vacuum cylinders to push back. Even so, after months in orbit, many need help walking when they first return to Earth.

## Work and fun

A typical day is packed: experiments, repairs, cleaning, exercise and calls with mission control. Weekends are lighter. Astronauts often gather at the window called the Cupola to [photograph Earth](earth-from-space), and they can phone and video-call their families.

They also have fun. They play with floating water blobs, do somersaults in mid-air and share meals together, often trading favourite foods from their home countries.

## Indians in orbit

[Rakesh Sharma](indian-astronauts) flew in 1984, spending nearly eight days on the Soviet space station Salyut 7. In 2025, Shubhanshu Shukla became the first Indian on the International Space Station, carrying out experiments that included growing seeds and studying muscle loss. India now plans to send astronauts on its own spacecraft through its [Gaganyaan](gaganyaan) programme. Like [Chandrayaan](chandrayaan), it is part of a long string of Indian firsts in space.
