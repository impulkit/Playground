## The short answer

The stars are so far away that kilometres become useless for measuring them. So astronomers use the [light-year](?lightyear): the distance light travels in one year.

Light is the fastest thing there is. It covers about 3,00,000 km every second, enough to go around Earth more than seven times. In a year, it travels about 9.5 trillion km. The nearest star after the Sun, Proxima Centauri, is about 4.2 light-years away. That is roughly 40 trillion km. A car driving nonstop at 100 km/h [would need about 45 million years](maths-speed-time) to get there.

## Seeing the past

Because light takes time to travel, you never see anything exactly as it is now. Moonlight is just over a second old when it reaches you. Sunlight is [about 8 minutes and 20 seconds old](what-if-sun-vanished).

Starlight is far older. The bright star Sirius is about 8.6 light-years away, so its light left before many readers of this article were born. Some of the stars you can see on a dark night are hundreds or even thousands of light-years away. When you look at the most distant of them, you are looking at light that set out before the [Taj Mahal](taj-mahal) was built.

## How do we know?

People once argued about whether light took any time at all to travel. In 1676, the Danish astronomer Ole Rømer noticed that Jupiter's moon Io slipped behind Jupiter a few minutes late whenever Earth was farther from Jupiter. The light was taking longer to reach us. From this he made one of the first estimates of its speed.

Measuring the distance to a star was even harder. The trick that finally worked is called parallax. Hold up a finger and look at it with one eye, then the other. It seems to jump against the background.

Earth makes the same jump on a giant scale as it goes around the Sun. Astronomers photograph a star in January and again in July, from opposite sides of Earth's orbit. Nearby stars seem to shift very slightly against faraway ones. Measuring that tiny shift gives the distance. In 1838, the German astronomer Friedrich Bessel became the first to do it, for a star called 61 Cygni, about 11 light-years away.

## A billion stars mapped

Today, the European Space Agency's Gaia spacecraft has measured the positions of more than a billion stars, again and again, from 1.5 million km away. From those tiny shifts, scientists have built the most detailed 3D map of our galaxy ever made.

For stars too far away for parallax, astronomers use other clues, such as how bright a certain kind of star really is compared with how bright it looks. Each method checks the next, like rungs on a ladder.

## The size of everything

Our galaxy, the [Milky Way](milky-way), is about 1,00,000 light-years across. The Andromeda Galaxy, about 2.5 million light-years away, is the farthest thing most people can see without a telescope, from a really dark place.

The [Webb telescope](webb-telescope) catches light from [galaxies](galaxies) that left them more than 13 billion years ago, when the universe was young. Even our fastest spacecraft are slow by comparison. Voyager 1 would need more than 70,000 years to travel as far as Proxima Centauri.
