## The short answer

Most scientists think the Moon was born in a giant crash. About 4.5 billion years ago, when Earth was young, a planet about the size of Mars slammed into it. Scientists have nicknamed this lost planet Theia.

The crash melted huge amounts of rock and flung it into space. That debris formed a ring around Earth. Over time, most of it clumped together into one big ball: the Moon. The biggest clue is in Moon rocks, which match Earth's rocks in some surprising ways.

## Older ideas

People have wondered about the Moon's origin for a long time. In the late 1800s, George Darwin, son of the naturalist Charles Darwin, suggested that young Earth spun so fast that a blob flew off and became the Moon. Others thought Earth's [gravity](?gravity) captured a passing Moon, or that the two formed side by side.

Each idea had problems. A spinning Earth could not have thrown off such a large Moon. A passing body would probably have zoomed past, not been caught. The answer needed better evidence.

## What the astronauts brought back

Between 1969 and 1972, the [Apollo astronauts](apollo-moon) brought about 382 kg of Moon rock and soil back to Earth. Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin collected the very first samples in July 1969. Scientists studied every crumb closely, and found three big clues.

First, the Moon's rocks have the same chemical fingerprints as Earth's, especially in the kinds of oxygen [atoms](?atom) they contain. Second, the Moon has very little water and very little iron, with only a small iron [core](?core). Third, its [oldest rocks](rocks-oldest) show that its surface was once covered in a deep ocean of molten rock.

In the 1970s, scientists put these clues together. A giant crash could explain them all. It would throw out mostly the rocky outer layers of Earth and Theia, not their iron centres, and the heat would melt everything.

## A puzzle still open

The crash idea has one big problem. Computer models suggest that most of the Moon should be made of Theia, not Earth. Yet Moon rocks match Earth's closely. Maybe Theia happened to be made of very similar stuff. Maybe the crash was so violent that the two planets' rock mixed completely in a giant spinning cloud. Scientists are still testing these ideas, which is why the details remain uncertain.

## Drifting away

The Moon is slowly moving away from us, about 3.8 cm every year. We know this very precisely because Apollo astronauts [left mirrors on the Moon](moon-landing-sites). Scientists fire [lasers](lasers) at them and time how long the light takes to bounce back.

Long ago, the Moon was much closer and would have looked much bigger in the sky. Earth also spun faster. Fossil corals about 400 million years old have daily growth lines that suggest a year then had about 400 days, each roughly 22 hours long.

## Why it matters to us

The Moon steadies the tilt of Earth's spin, which helps keep our [seasons](seasons-why) fairly regular over long periods. Its pull [raises the tides](moon-tides-life). Many animals use moonlight as a clock. On the [Great Barrier Reef](great-barrier-reef), for example, corals release their eggs all together on nights a few days after a full moon. Missions like India's [Chandrayaan-3](chandrayaan) are still studying its rocks, and every new sample adds a clue to the story of its birth.
