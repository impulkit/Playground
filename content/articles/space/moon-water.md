## The short answer

Yes, there is water on the Moon, but you couldn't swim in it. It comes in two main forms. Near the poles, ice is hidden in deep craters that never see sunlight. Across much of the surface, tiny amounts of water are trapped inside the dusty soil and rocks.

Scientists confirmed this in 2009. India's [Chandrayaan-1](chandrayaan) spacecraft carried a NASA instrument called the Moon Mineralogy Mapper, which spotted the chemical signature of water and related molecules spread over the Moon's surface. The same year, a NASA probe called LCROSS crashed into a dark crater near the south pole and found water in the cloud of dust it kicked up.

How much ice there is, and where it came from, are still big questions.

## The dry Moon that wasn't

When the Apollo astronauts brought back [Moon rocks](apollo-moon) between 1969 and 1972, scientists tested them and found almost no water. Many concluded that the Moon was completely dry.

New tools changed that. In 2008, scientists re-examined tiny beads of volcanic glass from Apollo samples and found traces of water inside them. Then came Chandrayaan-1. Its Moon Impact Probe and NASA's mapper both picked up signs of water in 2008 and 2009.

In 2020, NASA's SOFIA, a telescope flying inside a jumbo jet, found water molecules even in a sunlit crater. The amount was tiny, roughly a small bottle's worth spread through a cubic metre of soil. The Sahara Desert holds far more.

## Ice in the dark

The Moon's axis is tilted only slightly, so near its poles, the Sun always sits low on the horizon. Some craters there are so deep that their floors have stayed in shadow for billions of years. NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter measured temperatures in some of them below −240 °C. That is colder than the surface of [Pluto](pluto), and among the coldest places known in the solar system.

Any water that drifts into these craters freezes and stays. Instruments suggest ice is mixed into the soil there, but nobody knows yet whether it forms thick layers or is scattered thinly.

## Where did it come from?

There are several ideas, and the answer may be all of them. [Comets](comets) and some asteroids carry ice, and crashes could have delivered it. The [solar wind](solar-wind) brings hydrogen from the Sun, which can join with oxygen in Moon rock to make tiny amounts of water. Ancient volcanoes on the Moon may also have released water from inside.

Each source leaves a slightly different chemical fingerprint, so scientists hope future samples will settle the question.

## Why astronauts want it

Water is heavy, and launching it from Earth is very expensive. If future astronauts can dig up ice on the Moon, they can melt it to drink and use it to grow food. Splitting water gives oxygen to breathe. Hydrogen and oxygen are also the ingredients of powerful [rocket](rockets-how) [fuel](rocket-fuel).

That is why the Moon's south pole has become a popular target. In 2023, India's Chandrayaan-3 landed closer to the south pole than any spacecraft before it. In 2024, a US lander called Odysseus touched down even closer. NASA's Artemis programme plans to send astronauts to the same region, and other countries are planning missions there too. The ice in those dark craters may one day work like a petrol pump for spacecraft heading further into the solar system.
