## The short answer

Start with your own eyes. Find a place away from bright lights, such as a rooftop, a garden or a field, and go with an adult. Switch off your phone screen and wait. It takes about 20 to 30 minutes for your eyes to fully adjust to the dark, and the longer you wait, the more stars appear.

Learn a few bright patterns first, like Orion in winter or the Saptarishi, which Americans call the Big Dipper. Use them as signposts to find other stars. A free sky map or app can help you name what you see.

Once you know your way around, a pair of [binoculars](telescopes-make) will show you craters on the Moon, the moons of Jupiter and clusters of stars invisible to the naked eye.

## Let your eyes adjust

Your [eyes](eyes-see) have two kinds of light-sensing cells. One kind sees colours and works in bright light. The other works in dim light but sees only shades of grey. When you step into the dark, the dim-light cells slowly become more sensitive.

A single glance at a bright phone screen resets them. That is why many stargazers use a torch covered in red cellophane. Red light disturbs night vision the least.

Here is a trick astronomers use: to see a faint star, look slightly to one side of it. The edge of your vision is better at picking up dim light than the centre.

## Finding your way around

The sky looks like a jumble at first, but a few shapes help. In the winter months, Orion stands out, with three stars in a row for his belt. Follow the belt down to the left and you reach Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky.

In the northern sky, the seven stars of the Saptarishi, named after seven sages in Indian tradition, form a ladle shape. The two stars at the end of the bowl point to Polaris, the [North Star](north-star). In summer evenings, look south for Scorpius, with its red heart, Antares.

Planets wander slowly among the stars over weeks. They usually shine steadily instead of twinkling.

## Things that move

Not everything up there sits still. Watch for a bright, steady dot gliding across the sky with no flashing lights. It may be the [International Space Station](space-station), which circles Earth about every 90 minutes. NASA's Spot the Station website tells you when it will pass over your town.

Meteor showers are great for beginners because you need no equipment at all. The Perseids peak around 12 August and the Geminids around 14 December. Lie back, look up and be patient. On a dark night, you might see dozens of [shooting stars](shooting-stars) in an hour.

## The problem of light

Light from streets, buildings and signs scatters in the air and drowns out faint stars. A 2016 study found that the Milky Way is hidden from more than a third of humanity, including about 80% of North Americans.

Some places are fighting back. In 2022, the area around the Indian Astronomical Observatory at Hanle, in [Ladakh](ladakh), was declared India's first dark sky reserve. Villagers there have been trained as astronomy guides, and homes use shielded lights that point downwards.

Every big step up in stargazing comes from collecting more light. Binoculars and telescopes use [lenses](lenses-glasses) or mirrors to gather far more light than your eye can, showing things the ancient sky-watchers never saw. But the first step is the same as it was thousands of years ago: go outside and look up.
