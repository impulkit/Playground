## The short answer

Satellites are machines that circle Earth, high above the air. More than 10,000 of them are working right now. They do four big jobs. Weather satellites watch clouds, storms and oceans. Communication satellites carry TV, radio and some phone and internet signals. Navigation satellites, like [GPS](gps-how), help phones, ships and planes work out where they are. And Earth-watching satellites photograph forests, farms, cities and ice.

You may not notice them, but you probably used a satellite today. If you checked the weather, followed a map, watched live sport or paid by card, a satellite was likely somewhere in the chain.

## Watching the weather

Before satellites, forecasters could only see weather where there were people to report it. Huge storms could form over the ocean unseen. In 1960 the US launched TIROS-1, the first weather satellite, and its first fuzzy picture of clouds changed forecasting for good.

Today India's INSAT satellites and America's GOES satellites send fresh images every 15 minutes or less. They help forecasters track the [monsoon](monsoon) and spot [cyclones](cyclones-india) days before they reach land.

In October 2013, Cyclone Phailin, one of the strongest storms to hit India in years, struck [Odisha](odisha). Thanks to satellite tracking and good forecasts, authorities moved close to a million people to safety, and far fewer lives were lost than in a similar cyclone in 1999.

## Hanging still in the sky

In 1945, the science fiction writer Arthur C. Clarke pointed out something useful. A satellite about 36,000 km above the equator takes exactly one day to circle Earth. Because Earth turns at the same rate, the satellite seems to stay in one spot.

This is called a geostationary orbit. It is why the TV dish on a roof can point at one place and never move. Communication and weather satellites often sit there, each able to see a huge part of the globe at once.

Most other satellites fly much lower, a few hundred to a few thousand kilometres up, and race around Earth in about 90 minutes to two hours. Lower satellites can take sharper pictures. Signals to and from them also arrive with much less delay, which is why new internet satellites fly low.

## Television in the villages

In 1975, India borrowed time on an American satellite for a year-long experiment called SITE, the Satellite Instructional [Television](television-invented) Experiment. Community TV sets were placed in about 2,400 villages across six states. Every day they showed programmes on farming, health, hygiene and science, many in local languages.

For many villagers it was the first television they had ever seen. The experiment showed that satellites could reach places that cables never would, and it helped persuade India to build its own INSAT satellites. [India's satellites](satellites-india) now support everything from TV to fishing.

## Eyes on Earth

Earth-watching satellites have photographed the planet since the US launched Landsat 1 in 1972. They show farmers how healthy crops are, help track [forest fires](forest-fires) and floods, measure melting [glaciers](glaciers) and [rising seas](sea-level-rise), and help draw accurate maps. ISRO's satellites help India estimate harvests and plan where to dig wells.

Fishing boats along India's coast get messages, based on satellite readings of sea temperature and plankton, about where fish are likely to gather. That saves fuel and time.

## Keeping time

Many satellites carry extremely precise clocks. Phone networks, power grids and banks use satellite time signals to keep their computers in step. When you tap a card to pay, a satellite clock may help stamp the exact time of the payment.

Not everything goes through space, though. Most of the world's internet traffic travels through cables, many of them on the sea floor, as you can read in [how the internet works](internet-how).
