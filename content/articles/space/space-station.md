## The short answer

The International Space Station, or ISS, is a laboratory and home that circles Earth about 400 km up. Astronauts from many countries live on board, usually six or seven at a time, for about six months each. They run experiments, look after the station and study what living in space does to the human body.

The station is big. From end to end it is about the length of an American football field, and it has as much living space as a large six-bedroom house. On a clear night it is easy to see from the ground. It looks like a bright, steady star gliding across the sky in a few minutes, with no blinking lights.

## Built piece by piece

Nobody could launch something this size in one go. The first part, a Russian module called Zarya, went up on 20 November 1998. A US module followed two weeks later. Over the next 13 years, dozens of rocket and Space Shuttle flights carried up new pieces, and astronauts bolted them together on spacewalks.

The first crew moved in on 2 November 2000. Since that day there has never been a moment without people living in space.

The station was built by the space agencies of the United States, Russia, Europe, Japan and Canada. During the [Cold War](cold-war), the US and the Soviet Union had raced each other into space. A few years after it ended, their astronauts and cosmonauts were sharing meals in the same orbiting home.

## Always falling

The ISS travels at about 28,000 km/h. That is fast enough to go from Mumbai to Delhi in under three minutes. It goes around Earth every 90 minutes, so the crew sees about 16 sunrises and 16 sunsets every day.

Everything on board floats, but not because gravity has disappeared. At 400 km up, gravity is still about 90 per cent as strong as on the ground. The station and everyone inside are falling towards Earth all the time. They are also moving sideways so quickly that the ground keeps curving away beneath them. Scientists call this free fall, or [microgravity](?microgravity).

## What happens up there

The ISS is above all a science lab. Astronauts grow plants, study how flames, fluids and crystals behave without weight, and test new equipment. They also act as test subjects themselves. Months of floating weaken [muscles](muscles) and bones and change the eyes and heart, so doctors on the ground study the crew closely. You can read more about [how astronauts live](astronaut-life) and [what they eat](space-food).

Food, fuel and equipment arrive on robotic cargo spacecraft. Most of the water is recycled, including sweat and [urine](space-toilet), which is cleaned into drinking water.

## Unexpected guests

In June 2024, NASA astronauts Sunita Williams and Butch Wilmore flew to the station on Boeing's new Starliner spacecraft for a visit planned to last about eight days. The capsule had problems with its thrusters, so NASA sent it home empty. The two astronauts stayed on and joined the station crew, returning in March 2025, about nine months later.

In June 2025, Shubhanshu Shukla became [the first Indian astronaut](indian-astronauts) to visit the ISS, on a private mission called Axiom-4. He spent about 18 days on board, running experiments including some designed by Indian scientists.

## The end of the station

The ISS is getting old. The partners plan to keep it running until about 2030. After that, a special spacecraft will push it down into a remote part of the Pacific Ocean, where what doesn't burn up will fall into the sea. Private companies are designing [new stations to replace it](space-stations-future). China already has its own, called Tiangong, and India plans the Bharatiya Antariksh Station, to be completed around 2035.
