## The short answer

The Moon doesn't change shape. It is always a round ball, and the Sun always lights up half of it, just as it lights up half of Earth.

As the Moon travels around Earth, we see different amounts of that lit half. When the Moon is on the far side of Earth from the Sun, we see the whole lit face: a full moon, or Purnima. When it is roughly between us and the Sun, we see only the dark side: a new moon, or Amavasya. From one new moon to the next takes about 29.5 days.

## Growing and shrinking

After the new moon, a thin crescent appears in the west just after sunset. Each evening it grows fatter. This is the waxing Moon, called *Shukla paksha*, the bright fortnight, in [Indian calendars](indian-calendars). After about a week it is half lit, a shape astronomers confusingly call a first quarter, because the Moon is a quarter of the way through its cycle. This is the best time to look at it through [binoculars](stargazing). Sunlight strikes the Moon from the side, so craters and mountains near the edge of the lit part cast long shadows.

After the full moon it shrinks again, rising later and later each night. This is the waning Moon, *Krishna paksha*, the dark fortnight. Late in the cycle, you are more likely to spot it in the morning sky than the evening.

## The old moon in the new moon's arms

Look carefully at a thin crescent on a clear evening. You can often see the rest of the Moon too, glowing faintly grey. That glow is called earthshine. Sunlight bounces off Earth onto the Moon's dark part, and then back to your eyes.

About 500 years ago, [Leonardo da Vinci](leonardo) wrote down the right explanation in one of his notebooks. An old Scottish ballad describes it as the old moon held in the new moon's arms.

## Calendars and festivals

People have tracked the Moon for thousands of years, because its phases make an easy clock in the sky. The word "month" comes from "moon".

Many Indian [calendars](calendars) are lunar or partly lunar. Diwali falls on an Amavasya in October or November, which is why so many [lamps are lit](diwali-lights) on such a dark night. Holi falls on the Purnima of Phalgun. The Islamic calendar follows the Moon completely, so Eid begins when the new crescent is sighted, and it moves earlier by about 11 days each year. In the USA and Europe, the date of Easter is set by the first full moon of spring.

## One face, always

The Moon spins once on its axis in exactly the time it takes to go around Earth. So it always keeps the same face towards us. Nobody saw the [other side](moon-far-side) until the Soviet spacecraft Luna 3 photographed it in 1959. In 2019, China's Chang'e 4 became the first spacecraft to land there.

The far side is not permanently dark. It gets sunlight too. During a new moon, when our side is dark, the far side is in full daylight.

## Moon and sea

The Moon's [gravity](?gravity) also tugs on Earth's oceans. [The biggest tides](moon-tides-life) come at full moon and new moon, when the Sun and Moon pull along the same line.
