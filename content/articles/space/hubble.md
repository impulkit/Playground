## The short answer

The Hubble Space Telescope has helped show us how big, old and busy the universe is. It has photographed galaxies so far away that their light left them more than 13 billion years ago. It helped measure the age of the universe at about 13.8 billion years. And it helped show that the universe is growing faster and faster.

Hubble was launched in 1990 by NASA, working with the European Space Agency. It is still working more than 35 years later, which makes it one of the most successful science instruments ever built.

## A blurry start

Soon after launch, scientists realised something was badly wrong. Hubble's pictures were fuzzy. Its main mirror had been ground to slightly the wrong shape. The error was about 2 micrometres at the edges, around one-fiftieth the width of a human hair, but it was enough to spoil the focus.

The fix was a bit like giving Hubble [glasses](lenses-glasses). In December 1993, astronauts on the Space Shuttle Endeavour caught the telescope, installed a new camera and a set of small corrective mirrors, and released it again. The first sharp pictures were a huge relief. Astronauts returned four more times, the last in 2009, to fix parts and add better instruments.

## The deep field

In December 1995, astronomers pointed Hubble at a tiny, empty-looking patch of sky, about as big as a tennis ball seen from 100 m away. They collected light for ten days.

The result, the Hubble Deep Field, showed about 3,000 [galaxies](galaxies) in that speck alone. Each contains billions of stars. Later deep fields found even more. Since the sky is full of patches like that, astronomers now estimate there are hundreds of billions of galaxies in the part of the universe we can see.

## Big discoveries

Hubble helped show that almost every big galaxy has a giant [black hole](black-holes) at its centre, millions or billions of times heavier than the Sun.

In 1998, two teams studying exploding stars in distant galaxies found that the universe's growth is [speeding up](mystery-dark-energy). Hubble's measurements were part of the evidence. The leaders of those teams won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2011, and the mysterious push behind it is called dark energy.

In July 1994, Hubble watched pieces of a broken comet named [Shoemaker-Levy 9](comets-famous) slam into [Jupiter](jupiter-giant), leaving dark scars bigger than Earth in the planet's clouds.

## How the pictures are made

Hubble doesn't take colour photos the way a phone does. Its [cameras](cameras-how) record light through one coloured filter at a time, as black-and-white images. Scientists then combine them, giving each a colour. Sometimes they include light our eyes can't see, such as ultraviolet, and show it in a visible colour so we can study it.

The colours are chosen carefully to show real differences, such as which gases are glowing. The famous "Pillars of Creation", towers of gas and dust where new stars are forming, were made this way in 1995.

## Hubble and Webb

Hubble's orbit is slowly sinking as thin traces of air drag on it. NASA expects it to keep working into the 2030s. The [James Webb Space Telescope](webb-telescope), launched in 2021, has a bigger mirror and sees mainly infrared light. The two often look at the same objects and compare notes. Webb sees farther back in time. Hubble sees more of the visible and ultraviolet colours that Webb can't.
