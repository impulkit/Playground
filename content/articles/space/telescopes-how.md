## The short answer

A telescope is a light bucket. Its big [lens](magnifying-glass) or curved mirror collects light from a distant object over a much larger area than your eye can. It then brings that light together into a sharp image, which you can look at through a small eyepiece lens or record with a camera.

The pupil of your eye is at most about 7 mm wide in the dark. Even a small [backyard telescope](telescopes-make) with a 100 mm lens collects about 200 times more light. That is why a telescope shows thousands of stars where your eyes see a few, and turns smudges in the sky into [galaxies](galaxies).

## Lenses: the first telescopes

The first known telescopes were made in the Netherlands around 1608. Spectacle makers found that two [lenses](lenses-glasses) held in a line made faraway things look closer. In 1609 [Galileo](galileo) heard about the idea and built his own. He pointed it at the sky and saw mountains on the Moon, moons around Jupiter and countless stars in the Milky Way.

A telescope that uses lenses is called a refractor, because the glass refracts, or bends, the light. The big front lens bends light to meet at a point. A smaller lens near your eye then spreads out that image so it fills your view.

The largest working refractor is at Yerkes Observatory in Wisconsin, USA, with a lens just over 1 m wide, finished in 1897. Lenses bigger than that sag under their own weight, because they can only be held at the edges.

## Mirrors: bigger and better

In 1668 [Isaac Newton](newton-life) built a different kind of telescope, using a curved mirror instead of a front lens. A mirror shaped like a shallow bowl reflects light to a point, just as a lens bends it. These are called reflectors.

Mirrors have big advantages. Light bounces off the front surface, so the glass behind it doesn't have to be perfectly clear. A mirror can be supported from behind, so it can be made much larger. Nearly every big research telescope today is a reflector.

India's largest is the Devasthal Optical Telescope in the hills of Uttarakhand, with a mirror 3.6 m across, working since 2016. In Chile, astronomers are building the Extremely Large Telescope, whose main mirror, 39 m wide, is made of 798 hexagonal pieces. That is nearly twice the length of a cricket pitch, or about three school buses parked end to end.

## Seeing more than light

Not all telescopes look at the light our eyes can see. Radio telescopes use giant metal dishes to collect [radio waves](radio-waves) from space. Near [Pune](pune), India's Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope uses 30 dishes, each 45 m across, spread out over about 25 km. Working together, they act like one enormous telescope.

Other telescopes sense infrared, [X-rays](x-rays) or ultraviolet light. Many of those signals are blocked by Earth's air, so the telescopes have to go to space. [Hubble](hubble) and the [James Webb Space Telescope](webb-telescope) are the most famous.

## Looking back in time

Light takes time to travel. When a telescope catches light from a galaxy millions of light-years away, that light set off millions of years ago. So the farther a telescope looks, the further back in time it sees.

That makes every big telescope a kind of time machine. The bigger the mirror, the fainter and older the light it can catch.
