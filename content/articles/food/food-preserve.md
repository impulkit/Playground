## The short answer

Food goes bad mainly because tiny [microbes](?microbe), such as bacteria and moulds, grow on it. To grow, they need water, warmth and time. So every old way of keeping food fresh takes away one of those things.

Drying removes water. Salt and sugar soak water up, leaving microbes thirsty. Pickling in acid or oil makes food a hostile place to live. Smoke coats food with chemicals that slow germs down. And cold, from cellars, snow or carried ice, slows everything. People worked all of this out by trial and error, thousands of years before anyone saw a germ.

## Sun, salt and smoke

Drying is probably the oldest trick. Grapes become raisins, and fish and [mango slices](mango-varieties) shrivel in the sun. In Indian homes, summer is still the season for drying [papad](papad), vadi and sabudana chips on rooftops.

Salt goes further. Salted fish fed sailors on long voyages, and salted meat filled the barrels of armies. Native peoples of North America made pemmican, a mix of dried meat and fat that kept for months on long journeys. Smoking, often done alongside salting, gave hams and fish a flavour people came to love for its own sake.

## Pickles, jams and murabbas

A jar of Indian [achar](pickles-achar) is a small fortress. Salt, oil, [mustard](mustard) seeds and [chillies](chillies) make it very hard for spoiling microbes to survive, and some pickles are also soured by friendly bacteria through [fermentation](fermentation).

Sugar works in a similar way to [salt](salt). In a thick jam or a murabba of amla, there is so much sugar that microbes cannot pull water out of it. Vinegar pickles, popular in Europe and the United States, use acid instead.

## Cool tricks from hot countries

India had its own cooling methods. Water kept in an unglazed clay [matka](evaporation-cooling) stays cool because a little seeps through the walls and evaporates, carrying heat away. Curd and butter were often hung in pots in the shade or lowered into wells, where the air is cooler.

In some parts of north India and Persia, people even made ice in winter. They left water in shallow pits on clear, cold nights, and by dawn a thin skin of ice had formed. It was collected and stored in deep, insulated pits. Mughal emperors also had ice carried down from the mountains for cool drinks.

## Ice from across the ocean

Cold has always been the gentlest way to keep food. In winter, people in New England cut huge blocks of ice from frozen lakes and stored them in sawdust in [ice houses](frozen-food).

In 1833 a [Boston](boston) businessman named Frederic Tudor sent a shipload of this ice all the way to [Kolkata](kolkata), then called Calcutta. The voyage took about four months. Much of the ice survived the trip, and for years afterwards American ice was sold in the city.

## Sealed in a jar

Around 1810 a French cook named Nicolas Appert found that food sealed in glass jars and then boiled could last for a very long time. The French government, keen to feed its armies, gave him a prize. Soon others were sealing food in [tin cans](canned-food).

Appert did not know why his method worked. Decades later Louis Pasteur showed that heat kills microbes, and sealing keeps new ones out. Canned beans and tinned fish on a shop shelf today use the same idea.

## The fridge arrives

Electric home [refrigerators](refrigerator) became common in American homes from the 1920s onward, and spread more slowly in India in the decades after independence. Today the fridge does what cellars, snow and salt once did, mostly by slowing microbes down. But the older ways never vanished. Salted fish, dried mango and lime pickle are still made because people simply like the taste.
