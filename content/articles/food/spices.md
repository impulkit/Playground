## The short answer

India grows more kinds of spices than almost anywhere else, and has done so for thousands of years. [Black pepper](cardamom-pepper) and cardamom grow wild in the hills of Kerala. Turmeric, ginger and long pepper are ancient Indian crops. When a spice is right outside your door, you cook with it.

But there is more to it than nearby supply. Spices add smell, colour and heat, and Indian cooks learned to layer many of them in one dish, each in its own way. Some are roasted, some fried in oil, some ground with water into a paste, some sprinkled on at the end. India is now the world's biggest producer, user and exporter of spices, according to the Spices Board of India.

## Black gold from the Malabar coast

Pepper vines climb trees in the [wet forests](forests-india) of the [Western Ghats](western-ghats). Two thousand years ago, Roman traders sailed to the port of Muziris, near today's [Kochi](kochi), to buy pepper. Hoards of Roman gold coins have been dug up in South India.

Pepper was so prized in Europe that it was sometimes used to pay rent. Arab and Indian merchants controlled the sea trade for centuries. In 1498, the Portuguese sailor Vasco da Gama reached Kozhikode (Calicut), and European powers began a long and often violent struggle to control the spice trade. Christopher [Columbus](columbus) had set out a few years earlier, sailing west, hoping to reach the same riches.

## The hot-climate idea

In the 1990s, two American scientists studied thousands of recipes from dozens of countries. They found that recipes from hotter countries, like India and [Thailand](thailand), tended to use more spices than those from cold ones, like Norway.

Their explanation was that many spices kill bacteria. In a hot place without fridges, food spoils fast, so spicy recipes might have helped people stay healthy. It is a neat idea, and lab tests show that garlic, onion and some spices do slow germs. But many scientists point out that the amounts used in cooking are small, and that history, trade and taste explain a lot too.

## How Indian cooks use spice

An Indian dish often has spice added at several moments. Whole seeds such as cumin or mustard go first into hot oil and crackle, the step called tadka. Ground spices are cooked with onions until the oil separates. A pinch of garam masala, a blend of warm spices like cinnamon, cloves and cardamom, is stirred in at the end.

Each region has its own favourites. Kashmir loves fennel and dried ginger. Bengal uses a five-seed mix called panch phoron. Kerala's curries are rich with [black pepper](food-pepper-black-gold) and [curry leaves](curry-leaves). That variety is why "Indian food" tastes so different from one state to the next.

## Smell does most of the work

Your tongue can sense only a few basic tastes, such as sweet, salty and sour. Most of what you call "flavour" is really smell, drifting up the back of your throat to your [nose](nose-smell). Spices are packed with smelly oils, which is why a kitchen fills with aroma long before the food is ready.

That also explains a strange fact: with a heavy cold, a rich [biryani](biryani) can taste of almost nothing. Chilli is the exception. Its burn is felt by pain nerves, so you notice it even with a blocked nose. Chillies came to India only about 500 years ago, as you can read in [the chilli's journey](chillies).
