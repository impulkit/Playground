## The short answer

Umami is a savoury, meaty, mouth-filling taste. It is the fifth basic taste, alongside sweet, sour, salty and bitter. You find it in ripe [tomatoes](tomatoes), cheese, mushrooms, soy sauce, meat broth, dried fish and seaweed.

The taste comes mainly from glutamate, a building block of proteins. When proteins break down, through ripening, cooking, drying or fermenting, glutamate is set free, and special sensors on your [tongue](tongue-taste) pick it up. Umami tells your brain that a food probably contains protein, something your body needs. Sweet signals energy, salty signals minerals, and bitter often warns of poison, so each taste works like a quick lab test done in your mouth.

## A chemist and his soup

Kikunae Ikeda was a chemistry professor in Tokyo. His wife made dashi, a clear Japanese broth from kombu [seaweed](seaweed-food). Ikeda noticed it had a taste that was not sweet, sour, salty or bitter. In 1908 he boiled down huge amounts of kombu, crystallised the substance behind the taste and found it was glutamate.

He called the taste umami. The very next year, a company began selling his seasoning, a salt form of glutamate called monosodium glutamate, or MSG. Ikeda hoped it would make cheap, plain food tastier for poor families.

## Why scientists doubted it

For most of the 20th century, many Western scientists did not accept umami as a basic taste. They thought it was just a mix of salty and other flavours.

The proof came from the tongue itself. Around 2000 to 2002, researchers found taste receptors, tiny protein sensors on taste cells, that respond to glutamate and almost nothing else. A taste with its own receptor counts as a basic taste. Umami was in.

## The umami multiplier

Later, Japanese researchers found something odd. Two other substances, one in dried bonito fish and one in dried shiitake mushrooms, have only a little taste alone. Mixed with glutamate, they make umami many times stronger.

Cooks had used this trick for centuries without knowing why. Food scientists today call it umami synergy, and snack makers use it on purpose, which is one reason a packet of chips can be so hard to stop eating. Dashi combines kombu and bonito. Italians put parmesan on tomato sauce. A burger with ketchup, cheese and meat stacks several umami foods together.

## Umami in India and America

Indian cooking is full of umami, even if nobody uses the word. Onions and tomatoes slowly fried into a masala become richer as they brown. Dried fish is loved along the Konkan coast and in the Northeast. In Nagaland, a fermented soybean paste called axone has a strong, savoury smell that fills a whole kitchen. [Fermentation](fermentation) of rice and dal for idli and dosa batter also releases glutamate.

In the USA, ketchup, parmesan, mushrooms and slow-cooked barbecue all lean on umami. Even the ancient Romans loved it: they poured a salty fermented fish sauce called garum on almost everything.

## The MSG scare

In 1968, a doctor wrote a letter to a medical journal describing feeling unwell after meals at Chinese restaurants in the USA. Newspapers blamed MSG, and the idea of "Chinese restaurant syndrome" spread, along with unfair suspicion of Chinese food.

Since then, many careful studies, where people did not know if they were eating MSG, have found no reliable harm from normal amounts. A ripe tomato or a piece of parmesan contains the very same glutamate. But taste is never just the tongue. Hold your nose and much of a food's flavour disappears, because your [sense of smell](taste-smell-link) does more of the tasting than you think.
