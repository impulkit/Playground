## The short answer

The samosa came to India from Central Asia and the Middle East. Over a thousand years ago, cooks in Persia and the Arab world made small pastries filled with minced meat, called sanbusak or sanbusaj. In Central Asia, a baked version called samsa was popular.

Traders, soldiers and cooks travelling along the old routes into India carried the recipe with them. By the 1200s and 1300s, samosas were being served at the courts of the [Delhi Sultanate](delhi-sultanate). Back then they were stuffed with meat and nuts. The potato samosa, now the most common kind, came centuries later.

## A snack for travellers

Small, sealed pastries made sense for people on the move. They could be cooked over a campfire, carried in a saddlebag and eaten without plates. Traders crossing deserts and mountains along routes such as the [Silk Road](silk-road) took them along.

Arabic cookbooks from the 10th to 13th centuries describe sanbusaj, filled with meat and fried in oil. The name changed as it travelled. It became sambusa in Persia, samsa in Central Asia, and samosa or sambusa in India.

## Samosas at the sultan's court

The samosa appears in records from medieval India. The poet Amir Khusrau, who lived in Delhi around 1300, mentioned princes enjoying samosas made of meat, ghee and onion.

The [Moroccan](morocco) traveller [Ibn Battuta](ibn-battuta) visited the court of Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq in the 1330s. He described a pastry called sambusak, filled with minced meat, almonds, pistachios, walnuts and spices, served at royal feasts. In the 1500s, the *Ain-i-Akbari*, a record of [Akbar](akbar)'s court, gave a recipe for a similar dish.

## Then came the potato

Potatoes reached India from South America in the 1600s, brought by Portuguese traders. They took time to become popular. Once they did, the potato samosa took off.

Potatoes were cheaper than meat, and a filling of spiced potato and peas suited the many vegetarians in India. Today the potato samosa, eaten with green chutney or [tamarind](tamarind) sauce, is sold at tea stalls, railway stations and sweet shops across the country.

## A shape that works

A samosa is usually a [triangle](geometry-everyday) or a cone. Cooks fold a strip or half-circle of dough into a cone, fill it and seal the open edge. The pointed shape holds the filling tightly and helps the pastry fry evenly.

Triangles are strong shapes that don't bend easily, which you can read about in [shapes and angles](shapes-angles). A samosa with a well-sealed seam won't burst in the hot oil.

## Samosas around the world

The samosa's family is still spread far and wide. In Bengal, a smaller version is called singara. In East Africa, sambusas are made with meat or [lentils](lentils-world). In Portugal and Goa, a similar pastry is called chamuça. Central Asian samsa is baked in a clay oven, not fried.

Indians who moved to Britain, the USA and Canada took the samosa with them, and it's now sold in supermarkets from London to Toronto. During Ramadan, samosas are a favourite food to break the fast in many countries, from Pakistan to Somalia. A triangle of pastry has crossed more borders than most travellers.

## A snack for every occasion

In India, the samosa is the classic snack with afternoon [chai](food-chai-history). It's served at birthday parties, office meetings and cricket matches. It turns up as [street food](food-street-food-world) in [Mumbai](mumbai) and [Kolkata](kolkata) alike. Sweet shops in some places even make sweet samosas filled with khoa or dried fruit.
