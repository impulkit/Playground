## The short answer

Gujarati cooks like to balance tastes. A spoonful of jaggery or sugar often goes into dal, kadhi and vegetables, along with lemon, [tamarind](tamarind) or kokum for sourness, green chillies for heat, and salt. The result is food that tastes sweet, sour, spicy and salty at the same time.

Nobody knows for sure why this habit began. Many people link it to the hot, dry climate and to salty water and soil in parts of the state, which made food taste salty. Gujarat also has many vegetarian families, and a little sweetness may have helped make simple [lentils](lentils-world) and vegetables more filling and tasty.

## A sweet and sour balance

A Gujarati dal is thinner than many North Indian dals, with a sweet-sour taste from [jaggery](jaggery) and kokum or lemon. Kadhi, a yogurt curry thickened with gram flour, is made the same way. In Gujarat it's usually a little sweet. In Punjab it's sour and thick.

Some people explain the sweetness through the land. Parts of Gujarat are dry, and the [salt marshes](salt-pans) of the [Rann of Kutch](gujarat-rann) produce much of India's salt. Sweetness balances salt and spice and may help people drink more water in the heat. These ideas are popular, but historians haven't proved any of them.

## Vegetarian traditions

Gujarat has one of India's largest vegetarian populations. [Jainism](jain-agamas), a religion that teaches non-violence towards all living things, has deep roots there. Many Jains follow the teachings of [Mahavira](mahavira) and avoid meat and also root vegetables such as onions, garlic and potatoes, because pulling them up harms tiny creatures in the soil.

Many Hindu Vaishnav families in Gujarat are vegetarian too. So Gujarati cooks became experts at making lentils, gram flour, vegetables and milk exciting.

## Snacks called farsan

Gujarat is famous for its snacks, known as farsan. Dhokla is a steamed, spongy cake made from fermented rice and lentil batter or gram flour. Khandvi is thin rolls of cooked gram flour batter, topped with mustard seeds and coconut. Thepla is a soft flatbread with fenugreek leaves that stays fresh for days.

On Dussehra, Gujaratis eat huge amounts of fafda, crunchy gram flour strips, with sweet [jalebi](food-jalebi). Gujarat even has its own kind of snack shop, the farsan mart, selling dozens of kinds of namkeen.

## The Gujarati thali

A full Gujarati thali can be enormous. It might include dal, kadhi, two or three vegetables, rotli (thin rotis), rice, farsan, [pickles](pickles-achar), chutneys and a sweet like shrikhand, sweetened strained yogurt. Servers keep refilling your plate until you firmly say no.

In winter, families cook undhiyu, a mix of beans, purple yam, small brinjals, potatoes and fenugreek dumplings. The name comes from "undhu", meaning upside down, because it was traditionally cooked in clay pots placed upside down in a pit with fire on top.

## Not one Gujarati food

Gujarat has several distinct regional styles. Surat is famous for its love of food and for dishes like locho, a soft steamed snack. Kathiyawadi food from the Saurashtra peninsula is spicier, with garlic chutney, millet rotlas and [buttermilk](lassi-chaas). Kutch, near the desert, cooks with bajra and dairy.

Gujaratis have also carried their food around the world. In parts of New Jersey, London and East Africa, you can buy fresh dhokla and thepla from shops run by Gujarati families.
