## The short answer

Most food makes a long trip. A farmer grows and harvests it. It is packed and carried, by bullock cart, tractor, truck, train or ship, to a market. Traders buy it there in bulk and sell it on to shops, restaurants and street vendors. Finally someone buys it, carries it home and cooks it.

Along the way, food has to be kept clean and cool, and every person in the chain takes a small share of the price. Some foods travel a few kilometres. Others cross oceans.

## From field to mandi

In India, many farmers sell their crops at a mandi, a regulated wholesale market. [Delhi](delhi)'s Azadpur Mandi is one of the largest fruit and vegetable markets in Asia. Before sunrise, trucks arrive loaded with onions from Maharashtra, [apples](apples) from [Himachal Pradesh](himachal) and potatoes from Uttar Pradesh.

Commission agents and traders bid for the produce. It is then sold to smaller traders, who supply local markets, carts and [shops](shops-markets). By breakfast time, vegetables picked the day before can be on a sabziwala's cart in your street.

## Big farms, long roads

In the United States, many farms are very large and grow just one or two crops. Food often goes from the farm to a packing plant, then to a huge distribution warehouse, then by truck to supermarkets. California grows a large share of the country's fruit and vegetables, so lettuce may cross the whole continent to reach New York.

Some food travels even farther. Most [bananas](bananas) eaten in the US come from Central and South America, carried in refrigerated ships.

## The cold chain

Fresh food spoils quickly in heat. The answer is the cold chain, which keeps food chilled at every step, from cold stores near farms to refrigerated trucks to shop [refrigerators](refrigerator).

India produces huge amounts of fruit, vegetables and milk, but cold storage does not reach every village. A tomato picked in the heat and carried on an open truck may bruise or rot before it is sold. Better cold chains are one of the simplest ways to cut [food waste](food-waste).

## Who gets the money

When you pay for a kilo of onions, the money is shared out. The farmer gets a part, and so do the transporter, the trader, the market and the shop. Farmers often receive much less than half of the final price, though this varies a lot by crop and place.

That is why some farmers now sell directly, through farmers' markets in American towns or farmer producer groups and apps in India. Cutting out steps can mean fresher food and a fairer share.

## Packed for the trip

Food is often changed a little before it travels. Wheat is milled into atta, milk is pasteurised and packed, and peas are [frozen within hours of picking](frozen-food). Some fruit is picked before it is fully ripe so it does not get squashed on the way.

[Bananas](food-why-bananas-curve) are a good example. They are picked green and shipped cold, then ripened in special rooms filled with a natural plant gas called ethylene. Mangoes in Indian markets are sometimes ripened in warm piles of straw. Each step is a small race against time and [microbes](?microbe).

## Food miles and the planet

Many people think the best food is always local. Researchers at Our World in Data found that, for most foods, transport makes up only a small part of their climate impact. Shipping is fairly efficient. What matters more is how the food is produced, and especially whether it is meat, dairy or plants. The exception is food sent by air, like some fresh berries, which has a much larger footprint per kilo.
