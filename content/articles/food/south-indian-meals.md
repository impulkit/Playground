## The short answer

In much of South India, a full meal is served on a fresh green banana leaf instead of a plate. Rice sits at the centre, and around it come dal, sambar, rasam, vegetable dishes, chutneys, crunchy pappadams, [pickle](pickles-achar), curd and a sweet like payasam. You [eat with your right hand](food-eating-with-hands), mixing a little rice with each dish.

The leaf is waterproof, big, free in many places, and can simply be thrown away or fed to cows afterwards. At [weddings and festivals](festival-foods), hundreds of guests sit in long rows, and servers walk down the line ladling dish after dish onto each leaf.

## Why a leaf?

Banana plants grow easily in the warm, wet south. Their leaves are large, tough and waxy, so they hold hot food and liquids without leaking. Hot rice slightly softens the leaf, which some people say adds a faint, fresh smell.

The leaf also solves the washing-up problem. After a feast for five hundred people, there are no plates to scrub. Used leaves break down quickly, and in villages they're often given to cattle. Compared with [plastic or paper plates](pollution-plastic-land), they create almost no lasting rubbish, which is why some people point to them when talking about [recycling](recycling) and waste.

## How the meal flows

The order matters. In a typical Tamil or Karnataka meal, rice comes first with a spoonful of ghee and a thick dal. Next comes sambar, a lentil stew with vegetables and [tamarind](tamarind). Then rasam, a thin, peppery, sour soup that many people drink straight from their palm.

Between these come vegetable dishes like poriyal or thoran, cooked with coconut. The meal ends with curd rice or [buttermilk](lassi-chaas), which cools the mouth and stomach. A sweet may come in the middle or at the end.

## The Kerala sadya

The grandest banana-leaf meal is Kerala's sadya, served at weddings and especially at the festival of [Onam](onam-pongal). A sadya often has more than twenty items. They include avial (mixed vegetables in coconut and curd), olan, kalan, banana chips, sharkara upperi ([jaggery](jaggery)-coated banana chips), pickles, pappadam and two or more kinds of payasam.

The leaf is laid with its narrow tip to the diner's left. Side dishes are placed in a set position on the upper half, and rice is served on the lower half. In many homes, folding the leaf towards you after eating means you enjoyed the meal.

## Every state, its own meal

In Tamil Nadu, the meal is called saapadu, and temple towns are famous for feasts served to pilgrims. Andhra Pradesh and Telangana are known for spicier meals, with fiery pickles, pappu (dal) and a sour green called gongura.

In Karnataka, an oota might include bisi bele bath, a hot rice and lentil dish. Udupi, a temple town on the Karnataka coast, gave its name to vegetarian restaurants across India, many of which serve "meals" on a steel plate lined with a banana leaf.

## Eating with your hand

Most South Indians eat meals with their right hand. The fingers mix rice with sambar or rasam into small balls, and the thumb pushes each mouthful in. Many people say food tastes better this way, because you feel its warmth and texture before tasting it.

In cities, many people now eat from steel plates, and paper or areca-leaf plates are common at big events. But for festivals and weddings, the green [banana leaf](bananas) still comes out.
