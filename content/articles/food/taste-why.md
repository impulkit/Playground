## The short answer

Some likes and dislikes are built in. Newborn babies smile at sugar water and pull faces at [bitter tastes](body-taste-change). Sweetness usually means energy, and bitterness can be a warning of poison, so these instincts probably helped [our ancestors](human-evolution) survive.

Almost everything else is learned. You tend to like foods you have eaten often, foods your family enjoys, and foods that made you feel good afterwards. Your genes, your nose and even your age also play a part. That is why a child in Chennai might love rasam while a child in Chicago finds it [strange](food-weird-world), and the other way round.

## Five tastes and a nose

Your [tongue](tongue-taste) has a few thousand [taste buds](taste-smell-link), each holding a cluster of sensing cells. They pick up five basic tastes: sweet, sour, salty, bitter and [umami](food-science-umami). Umami is the savoury taste of tomatoes, mushrooms, cheese and soups. A Japanese chemist named Kikunae Ikeda named it in 1908 after studying a [seaweed broth](kelp-forests).

But taste is only a small part of flavour. Smells from food drift up the back of your throat into your [nose](nose-smell), which can tell apart a vast number of different smells. When you have a blocked nose, food tastes dull for exactly this reason.

## Why bitter seems worse when you are young

Children often find karela or broccoli much more bitter than adults do, and they are not imagining it. Many children are more sensitive to bitter tastes, and that sensitivity often fades as people grow up.

[Genes](genes-family) matter too. Some people inherit a version of a bitter-taste gene that makes certain vegetables taste very harsh. Others barely notice. Scientists have also found that some people have a gene that makes fresh coriander taste like soap, which explains a lot of arguments about chutney.

## Learning to like

The best way to learn to like a food is to taste it again and again. Researchers find that children often need to try a new food ten times or more before it feels normal. Each time, the brain learns that the food is safe.

Family meals help a lot. Children tend to copy what the people around them eat, especially older brothers, sisters and friends. That is one reason children in Mexico and India often learn to enjoy chilli, while many children elsewhere never do.

## The bad memory effect

There is one quick way to learn to hate a food. If you eat something and then feel sick soon after, your brain may link the two and make that food seem disgusting for years, even if the food was not to blame. Scientists call this a taste aversion. It is a strong, old survival trick: one bad meal, and you never risk it again.

## Changing tastes

Taste buds are replaced about every week or two, and your tastes keep shifting all your life. Many adults love [coffee](coffee), [dark chocolate](chocolate-world) or methi, all bitter foods they disliked as children.

Food companies study all of this. They know that people are drawn to the mix of sugar, salt and fat found in many snacks, which is part of why [junk food](junk-food) is so easy to keep eating. Knowing how your tastes work will not make karela sweet, but it might make the tenth try a little easier.
