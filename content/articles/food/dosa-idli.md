## The short answer

Idli and dosa batter is made by soaking rice and urad dal, grinding them into a thick paste, and leaving it overnight. While the kitchen sleeps, [microbes](?microbe) in the batter wake up and feed.

Lactic acid bacteria turn some of the sugars into acid, which gives the batter its gentle sour taste. Bacteria and yeasts also give off bubbles of carbon dioxide gas, so by morning the batter has risen and is full of [tiny holes](food-why-cheese-holes). Steam it and you get soft, spongy idlis. Spread it thin on a hot griddle and you get a crisp dosa. Without the overnight wait, both would come out flat and heavy.

## Who is doing the work

No one adds yeast to idli batter. The microbes are already there, on the rice and dal, on the [grinder](how-mixer-grinder) and on the cook's hands. Scientists who studied idli batter found that one group of lactic acid bacteria grows fastest and makes most of the gas.

As the bacteria multiply, the batter becomes more acidic. Many harmful germs cannot live in acid, so the [fermentation](fermentation) also helps keep the batter safe. It works best between about 25 and 35°C, which is why idli comes so easily in a hot South Indian kitchen.

## Why urad dal matters

Rice alone would not rise well. Urad dal, a small black lentil that is white once its skin is removed, contains a slippery, gummy substance. When it is ground, this goo forms a sticky web in the batter.

That web traps the gas bubbles the way gluten does in wheat bread. Cooks say the dal should be ground until it is fluffy and light, almost like whipped cream, and many grind it separately from the rice for this reason. Dal also adds protein, so a plate of idli with sambar is a well-balanced meal.

## Good for your stomach

Fermentation partly breaks down the starch and proteins before you eat them. That is one reason idli is a common first food for babies and a gentle meal for people who are unwell. Studies have found that fermentation can raise the amounts of some B [vitamins](vitamins) in the batter.

Steaming adds no oil at all. A typical idli is light, which is why it is a [favourite breakfast](indian-breakfasts) in hospitals and homes from [Bengaluru](bengaluru) to New Jersey.

## Where idli came from

Dosai, an early form of dosa, appears in Tamil writing from well over a thousand years ago, according to the food historian K.T. Achaya. The first clear mention of idli, called *iddalige*, is in a Kannada text written in 920 CE. But that early idli seems to have been made from dal alone and not fermented.

Achaya suggested that the fermented, steamed idli may have come from [Indonesia](bali), where similar steamed cakes were made, perhaps carried by traders or cooks between the 8th and 12th centuries. Other historians are not convinced. What is clear is that by a few hundred years ago, the rice-and-dal idli had become South India's own.

## A breakfast that went everywhere

Udupi in coastal Karnataka is famous for its [vegetarian restaurants](restaurants-history), and cooks from there opened eateries across India in the 20th century. Masala dosa, a crisp dosa wrapped around spiced potato, became a favourite in [Chennai](chennai), Mumbai and far beyond.

Today you can buy ready-made batter in packets in Indian shops in Singapore, London and Texas. Even so, the morning check of the rising batter, with a lifted lid and a quick sniff, is a ritual in millions of homes.
