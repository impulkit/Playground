## The short answer

Most candy is made by dissolving [sugar](sugar) in water and boiling it. As the syrup boils, water escapes as steam and the syrup gets thicker and hotter. Candy makers stop at a particular temperature depending on what they want to make.

Stop at a lower temperature and you get soft fudge or chewy caramel. Keep going and you get toffee. Go all the way to about 150 °C and almost no water is left, so the syrup cools into hard, glassy candy, like a lollipop. Colours, flavours, milk, butter, nuts or [chocolate](chocolate-world) are added along the way. Boiling sugar is far hotter than boiling water and sticks to skin, so it is a job for experienced adults.

## The thread test

Before cheap thermometers, cooks tested syrup by feel and by eye. Indian sweet-makers still do. They dip a finger and thumb in a little cooled syrup, called chashni, and pull them apart. If one sticky thread forms, it is ek taar, one-string syrup. Two threads, do taar, mean a thicker syrup. Each sweet needs its own stage, and a minute too long on the fire can turn a soft [laddoo](food-laddoo) into a rock.

Candy makers in Europe and America used a similar trick. They dropped a little syrup into cold water and checked whether it formed a soft ball, a firm ball or snapped like glass. Recipe books still use these names today: soft ball for fudge, hard crack for brittle and lollipops.

## Crystals or glass

Sugar loves to form [crystals](gems-crystals). Whether candy ends up crystal or glass depends on how it cools.

Rock sugar, called mishri in India, is made of big crystals grown slowly from syrup over days. Fudge and many Indian burfis are stirred while they cool, which makes millions of tiny crystals, so the texture is smooth and crumbly. For hard candy, makers want no crystals at all. They boil the syrup very hot, add a little glucose syrup or acid to block crystals, and cool it fast. The sugar freezes in a jumble, like glass, and that is why a clear lollipop lets light through.

## A word that came from India

People in India were among the first to turn sugarcane juice into solid crystals, more than 1,500 years ago. The [Sanskrit](sanskrit) word for a piece of sugar was *khanda*. It passed into Persian and then Arabic as *qand*, and came into English as "candy". Even the word "sugar" can be traced back to the Sanskrit *sharkara*.

For a long time sugar was rare and costly in Europe, and sweets were a luxury for the rich. Only in the 1800s, when sugar became cheap and machines could make sweets in huge amounts, did candy become something ordinary children could buy with a coin.

## Spun sugar and fairy floss

Cotton candy is hard candy spun into threads. A machine heats sugar until it melts, then flings it through tiny holes in a spinning bowl. The streams cool instantly into threads thinner than a hair, which collect like wool around a stick.

The electric machine was patented in the 1890s by a dentist, William Morrison, and a sweet-maker, John C. Wharton. They sold their "fairy floss" at the 1904 World's Fair in St. Louis. In India it is often called buddhi ke baal, an old woman's hair, and is sold at fairs and beaches.

## Sweet, but sticky

Hard and sticky sweets stay in your mouth a long time, which gives bacteria on your teeth a long feast. That is why dentists worry about them more than about a piece of cake eaten quickly. Brushing and drinking water after sweets help protect [your teeth](sugar-teeth).
