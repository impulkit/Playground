## The short answer

Northeast India is eight states and hundreds of communities, so there's no single "Northeast food". But many kitchens share some habits. Rice is [the main grain](grains-world). Food is often boiled, steamed or smoked rather than fried, and many dishes use very little oil and few dry spices.

Flavour comes from fresh herbs, [bamboo](bamboo) shoots, fermented foods and fierce local chillies, including the bhut jolokia, once named the hottest chilli in the world. Pork, fish and chicken are common in many communities, while others, especially in Assam's plains, eat lots of fish and vegetables.

## Rice, rain and hills

The northeast is one of the wettest parts of India, and rice grows well in its valleys and on terraced hill slopes. Many communities grow their own varieties. Manipur's chak-hao is a black rice that turns purple when cooked, and it's used to make a sweet pudding. Assam's joha is a small, fragrant rice, and some sticky rices are steamed inside bamboo tubes.

In the hills, rain and cold made it hard to keep fresh food for long. So people learned to dry, smoke and ferment it. Meat hung over the kitchen fire slowly smokes for weeks, and smoked pork is a favourite in Nagaland and other hill states.

## The power of fermentation

[Fermentation](fermentation) is at the heart of many northeastern kitchens. Microbes slowly change the food, adding deep, strong flavours and helping it keep.

In Nagaland, axone (also spelt akhuni) is [fermented soybean](soybeans) with a powerful smell. Manipur's ngari is fermented fish, used in chutneys and stews. Khasi cooks in [Meghalaya](meghalaya) make tungrymbai, another fermented soybean dish. Fermented bamboo shoots add sourness to curries across the region. In Sikkim, gundruk is made from fermented leafy greens, dried and used in soups.

## Chillies that bite

The bhut jolokia, or ghost pepper, grows in Assam, Nagaland and Manipur. In 2007, Guinness World Records named it the world's hottest chilli. Hotter chillies have since been bred elsewhere, but it's still one of the [spiciest](spicy-hot) in the world.

People in the region use it carefully, often in a fiery chutney, pounded with tomatoes or fermented fish. Cooks sometimes wear gloves when handling it, because its juice can sting the skin and eyes. Even a small piece can make a whole pot of curry hot.

## Assam's sour and simple dishes

Assamese food is gentler. A favourite is masor tenga, a light, sour fish curry flavoured with tomato or lemon. Many meals start with khar, a dish made with an alkaline liquid filtered through the ash of dried banana peels.

Assam is also famous for its tea. Its strong black tea is drunk across India and exported around the world. Rural Assamese families still make jolpan, a breakfast of flattened or puffed rice with curd and [jaggery](jaggery).

## Momos, thukpa and Sikkim

In Sikkim and the hills near Darjeeling, Tibetan and Nepali influences are strong. [Momos](food-momos), steamed dumplings stuffed with vegetables or meat, and thukpa, a noodle soup, are everyday foods. Momos are now sold at street stalls across Indian cities, from [Shillong](shillong) to Bengaluru.

Sikkim's farms became fully organic in 2016, and its fresh greens, mushrooms and herbs go into simple soups and stir-fries. For many travellers, a plate of northeastern food is a first taste of how different Indian cooking can be from one end of the country to the other.
