## The short answer

Saffron is expensive because it is slow, fiddly work to make. Each thread is the [stigma](?stigma) of a purple crocus flower, the part that catches [pollen](?pollen). Every flower has only three. They must be picked by hand, in the few weeks of autumn when the flowers bloom, usually early in the morning before the petals wilt.

It takes about 1.5 lakh flowers to make one kilogram of dried saffron. No machine does this job well, so it all comes down to human fingers. A kilogram can cost a few lakh rupees, or thousands of dollars. Luckily, a pinch of a dozen threads is enough to colour a whole pot of [biryani](biryani).

## A flower that needs people

The saffron crocus, *Crocus sativus*, has a strange secret. It cannot make seeds that grow into new plants. Botanists think it was bred long ago from a wild crocus, and it lost the ability somewhere along the way.

So farmers do the job for it. Under the ground, each plant has a corm, a round, bulb-like food store. Every year it grows small new corms beside it. Farmers dig them up, split them and plant them in new rows. Every saffron field in Iran, Spain and Kashmir comes from corms passed from hand to hand for thousands of years.

## A morning in Pampore

India's saffron grows mainly around Pampore, a town near Srinagar in [Kashmir](kashmir). For most of the year the fields look like bare brown earth. Then, in late October, they turn purple almost overnight.

Whole families walk the rows at dawn with baskets, snapping off flowers. Back home, they sit together and pull out the three red threads from each one. The threads are dried, and they shrink to about a fifth of their fresh weight. In 2020 Kashmir saffron received a GI tag, a label that says it can only come from this region.

## An ancient luxury

People have prized saffron for at least 3,500 years. On the Greek island of Santorini, a wall painting from the Bronze Age shows young women gathering crocus flowers into baskets. It was buried by a volcanic eruption and dug up in the 20th century.

Saffron was used as a dye and a [medicine](oddity-old-medicine) as well as a food. In India, the deep orange-yellow colour named after it became linked with holiness and with giving up worldly things. Its name probably comes from *zaʿfarān*, an Arabic word, and in Hindi and many Indian languages it is called kesar.

## Colour, taste and smell

Saffron does three jobs at once. A natural [pigment](art-materials) called crocin turns food golden. Another chemical gives a slightly bitter, honey-like taste. A third, called safranal, gives the smell that people describe as hay or honey.

That is why cooks use so little. Saffron goes into kesar pedas, kheer, Kashmiri kahwa tea and Lucknow biryani. In Spain it colours paella, and in Milan it turns risotto yellow. Iran grows about nine out of every ten threads in the world, and Persian cooks use it in rice dishes too.

## Fakes and look-alikes

Anything this costly gets copied. People have sold dyed corn silk, safflower petals and even shredded paper as saffron. In medieval Germany, selling fake saffron could get a trader punished severely.

Today, a few simple checks help. Real threads are trumpet-shaped at one end. They should smell of honey and hay, not of nothing. In water, they colour it slowly and keep their own red colour. Many shoppers also buy from trusted [spice](spices) sellers, because a cheap price is usually the biggest clue of all.
