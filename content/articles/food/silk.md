## The short answer

A silkworm is not a worm. It is the caterpillar of a moth called Bombyx mori. It spends about a month eating mulberry leaves and growing. Then it spins a [cocoon](?cocoon) around itself, where it will change into a moth.

The cocoon is made from one long, unbroken thread of silk, a [protein](?protein) the caterpillar squeezes out from glands near its mouth. Silk farmers soften the cocoons in hot water and carefully unwind that thread. Several threads are twisted together to make yarn, which is then woven into shining cloth.

## A caterpillar that spins

A silkworm hatches from an egg smaller than a pinhead. For about four weeks it does almost nothing but eat mulberry leaves, shedding its skin four times as it grows.

When it is ready, it begins to spin. Moving its head in a figure-of-eight, it pushes out two strands of silk glued together with a gum called sericin. Over two or three days it wraps itself in a cocoon about the size of a peanut. A single cocoon's thread is often several hundred metres long, and sometimes much more.

## From cocoon to cloth

If the caterpillar were left alone, it would become a moth and break out of the cocoon, cutting the long thread into short pieces. So most silk farmers steam or boil the cocoons first, which kills the pupa inside.

The hot water also softens the gum, so workers can find the end of the thread and reel it off. Several threads are wound together to make one strand of raw silk. Because many silkworms die in this process, some people choose ahimsa silk, made after the moths have escaped.

## The secret of China

Silk-making began in [China](china-today) at least 5,000 years ago. Chinese legend says that Empress Leizu discovered it when a cocoon fell into her tea and began to unravel. For centuries, China guarded the secret closely, and silk became so valuable that it was traded across Asia to Rome along the [Silk Road](silk-road).

The [Byzantine](byzantium) historian Procopius wrote that, around the year 550, monks smuggled silkworm eggs out of Asia to the emperor Justinian. Whether every detail is true is unclear, but silk-making did spread west after that.

## India's four silks

India is the world's second-largest silk producer, after China. Most is mulberry silk, and Karnataka grows the most, which is why Mysore silk is famous. But India also makes three kinds of wild or semi-wild silk.

Tasar silk comes from moths that feed on trees like arjun and sal in Jharkhand and nearby states. Eri silk, from Assam, comes from caterpillars that eat castor leaves. Muga silk, with its natural golden shine, is made almost entirely in Assam in [Northeast India](northeast-india).

## Woven into tradition

Silk is woven into some of India's most loved cloth. Kanchipuram [sarees](weaving-saree) from Tamil Nadu are heavy silks with bright borders. Banarasi sarees from [Varanasi](varanasi) are woven with gold and silver threads. Many families save silk sarees for weddings and festivals, and pass them down for generations.

## Spinners of the animal kingdom

Silkworms are not the only animals that spin silk. Spiders make several kinds from glands on their bodies, and some insects use silk for nests. Scientists study these threads because they are strong, light and stretchy. Some labs are even trying to make artificial silk from proteins grown by microbes.
