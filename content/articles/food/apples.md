## The short answer

Inside an apple's cells, two things are kept in separate compartments: an [enzyme](?enzyme) called polyphenol oxidase and natural chemicals called polyphenols. When you cut or bite an apple, you break open millions of cells and the two meet. With oxygen from the air, the enzyme turns the polyphenols into new chemicals that link up into brown pigments, related to the [melanin](?melanin) in your skin.

That brown layer may help protect the fruit. In nature, it may slow down insects and germs at a wound, a little like a scab. For us, it's just a sign that air has been at work.

## Ways to slow it down

Anything that blocks the enzyme or the oxygen slows [browning](food-science-apples-brown). [Lemon juice](citrus) is acidic, and the enzyme works poorly in acid. Lemon also contains vitamin C, which grabs oxygen first.

Cold slows the enzyme too, which is why fruit salad keeps better in the fridge. Dunking slices in water keeps air away for a while. Cooking destroys the enzyme altogether, so stewed apple doesn't keep darkening. The same reaction turns cut potatoes, bananas and brinjal brown.

## Rust and browning

Browning is a kind of oxidation, a reaction with oxygen. It's related to the way iron turns into orange flakes when left out in damp air, which you can read about in [rust](rust). Both happen faster when it's warm.

In 2015, the US Department of Agriculture approved a genetically modified apple called the Arctic apple. Scientists had switched off the genes for the browning enzyme, so its cut slices stay pale. Some people welcome less waste, while others prefer not to eat genetically modified food.

## Where apples began

The wild ancestors of today's apples still grow in the Tian Shan mountains of Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan and western [China](china-today). Forests of wild apple trees there have fruit of many shapes, colours and tastes. The name of Kazakhstan's biggest city, Almaty, is often linked to the Kazakh word for apple.

Traders and travellers along the [Silk Road](silk-road) carried apples east and west. The Romans spread them across Europe, and European settlers brought them to North America.

## Seeds that surprise

Apple seeds don't grow "true". Each seed has a mix of [genes](dna) from two parents, so its fruit is usually small and sour, and different from the apple it came from.

To get the same apple every time, growers use grafting: they attach a twig from a good tree onto the roots of another. Every Gala or Fuji apple tree is grown this way. In the early 1800s, an American named John Chapman, known as Johnny Appleseed, planted apple nurseries from seed across Ohio and Indiana. His apples were mostly used to make cider.

## Apples in India

India's apples grow mainly in the cool hills of Jammu and [Kashmir](kashmir) and [Himachal Pradesh](himachal). In 1916, an American named Samuel Evans Stokes, who had settled in Himachal, brought saplings of Red Delicious and other kinds from the USA to Kotgarh. Apple growing spread across the hills and changed the lives of many farming families.

In the USA, Washington State grows the most apples by far. In both countries, apples bruised on the way to market are often wasted, though they can still make good juice, jam or chutney, as you can read in [food waste](food-waste).
