## The short answer

The very first kitchen was simply a fire. Early humans cooked over open flames, in caves or outdoors. For many thousands of years after that, most cooking still happened over fire, often on the floor, in a corner of the main room, with smoke drifting through the house.

Over the last two centuries, kitchens changed faster than ever. Iron [stoves](induction-stoves), chimneys, gas and electricity, running water, fridges, pressure cookers and microwaves all arrived. Each one changed how long cooking took, what people ate and who did the work.

## Fire on the floor

Cooking may have begun hundreds of thousands of years ago, after people learned to control [fire](fire-discovery). For most of history, a kitchen meant a hearth, a ring of stones or a clay stove.

In India, the chulha, a U-shaped clay stove fed with wood, cow dung or crop waste, was used in most homes for centuries. Cooks sat on the floor, with a sil-batta [grinding stone](how-mixer-grinder), clay pots and brass vessels within reach. Many Indian homes kept the kitchen as a special, clean space, and people often removed their shoes before entering.

## Smoke and health

Open fires and chulhas produce a lot of smoke, which fills small rooms. The World Health Organization says smoke from cooking with wood, dung and coal harms the [lungs](lungs-breathe) and heart, especially for women and young children who spend the most time near the fire.

That makes cleaner kitchens a health issue too, part of the wider problem of [air pollution](air-pollution). In 2016 India launched the Ujjwala Yojana, which has given free LPG gas connections to crores of poor households. Many families still go back to the chulha when refills feel too costly.

## Iron, gas and electricity

In Europe and America, cast-iron stoves spread in the 1800s, replacing open hearths. They used less fuel and kept the fire contained. Gas stoves followed in the late 1800s, and electric stoves in the early 1900s.

Electric [refrigerators](refrigerator) became common in American homes in the 1930s and 1940s. In India, fridges, gas stoves and mixer-grinders spread mostly from the 1970s onwards, first in cities and then in towns.

## The fitted kitchen

In 1926 an Austrian architect named Margarete Schütte-Lihotzky designed a small, efficient kitchen for new flats in Frankfurt, Germany. She studied how cooks moved and placed everything within a step or two. It had built-in cupboards, a work surface and storage bins. This Frankfurt Kitchen is often called the first fitted kitchen, and most modular kitchens today follow its idea.

## Machines that save time

In 1945 Percy Spencer, a radar engineer in the United States, noticed that a chocolate bar in his pocket had melted near a radar tube. That led to the [microwave](microwave) oven. Early ones were as tall as a refrigerator, but by the 1970s smaller versions were selling to families.

In India, the [pressure cooker](pressure-cooker) became a kitchen hero from the 1960s. It cooks dal and rice much faster and saves fuel. Its [whistle](whistles) is one of the most familiar sounds in Indian homes.

## Who cooks

Kitchens changed who did the work too. For most of history, cooking was done mainly by women, often for many hours a day. Machines saved time, and in many homes, men and children now share the cooking. The kitchen is still the heart of many homes, the place where families gather and talk.
