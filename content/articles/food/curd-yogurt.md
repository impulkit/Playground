## The short answer

Milk turns into curd because of bacteria. You warm some [milk](milk), let it cool until it feels just warm on your finger, and stir in a spoonful of curd from yesterday. That spoonful contains huge numbers of lactic acid bacteria.

Over the next few hours, the bacteria feed on lactose, the natural sugar in milk, and turn it into lactic acid. The acid makes the milk proteins unravel and link up into a soft, wobbly gel that traps the water. By morning the liquid milk has become thick, tangy curd. The sour taste is the acid. The thickness is the protein net.

## The magic spoonful

In Indian homes, the spoonful of old curd is called the starter or *jaamun*. It carries a whole community of microbes. Families pass starters to neighbours, and a new bride sometimes brings one from her mother's kitchen.

Because each starter has its own mix of microbes, curd can taste a little different from house to house. Scientists who study traditional Indian curd find many kinds of lactic acid bacteria in it, along with some yeasts. Commercial yogurt is usually made with just two carefully chosen bacteria.

## Why warmth matters

The bacteria in curd grow best between about 30 and 45°C. That is why curd sets quickly in May and slowly in December. If the milk is too hot, the bacteria die. If it is too cold, they hardly move.

So families keep curd somewhere snug in winter, wrapped in a cloth or tucked inside a closed casserole. In the past, some kept clay pots of curd near the warm kitchen fire. Clay also soaks up some water, which helps make the curd extra thick, as in Bengal's famous sweet *[mishti doi](bengali-food)*.

## A student and his microscope

In 1905, a 27-year-old Bulgarian medical student named Stamen Grigorov was studying in Geneva. He looked at yogurt from his home country under a [microscope](microscopes) and identified the rod-shaped bacterium that helps it set. It was named *Lactobacillus bulgaricus*.

{{story:microscopes}}

Around the same time, the Russian scientist Élie Metchnikoff noticed that some Bulgarian villagers lived long lives and suggested yogurt might be part of the reason. His idea made yogurt popular in Europe and America. Scientists still don't know whether yogurt helps people live longer.

## Curd in Indian meals

Curd cools a meal in hot weather and calms a fiery curry. It becomes raita with cucumber, [chaas](lassi-chaas) or lassi when whisked with water, and kadhi when cooked with gram flour. In Tamil Nadu and Karnataka, a meal often ends with curd rice.

In India, "curd" means dahi. In Britain and America, "curds" usually means the lumps that form when making cheese, so Indian visitors abroad sometimes find the word confusing.

## Curd and your stomach

The acid in curd keeps many harmful germs from growing, which is why it was a safe way to store milk before [fridges](refrigerator). Some of its lactose has already been used up by bacteria, so many people who get tummy aches from milk can eat curd.

Live bacteria in curd may also join the [friendly microbes](microbes-friendly) in your [gut](digestion-gut) for a while. Researchers are studying how much they help. What is clear is that a bowl of curd is a good source of protein and calcium, and it has been part of Indian meals for thousands of years.
