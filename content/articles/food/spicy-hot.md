## The short answer

Chillies contain a chemical called [capsaicin](?capsaicin). In your mouth it attaches to tiny sensors on your [nerve endings](nervous-system). These sensors normally switch on when something is dangerously hot, above about 43 °C.

[Capsaicin](food-science-spicy-milk) flips the same switch without any real heat. Your brain gets a message that says "burning", so your face goes red, your eyes water and your nose runs, as if you had bitten into something scorching. Nothing is actually being cooked. It is a very convincing false alarm.

## A sensor for heat

The heat sensor that capsaicin triggers is called TRPV1. Scientists found it in 1997 by using capsaicin as a clue. They searched for the exact part of a nerve cell that reacted to chilli, and discovered that it also reacted to real heat.

The American scientist David Julius led that work, and in 2021 he shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for it. Understanding TRPV1 has helped doctors study [pain](pain), and some pain-relief creams use capsaicin itself.

## Measuring the burn

In 1912 an American chemist, Wilbur Scoville, invented a way to rank chillies. He dissolved chilli extract in sugar water and asked tasters to say when they could no longer feel any heat. The more it had to be diluted, the hotter the chilli.

Today labs measure capsaicin with machines, but the results are still given in Scoville heat units. A capsicum scores zero. A jalapeño scores a few thousand. The bhut jolokia, or ghost pepper, scores over a million.

## The pepper from the Northeast

The bhut jolokia grows in Assam, Nagaland and Manipur in [Northeast India](northeast-india). In 2007 [Guinness World Records](world-records) named it the hottest chilli in the world. Hotter chillies bred in the United States, such as the Carolina Reaper, have since taken the title.

In Nagaland, the bhut jolokia goes into chutneys and pork dishes. It has even been used in smoke bombs and fences to keep wild elephants away from fields.

## How chillies came to India

It can be surprising, but India had no chillies until about 500 years ago. Chillies come from Central and South America. Portuguese traders brought them to [Goa](goa) in the 1500s, and within a century they had spread across the subcontinent.

Before that, Indian cooks got their heat from black pepper, ginger and long pepper. Chillies were cheap and easy to grow, so they quickly became part of everyday food. India is now the world's largest producer of dried chillies.

## Cooling the fire

When your mouth is burning, water does not help much. Capsaicin is an oily chemical, and like oil on a plate, water slides right past it. Milk, curd and [lassi](lassi-chaas) work better because their fat and proteins help carry the capsaicin away.

That may be one reason so many spicy cuisines serve cooling foods on the side. India has raita and buttermilk, [Mexico](mexico) has sour cream, and [Thailand](thailand) has coconut milk. Sugar and starchy rice also help a little. The feeling fades by itself after a few minutes, as the nerve sensors calm down.

Capsaicin seems to protect chilli plants. Mammals that chew seeds, like mice, feel the burn and stay away. Birds swallow seeds whole and carry them far away in their droppings, and birds cannot feel capsaicin. Capsaicin also helps fight off some fungi that attack the fruit.

So humans are odd. We are mammals that eat chillies anyway, often on purpose. Some scientists think people enjoy the rush of a safe danger, like a scary ride, and the body's own pain-relief chemicals that follow.
