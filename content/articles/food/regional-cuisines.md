## The short answer

Food in India changes with the land, the weather and history. In the wet east and south, where rice grows well, rice is the main grain. In the cooler, drier north and northwest, people eat more wheat breads. In hot, dry areas, millets like bajra and jowar were long the everyday grain.

Coastal regions cook with fish and coconut. Inland, [lentils](lentils-world), vegetables and milk matter more. On top of that, traders and rulers from Persia, Portugal, Britain and beyond brought new ingredients and dishes. That's why a meal in Kolkata can taste nothing like a meal in Chennai or Jaipur.

## The land decides the grain

India's food map follows its rain. The [monsoon](monsoon) drops heavy rain on the east, the northeast and the west coast. Rice needs plenty of water, so these places became rice country. Bengal, Odisha, Assam, Kerala and Tamil Nadu all eat rice at most meals.

{{story:monsoon}}

The northwest gets less rain but has big rivers and cool winters, which suit wheat. Punjab and Haryana became India's main wheat lands, and rotis and parathas are the everyday food. In Rajasthan and parts of the Deccan, rain is scarce, so tough grains like bajra and jowar were common. Bajra rotis in Rajasthan and jowar bhakri in Maharashtra are still popular.

## Each region's favourite fat

The cooking fat changes the taste of a whole cuisine. Kerala cooks with [coconut oil](oils-cooking). Bengal and Odisha love pungent mustard oil. Gujarat and much of the Deccan cook with groundnut oil, while Tamil Nadu often uses sesame oil, called gingelly or nallennai. In the north, ghee is prized.

Each fat came from what grew nearby. Coconut palms line Kerala's coast, mustard grows across the northern and eastern plains, and groundnuts thrive in Gujarat's soils. A [fish curry](bengali-food) cooked in mustard oil in Kolkata and one cooked in coconut oil in [Kochi](kochi) can taste like two different dishes, even with the same fish.

## Spices, old and new

[Black pepper](food-pepper-black-gold) grows wild in the forests of the [Western Ghats](western-ghats). Turmeric, cardamom, ginger and cinnamon have been used in South Asia for thousands of years. Traders from Rome, Arabia and China came to India for them.

Then, in the 1500s, Portuguese traders brought plants from the Americas: [chillies](chillies), tomatoes, potatoes, peanuts and [cashews](cashew). Within a century or two, chillies had spread so widely that it's now hard to picture Indian food without them.

## Rulers and travellers

The [Mughals](mughals), who ruled much of North India from the 1500s, brought Persian and Central Asian cooking. Rich gravies with nuts and cream, kebabs and pulao became famous in Delhi, Lucknow and Hyderabad.

The Portuguese shaped [Goan food](goan-food), with vinegar and bread. Parsis who came from Persia created dishes like dhansak. Chinese families in Kolkata invented [Indian-Chinese food](chinese-food-india), such as chilli chicken and hakka noodles. The British left behind tea time, cutlets and the word "curry".

## Faith and habit

Religion and custom shape the plate too. Many Jains avoid root vegetables such as onions, potatoes and garlic. Some Hindu communities eat no meat, while others, including many Bengali and Kashmiri families, eat fish or meat regularly. Gujarat and Rajasthan have large vegetarian populations.

Festivals bring their own dishes, from modaks for Ganesh Chaturthi in Maharashtra to pithas in Assam and Odisha. Across India, you could eat a different regional dish every day for a year and still not run out.
