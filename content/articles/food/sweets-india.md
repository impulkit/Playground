## The short answer

India has a long and close relationship with sugar. Indians were the first to turn sugarcane juice into crystals, more than 1,500 years ago, so sweet things have been part of Indian life for a very long time. India also produces more milk than any other country, and milk is the base of [many sweets](sweets-world).

But sweets in India are about more than taste. They mark happy moments. People hand out sweets when a baby is born, when exams are passed and when a new job begins. Sweets are offered to gods in temples and [shared at every festival](festival-foods). In many families, saying "muh meetha karo", sweeten your mouth, is part of celebrating good news.

## Sugar, milk and ghee

The word sugar itself comes from the Sanskrit *sharkara*, as you can read in [where sugar comes from](sugar). Jaggery and honey were used in sweets for centuries before refined sugar.

Many Indian sweets start with [milk](milk). Boiled slowly, milk thickens into khoa, which becomes peda, barfi and the base of [gulab jamun](food-gulab-jamun). Rice cooked in milk becomes kheer or payasam. Ghee adds richness to halwas and [laddoos](food-laddoo) made from wheat, besan or semolina.

## Bengal's special sweets

In Bengal and Odisha, sweet makers use chhena, fresh cheese curds made by splitting milk with acid. Kneaded smooth, chhena becomes sandesh and [rasgulla](bengali-food).

Rasgulla, soft white balls cooked in sugar syrup, is claimed by both West Bengal and Odisha. Odisha links it to an old tradition at the Jagannath temple in Puri. West Bengal credits a [Kolkata](kolkata) sweet maker named Nobin Chandra Das in 1868. Each state now has a GI tag for its own version.

## Sweets that travelled

Some Indian sweets came from elsewhere. Gulab jamun is related to luqmat al-qadi, a Persian and Arab fried dough ball in syrup. [Jalebi](food-jalebi) probably came from West Asian zalabiya. Halwa takes its name from an Arabic word for sweet.

India made each of them its own. Indian jalebi is crisp, orange and soaked in syrup, often eaten hot for breakfast with rabri in the north. Mysore pak, made from besan, sugar and lots of ghee, is often said to have been invented in the kitchen of the Mysore palace.

## Sweets for the gods

Many festivals have a special sweet. At [Ganesh Chaturthi](ganesh-chaturthi), families make modak, a steamed dumpling filled with coconut and [jaggery](jaggery), said to be Ganesha's favourite. At Eid, families serve sheer khurma, made with vermicelli, milk and dates. At [Christmas](christmas) in Goa, homes make kulkuls and [bebinca](goan-food).

At Diwali, boxes of sweets are exchanged between families, friends and neighbours. Temples prepare prasad, food offered to the gods and then shared. The laddu of the Tirupati temple in Andhra Pradesh is so famous it has a GI tag.

## The halwai and the silver leaf

A traditional sweet maker is called a halwai. Some sweet shops in old cities have been run by the same family for more than a hundred years. Sweets are often decorated with varq, sheets of edible silver so thin they flutter when you breathe on them.

In the USA, Indian sweet shops now sell barfi and jalebi in New Jersey, Houston and California, especially during Diwali. American children have their own sugar-filled festival, [Halloween](halloween), which shows that sweets and celebrations go together almost everywhere.

## A sweet balance

Indian sweets are rich in sugar and fat, which taste wonderful but aren't meant for every meal. Sugar left on teeth feeds bacteria that cause cavities, which you can read about in [sugar and teeth](sugar-teeth). That's why many families enjoy sweets mostly on special days, and why a single piece can feel like a celebration.
