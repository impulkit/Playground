## The short answer

A weather balloon carries a small box of instruments, called a radiosonde, up through the [atmosphere](?atmosphere). As it rises, the box measures temperature, humidity and air pressure, and radios the numbers back to the ground every second or two. By tracking where the balloon drifts, forecasters also learn how fast and in which direction the wind is blowing at every height.

These readings feed the [computer models](supercomputers) that make [weather forecasts](weather-forecasts). Every day at the same two moments, midnight and noon on the clock used by scientists, balloons go up from about 900 sites around the world. Together they give a snapshot of the whole sky at once.

## A two-hour trip to the edge of space

A weather balloon is made of thin rubber and filled with hydrogen or helium, gases lighter than air. It rises at about 5 m every second, roughly the speed of a lift in a tall building. In about two hours it reaches 30 km or more, nearly four times higher than Mount Everest.

As it climbs, the air around it gets thinner and colder, dropping below minus 50 °C. With less air pushing on the outside, the balloon stretches wider and wider until it finally bursts. A small orange [parachute](parachutes) opens, and the radiosonde floats back down, sometimes landing in a field, a forest or the sea. In the United States, only about one in five is ever found and returned.

## How balloons found a new layer of sky

In the 1890s, a French scientist named Léon Teisserenc de Bort began sending up hundreds of unmanned balloons with instruments. He expected the air to keep getting colder the higher it went. Instead, his records showed that above about 11 km, the temperature stopped falling.

In 1902 he announced that the atmosphere has layers. The lowest, where all our weather happens, is now called the troposphere. The calmer layer above is the stratosphere. Today's balloons cross from one to the other on every flight. The boundary between them is not fixed. It sits higher over the hot tropics, around 16–17 km above places like Chennai, and lower near the poles, which balloon records helped scientists discover.

## From the monsoon to the jet stream

The India Meteorological Department launches weather balloons from stations across the country. Their readings help track the [monsoon](monsoon) winds as they push north each summer, and follow [cyclones forming over the Bay of Bengal](cyclones-india) and the Arabian Sea.

Balloons also measure the [jet streams](jet-streams), fast rivers of wind about 10 km up. Modern radiosondes work out their position using [satellite navigation](gps-how), so forecasters can calculate wind speeds very precisely as the balloon is blown along. Pilots care about these numbers too. A plane flying east across the United States with a strong jet stream behind it can arrive well ahead of schedule, while one flying west into it burns extra fuel.

## A balloon mistaken for a flying saucer

In 1947, a rancher near Roswell, New Mexico, found strange wreckage of foil, rubber and sticks. Rumours spread that it came from an alien spacecraft. In 1994, a report by the US Air Force concluded the debris came from Project Mogul, a secret string of balloons built to listen for distant nuclear tests.

Ordinary weather balloons are still sometimes reported as UFOs, especially at sunset, when a balloon high in the sky catches sunlight long after the ground below has gone dark.
