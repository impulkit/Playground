## The short answer

Jet lag is what happens when your body's inner clock and the clock on the wall stop agreeing. Your body runs on a roughly 24-hour rhythm that tells you when to feel sleepy, [hungry](hunger) and alert. If you fly across several [time zones](time-zones) in a few hours, that rhythm is still set to home.

So you may feel wide awake at 3 a.m., drowsy at lunch, or hungry at strange times. Your stomach can get upset and your mood can dip. The good news is that the inner clock resets itself. Most people adjust by about one time zone a day, so the effects usually fade within a few days to a week.

## The clock inside your head

Deep in your brain, just above the point where the nerves from your two eyes cross, sits a tiny cluster of about 20,000 [nerve cells](?neuron). It is the body's master clock. It keeps time on its own, but it is corrected every day by light coming through your eyes.

When evening comes and light fades, the clock tells a small gland in your brain to release melatonin, a [hormone](?hormone) that makes you sleepy. [Body temperature](body-temperature), appetite and alertness all rise and fall on their own schedules too. After a long flight, the light outside suddenly says "morning" while every part of your body still says "night".

## Why east is harder

Left in a cave with no clocks or windows, most people's inner clock runs slightly longer than 24 hours. That means it is easier to stretch your day than to shrink it.

Flying west, from Delhi to New York, lengthens your day. You stay up late and it feels almost natural. Flying east, from New York to Delhi, shortens your day by about ten hours. You have to fall asleep when your body thinks it is still afternoon. That is why many travellers find eastward trips harder to recover from.

## A prize for clock genes

How does a cell keep time? In the 1980s, three American scientists, Jeffrey Hall, Michael Rosbash and Michael Young, studied fruit flies to find out. They found genes that make a protein which slowly builds up in cells overnight and then breaks down during the day, like sand in an hourglass.

Humans have very similar genes. Almost every cell in your body has its own little clock, and the master clock in the brain keeps them all in time. In 2017, the three scientists won the Nobel Prize for this work.

## Helping your clock catch up

The strongest signal for your [body clock](body-clock) is daylight. Spending time outdoors in the morning or afternoon at your destination helps it shift faster. Eating meals and going to bed at local times helps too, even if you don't feel like it. Many travellers start shifting their bedtime a little before they leave. Short naps can help on the first day, but long ones make the first night harder.

If you travel and feel unwell for longer than expected, tell a parent or a doctor. Jet lag is common and harmless for most people, but sleep matters, as anyone who has [slept badly](sleep-why) before an exam knows.

Astronauts face a stranger version. On the [International Space Station](space-station), the Sun rises and sets about 16 times a day. So the crew lives on one fixed clock time, Greenwich Mean Time. Lights inside the station can shift towards blue in the morning and warmer colours at night, to help their body clocks keep up.
