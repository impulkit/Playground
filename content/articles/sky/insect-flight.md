## The short answer

Fast. A honeybee beats its wings about 230 times a second, a housefly about 200, and some mosquitoes 600 to 800 times. That is why insects buzz and whine. The sound you hear is the air being pushed by wings moving too quickly to see. Faster wings make a higher note, as you can read in [how sound travels](sound-travel).

Not all insects are so fast. Big butterflies may flap fewer than 20 times a second, and you can watch each beat. In general, the smaller the wing, the faster it needs to move. A tiny wing has less surface to push the air with, so it must make up for it with speed.

## Muscles that work like springs

Ordinary muscles can't be switched on and off by nerves 700 times a second. So how do mosquitoes manage it? Many insects, including flies, [bees](pollinators) and beetles, have special flight [muscles](muscles) that do not pull on the wings directly. Instead they squeeze the box-shaped middle of the body, called the thorax.

The thorax is springy. When one set of muscles squeezes it one way, it snaps back, which stretches the other set and triggers it to contract. The wings click up and down with each snap. One signal from the [nervous system](nervous-system) can lead to many wingbeats. Dragonflies are different. Their muscles pull on each of their four wings directly, so each wing can move on its own. This lets them hover, dart sideways and even fly backwards.

## Swirls of air

An insect wing does not just flap up and down. It sweeps forwards and backwards and flips over at each end of the stroke, a bit like a swimmer's hand. As it sweeps, it creates a small whirl of air along its front edge. That whirl lowers the pressure above the wing and gives extra lift.

In 2017, scientists filmed mosquitoes with very fast cameras. They found mosquito wings move through only a small arc, but rotate cleverly at each end. The rotation creates extra swirls that help keep the insect up. Honeybees use their wings for more than flying: they also fan them to cool the hive, and they [dance](bees-dance) to share directions.

## The fly's secret balance

Houseflies and mosquitoes have only two wings. Their back pair has shrunk into two tiny knobs on stalks, called halteres. As the fly moves, the halteres buzz up and down very fast. When the fly turns, they get twisted slightly, and sense organs at their base notice. This tells the fly at once that it is spinning, a bit like a built-in gyroscope.

Halteres help explain why flies are so hard to swat. A fly also sees fast movement very well. Studies with [high-speed cameras](how-cameras-speed) showed that a fly can spot a threat and plan its escape in a fraction of a second. It shifts its legs so that it leaps away from the danger as it takes off.

## The first fliers

Insects were the first animals to fly, more than 300 million years ago. That was long before pterosaurs, birds or bats. Some ancient griffinflies, relatives of dragonflies, had wings about 70 cm across, wider than two school rulers laid end to end.

Some insects fly very far. Globe skimmer dragonflies cross the Indian Ocean between India and East Africa, a trip that takes several generations to complete. In North America, [monarch butterflies](monarch-migration) fly thousands of kilometres to Mexico, and you can read about their lives in [the butterfly's life cycle](butterflies-life).
