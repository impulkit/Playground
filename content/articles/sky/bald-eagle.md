## The short answer

In 1963, only about 417 pairs of bald eagles were nesting in the lower 48 states of the USA. The cause was a pesticide called DDT, sprayed on farms and towns to kill insects. It washed into rivers, built up in fish, and then in the eagles that ate them. It made their eggshells so thin that they cracked when the parents sat on them.

The USA banned DDT in 1972 and protected the birds by law. Slowly, the eagles came back. In 2007 they were taken off the endangered list. A government survey published in 2021 estimated more than 3 lakh (300,000) bald eagles in the lower 48 states.

## Poison that piles up

DDT does not break down easily. A fish swallows a tiny amount with its food, and the chemical stays in its fat. An eagle eats hundreds of fish, so it collects their DDT too. Scientists call this [biomagnification](?biomagnification): a poison becomes more concentrated at each step up a [food chain](?foodchain).

In female eagles, the chemical upset the way their bodies built eggshells. The eggs looked normal but were too thin to survive being sat on. Ospreys, brown pelicans and [peregrine falcons](sky-peregrine) suffered in the same way. In 1962 the scientist and writer Rachel Carson warned about pesticides in her book *Silent Spring*. It helped turn many Americans against DDT.

## A similar story in India

India has its own version of this story. From the 1990s, India's [vultures](vultures-india) began dying in huge numbers, and more than 95 per cent of some species vanished. Scientists traced the deaths to diclofenac, a painkiller given to cattle. Vultures that fed on dead cattle suffered kidney failure. India banned diclofenac for animals in 2006, and the fall has slowed.

## A bird for a nation

The bald eagle has been on the [Great Seal of the United States](american-revolution) since 1782. In December 2024 a new law made it the official national bird. Not everyone has liked it. In a 1784 letter, Benjamin Franklin called it a bird of "bad moral character", because it often steals fish from other birds.

It isn't bald, either. The name comes from an old English word for white, describing the white head. Young eagles are brown all over and only get their white heads at about four or five years old. India chose a very different bird for its [national symbols](national-symbols): the colourful peacock.

## How the comeback worked

Banning DDT was only the start. In 1973 the USA passed the Endangered Species Act, which made it illegal to harm bald eagles or their nests. Biologists also moved young eagles from places with many birds to places with none, raising them on tall platforms until they could fly. Cleaner rivers meant healthier fish, and healthier fish meant healthier eagles.

By the 1990s the numbers were climbing fast. Eagles still face dangers. Some swallow lead fragments from hunters' bullets in the carcasses they scavenge, and lead is a poison. Others are hit by cars or fly into power lines. Scientists keep counting nests, because the eagle's story showed how quickly a top hunter can warn us that something is wrong further down the chain.

## Life in a giant nest

Bald eagles mostly eat fish, which they snatch from the water with their feet. A pair often uses the same nest for many years and adds new sticks every season. One famous nest in St Petersburg, Florida, was about 2.9 m wide and 6 m deep. It was thought to weigh more than 2 tonnes, about as much as a large car.

With wings up to about 2.3 m across, an eagle can [soar for hours on rising warm air](gliders), a trick explained in [how birds fly](how-birds-fly). Like the [American bison](bison-return), it shows that a species can recover when people remove the danger and give it room.
