## The short answer

Every autumn, millions of monarch butterflies fly from Canada and the eastern USA to a few mountain forests in central [Mexico](mexico), up to 4,800 km away. None of them has been there before. They steer using the Sun as a compass, checked against a [body clock](jet-lag) in their antennae.

The Sun moves across the sky during the day, so a butterfly needs to know the time to use it. The antennae clock keeps track, and the monarch adjusts its heading. On cloudy days, monarchs may also sense [Earth's magnetic field](earth-magnet). Exactly how they find the same small patches of forest, year after year, is still a mystery.

## A super generation

Summer monarchs live just a few weeks. They hatch, feed, lay eggs on milkweed plants and die. But the ones born at the end of summer are different. They don't lay eggs straight away, and they live for about eight months. This super generation makes the long journey south.

In spring, the same butterflies fly north to Texas and nearby states, lay eggs and die. Their children continue north, and so do their grandchildren. It takes three or four generations to get all the way back to Canada. You can follow each stage, from egg to caterpillar to wings, in [a butterfly's life](butterflies-life).

## The search for the forest

For decades, nobody outside central Mexico knew where the monarchs went. A Canadian scientist, Fred Urquhart, spent years putting tiny tags on butterflies and asking volunteers to report them. Each tag was a tiny sticker pressed onto a wing, printed with a number and an address to write to. In 1975, two of his helpers, Ken Brugger and Catalina Aguado, found the winter colony on a mountain in Mexico. Local people had known about the butterflies for a long time.

The fir forests there are high, cool and damp. The butterflies cluster on the trees in their millions, turning branches orange. Today the area is protected as the Monarch Butterfly Biosphere Reserve, a UNESCO [World Heritage](unesco-sites) Site since 2008.

## The western monarchs

Monarchs that live west of the [Rocky Mountains](rocky-mountains) take a shorter route. Most spend the winter in groves of trees along the coast of California, in towns such as Pacific Grove. Volunteers count them every year around [Thanksgiving](thanksgiving).

Their numbers have swung wildly. In 2020 the volunteers found fewer than 2,000 butterflies, a tiny fraction of the millions counted in the 1980s. The next year they counted about 2.5 lakh (250,000). Scientists are still working out why numbers jumped so quickly, and whether it will last. In 2024 the count fell again, to about 9,000, and in 2025 it was only a little higher, at about 12,000.

## A bitter meal and a warning

Monarch caterpillars eat only milkweed, a plant with bitter poisons in its sap. The caterpillars store the poison, and adult monarchs taste bad to most birds. Their bright orange wings act as a warning.

Monarch numbers have fallen a lot since the 1990s, partly because milkweed has been cleared from farms and roadsides. In 2024, the US government proposed protecting the monarch as a threatened species, and many families now plant milkweed in gardens and schoolyards.

India has its own relatives of the monarch. The plain tiger butterfly, common in Indian gardens, belongs to the same group. In southern India, huge numbers of tiger and crow butterflies migrate between the Western and Eastern Ghats around the monsoon. Like the falcons in [bird migration](migration), they travel by instinct to places they have never seen.
