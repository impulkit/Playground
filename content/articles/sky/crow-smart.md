## The short answer

In many tests, crows are as clever as great apes. They make and use tools, solve puzzles with several steps, and remember people for years. Their brains are small, about the size of a walnut, but they are crowded with brain cells. In crows and parrots, scientists found as many cells in the thinking part of [the brain](brain-works) as in some monkeys.

Crows belong to a bird family called corvids, which also includes ravens, jays and magpies. In India you probably see two kinds: the grey-necked house crow and the all-black jungle crow. Both are bold, curious and very good at living around people. And like every bird, crows are a kind of dinosaur, as you can read in [how birds came from dinosaurs](birds-dinosaurs).

## The thirsty crow, tested

Many children in India and America learn [Aesop's](aesop) fable of the thirsty crow. It drops pebbles into a pitcher until the water rises high enough to drink. In 2014, scientists at the University of Cambridge and in New Zealand tested this with New Caledonian crows. They put a floating treat in a tube of water, just out of reach.

The crows dropped stones in to raise the water. Given a choice, they picked heavy stones over light ones that float. They chose a tube of water over a tube of sand. Their results matched those of children aged about five to seven.

## Betty and the bent wire

In 2002, a New Caledonian crow called Betty became famous at the University of Oxford. A small bucket of food sat at the bottom of a tube. Betty was given a straight piece of wire. She bent it into a hook with her beak and fished the bucket out. Nobody had trained her to do this.

In the wild, these crows shape twigs into hooks and cut stiff leaves into barbed tools to pull insects out of holes. Few animals except humans make tools this carefully. Among invertebrates, the [octopus](octopus-smart) is a rare puzzle-solving rival.

## Planning and problem solving

Ravens, the crows' bigger cousins, seem to plan ahead. In a 2017 experiment in Sweden, ravens learned that a certain stone could open a box holding a treat. Later, when the box was taken away, they were offered a choice of objects. They picked the stone and kept it until the box came back, even many hours later. Planning like this was once thought to be something only humans and great apes did.

[City crows](urban-wildlife) find their own solutions. In Sendai in Japan, carrion crows have been seen placing walnuts on roads so that passing cars crack them open. The birds then collect the pieces when the road is clear. Nobody taught them this. Other crows seem to have learned it by watching.

## Crows never forget a face

At the University of Washington in Seattle, researchers wore a scary rubber mask while catching wild crows to put rings on their legs. For years afterwards, crows scolded anyone wearing that mask, while ignoring people in other masks. Some crows that had never been caught joined in. They seemed to have learned who was dangerous from other crows.

In India, crows have long had a place in people's lives and stories. Crows appear in the [Panchatantra](panchatantra-tales), and during the Hindu ritual of shraddha many families offer food to crows, believing it reaches their ancestors. A crow waiting on a wall at mealtime may be doing more thinking than it lets on.
