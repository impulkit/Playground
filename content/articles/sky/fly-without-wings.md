## The short answer

Yes. Wings are only one way into the air. A hot-air balloon floats because warm air is lighter than the cooler air around it, so the balloon rises like a bubble in water. A rocket blasts gas downward so hard that it shoots upward, and it works even in space, where there is no air at all.

Living things have their own tricks. Dandelion seeds drift for kilometres under a fluffy [parachute](parachutes). Baby spiders float away on threads of silk. And in Indian forests, some snakes, lizards and squirrels glide between trees. [Gliding](gliders) is not quite flying, because they slowly lose height, but it gets them a long way.

## Lighter than air

On 21 November 1783, two men rose over Paris in a balloon made by the Montgolfier brothers. It was the first time people had been carried into the sky. Their balloon was filled with hot air from a fire burning below. Today, [balloons filled with light helium gas](weather-balloons) carry weather instruments up to about 30 km high, and weather services around the world launch them every day.

Rockets work in a different way. They follow [Isaac Newton](newton-life)'s rule that every push has an equal push back. As the engine forces gas out of the bottom, the gas pushes the rocket up. That is why [rockets](rockets-how) can fly where balloons and wings cannot.

## Spiders on the wind

In 1832, while sailing off South America, Charles Darwin noticed tiny spiders landing on his ship, the Beagle, far from land. They had travelled through the air on silk.

Young spiders climb to a high point, raise their bodies and let out fine threads. Then they lift off. Scientists long assumed the wind did all the work. But in 2018, researchers in Britain showed that spiders also react to Earth's natural electric field, which may help pull their charged silk upward.

## Fish and frogs in the air

Some animals that glide live in water. [Flying fish](ocean-flying-fish) burst out of the sea to escape hungry tuna and dolphins. They spread their large front fins like wings and can glide for more than 100 m. Some flick their tails on the surface as they come down, launching themselves into a second glide. Sailors crossing the Arabian Sea and the Bay of Bengal often see them skimming over the waves.

In the [rainforests](rainforests) of the [Western Ghats](western-ghats) lives the Malabar gliding frog. It has big webbed feet that work like four small parachutes. When it leaps from a high branch, it spreads its toes and drifts down to a pond or a lower tree. It lays its eggs in a foam nest hanging over water, so the tadpoles drop straight in when they hatch.

## Seeds and gliders

A dandelion seed's fluffy top is mostly empty space. In 2018, scientists found that air flowing through the bristles makes a small swirling ring just above the seed. The ring helps keep it aloft. Other plants spin their seeds like [helicopters](helicopters), as you can read in [how seeds fly](seeds-fly).

The forests of India hold some skilled gliders. The ornate flying snake flattens its body and sails between trees. Small lizards called flying dragons, found in the Western Ghats, open flaps of skin along their sides. Giant flying squirrels can glide the length of a cricket ground and more. None of them flaps. They have all found a way to fall very slowly and in the right direction.
