## The short answer

Most small drones are quadcopters, with four propellers pointing up. Each one pushes air down, and the air pushes the drone up. By making some propellers spin faster than others, the drone can tilt, turn and move in any direction.

Flying "by itself" is the job of a small computer called the flight controller. It reads tiny motion [sensors](?sensor) hundreds of times a second and adjusts each motor to keep the drone level. Add GPS and [cameras](cameras-how), and a drone can hold its position in a breeze, follow a planned route, avoid obstacles and come home when its battery runs low.

## Four propellers, no steering wheel

A quadcopter has no rudder or flaps. Everything comes from propeller speed.

Two propellers spin clockwise, and the two on the opposite corners spin anticlockwise. That way, their twisting forces cancel out, and the drone doesn't spin like a top. To rise, all four speed up. To move forward, the back two spin a bit faster, tipping the drone nose-down. To turn on the spot, the clockwise pair speeds up while the other pair slows, and the body twists round. It is the same problem a [helicopter's](helicopters) tail rotor solves, done a different way.

## The balancing brain

A drone is naturally wobbly. A gust of wind or a tiny difference between motors would flip it over in a second. So the flight controller watches constantly.

It uses gyroscopes, which sense turning, and accelerometers, which sense speeding up and tilting. These are tiny chips, the same kind that let your phone know when you turn it sideways. If the drone starts to tip, the computer speeds up the motors on the low side within thousandths of a second. A barometer senses height from air pressure, and [GPS](gps-how) tells the drone where it is on the map.

## Thinking ahead

More advanced drones use cameras and other sensors to "see" around them, like [self-driving cars](self-driving). They can spot a tree or a wall and steer around it. Some can follow a running person or a cyclist, keeping them in the camera frame.

Many drones can fly a mission set in advance. Someone taps points on a map, and the drone flies from one to the next, takes photos and lands. But a person usually still watches and can take control if something goes wrong.

## What drones do

Drones film cricket matches, weddings and wildlife. They inspect bridges, power lines and [wind turbines](wind-turbines) without anyone having to climb. In Rwanda, a company called Zipline has used drones since 2016 to fly blood supplies to hospitals in hilly areas.

In India, drones spray crops on farms and map land. In 2023, the government launched a scheme to give drones to women's self-help groups, so they can offer spraying services to farmers. In January 2022, about 1,000 drones made glowing pictures over Delhi during the Beating Retreat ceremony, all flown by computers.

## Where the name came from

In the 1930s, Britain built a radio-controlled target plane called the Queen Bee, for gunners to practise on. Pilotless target planes soon became known as drones, the name for male bees. Many people say the buzzing sound of modern drones makes the name fit even better. Real [bees](bees-dance), though, still beat drones at landing on a flower in a breeze.

## Rules of the sky

Drones share the sky with planes, helicopters and birds, so there are rules. In India, the Drone Rules of 2021 divide the map into green, yellow and red zones. Red zones, such as areas around [airports](airports), are off-limits without special permission. In the USA, the Federal Aviation Administration says most drones heavier than 250 g must be registered. Always fly with an adult, in an open space, away from people.
