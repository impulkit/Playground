## The short answer

Seeds fly by being shaped to catch the wind. A plant can't move, so if its seeds just fell straight down, the young plants would have to grow in their parent's shade, competing with it for light, water and soil. Seeds that travel get a better start.

Many plants solve this by building seeds that fall slowly. Some have fluffy [parachutes](parachutes), like the dandelion. Some have a wing that makes them spin like a [helicopter](helicopters), like the maple. A few have wide, papery wings that glide like aeroplanes. The slower a seed falls, the farther a breeze can carry it.

## Parachutes made of fluff

Blow on a dandelion clock and a hundred tiny parachutes drift away. Each seed hangs below a crown of about 100 fine bristles.

For years, scientists weren't sure how such a gappy parachute worked. In 2018, researchers in Scotland filmed dandelion seeds in a wind tunnel. They found that air flowing through the bristles forms a stable ring of swirling air, a [vortex](?vortex), just above the crown. That ring lowers the pressure above the seed and helps hold it up. A dandelion seed can stay aloft long enough to travel a kilometre or more on a breezy day.

India has its own fluffy fliers. The aak, or madar, a tough roadside shrub, splits open to release flat brown seeds with tufts of silky white hair. In spring, the silk-cotton tree, or semal, drops pods that burst into clouds of fluff. People have used semal fluff to stuff pillows.

## Spinning seeds

Maple seeds have a single wing and spin as they fall. In 2009, scientists showed that a small whirlwind forms along the front edge of the spinning wing, giving it extra lift and slowing its fall. On a breezy day, a seed dropped from a tall tree can land tens of metres away.

Many Indian trees use the same trick. Sal trees, which cover huge areas of central and northern India, drop seeds with several wing-like flaps that whirl to the ground. Mahogany seeds spin too. The drumstick tree, whose long pods go into sambar, makes seeds with three papery wings.

## The seed that inspired a plane

In the [rainforests](rainforests) of Southeast Asia, a climbing plant called the Javan cucumber, *Alsomitra macrocarpa*, grows high in the trees. Its seeds have thin, curved wings about 13 cm wide. Released, they glide smoothly in wide circles, sometimes a long way from the parent.

In the early 1900s, the Austrian aircraft designer Igo Etrich studied these seeds and used their shape for his early [gliders](gliders) and planes. Seeds were teaching flight before most people had seen an aeroplane. Their gentle glide is a lot like a well-made [paper plane](paper-planes).

## Why bother travelling?

Seeds that travel can reach empty ground after a fire, a flood or a landslide, and start new plants there. Some find new islands. After the volcano of Krakatoa in Indonesia erupted in 1883 and wiped out life on the remaining island, ferns and grasses with wind-blown spores and seeds were among the first to return.

Not all seeds fly. [Coconuts](coconut) float across seas. Many fruits are eaten by birds, which drop the seeds far away, which is how many [banyan](banyan) trees get planted. Burrs hitch rides on animal fur. The garden balsam fires its seeds out of pods that burst when touched. Flying is just one of the ways plants have found to [move](plants-move) without walking.
