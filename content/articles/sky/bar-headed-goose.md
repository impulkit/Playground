## The short answer

Bar-headed geese spend the winter on lakes and rivers in India. Each spring they fly north over the Himalayas to breed in Tibet, Mongolia and Central Asia, then return in autumn.

Up there the air is thin. At 5 km, each breath holds only about half the oxygen it would at sea level, and many people get altitude sickness. The geese cope because their blood grabs oxygen unusually well, and their lungs, hearts and flight [muscles](muscles) are built to make the most of every breath. They usually fly through mountain passes 5 to 6 km high, following the shape of the land instead of staying high all the time.

## Bodies built for thin air

A goose's blood contains haemoglobin, the protein that carries oxygen, just as yours does. But a tiny change in the goose's version makes it hold on to oxygen more tightly, even when there is little of it around. Their lungs are large and, like all birds' lungs, air flows through them in one direction, so fresh air is always passing over. Compare that with the in-and-out breathing inside [your own lungs](lungs-breathe).

Their flight muscles are also packed with tiny blood vessels. That brings oxygen right to the cells doing the hard work of flapping. Their wings are also slightly larger for their weight than those of most geese, which helps them stay up in thin air.

## A roller-coaster ride

In 2015, scientists fitted geese with small devices that recorded their heartbeat, height and wingbeats. They expected the birds to climb high and stay there. Instead, the geese rose and fell with the ground below, like a roller coaster.

Climbing costs a lot of energy, but flying in thin air costs even more, because each wingbeat has less air to push against. Hugging the slopes kept them in denser air. The highest any tracked goose went was about 7.3 km. Some crossings took about seven or eight hours from the plains to the Tibetan plateau.

## Winter guests in India

Bar-headed geese reach India around October and leave again by March or April. They are named for the two dark bars across the back of their white heads. Big flocks winter at Pong Dam lake in [Himachal Pradesh](himachal), and smaller groups spread south across the country to lakes and rivers in states such as Karnataka and Tamil Nadu.

In winter they rest on water by day and often graze at night on grasses and young crops in fields nearby, which does not always please farmers. Like many geese, pairs usually stay together for years and travel as families, with the young birds learning the route from their parents. So when you see a flock on a lake near you, some of those birds may have crossed the Himalayas many times.

## An old journey over young mountains

The [Himalayas](himalaya-form) rose as India pushed into Asia, and they are still rising by a few millimetres a year. The geese may have been making this crossing for a very long time, but nobody knows exactly how long.

[Climbers on Everest](mountaineering) need weeks to get used to the height, and most carry oxygen tanks near the top. When Tenzing Norgay and Edmund Hillary reached the summit in 1953 ([read their story](everest-first)), they breathed bottled oxygen. A goose makes a similar climb in one day, flapping all the way. In winter you can see these geese, with two dark bars on their white heads, on lakes from Himachal Pradesh down to Karnataka. Their journey is one of many described in [bird migration](migration).
