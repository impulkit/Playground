## The short answer

A glider flies the same way any plane does: air flowing over its wings creates [lift](?lift). But with no engine to push it forward, a glider must use gravity. It points slightly downhill through the air, trading height for speed, like a cyclist coasting down a long slope.

On its own, that would mean a slow, gentle trip to the ground. Glider pilots stay up by finding air that is rising faster than the glider is sinking. Warm air columns, wind pushed up over hills, and great waves of air behind mountains can all carry a glider upwards for hours.

## Built to fall slowly

Look at a modern glider and the first thing you notice is the wings. They are very long and very thin, sometimes over 25 m from tip to tip, longer than a cricket pitch. Long, slender wings make lift with very little [drag](?drag), the air resistance that slows a plane down.

The body is smooth and narrow, and the pilot lies almost flat in a tight cockpit. The result is a machine that can travel more than 50 km forward for every kilometre of height it loses. A jumbo jet with its engines switched off manages about 15 to 20.

## Riding invisible lifts

The sun does not heat the ground evenly. A ploughed field, a car park or a rocky hillside warms faster than a lake or a forest. Air above these warm patches rises in columns called thermals, often topped by a small, puffy cloud.

Birds found this out long before people. [Vultures](vultures-india) and kites rarely flap once they are high above an Indian city. They spiral inside thermals, then glide off in search of the next. Glider pilots do exactly the same, watching for circling birds and new clouds. A small instrument called a variometer beeps faster as the glider rises, so the pilot can feel the edges of a thermal without looking down.

Wind blowing against a ridge is forced upwards too. When strong [wind](wind-blows) crosses a mountain range, it can set up huge invisible waves of air behind it, reaching far higher than any thermal.

## Gliders that made history

In the 1890s, the German engineer Otto Lilienthal made around 2,000 flights in gliders he built himself, running down hills near Berlin. His work inspired two bicycle makers from Ohio. Between 1900 and 1902, the [Wright brothers](wright-brothers) flew gliders over the sand dunes of [Kitty Hawk](wright-to-moon), North Carolina, learning how to steer and balance. Only then did they add an engine in 1903.

In 2018, the Perlan 2 glider rode mountain waves over the Andes in Argentina to about 23 km. That is roughly twice the height at which [airliners](airplanes-fly) usually cruise. The two pilots wore pressure suits, like astronauts, because the air up there is far too thin to breathe.

## When big jets become gliders

Every plane can glide, even huge ones. In 1983, an Air Canada Boeing 767 ran out of fuel at 12 km after a mix-up between pounds and kilograms. The crew glided it to a former air force base at Gimli, Manitoba, and everyone on board survived. The plane became known as the Gimli Glider.

The American space shuttle landed as a glider too. It had no engines for landing, so it came in steeply from space and had a single chance to touch down. Pilots practised in special training jets set up to fly like the shuttle, gliding down again and again until the steep approach felt normal.
