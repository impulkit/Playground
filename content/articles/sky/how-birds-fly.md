## The short answer

A bird's wing pushes air down and back. The air pushes the bird up and forward. On each downstroke, the long flight feathers close together into a solid surface that grips the air. On the upstroke, they twist and open slightly, so the wing slips back up more easily.

Flight needs a lot of power and very little weight. Birds have both. Their chest [muscles](muscles) are huge, sometimes a third of their weight. Their skeletons are light, with many hollow bones. Many of these features, including hollow bones, wishbones and feathers, were already present in their dinosaur ancestors, as you can read in [how birds came from dinosaurs](birds-dinosaurs).

## Flapping, gliding, soaring

Different birds fly in different ways. Small birds like sparrows flap fast and often. Their short, rounded wings are good for quick dodges among bushes. Swallows have long, pointed wings, built for speed in open air. [Hummingbirds](hummingbirds) beat their wings up to about 80 times a second and can hover, and even fly backwards.

Big birds save energy. [Eagles](eagles-hunt) and India's [vultures](vultures-india) circle on [thermals](gliders), columns of warm air rising from the sun-heated ground, and barely move their wings for hours. The wandering albatross, with wings about 3.5 m across, glides over the Southern Ocean using the wind just above the waves. It can travel hundreds of kilometres in a day with hardly a flap.

## Light but strong

Many bird bones are hollow, braced inside with tiny struts like the girders of a bridge. Surprisingly, bird bone itself is not lighter than ours. In 2010, a study found it is actually denser. Birds stay light because their bones are thin-walled and many are fused together, so they need fewer of them.

Birds also breathe in a special way. Air sacs, spread through the body and even into some bones, pump fresh air through the [lungs](lungs-breathe) in one direction. This gives flight muscles a steady supply of oxygen. It is one reason birds can fly at heights where people struggle to breathe.

## Taking off, landing and staying up

Taking off is the hardest part. Small birds leap into the air, and that jump from the legs gives a lot of the starting push. Big birds such as swans and albatrosses need a run-up, pattering across the water into the wind before they lift off. Landing is a careful stall: the bird swings its body upright, spreads its wings and tail like airbrakes and reaches forward with its feet.

Some birds hardly land at all. In 2016, scientists fitted [common swifts](swifts-swallows) with tiny data loggers and found that some stayed in the air for about ten months without stopping. They eat insects on the wing and seem to doze while gliding high up. Frigatebirds, too, can stay over the ocean for weeks, taking short naps in flight.

## Flying in teams

Geese, cranes and pelicans often fly in a V. Each bird rides on rising air that spins off the wingtip of the bird ahead, which saves energy. In 2014, scientists who fitted ibises with tiny data loggers found the birds even timed their wingbeats to catch that rising air. The birds also take turns at the front, where flying is hardest.

The same basic idea of pushing air down to get [lift](?lift) keeps a jumbo jet in the sky. Planes do it with fixed wings and engines instead of muscles, as you can read in [how planes fly](airplanes-fly).
