## The short answer

Flying is hard work. A bird must keep its body light, grow big flight muscles and burn a lot of food to stay in the air. When a bird can survive without flying, its body slowly changes over many generations, and the ability to fly can fade away.

That has happened again and again. Some birds grew too big and heavy to fly, like the ostrich and the emu. Some lived on islands with no hunters on the ground, like the dodo. And some, like penguins, [swapped the sky for the sea](ocean-penguins-swim), turning their wings into flippers for "flying" underwater.

## Too big to take off

Ostriches, emus, rheas, cassowaries and kiwis belong to a group of birds called ratites. Their breastbones are flat, without the tall keel that anchors big flight muscles in [flying birds](how-birds-fly). A large male ostrich can weigh well over 100 kg, heavier than most grown-ups.

Instead of wings, they invested in legs. An ostrich can run at about 70 km/h and kick hard enough to scare off a lion. Emus in Australia can run at around 50 km/h. The cassowary of New Guinea and northern Australia has a dagger-like claw on each foot and is treated with great respect by people who live near it. For a big bird on open grassland, running fast and seeing far is a better plan than flapping.

Ostriches once lived in India too. Pieces of their eggshell, some carved into beads by [Stone Age](stone-age) people, have been found at many sites across the country.

## Islands without enemies

On islands with no cats, dogs or snakes, a bird that feeds on the ground has little reason to fly away. Over time, many island birds lost the ability. The [dodo](dodo) of Mauritius, a giant pigeon, was one.

[New Zealand](new-zealand) had almost no land mammals before people arrived, so birds took their place. The kakapo is a heavy, green, flightless parrot that climbs trees and waddles at night. It nearly vanished after people brought [rats, cats and stoats](invasive-species). Thanks to careful protection, and a record breeding season, about 325 kakapo were alive in 2026.

## Flying underwater

Penguins are the most extreme case. Their wings have become short, stiff flippers, and their bones are solid and heavy rather than hollow, which helps them dive. [Emperor penguins](wild-penguin-dads) can reach more than 500 m below the surface.

Why not do both? A study of thick-billed murres, Arctic seabirds that fly and dive, gave a clue. Their wings are a compromise, and flying costs them more energy than almost any bird ever measured. Scientists think penguins' ancestors faced the same squeeze. Better swimming wings made flying harder, until flying was no longer worth it. In return, emperor penguins became superb divers in the [freezing seas of Antarctica](penguins-cold).

## Not quite grounded

Some birds sit in between. Chickens can flutter onto a wall but can't travel far. [Galápagos cormorants](galapagos) have stubby wings and swim for their food. Even strong fliers can become flightless for a while. Ducks and geese shed all their main wing feathers at once each year. They stay grounded for a few weeks, often hiding on lakes, until new ones grow.

Some flightless giants are already gone. The [giant moa](lost-moa) of New Zealand could stretch its head up to about 3.6 m, higher than a basketball hoop. It had no wings at all, and it died out within a few centuries of people arriving.
