## The short answer

Satellite internet sends data between a dish on the ground and a satellite in space. The satellite passes it down to a ground station connected to the wider [internet](internet-how), and the answer travels back the same way.

For decades, this meant using a few big satellites in geostationary orbit, about 36,000 km up. They gave coverage almost everywhere, but each request took about half a second to go there and back. New networks, like SpaceX's Starlink, use thousands of small satellites much closer, mostly around 550 km up. The delay drops to a few hundredths of a second, close to that of a home broadband line.

## The problem with being far away

Light travels about 300,000 km every second, which sounds unbeatable. But when you click a link using a geostationary satellite, your request goes up 36,000 km and down 36,000 km to a ground station. The reply then makes the same trip back. That's more than 140,000 km in total, about half a second at the speed of light.

Half a second doesn't sound like much. In a video call, though, it makes people talk over each other. In an online game, you'd lose every race.

## Low and fast

A Starlink satellite is about 65 times closer than a geostationary one. But at that height, a satellite doesn't stay in one place in the sky. It whizzes round the Earth roughly every hour and a half, crossing from horizon to horizon in a few minutes.

So you need lots of them, spread out like a moving net, with another always rising as one sets. That's why Starlink has launched thousands. The first 60 went up in May 2019. By early 2026 there were more than 10,000 in orbit.

## A flat dish that steers itself

Users have a flat dish about the size of a small suitcase lid. It doesn't move to follow satellites. Instead, it contains hundreds of tiny antennas. By timing their signals very precisely, the dish steers its radio beam electronically, switching from one satellite to the next in a fraction of a second.

Newer satellites talk to each other with [laser](lasers) beams. Data can hop from satellite to satellite across an ocean before coming down, which helps ships, aircraft and remote islands far from any ground station.

## Who uses it

Satellite internet is most useful where cables are hard to lay: mountain villages, deserts, islands, oil rigs, ships and aircraft. In the United States, many rural families who had no fast connection now use it. It has also been used after earthquakes and [hurricanes](hurricanes), when towers on the ground were knocked out.

In 2025, India granted licences for satellite internet companies, including Starlink, to offer services, alongside Indian partners. ISRO and private Indian companies are also building [communication satellites](satellites-india) of their own.

## Crowded skies

Thousands of satellites bring new problems. Astronomers worry because sunlight bouncing off them leaves streaks across telescope photos, and their radio signals can drown out faint signals from space. SpaceX has added darker coatings and sunshades to make its satellites dimmer, but the [night sky](night-sky-dark) is still changing.

There is also the risk of collisions and [space junk](space-junk). Low satellites have one advantage here: if they stop working, the thin upper atmosphere drags them down within a few years, and they burn up on the way. Satellites in higher [orbits](spacecraft-move) can stay there for centuries. Other companies, including Eutelsat OneWeb and Amazon Leo (once called Project Kuiper), are launching their own networks, so the sky will get busier still.
