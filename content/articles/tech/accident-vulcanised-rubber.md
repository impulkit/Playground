## The short answer

Rubber comes from latex, a milky sap that oozes from cuts in the bark of rubber trees. On its own, dried latex is a poor material. It turns sticky and smelly in hot weather and stiff and brittle in cold.

In 1839, an American inventor named Charles Goodyear heated rubber mixed with sulphur. The rubber stopped melting and stopped cracking. The process came to be called vulcanisation, after Vulcan, the Roman god of fire. Almost every rubber thing you touch, from [tyres](tyres) to shoe soles, is made this way.

## The first rubber experts

Long before Europe, people in Mexico and Central America knew rubber well. About 3,600 years ago, they mixed latex with juice from a morning glory vine, which made it bouncier. Scientists at MIT recreated the recipe in the 1990s and found it worked.

They used rubber for sandals, bands and solid balls, some weighing a few kilograms. The balls were for a game played in stone courts by the Olmec, and later by the [Maya and Aztecs](aztecs-maya). When Spanish visitors saw these balls in the 1500s, they were astonished. Nothing in Europe bounced like that.

## A sticky craze

In the early 1830s, Americans went wild for rubber. Factories made [raincoats](raincoats-waterproof), boots and [life jackets](life-jackets). Then summer arrived. Coats melted, boots oozed, and the smell of rotting rubber filled warehouses. Customers threw their goods away and many rubber companies collapsed.

Charles Goodyear became obsessed with fixing it. He mixed rubber with almost anything he could think of, from magnesia to nitric acid. He fell so deep into debt that he was jailed several times for unpaid bills, and his family sometimes went hungry.

## The hot stove

Goodyear's own story goes like this. One winter day in 1839, in Woburn, Massachusetts, a lump of rubber mixed with sulphur touched a hot stove. Instead of melting, it charred at the edge like leather. Just inside the burnt rim was a firm, springy band of rubber that stayed bendy even when he nailed it outside in the frost.

It took him years more to get the heat and timing right. He patented the process in the USA in 1844.

## What sulphur does

Rubber is made of [polymers](?polymer), very long chain molecules tangled like cooked noodles. When warm, the chains slide past each other, and the rubber flows. When sulphur is heated with them, it forms tiny bridges between the chains, linking them into a net.

Now the rubber can stretch, because the chains uncoil, but it snaps back, because the bridges hold. A little sulphur makes soft rubber bands. Much more makes a hard, black material called ebonite, once used for bowling balls and combs.

## A race for the patent

In England, Thomas Hancock studied samples of Goodyear's rubber and worked out a similar method. He patented it in Britain in 1843, a few weeks before Goodyear applied there. A friend of Hancock's suggested the name "vulcanisation". Goodyear spent years in court, and despite his discovery he died in debt in 1860.

## Seeds that crossed oceans

Rubber trees grow wild in the Amazon. In 1876, the Englishman Henry Wickham shipped about 70,000 seeds from [Brazil](brazil-amazon) to London. Seedlings went on to Sri Lanka and Malaysia, and plantations spread across Asia. Today most of the world's natural rubber comes from South-East Asia, and in India, [Kerala](kerala) grows most of it.

Roughly half of all rubber used today is natural, and the rest is synthetic, made from oil. Most of both ends up in tyres. Rubber also lives on your desk: before rubber, people rubbed out pencil marks with bread, and the story of the [eraser](erasers) began when someone tried a lump of latex instead.
