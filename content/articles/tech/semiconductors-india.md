## The short answer

Almost every modern machine runs on [chips](chips-silicon): tiny slices of silicon carrying millions or billions of switches called [transistors](?transistor). India uses huge numbers of them in phones, cars, trains, power grids and satellites, but it buys nearly all of them from abroad.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, a global chip shortage showed how risky that is. Factories in many countries had to stop making cars because a few small chips were missing. So India, like the United States and Europe, decided to build chip factories at home. In December 2021 the government launched the India Semiconductor Mission and promised large subsidies to companies willing to build plants.

## Why chips are so hard to make

Making a modern chip may be the most difficult manufacturing job on Earth. It starts with [sand](sand-where), purified into silicon so pure that only about one atom in a billion is something else. The silicon is grown into a large crystal, sliced into thin discs called wafers, and polished like a mirror.

Patterns are then printed onto the wafer with light, layer after layer, hundreds of steps in all. The smallest features are thousands of times thinner than a human hair. All of this happens in a cleanroom, where workers wear full-body suits and the air is filtered constantly, because one speck of dust can ruin a chip.

A factory, called a fab, that makes the most advanced chips can cost more than 1 lakh crore rupees (over US$10 billion). That is more than many countries spend on their whole army in a year.

## Designers yes, factories no

India is already strong at chip design. Big companies from the United States, Europe and Asia run large design centres in [Bengaluru](bengaluru), [Hyderabad](hyderabad), Noida and other cities, and Indian engineers work on many of the world's best-known chips. The designs, though, are mostly sent abroad to be manufactured.

The world's most advanced chips are made mainly in Taiwan, by a company called TSMC. [South Korea](seoul), the United States, Japan and China make many others. Relying on a few places is risky. An earthquake, a war or a trade dispute could cut off supply. That is why many countries now want factories of their own, and why chips have become a big topic in [world trade](trade-world).

## The plants being built

In 2023 the American memory-chip maker Micron announced a plant at Sanand in Gujarat, near [Ahmedabad](ahmedabad). It doesn't make chips from scratch. It assembles and tests them, a step called packaging. Several other packaging plants have been announced in Gujarat and Assam.

In 2024, Tata Electronics announced India's first large commercial fab at Dholera in Gujarat, working with PSMC, a chip company from Taiwan. It is planned to make chips for cars, phones and power equipment, not the very smallest kind.

India's chip story isn't brand new. The Semi-Conductor Laboratory in Mohali, [Punjab](punjab), began making chips in the 1980s. In 1989 a fire badly damaged it, setting India's plans back by years while other countries raced ahead. Today it makes chips for ISRO's rockets and satellites and for defence.

## What could go wrong

Building fabs needs a steady supply of clean water, uninterrupted power, special gases and chemicals, and thousands of trained technicians. Taiwan and South Korea spent decades building all this. Some experts think India should focus on packaging and older, simpler chips first, which cars and appliances use in huge numbers. Others want it to aim for the most advanced chips quickly. For a country of more than 140 crore people that buys hundreds of millions of [phones](smartphones), the stakes are large either way.
