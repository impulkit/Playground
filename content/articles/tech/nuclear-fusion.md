## The short answer

Nuclear fusion is when two very light [atoms](?atom) crash together so hard that their centres, called nuclei, join into one heavier atom. A little bit of mass goes missing in the process, and it turns into a huge amount of energy. This is what [Einstein's](einstein) famous equation, E = mc², describes.

Fusion powers the [Sun](sun-what) and [every other star](star-life-cycle). In the Sun's core, hydrogen atoms fuse into helium, giving off the light and heat that reach us eight minutes later. Scientists have spent about 70 years trying to make fusion work on Earth as [a clean source of electricity](future-energy). It is one of the hardest engineering problems ever tackled.

## Why it's so hard

All atomic nuclei carry positive electric charge, and like charges push each other away. To make two nuclei fuse, you have to slam them together fast enough to overcome that push.

The Sun manages this with crushing [gravity](gravity-fall) and a core temperature of about 15 million °C. On Earth we can't copy the Sun's weight, so we make up for it with heat. Fusion machines must reach over 100 million °C. At that temperature, atoms break apart into a glowing soup of charged particles called [plasma](?plasma). No container could touch it without melting, and the plasma would cool and stop fusing the moment it touched a wall. The fuel is usually two heavier kinds of hydrogen, called deuterium and tritium, which fuse more easily than ordinary hydrogen.

## Holding a star in a magnetic bottle

The most common answer is a machine called a tokamak, first designed in the Soviet Union in the 1950s. It is shaped like a doughnut. Powerful [magnets](magnets-how) wrapped around it create invisible fields that hold the plasma in a ring, floating away from the walls.

The biggest tokamak ever is being built in southern France. Called ITER, Latin for "the way", it is a joint project of more than 30 countries, including India, the United States, the European Union, China, Japan, [Russia](russia) and [South Korea](seoul). India's share includes the cryostat, a giant steel vessel that surrounds the machine, built by the company Larsen & Toubro. India also runs its own smaller tokamaks at the Institute for Plasma Research in Gandhinagar, Gujarat.

## A breakthrough with lasers

There is another approach. At the National Ignition Facility in California, 192 giant [lasers](lasers) fire at a fuel pellet smaller than a peppercorn, crushing and heating it in a billionth of a second.

In an experiment on 5 December 2022, for the first time, the fusion reaction there released more energy than the lasers delivered to the fuel. It was a historic moment. But the lasers themselves needed far more electricity from the grid than the reaction produced, so there is still a long road to a working power station.

## The prize

Fusion fuel can come from seawater and from lithium, so supplies would last a very long time. Fusion makes no carbon dioxide while running, so it would not add to [climate change](climate-change). It also can't run out of control the way some people fear, because if anything goes wrong, the plasma simply cools and stops.

Scientists like to joke that fusion power has been "30 years away" for decades. Many private companies now say they will do it sooner. Others are more cautious. If it works, fusion could one day give the world electricity from the same process that lights the stars.
