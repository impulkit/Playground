## The short answer

Staying safe online mostly comes down to [a few habits](habits). Keep personal details private, such as your full name, address, school, phone number and passwords. Be careful about who you talk to, because people online aren't always who they say they are. Think before you click links or download files, especially from surprise messages. And if anything makes you feel worried, scared or uncomfortable, tell a trusted adult.

None of this means the [internet](internet-how) is a bad place. It is where people learn, play, create and stay in touch. Safety habits are like looking both ways before crossing a road: quick, simple and worth doing every time.

## What to keep private

Some information can be used to find you in real life, or to pretend to be you. Your home address, school name, phone number and daily routine fall into this group. So do photos that show your school uniform or the street sign outside your house.

Your [passwords](passwords) and one-time codes are private too, even from friends. A friend today might be a former friend next year, and some "friends" online are strangers. Sharing a password with a parent or guardian is different, because they're responsible for keeping you safe.

## Tricks to watch for

Many online dangers are tricks rather than hacking. Phishing messages pretend to come from a bank, a game company or a friend, and ask you to click a link or share details. They often try to rush you, with warnings that your account will be deleted or news that you've won a prize.

Warning signs include spelling mistakes, strange web addresses, [offers that seem too good to be true](tech-spam-email), and requests for passwords, codes or money. In India, some scammers phone people pretending to be from a bank and ask for the OTP sent to their phone. Real [banks](money-banks) never ask for it. The government runs a national cybercrime helpline, 1930, and a website for reporting fraud.

## People are not always who they seem

Online, anyone can pick a name and a photo. Someone who claims to be 12 might be much older. Most people are friendly, but a few try to win a child's trust, then ask for photos, secrets or a meeting.

Clear rules help. Don't meet anyone from online in person without a parent's permission and company. If someone asks you to keep your chats secret, sends things that upset you, or pressures you to share pictures, stop replying and tell a trusted adult. You won't be in trouble for telling. ## When things go wrong online

[Cyberbullying](cyberbullying) means using messages, posts or games to hurt or embarrass someone, often again and again. It can feel worse than other bullying because it follows you home on your phone.

If it happens to you, don't reply to [the bully](bullying). Take screenshots as a record, block the person and report them using the app's tools. Then tell an adult you trust. If you see it happening to someone else, a kind message to them, or a report, can help.

Remember too that not everything you see online is true. Learning to spot [false information](fake-news) is part of staying safe.

## Age rules and privacy laws

Most big [social media](social-media) apps say users must be at least 13. This comes largely from a US law that limits how companies can [collect data](app-safety) from younger children. In India, the Digital Personal Data Protection Act of 2023 says companies need a parent's permission before handling the personal data of anyone under 18.

Time matters as well as safety. Long hours online, especially late at night, can affect sleep and mood, as [screen time](screen-time) research shows. Many families agree simple rules together, such as no phones at meals or in bedrooms at night.
