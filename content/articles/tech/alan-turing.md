## The short answer

Alan Turing was a British mathematician who helped invent the idea of the modern computer. In 1936 he described a simple imaginary machine, now called a Turing machine, that could carry out any step-by-step calculation. Every computer, phone and smartwatch works on that principle.

During the Second World War he was a leading codebreaker at Bletchley Park, helping read secret German messages. He later asked whether machines could think, a question at the heart of [artificial intelligence](ai-what). His life ended sadly and early, but his reputation has grown and grown.

## A boy who did his own experiments

Turing was born in London in 1912. His parents worked in India, where his father was in the Indian Civil Service, so Alan and his brother were mostly raised by a family in England. He was untidy, shy and always experimenting. At boarding school, teachers complained he cared more about science than anything else.

He was also a very fast runner. While at Bletchley Park, he sometimes ran up to about 64 kilometres to London for meetings. In 1947 he ran a [marathon](running-marathon) in 2 hours 46 minutes, close to the time needed for the British Olympic team.

## A machine made of ideas

In 1936, aged 24, Turing wrote a paper about which maths problems can be solved by following fixed rules. To answer it, he imagined a machine reading and writing symbols on an endless strip of tape, following a short list of instructions.

He showed that one such machine, given the right instructions, could copy what any other did. That is the idea behind a computer that can run any program. Every [algorithm](algorithms) on your phone fits inside it.

## The codebreakers

In 1939, as the [Second World War](world-war-two) began, Turing joined Britain's secret codebreaking centre at Bletchley Park, north of London. The German forces used a machine called Enigma to scramble their radio messages. Its settings changed every day, giving millions upon millions of possible combinations.

Polish mathematicians had already cracked earlier versions and built a machine to help. Building on their work, Turing designed a much faster machine called the Bombe, which tested possible settings at high speed. He also led work on the hardest code of all, used by [German submarines](submarines) in the Atlantic. Reading those messages helped ships carrying food and supplies avoid attack.

## Spots, stripes and thinking machines

After the war, Turing designed one of the first stored-program computers and then worked on early computers in Manchester. In 1950 he wrote a paper asking "Can machines think?" and proposed the imitation game, now known as the Turing test.

In 1952 he turned to biology. He suggested how simple chemicals spreading and reacting in a growing animal could make spots and stripes. Scientists have since found his ideas at work in the patterns of fish, shells and perhaps even [tiger stripes](tiger-stripes).

## Late apology

In 1952 Turing was convicted under British laws of the time that made relationships between men a crime. He lost his security clearance and was forced to undergo hormone treatment. He died in 1954, aged 41. An inquest said he had taken his own life, though some historians have questioned this.

Britain later changed those laws. In 2009 the Prime Minister made a public apology, and in 2013 Turing received a royal pardon. Since 2021, his face has appeared on the Bank of England's £50 note. The top prize in computer science, the Turing Award, has carried his name since 1966.
