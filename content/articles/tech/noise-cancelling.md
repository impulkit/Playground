## The short answer

Sound is a wave of air pressure: tiny squeezes and stretches of air rushing towards your ear. Noise-cancelling [headphones](sound-recording) have small [microphones](microphones) that listen to the noise around you. A chip inside works out the exact opposite wave, with a stretch wherever the noise has a squeeze, and plays it through the [speaker](loudspeakers).

When the two waves meet, they cancel each other out, and you hear much less noise. This is called destructive interference. It works best on low, steady sounds like the drone of a plane engine, the hum of an air conditioner or the rumble of a Mumbai local train.

## Waves that cancel

[Sound travels](sound-travel) as pressure waves. Picture a wave drawn as a wiggly line, with peaks and dips. If two identical waves line up peak to peak, they add up and get louder. If one is flipped, so its peaks line up with the other's dips, they cancel.

You can see the same thing with [water waves](waves-how). When ripples from two sides meet, some spots rise higher and others go flat. [Noise-cancelling headphones](how-headphones) try to make your ear one of those flat spots.

## Listening and answering, fast

To cancel noise, the headphones have to react extremely quickly. A microphone on the outside hears noise coming. Another inside the ear cup checks what's actually reaching your ear. The chip compares them and adjusts the anti-noise thousands of times a second.

Low sounds have long, slow waves, which are easier to predict and match. High sounds have short, fast waves, and voices and clatter change suddenly. So electronics handle the low rumble, and the cushions and ear tips do the rest by blocking higher sounds, a method called passive isolation. Together they cut a lot of noise, but never all of it.

## From an old patent to the cockpit

In the 1930s a German physicist, Paul Lueg, patented the idea of cancelling sound with an opposite sound wave. But the electronics of his time were too slow and bulky to make it practical.

The idea took off with pilots. In 1978 the American engineer Amar Bose, founder of the Bose company, was on a flight and found his headphones useless against the cabin noise. He started working on noise-cancelling headsets. In 1986 the pilots Dick Rutan and Jeana Yeager flew the Voyager aircraft non-stop around the world in nine days without refuelling. They wore early noise-cancelling headsets to protect their ears from the engine roar. Pilots and [aircraft](airplanes-fly) crews were the first big users.

## Protecting your ears

Loud noise over time damages the tiny hair cells in your [inner ear](ears-hear), and they don't grow back. This can lead to [hearing loss](hearing-loss). On a noisy bus or plane, people often turn their music up very loud to drown out the background. With noise cancelling, they can hear clearly at a lower, safer volume. The World Health Organization suggests keeping volume low and taking listening breaks.

There's a safety side too. Blocking out sound can make people miss car horns, bicycle bells or announcements. Many headphones now have a "transparency" mode that uses the outside microphones to let important sounds back in.

## Noise cancelling everywhere

The same idea is used beyond headphones. Many phones use extra microphones to cancel background noise during calls, so your friend hears [your voice](voice) and not the traffic. Some cars cancel engine boom inside the cabin through the speakers. Engineers are also testing it in [quiet rooms](soundproofing), air vents and even windows, though cancelling sound across a whole space is much harder than inside a small ear cup.
