## The short answer

The first true battery was invented by the Italian scientist Alessandro Volta around 1799. He stacked discs of zinc and copper or silver, with pieces of cloth or cardboard soaked in salty water between each pair. When he connected the top and bottom of the stack with a wire, a steady electric current flowed. He described it in a letter to the Royal Society in London in 1800.

Before Volta, scientists could only make [electricity](electricity-made) in short sparks, by [rubbing things together](static-electricity). His "pile" gave a continuous flow for the first time. Every battery since, from torch cells to the lithium battery in a phone, works on his basic idea: two different materials and a chemical between them.

## An argument about frogs

In the 1780s, Luigi Galvani, a doctor in Bologna, was studying [frogs](frogs-monsoon). He noticed that a dead frog's leg twitched when he touched it with two different metals at once. Galvani decided the frog's body must contain "animal electricity".

Volta, a physics professor in Pavia, was not convinced. He thought the electricity came from the two metals touching the wet frog, and the leg simply reacted to it. The two men argued politely through letters and papers for years. To settle it, Volta built his pile with no animal at all. It made electricity. In a way, both were partly right: [our nerves](nervous-system) really do use electrical signals, which Galvani had spotted.

## How a battery makes current

In a battery, one material gives up tiny charged particles called electrons more easily than the other. A chemical in between, called the electrolyte, lets charged atoms move inside the battery but stops electrons crossing directly. So the electrons take the only route left: out through a wire, through your torch bulb or toy, and back in at the other end. That flow is electric current.

The push behind the current is measured in volts, named after Volta. One of his zinc and copper pairs gave roughly one volt. Stacking more pairs gave a bigger push.

## Batteries you can recharge

Volta's pile ran down and couldn't be refilled. In 1859, the French physicist Gaston Planté invented the lead-acid battery, which could be recharged by pushing current back through it. Updated versions still start most petrol cars and back up inverters in Indian homes during [power cuts](what-if-no-electricity).

In 1866, the French engineer Georges Leclanché invented a cell that later developed into the "dry cell", with a paste instead of a liquid. That made batteries safe to carry around and led to the torch batteries and AA cells you know today.

## The lithium revolution

In the 1970s, an English chemist working in the USA, Stanley Whittingham, built a battery using lithium, a very light metal. It was powerful but could catch fire. John Goodenough, working in Oxford, found a better material for one side of the battery in 1980. In 1985, Akira Yoshino in Japan made a safer, practical version. Sony began selling lithium-ion batteries in 1991.

[Lithium-ion batteries](batteries-future) are light and hold a lot of energy, and they can be recharged hundreds of times. They made mobile phones, laptops and [electric cars](electric-cars) possible. In 2019, the three scientists shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Goodenough was 97.

Batteries also store power from [solar panels](solar-panels) for use at night, a big part of plans for cleaner energy. And Volta's name is printed on every one, in the little "V" on the label.
