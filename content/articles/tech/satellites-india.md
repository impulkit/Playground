## The short answer

India built its first satellites [with very little money](india-space-budget), borrowed equipment and a lot of determination. The space programme began in the 1960s under the scientist [Vikram Sarabhai](vikram-sarabhai), who believed space technology could help a poor country with [weather forecasts](weather-forecasts), education and communication.

Indian engineers first learned with small [sounding rockets](rockets-how), which shoot up and fall back down. Then they designed a satellite, [Aryabhata](space-aryabhata-satellite), in a group of sheds in Peenya, an industrial area of [Bengaluru](bengaluru). It was launched on a Soviet rocket on 19 April 1975. Five years later, in 1980, India launched a satellite on a rocket of its own.

## A church by the sea

In the early 1960s, scientists chose Thumba, a fishing village near Thiruvananthapuram in [Kerala](kerala), as India's first rocket launch site. Thumba lies close to the magnetic equator, which made it a good place to study the upper atmosphere.

The local church, St Mary Magdalene, was handed over to the scientists. It became an office and workshop, and the bishop's house became a laboratory. On 21 November 1963, a small American-made rocket called a Nike-Apache rose from Thumba. A famous photograph shows [rocket parts being carried on a bicycle](space-rocket-bicycle). The church is now a space museum.

## Building Aryabhata

In 1969, the Indian Space Research Organisation, ISRO, was formed. Soon after, its engineers started work on a satellite. Most had never built one. They had to make or adapt almost everything themselves, from solar panels to radio equipment.

Aryabhata, named after the great Indian mathematician [Aryabhata](aryabhata), was a 26-sided shape about 1.4 metres across, covered in solar cells. It weighed about 360 kg. The Soviet Union offered to launch it, and on 19 April 1975 it lifted off from a site in Russia.

After a few days in orbit, a power problem stopped its experiments. But the satellite had proved India could design, build and control a spacecraft. The Reserve Bank of India later printed Aryabhata on the back of the two-rupee note.

## A rocket of its own

India also needed its own rocket to reach orbit. The SLV-3 project was led by a young engineer named A.P.J. Abdul Kalam, who later became President of India. Its first attempt, in 1979, failed and fell into the sea. Kalam later wrote that ISRO's chairman, Satish Dhawan, faced the press himself and took the blame. When the next launch succeeded, Dhawan let Kalam announce the good news.

On 18 July 1980, SLV-3 lifted off from Sriharikota, an island off the coast of Andhra Pradesh, and placed the small Rohini satellite in orbit. India had joined the small group of countries that could launch satellites on their own rockets.

## A satellite on a bullock cart

In 1981 came APPLE, India's first experimental communications satellite. It was launched by a European rocket. Before launch, engineers needed to test its antenna away from metal objects that might interfere. So they put it on a wooden bullock cart. A photograph of the shiny satellite riding the cart became one of the best-known pictures in Indian science.

Those small beginnings led to [INSAT satellites](satellite-tv) for TV and weather in the 1980s, and to the PSLV rocket, which in 2017 launched 104 satellites on a single flight. The country that once carried rockets on [bicycles](bicycles-history) later reached the Moon and Mars.
