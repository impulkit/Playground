## The short answer

ISRO, the Indian Space Research Organisation, launches most of its rockets from the [Satish Dhawan Space Centre](launch-sites) at Sriharikota. It is an island on the coast of Andhra Pradesh, about 80 km north of Chennai. A rocket is put together in a huge building, rolled to the launch pad, filled with fuel, and launched when a [computer-controlled countdown](maths-countdown) reaches zero.

The rocket burns its fuel in stages. Each stage fires, empties and falls away into the sea, making the rocket lighter. Within about 20 minutes, the final stage releases the satellite into [orbit](?orbit) around the Earth.

## Why an island in the east?

Sriharikota sits close to the equator, at about 13.7° north. The Earth spins eastwards, and near the equator the ground moves at more than 1,600 km/h. A rocket launched east carries that speed with it, a free head start that saves fuel.

The island also has the Bay of Bengal to its east. Spent stages and any failed rocket fall into empty sea, not on villages. The site was chosen in the late 1960s and first used in 1971.

## Bicycles and a church

India's space story began at Thumba, [a fishing village](people-fishing-villages) near Thiruvananthapuram, which sits almost on the magnetic equator, a good place to study the upper [atmosphere](?atmosphere). On 21 November 1963, scientists launched a small American-made sounding rocket there. A local church became an office, and rocket parts were famously carried on [bicycles](bicycles-history) and a bullock cart.

The programme was led by [Vikram Sarabhai](vikram-sarabhai). A young engineer named [A.P.J. Abdul Kalam](apj-kalam) later led the team that built SLV-3. On 18 July 1980 it put the Rohini satellite into orbit, making India one of only a handful of countries able to launch its own satellites.

## Stages, solid and liquid

ISRO's workhorse is the PSLV, the Polar Satellite Launch Vehicle. It has four stages that alternate: [solid fuel](rocket-fuel), then liquid, then solid, then liquid. Solid fuel is like a giant firework packed with a rubbery mix that burns fiercely. Liquid engines can be throttled and switched off, which helps place a satellite precisely.

For heavier loads, ISRO uses the GSLV and the LVM3. Their top stages are cryogenic, burning [liquid hydrogen](hydrogen-fuel) and liquid oxygen chilled to below minus 180°C. India spent years developing this hard technology on its own. In July 2023, an LVM3 launched [Chandrayaan-3](chandrayaan), which landed near the Moon's south pole the next month.

## The countdown

For days before a launch, teams check every valve, wire and computer. The fuel is loaded, the launch pad is cleared, and engineers in the Mission Control Centre watch hundreds of readings on their screens. In the final minutes, an automatic launch sequence takes over. If any reading looks wrong, the computer can stop the countdown.

After lift-off, ground stations across India and abroad track the rocket. The [rocket's](rockets-how) path is planned in advance, but small engines and steering nozzles keep it on course, adjusting many times a second.

## 104 at once

On 15 February 2017, a PSLV carried 104 satellites into orbit in one launch, a world record at the time. Most were small imaging satellites the size of a shoebox, owned by an American company. They were released one after another over about ten minutes, each into a slightly different path so they wouldn't bump into each other.

ISRO has also launched hundreds of satellites for other countries. Its rockets are thrown away after one use, but ISRO is now testing a winged, [reusable](reusable-rockets) vehicle called Pushpak that glides back to land on a runway like a plane.
