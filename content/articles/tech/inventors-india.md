## The short answer

Many everyday things began in India. The biggest is the way we write numbers: ten digits, 0 to 9, where a digit's place tells you its value. That system, with [zero](zero-invent) as a real number, was worked out by Indian mathematicians and carried west by Arab scholars.

India also gave the world cotton cloth, an early form of chess, crystal sugar, the board game we call snakes and ladders, and some of the earliest written guides to surgery. [In modern times, Indian inventors](inventions-india-modern) have built [cheap artificial legs](prosthetics), helped invent fibre optics and created a way to send money by phone that the rest of the world now studies.

## Cotton, sugar and games

Archaeologists have found traces of cotton thread in the Indus region from over 5,000 years ago. Greek writers who came to India were amazed by a "wool" that grew on trees. For centuries, Indian cloth such as Dhaka muslin was so fine and so prized that it was traded across Asia, Africa and Europe.

By around the Gupta period, Indians had also learned to boil sugarcane juice until it formed crystals. The Sanskrit word khanda, meaning a piece of sugar, is probably where the English word "[candy](candy-history)" comes from.

Two [board games](board-games) also started here. [Chaturanga](chess-india), a battle game with elephants, [chariots](wheel-to-chariot), horses and foot soldiers, most likely grew into [chess](pop-chess-pieces). Gyan chaupar, a game about good and bad deeds, became [snakes and ladders](snakes-ladders) once it reached Britain.

Indian metalworkers were famous too. From at least around 2,000 years ago, smiths in southern India made a very hard steel called wootz, sealed in small clay pots and heated for hours. Traders carried it to the Middle East, where it was forged into the patterned "Damascus" swords that later impressed European travellers. Modern scientists have studied wootz blades and found tiny structures inside that help explain their strength.

## Healing and helping

The ancient physician [Sushruta](sushruta) is known for a Sanskrit text that describes many surgical tools and operations. One of them is a way of rebuilding a damaged nose using a flap of skin from the cheek. Doctors still recognise it as an early form of plastic surgery.

A modern example comes from [Jaipur](jaipur). In 1969, Dr P. K. Sethi and a craftsman named Ram Chandra Sharma designed a rubber artificial foot that was cheap, waterproof and flexible enough to squat, sit cross-legged and walk barefoot. The Jaipur foot has since been fitted for well over a million people around the world, often free of charge.

## Light through glass

Narinder Singh Kapany was born in Moga, [Punjab](punjab), in 1926. In the 1950s, working in London and later the United States, he did important early experiments on sending light along thin glass fibres. In 1960 he wrote an article that made the term "fibre optics" popular. Today, glass fibres carry most of the [internet](internet-how)'s data across oceans and cities.

Ajay Bhatt, who grew up in India and later worked at the American company Intel, was one of the team who designed the USB plug used to connect devices.

## Money on a phone

In 2016, India launched the Unified Payments Interface, or [UPI](upi-payments). It lets anyone with a bank account and a phone send money instantly by scanning a QR code. A chaiwala on a Pune street corner can take payments as easily as a big shop. Several countries have since signed agreements to use or link with it.
