## The short answer

A laser is a device that makes a very narrow, powerful beam of light, all of a single colour. Ordinary light, from a bulb or the Sun, is a jumble of colours, with its waves going in every direction and out of step with each other. Laser light is neat. Its waves are the same [wavelength](?wavelength), travel in the same direction and rise and fall together, like soldiers marching in step.

Because of this, a laser beam spreads out very little. It can travel long distances, carry information, measure things precisely or focus energy onto a tiny spot. The word laser comes from "Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation".

## How the light gets in step

Inside a laser is a material such as a crystal, a gas or a special piece of glass. Energy is pumped in, often with a flash of light or an electric current. This lifts the material's atoms into an excited, high-energy state.

When one excited atom gives off a tiny packet of light, called a photon, that photon can nudge a neighbouring excited atom into giving off an identical photon, going the same way. This is "stimulated emission", an idea [Albert Einstein](einstein) worked out on paper in 1917. Mirrors at each end of the laser bounce the light back and forth, so the photons multiply in a chain reaction. One mirror lets a little light leak out. That leak is the beam.

## A ruby in California

For decades, stimulated emission was only theory. In 1960, Theodore Maiman, a physicist at Hughes Research Laboratories in Malibu, California, built the first working laser. He used a small rod of pink ruby, wrapped in a coiled flash lamp like those used in cameras.

On 16 May 1960 it produced pulses of deep red light. At first nobody knew quite what to do with it, and some called it "a solution looking for a problem". It turned out to solve hundreds.

## Mirrors on the Moon

In July 1969, the [Apollo 11 astronauts](moon-landing-sites) set up a panel of special mirrors on the Moon. Later Apollo missions and two Soviet [robot rovers](tech-robots-space) left more. These reflectors bounce light straight back to where it came from.

Observatories on Earth fire powerful laser pulses at them and time how long the light takes to return, about two and a half seconds. From this, scientists can work out the distance to the [Moon](apollo-moon) to within a few centimetres. The measurements show the Moon is drifting away from Earth by about 3.8 cm a year, roughly as fast as your [fingernails](nails) grow.

## Lasers all around you

You probably use lasers every day without noticing. Supermarket scanners use a laser to read [barcodes](barcodes). Internet data races under oceans as flashes of laser light inside glass fibres thinner than a hair. [Laser printers](printers) use a beam to draw each page.

Doctors use lasers to reshape the front of the eye so people can see without [glasses](lenses-glasses), and to treat some skin conditions. Factories use high-powered lasers to cut steel and weld car bodies. Music once came on CDs, read by a tiny laser. [Self-driving cars](self-driving) use lasers to measure distances to everything around them.

Even a small laser pointer can damage eyes. Pointing one at a pilot is dangerous and is a serious crime in many countries, including the United States.
