## The short answer

India built a set of digital tools that work together, and made them cheap enough for almost anyone. The first was [Aadhaar](aadhaar), a 12-digit ID number linked to [fingerprints and iris scans](biometrics), which began in 2010. Then came hundreds of millions of new bank accounts. Then very cheap mobile data. And then [UPI](upi-payments), which lets anyone send money from a phone in seconds.

Experts often call the first three the "JAM trinity": Jan Dhan accounts, Aadhaar and Mobile phones. Because they were built as shared public systems, any bank or app could plug into them. That's a big reason India went from mostly cash to billions of digital payments a month within about a decade.

## An ID for everyone

Before Aadhaar, many Indians, especially the poor and people who had moved for work, had no official proof of who they were. That made it hard to open a bank account or get a SIM card. In September 2010, the first Aadhaar numbers were given out in Tembhli, a village in Maharashtra's Nandurbar district.

Today more than 130 crore people have one. It lets someone prove who they are with a fingerprint at a ration shop or a bank, without carrying papers.

## A bank account in every home

In 2014 the government launched the Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana, a drive to open free [bank accounts](money-banks) for people who had none. Within a few years, hundreds of millions were opened. By 2023 there were more than 50 crore of these accounts.

With an account linked to Aadhaar, the government could send money like [pensions](pensions-old-age), scholarships and gas subsidies straight to people, instead of through layers of officials. This is called direct benefit transfer, and it cut out a lot of middlemen.

## Data for the price of a cup of tea

In 2016 a new mobile company, Jio, offered free calls and very cheap data. Other companies had to cut prices too. Within a few years, mobile data in India became among the cheapest in the world. Cheap [smartphones](smartphones), many made in India, spread to small towns and [villages](villages-india).

Suddenly, people who had never owned a computer were watching videos, using maps and joining video calls. Many skipped computers altogether and went straight to phones.

## UPI changes how India pays

In 2016 the National Payments Corporation of India launched the Unified Payments Interface. It lets people send money using a simple ID, a phone number or a QR code, from any bank to any bank, for free. Shopkeepers only need a printed QR code, [no card machine](nfc-tap).

UPI took off after November 2016, when the government withdrew [₹500 and ₹1,000 notes](life-money-world) and people were short of cash. It grew even faster during the COVID-19 pandemic. By 2025, it handled more than 15 billion payments a month, from paying for chai to buying train tickets.

## Questions and gaps

Not everyone welcomed every part. Critics worried that Aadhaar could be used to track people and that their data might leak. In 2017 the Supreme Court ruled that [privacy](privacy) is a fundamental right, and in 2018 it upheld Aadhaar but limited where it could be demanded. India passed a data protection law in 2023.

There are other problems. Online fraud has grown alongside digital payments. Fingerprint scanners sometimes fail for labourers with worn fingertips, leaving people without rations. And many Indians, especially women and older people in villages, still aren't online. Even so, other countries, from [Singapore](singapore) to France, now let visitors pay with UPI, and several are studying how India built its system.
