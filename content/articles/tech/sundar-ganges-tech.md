## The short answer

Several of the world's biggest technology companies are run by people who grew up in India. Sundar Pichai leads Google and its parent company Alphabet. Satya Nadella leads Microsoft. Shantanu Narayen has led Adobe since 2007 and is handing over in December 2026 to Anil Chakravarthy, another IIT graduate. Arvind Krishna leads IBM. Many more Indian-born engineers run teams and start companies in [Silicon Valley](silicon-valley).

There isn't one simple reason. Experts point to a mix: India's strong engineering colleges, the English language, a culture that prizes education, and United States immigration rules that welcomed highly educated engineers. Timing mattered too. These leaders arrived just as the computer and internet industries were growing fastest.

## Schools built for a new nation

After independence in 1947, Prime Minister [Jawaharlal Nehru](nehru) wanted India to train its own scientists and engineers. The first Indian Institute of Technology opened in 1951 at Kharagpur in West Bengal, in a building where the British had once imprisoned freedom fighters. More IITs followed in Bombay, Madras, Kanpur and Delhi.

Entry was fiercely competitive, and still is. Each year more than a million students sit the main engineering entrance test, and only a small share get IIT seats. Graduates were very well trained in maths and engineering. Sundar Pichai studied at IIT Kharagpur. Satya Nadella studied at Manipal, in [Karnataka](karnataka).

## The door to America opens

Until 1965 the United States allowed very few immigrants from Asia. A new law that year removed those limits and favoured people with skills. Indian doctors, scientists and engineers began moving to the United States for further study, often a master's degree, then stayed to work.

From the 1990s, special work visas brought tens of thousands more Indian engineers each year, many to work in software. Speaking [English](english-spread), which India's schools and universities had used since British rule, made the move much easier. Their families joined them, and their children grew up as Americans, one strand among the many [immigration stories](immigration-stories) that shaped the country.

## From Chennai to California

Sundar Pichai grew up in [Chennai](chennai), where his father was an electrical engineer and his mother had worked as a stenographer. He has said his family didn't get a telephone until he was about 12, and he remembered phone numbers easily. He went to Stanford University on a scholarship, joined Google in 2004, and became its chief executive in 2015.

Satya Nadella grew up in [Hyderabad](hyderabad), loved cricket and studied electrical engineering before moving to the United States. He joined Microsoft in 1992 and became its chief executive in 2014.

## Networks and selection

Once a few Indians reached top jobs, they helped others. Indian engineers formed groups to support each other, share advice and invest in each other's startups.

There's also what scientists call selection. The Indians who moved to America were not typical Indians. They were mostly those who had won places at top colleges and could pass strict visa rules. So their success says more about education and opportunity than about Indians in general. Indian Americans now have one of the highest average household incomes of any group in the United States, which reflects this careful selection.

## Brain drain or brain gain?

For decades, people in India worried about "brain drain": the country paid to train engineers, who then left. Others now talk of "brain circulation". Many engineers have returned to India to start companies, and India receives more money sent home by workers abroad than any other country. India's own tech giants, such as TCS, Infosys and Wipro, grew into [global companies](global-companies), and many of India's [startups](indian-startups) were founded by people who once worked in the United States.
