## The short answer

A data centre is a building packed with computers called [servers](?server). They are stacked in tall metal cabinets called racks, row after row, often in halls as big as a football field. Each server stores files or runs programs for people far away.

When you watch a video, send a message or ask a map app for directions, your phone talks to servers in a data centre. These buildings need three things above all: a steady supply of electricity, fast connections to the internet, and a way to get rid of the heat. Everything else is designed around those needs.

## Racks, cables and blinking lights

Walk into a data hall and the first thing you notice is the noise: a loud, steady roar from thousands of fans. Each rack holds dozens of flat servers slotted in like books on a shelf. Bundles of cables run overhead or under a raised floor, carrying power and data.

Some servers do the thinking, with powerful chips. Others are mostly storage, crammed with hard drives or memory chips. Fibre-optic cables connect the building to the wider [internet](internet-how), often through several different routes, so one cut cable doesn't knock everything offline. Security is tight. Many sites have fences, guards and fingerprint or card scanners at every door.

## The heat problem

Almost every bit of electricity a server uses turns into heat. Packed together, servers would quickly overheat and break. So a large part of a data centre is really a giant cooling machine.

The usual trick is to arrange racks in "hot aisles" and "cold aisles". Cool air is blown in at the front of the servers and hot air leaves at the back, where it is pulled away. Big chillers work like an [air conditioner](air-conditioning) the size of a house. Newer chips for artificial intelligence run so hot that some are cooled with liquid flowing through pipes right next to them.

Location helps. Google's data centre at Hamina in Finland uses cold seawater from the Baltic. In hot places such as Chennai or Arizona, cooling costs more.

## Never switch off

A data centre is expected to keep running even during [a power cut](what-if-no-electricity). Rooms full of [batteries](batteries) take over instantly if the mains supply fails. Within seconds, diesel generators the size of a shipping container start up and keep the lights on for hours or days.

The biggest companies copy your data to several buildings, sometimes in different countries. If a flood or fire hits one, another takes over. That is why your photos don't vanish when one machine dies.

## Where they are, and the cost

Northern Virginia, near Washington DC, has more data centres than anywhere else on Earth, partly because some of the early internet's main connections met there. In India, Mumbai and Navi Mumbai host the largest share, helped by the [undersea cables](undersea-internet) that land on the coast nearby. Chennai, Hyderabad and Delhi's surrounding region are growing fast.

All this uses a lot of power. The International Energy Agency estimates data centres used about 1.5% of the world's electricity in 2024, and expects that to rise quickly as artificial intelligence spreads. If that electricity comes from coal, it adds to [climate change](climate-change). So companies are signing deals for solar and wind power and trying to use less water for cooling. In parts of Scandinavia, the waste heat from servers is piped into homes to keep them warm in winter.
