## The short answer

The Internet of Things, or IoT for short, means everyday objects that connect to the internet and share information. A fitness band that sends your steps to an app is one. So is a smart bulb you switch on from your phone, or a water meter that reports its own reading.

Each one usually has three parts. A [sensor](?sensor) notices something, such as heat, light, movement or sound. A tiny computer chip makes sense of it. And a wireless link, like [Wi-Fi](wifi) or Bluetooth, sends the information onward, often to an app or a company's computers.

## The drinks machine that went online

One of the first "things" on a network was a [Coca-Cola machine](soft-drinks) at Carnegie Mellon University in Pittsburgh. In 1982, students tired of walking to the machine only to find it empty, or the cans still warm. So they wired sensors to it and linked it to the university's computer network. Anyone could type a command and check how many cans were left and how long they had been chilling.

In 1999 a British technology researcher, Kevin Ashton, coined the phrase "Internet of Things" while talking about tags that could track products in shops and warehouses. The name caught on.

## How a smart gadget works

Take a smart thermostat, which controls heating or cooling. A temperature sensor measures the room many times a minute. Its chip compares the reading to the setting you chose. If the room is too warm, it switches on the air conditioner. It also sends the data to an app, so you can change the setting from across town.

The heavy work often happens in [cloud computing](cloud-computing) centres, big buildings full of servers far away. Your gadget stays small and cheap, and the clever bits run elsewhere.

## On farms, roads and rivers

IoT reaches far beyond homes. Farmers in India and the USA use sensors buried in fields to measure how wet the soil is, so they water only when crops need it. Water companies fit sensors to pipes to spot leaks. Cities use them to time [traffic lights](traffic-lights), track buses and warn of floods. Hospitals use them to watch patients' [heartbeats](heart-beat) from a nurse's desk.

Planners who design [smart cities](smart-cities) hope that millions of small sensors working together will make streets safer and save water and power.

## When things go wrong

More connected devices also means more doors for hackers. Many cheap gadgets come with simple default passwords, like "admin", that owners never change.

In 2016 a piece of malicious software called Mirai searched the internet for such devices. It took over hundreds of thousands of cameras and routers and ordered them all to flood a company that helps run the internet's address system with junk traffic. Popular websites stopped working for hours in parts of the USA and Europe. [Cybersecurity](cybersecurity) experts still use it as a warning.

## Privacy questions

[A smart speaker](voice-assistants) listens for its wake word. A doorbell camera films the street. A fitness band knows when you sleep. All that data has to be stored somewhere, and people reasonably ask who can see it and how long it is kept.

Countries are starting to set rules. The United Kingdom, for example, banned easy-to-guess default passwords on new [smart devices](smart-homes) from 2024, and the USA has started a label to show which gadgets meet basic security standards.
