## The short answer

A pixel is one tiny dot in a digital picture. The word is short for "picture element". A screen or a photo is a grid of pixels, each showing one colour. Put enough of them close together and your eye stops seeing dots and sees a face, a sunset or a cricket match.

A Full HD TV has 1920 pixels across and 1080 down. That is about 2 million pixels, each changing colour many times a second. A 4K screen has about 8 million. On a phone they are packed so tightly that you need a [magnifying glass](magnifying-glass) to see them. A good phone screen fits more than 400 pixels into every inch, about 2.5 cm.

## Red, green and blue

Look closely at a screen pixel and it is not really one dot. Each pixel has three even smaller parts, called subpixels: one red, one green and one blue. By making each part brighter or dimmer, the screen can mix almost any colour.

All three at full brightness look white. All three off look black. Red with green looks yellow. This works because your [eyes](eyes-see) have three kinds of colour-sensing cells, tuned roughly to red, green and blue. A screen is a trick designed to fit your eyes.

## A picture made of numbers

To a computer, a picture is a long list of numbers. In a common system, each of the three colours in a pixel gets a number from 0 to 255. That gives 256 × 256 × 256, which is about 16.7 million possible colours.

The computer stores those numbers in [binary](binary-code), as 0s and 1s. That is why a photo takes up space on your phone. A 12-megapixel photo has 12 million pixels, each needing several numbers. Phones shrink the file with clever tricks that throw away detail your eye won't miss. If you squeeze a photo too much, those tricks start to show as blocky smudges, especially around sharp edges and in clear blue skies.

## The baby in the first scan

In 1957, an engineer named Russell Kirsch worked at the US National Bureau of Standards, now called NIST. He and his team built a machine that could scan a photograph into a computer. The first picture they chose was a photo of his baby son, Walden.

The scan was 176 pixels wide and 176 tall, in plain black and white. It looks blocky today, but it was the start of every digital photo, medical scan and satellite image since. Kirsch later said he regretted using square pixels and wished he had tried other shapes. He spent part of his retirement working on pixels that could change shape to follow the edges in a picture.

## Dots before computers

The idea of making pictures from dots is much older than computers. In the 1880s, the French painter Georges Seurat painted whole scenes with tiny dots of pure colour, close to the style of the [Impressionists](impressionism). From across the room, the dots blend.

[Newspapers](newspapers) printed photos as patterns of small dots for over a hundred years. Ancient Roman mosaics used little stone tiles in the same way. A pixel is the newest version of a very old idea: lots of small pieces, seen from far enough away, become one picture.
