## The short answer

A smartphone is a small, powerful computer with a phone, camera and many [sensors](?sensor) packed inside a slim case.

Behind the screen sits a circuit board holding the main chip, which contains the processor, graphics unit and more. Memory chips hold apps and photos. Several antennas send and receive radio signals for calls, [Wi-Fi](wifi), [Bluetooth](bluetooth) and satellite navigation. Cameras, microphones and speakers are fitted in tiny gaps. Most of the space is taken by a flat battery.

Put together, it's a pocket-sized combination of devices that once filled a whole desk, or a whole room.

## The brain on a chip

The main chip in a phone is called a system-on-a-chip, because it combines many parts that once needed separate chips. It includes the processor that runs apps, a graphics unit for games and videos, and often a special unit for [artificial intelligence](ai-what) tasks like recognising faces in photos.

These chips contain billions of [transistors](?transistor), each so small that thousands would fit across the width of a human hair. Making them takes some of the most precise factories on Earth.

The [Apollo 11 guidance computer](apollo-moon), which helped land astronauts on the Moon in 1969, had only a tiny fraction of a modern phone's memory and speed.

## A box of sensors

Your phone is always sensing its surroundings. An accelerometer feels movement and tilt, which is how the screen knows to rotate and how [step counters](smartwatches) work. A gyroscope senses turning. A magnetometer acts as a compass.

A proximity sensor near the top notices when you hold the phone to your ear and switches off the screen so your cheek doesn't press buttons. A light sensor adjusts the brightness. A satellite receiver listens for signals from navigation satellites, so maps can show where you are.

## Screen, cameras and battery

The screen is a sandwich of layers: tough glass, a [touch-sensing layer](touchscreens), and a display made of millions of tiny coloured dots. Many phones use OLED displays, where each dot makes its own light.

Most phones now have several cameras, each with a different lens for wide, zoomed or close-up shots. Software combines their pictures.

The battery is lithium-ion, the same kind used in laptops and [electric cars](electric-cars). It stores energy by moving charged lithium atoms, called ions, between two layers.

## What it's made of

A phone contains several dozen chemical elements. Silicon makes the chips. Lithium and cobalt go into the battery. Copper wires carry electricity. Tiny amounts of [gold](gold-where) coat connectors because gold doesn't rust. Rare earth elements help make speakers, vibration motors and screen colours.

Many of these materials are mined in just a few countries, sometimes in difficult or unsafe conditions. That is one reason people encourage repairing phones and [recycling old ones](e-waste), so those metals can be recovered.

## How phones changed the world

The IBM Simon of 1994 is often called the first smartphone, with a touchscreen and simple apps. The iPhone, launched in 2007, and [phones running Android](open-source), launched in 2008, made smartphones popular across the world.

India now has hundreds of millions of smartphone users, more than any country except China. Cheap phones and low-cost [mobile data](mobile-networks) brought many families online for the first time, for video calls, online classes and payments. A device that was rare when your parents were children is now, for many people, the main way they reach the internet.
