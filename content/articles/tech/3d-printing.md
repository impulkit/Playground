## The short answer

A 3D printer makes a solid object by adding material one thin layer at a time. The most common kind for homes and schools pulls in a long thread of plastic, melts it in a hot nozzle at around 200 °C and squeezes it out like [toothpaste](made-toothpaste). The nozzle moves around, drawing the outline of one layer. The plastic cools and hardens in seconds.

Then the platform drops a fraction of a millimetre, or the nozzle rises, and the printer draws the next layer on top. Stack a few hundred of those and you have a toy, a phone stand or a spare part for a broken fan.

## From a drawing to a stack of slices

Every print starts as a 3D drawing on a computer, a little like a [digital sculpture](sculpture). A program then cuts that drawing into hundreds of flat slices, each perhaps 0.2 mm thick. For each slice, it writes instructions telling the nozzle exactly where to go.

Hollow parts are a puzzle. A printer cannot lay plastic on thin air, so for overhangs it builds thin supports underneath, which you snap off later. Inside, objects are often not solid but filled with a honeycomb pattern. That saves plastic and time while keeping the object strong.

## Who invented it

In the early 1980s, an American engineer named Chuck Hull was using ultraviolet light to harden coatings on tables. He realised he could harden a liquid resin one thin layer at a time and build up a shape. He called it stereolithography and was granted a patent in 1986.

For years, 3D printers were large and expensive, used mainly by companies to test designs. When some early [patents](patents) ran out around 2009, cheaper machines appeared. Now [many schools](future-schools) have one. Some printers cost less than a good bicycle. Students use them to print parts for science projects, models of molecules and replacement pieces for board games.

## Printing rockets and houses

Bigger printers work with tougher stuff. Some fire a laser at fine metal powder to melt it into shape, making parts for [jet engines](plane-jet) and rockets. In 2024, the Chennai company Agnikul Cosmos launched a small test [rocket](rockets-how) whose engine was 3D-printed as one single piece.

Others print with [concrete](cement-concrete). In 2023 a post office in Cambridge Layout, [Bengaluru](bengaluru), opened with walls printed by a giant machine that squeezed out concrete in curving layers. It took weeks, not the many months a normal building might take. Printed walls can curve easily, because the machine doesn't care whether it is drawing a straight line or a wavy one. In the United States, companies have used the same method to print whole houses in Texas.

## Printing in space

Astronauts can't pop to a shop if a tool breaks. In 2014, a 3D printer on the [International Space Station](space-station) made its first object, a small part for the printer itself. Since then astronauts have printed tools and spare parts. Future crews on the [Moon or Mars](mars-colonies) might print shelters using local dust and rock.

[Doctors](jobs-doctor) use 3D printing too. They print models of a patient's heart or bones to plan an operation, and make [artificial hands and legs](prosthetics) shaped to fit one person exactly. A plastic printer in a classroom works on the same idea as all of these: slice it, then stack it.
