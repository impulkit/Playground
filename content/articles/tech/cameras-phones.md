## The short answer

A phone camera has a tiny lens and a sensor smaller than your fingernail. On their own, they would take grainy, blurry photos, especially in dim light. The secret is software. When you take a picture, the phone often grabs a burst of frames, lines them up, and combines the best parts of each into one photo. This is called computational photography.

It means the phone can brighten shadows without blowing out a bright sky, cut down grain at night and blur the background behind a face. Much of a phone photo's quality comes from the processor, not just the lens.

## From light to numbers

Light enters through the lens and lands on the sensor, a chip covered with millions of tiny light-catching squares called photosites. Each one measures how much light hits it and turns that into a number. Those numbers become the [pixels](pixels) of your photo.

The photosites can only measure brightness, not colour. So a grid of tiny red, green and blue filters sits on top, a pattern invented by Bryce Bayer at Kodak in the 1970s. There are twice as many green squares as red or blue, because human [eyes](eyes-see) are most sensitive to green. Software then fills in the missing colours for each pixel by looking at its neighbours, using the way red, green and blue [light mixes](colour-mix) to make every other colour.

## Many photos in one

Many phone cameras keep recording a few frames even before you press the button, storing them in memory. When you tap, the phone already has several shots to work with, which is why there's almost no delay.

For a high dynamic range (HDR) photo, the phone combines frames taken at different brightness levels, so both the dark shadows and the bright sky show detail. In night mode, it may take a dozen or more frames over a few seconds. It lines them up to cancel out the shake of your hands, then [averages](averages) them to cut the speckly "noise".

## Several cameras, several lenses

Look at the back of many phones and you'll see two, three or four cameras. Each has a different [lens](lenses-glasses): a main wide lens, an ultra-wide one for big scenes, and a telephoto for zooming in. Some telephoto lenses are too long to fit in a thin phone standing up, so they lie sideways with a tiny prism, like a [periscope](periscopes), bending the light round the corner.

Portrait mode uses two cameras, or a depth sensor, or artificial intelligence to work out how far away each part of the scene is. Then software blurs the background, copying the look of a big camera with a large lens.

## Tiny sensors, big tricks

Some phones now have sensors with 50 or even 200 megapixels. That sounds great, but each photosite becomes very small and catches little light. So in dim conditions, the phone joins groups of four or more photosites together and treats them as one bigger pixel, a trick called pixel binning.

Phones also use [artificial intelligence](ai-what) to [recognise scenes](computer-vision), like faces, food or sunsets, and adjust colour and sharpness to suit. Some people feel this makes photos look too perfect, with skin smoothed and skies made bluer than real life.

## From toaster to pocket

In 1975 a young Kodak engineer, Steven Sasson, built the first digital camera. It was the size of a toaster, took 0.01 megapixel black-and-white pictures and needed 23 seconds to save each one onto a cassette tape. In 2000, the Sharp J-SH04 went on sale in Japan, one of the first phones with a built-in camera. Its photos were about 0.1 megapixels. Today, more photos are taken with phones every year than with all other cameras put together, a long way from the [first photographs](photography-history), which needed exposures lasting minutes or even hours.
