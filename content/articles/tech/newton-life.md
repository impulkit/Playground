## The short answer

Isaac Newton was an English scientist and mathematician who lived over 300 years ago. He worked out three laws of motion that explain how things move, and a law of [gravity](gravity-fall) that explains why [apples](apples) fall and why the Moon goes round the Earth. He showed that white light is made of many colours and built [a new kind of telescope](telescopes-make). He also created calculus, a powerful kind of maths for things that change.

Engineers still use his laws to design [bridges](bridges) and cars and to plan the paths of spacecraft. Not bad for someone whose own mother wanted him to be a farmer.

## A lonely childhood

Newton was born in 1642 at Woolsthorpe Manor, a stone farmhouse in Lincolnshire. His father had died three months earlier. When Isaac was three, his mother remarried and left him with his grandmother. He grew up clever, quiet and often lonely.

At school he built model windmills, water clocks and sundials. His mother took him out of school to run the farm, but he was hopeless at it. A schoolmaster and an uncle persuaded her to send him to Trinity College, Cambridge, instead.

## The plague years

In 1665 [a deadly plague](black-death) swept through England, and Cambridge closed. Newton, then 22, went home to Woolsthorpe for about two years. Working alone, he made progress on calculus, studied light and began to think about gravity.

Newton later said that seeing an apple fall in the orchard set him wondering. If gravity pulls an apple down, might it reach as far as the Moon? The tree is still alive in the garden at Woolsthorpe, now looked after by the National Trust.

## Splitting light

People had long seen colours appear when light passed through glass. Most thought the glass added the colours. Newton tested this. He let a beam of sunlight through a hole in his shutter, split it into colours with one prism, then used a second prism to join them back into white. Light, he showed, is a mix of colours. That is also why a [rainbow](rainbow-how) always shows them in the same order.

Lenses in early telescopes made coloured blurs around stars, so Newton designed a telescope that used a curved mirror instead. His small reflecting telescope, built around 1668, was about 15 cm long. Today the biggest [telescopes](telescopes-how) all use mirrors.

## The Principia

In 1687, encouraged by the astronomer Edmond Halley, Newton published a book in Latin usually called the Principia. It set out his three laws of motion. An object keeps moving unless a force acts on it. A bigger force gives a bigger push. Every push has an equal push back, which is how [rockets](rockets-how) fly.

It also showed that one law of gravity explains falling apples, the tides and the [orbits](spacecraft-move) of planets. The book made him the most famous scientist in Europe. His work built on astronomers like [Galileo](galileo), and he once wrote that he had seen further by "standing on the shoulders of giants".

## Coins, quarrels and alchemy

In 1696 Newton moved to London to work at the Royal Mint. He tracked down counterfeiters and made sure coins had the right amount of silver. He was knighted in 1705.

He could be prickly. He argued bitterly with the German mathematician Leibniz over who invented calculus first. Historians now agree they developed it separately. Newton also spent years on alchemy, the old attempt to turn metals into gold, and on religious writings. He died in 1727 and was buried in Westminster Abbey.
