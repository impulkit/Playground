## The short answer

The internet crosses oceans through cables laid on the sea floor. Inside each one are a few hair-thin strands of glass called optical fibres. Data travels along them as flashes of [laser](lasers) light, millions of times a second.

More than 500 of these cables wrap the planet, linking every continent except Antarctica. Together they carry about 99% of the data travelling between continents. When you stream a video from a server in the United States to a phone in [Kochi](kochi), it probably crossed the sea this way, in well under a second.

## Thin as a hose, long as a continent

In the deep ocean, a cable can be about as thick as a garden hose. There, kilometres down, it is safe from most dangers, so it needs little protection. Near the shore it is wrapped in layers of steel wire and buried under the sea bed, because shallow water is where the trouble is.

Light fades as it travels through glass. So every 50 to 100 km or so, a device called a repeater boosts the signal. A cable from India to Europe may have dozens of them lying on the sea floor, powered by electricity sent down the cable from land.

## Laying a cable

Special cable ships carry thousands of kilometres of cable coiled in huge tanks. Before laying starts, surveyors [map the sea floor](ocean-floor-maps) to avoid cliffs, volcanoes and [shipwrecks](shipwrecks). The ship then sails slowly across the ocean, paying out cable behind it, while a plough buries it near the coast.

When a cable breaks, a repair ship sails out, hooks the broken ends with a grapnel (a kind of grappling hook), lifts them to the deck and splices in a new section. Most breaks come from fishing trawlers and ship anchors. Sharks have been seen biting cables, and some were wrapped in extra tough material, but bites cause very few faults.

## From telegraph to fibre

The first cable across the Atlantic, in 1858, carried [telegraph](telegraph) messages in Morse code. It worked for only about three weeks before failing. In 1866 a giant steamship, the Great Eastern, laid a cable that lasted. By 1870 a telegraph line under the sea linked Britain to Bombay, and news that once took weeks to arrive by ship took minutes.

The first fibre-optic cable across the Atlantic, called TAT-8, opened in 1988. A single modern cable can carry far more data than all the old telegraph and telephone cables put together.

## India's landing points

Cables come ashore at buildings called landing stations. India's main ones are in Mumbai and Chennai, with others in places such as Kochi and Thoothukudi. From there, the data flows inland through fibre buried beside roads and railway lines.

In 2020 a cable of about 2,300 km opened between Chennai and the [Andaman and Nicobar Islands](andaman). Before that, the islands relied on slower satellite links. People there could suddenly stream videos and hold video lessons that had been almost impossible before.

## Why not just use satellites?

Satellites are useful where cables can't reach, such as ships in mid-ocean or remote villages. [Satellite internet](starlink) from low orbit is getting much better. But one fibre cable can carry far more data than a whole fleet of satellites, and it costs less for each bit sent. That is why companies including Google and Meta now pay to lay their own cables, threading new strands of glass across the deep sea floor where whales swim overhead.
