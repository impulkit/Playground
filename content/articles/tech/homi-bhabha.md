## The short answer

Homi Jehangir Bhabha was an Indian physicist who studied cosmic rays and the tiny particles inside atoms. He became the founder of India's nuclear science programme. He started two great research centres, the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research and what is now the Bhabha Atomic Research Centre, both in [Mumbai](mumbai).

He also planned how India might one day make [electricity](electricity-made) from its own supplies of thorium. Outside the laboratory he was a painter, a lover of classical music and a keen collector of modern Indian art.

## From engineering to physics

Bhabha was born in 1909 into a wealthy Parsi family in Bombay. His father hoped he would become an engineer and join the Tata steel company. So he went to Cambridge to study mechanical engineering. He passed with top marks, but he had fallen for physics. He wrote to his father that engineering was not his line and that physics was what he wanted to do.

At Cambridge he worked on how [fast-moving particles](particle-accelerators) behave when they collide. In 1935 he worked out how electrons and their mirror-image partners, positrons, scatter off each other. This is still called Bhabha scattering. With the German physicist Walter Heitler, he also explained how a single cosmic ray hitting the upper air can set off a shower of millions of particles.

## Stuck at home

In 1939, Bhabha came to India for a holiday. Then the [Second World War](world-war-two) broke out, and he could not return to Europe. He took a job at the Indian Institute of Science in Bangalore, working in the department led by the Nobel winner C. V. Raman.

It turned out to be a lucky accident. Bhabha decided India needed its own centre for advanced physics. In 1944 he wrote to the Sir Dorabji Tata Trust asking for support. The next year the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research opened, first in borrowed rooms at the institute in Bangalore and then, from late 1945, in his aunt's bungalow in Bombay.

## Atoms for India

After independence in 1947, Prime Minister [Jawaharlal Nehru](nehru) asked Bhabha to lead India's atomic work. He became the first chairman of the Atomic Energy Commission in 1948. At Trombay, on the edge of Bombay harbour, he built a huge research campus.

There, in 1956, India switched on Apsara, the first nuclear research reactor in Asia. It made [radioactive substances](accident-radioactivity) for medicine and research. Some of these have a known [half-life](half-life), which makes them useful for tracing and timing.

Bhabha knew India had little uranium but plenty of thorium in its coastal sands. He designed a three-stage plan for [nuclear power](nuclear-power) that would eventually run on thorium. Decades later, India is still building towards it.

## The artist scientist

Bhabha sketched and painted all his life. He chose the art for his institutes and supported young painters such as M. F. Husain. The TIFR art collection is now one of the notable collections of modern Indian art.

In 1955 he chaired the first big [United Nations](united-nations) conference on peaceful uses of atomic energy, in Geneva. On 24 January 1966, the Air India plane carrying him on his way to Vienna crashed into Mont Blanc in the Alps. He was 56. A year later, the Trombay centre was renamed the Bhabha Atomic Research Centre in his memory, and his close colleague [Vikram Sarabhai](vikram-sarabhai) took over his work.
