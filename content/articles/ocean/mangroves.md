## The short answer

Mangroves are trees and shrubs that grow along warm coasts, where rivers meet the sea. Twice a day the [tide](tides) rises and floods their roots with salty water, then falls and leaves them in thick mud. For most trees, that much salt would be deadly.

Mangroves have three main tricks. Some block most of the salt at their roots, letting in almost pure water. Some take in salty water and push the extra salt out through glands on their leaves. Others store salt in old leaves, then drop those leaves. Many mangroves use more than one trick. They also have special roots to cope with mud that has little oxygen. Together, these let them live where no other trees can.

## Roots that breathe

Mangrove mud is soft, wet and almost airless. Roots need oxygen, so mangroves have found ways to reach the air. Some, like the grey mangrove, send up hundreds of pencil-like roots that poke out of the mud like a bed of nails. These breathing roots are called [pneumatophores](?pneumatophore). They have tiny pores that take in air at low tide.

Red mangroves stand on stilts. Arching prop roots grow from the trunk and branches down into the mud, holding the tree steady against waves and currents. In places, the roots form a tangle so thick that even a small boat cannot pass.

## Babies that start growing on the tree

Most seeds fall to the ground and wait before they sprout. Many mangroves do something unusual. Their seeds start growing while still attached to the parent tree. The young plant grows into a long, pointed spear called a propagule.

When it drops, the propagule may stick upright in the mud and start growing right away. Or it may float off on the tide and drift for weeks, even months, until it lodges on a new shore. This is how mangroves spread along coasts and onto islands far away. River mouths and [deltas](deltas), where mud builds up, make good landing places.

## The Sundarbans

The largest mangrove forest in the world is the Sundarbans, shared by India and [Bangladesh](bangladesh) where [the Ganga](rivers-himalaya), Brahmaputra and Meghna rivers reach the sea. It covers about 10,000 square km, and it is a [UNESCO World Heritage Site](unesco-sites). Its name is often said to come from the sundari, one of its mangrove trees.

The Sundarbans is home to Bengal tigers that swim between islands and hunt in the mangroves, as you can read in [Sundarbans tigers](sundarbans-tigers). It also has [crocodiles](croc-ancient), river dolphins, mudskippers and [fiddler crabs](crabs). Millions of people live around it, fishing, collecting [honey](honey) and farming.

India has other mangroves too: Bhitarkanika in Odisha, Pichavaram in Tamil Nadu, the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, and even the creeks of [Mumbai](mumbai), where mangroves grow beside highways and tall buildings. In the USA, mangroves line much of southern Florida, including the Everglades.

## Living sea walls

Mangroves protect the coast. Their tangled roots slow down waves and hold mud in place. When a cyclone pushes the sea inland, a wide belt of mangroves can reduce the damage behind it. After a super cyclone hit Odisha in 1999, a study found that villages sheltered by mangroves suffered fewer deaths than villages without them.

Mangroves are nurseries for sea life. Young fish, prawns and crabs hide among the roots before moving out to sea, so mangroves help feed many fishing families. Their mud also stores large amounts of carbon, which helps slow [climate change](climate-change). Yet many mangroves have been cleared for farms, prawn ponds and buildings. In many places, including India, people now plant and protect them again.
