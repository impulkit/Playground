## The short answer

India has a long coastline, more than 7,500 km by the older official measurement and about 11,000 km by a newer one that counts every bend. Along it live millions of people whose families have fished for generations. Before radios and phones, they learned to read the sea with their senses. A change in the colour of the water could point to fish. So could a flock of seabirds diving into the waves. A shift in the wind or a line of clouds on the horizon could warn that a storm was coming.

That knowledge was passed down by watching, listening and doing. Today, [many fishers](people-fishing-villages) combine it with [satellite](satellites-help) forecasts and mobile phones. The sea is the same, but the tools have changed.

## Signs in the water and sky

Experienced fishers notice details that others miss. Seabirds diving in one spot often mean a shoal of small fish just below. A patch of water with a different colour or texture can mean fish are gathering. Dolphins chasing fish near the surface are another clue.

The sky matters too. Wind direction, the shape of clouds and the swell rolling in from far away all hint at the coming weather. Waves made by a distant storm can arrive before the storm itself, as you can read in [how waves form](waves-how). Fishers also plan around the [tides](tides) and the phases of the Moon, which change how fish move along the shore.

## Boats shaped by the coast

India's fishing boats are as varied as its coastline. On the Coromandel Coast of Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh, fishers used the kattumaram, logs tied together into a raft that rides over the surf. Its name [gave English the word](words-borrowed) "catamaran". Kerala has long, narrow wooden vallams. In [Kochi](kochi), giant cantilevered fishing nets, said to have come with traders from China centuries ago, still dip into the water along the shore.

On the coast of Gujarat, sailors from Mandvi and nearby ports built wooden dhows and sailed them to Arabia and East Africa. Their navigators, called malams, steered by the stars, the winds and the colour of the sea. In [Mumbai](mumbai), the Koli fishing community lived on the islands long before the city grew around them.

## The monsoon year

The [monsoon](monsoon) shapes the fishing calendar. From June, strong winds and high waves make the Arabian Sea dangerous for small boats. Many fish also breed at this time. So the government bans most mechanised fishing for 61 days. On the west coast the ban runs through June and July. On the east coast, where the stormy season comes at a different time, it runs from mid-April to mid-June.

During the break, families mend nets, repair boats and wait for the sea to calm. When fishing restarts, there are often big catches. Cyclones are still a serious danger, which is why fishers now listen carefully to weather warnings broadcast on radio and sent to phones.

## Phones and satellites

Technology has added new ways of reading the sea. INCOIS, a government centre in Hyderabad, uses [satellites](satellites-india) to measure the temperature and colour of the sea's surface. Where cooler, greener water rich in plankton meets warmer water, fish often gather. INCOIS sends maps of these likely fishing zones to fishers in many languages.

[Mobile phones](mobile-networks) changed fishing in [Kerala](kerala) too. A well-known study found that once fishers could call markets from their boats, they sold their catch where prices were best, and less fish [went to waste](food-waste). In 2018, during terrible [floods](floods-why), Kerala's fishers brought their boats inland and rescued thousands of people. Their knowledge of water saved lives far from the sea.
