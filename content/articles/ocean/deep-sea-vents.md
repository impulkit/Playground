## The short answer

Far below the waves, where no sunlight ever reaches, the sea floor has cracks above hot rock. Cold seawater seeps down, gets heated by [magma](?magma), picks up minerals and gushes back out like an underwater chimney. These places are called hydrothermal vents, and they are crowded with life.

Giant tubeworms as long as a grown-up is tall wave red plumes. White crabs scuttle over the rocks. Clams, mussels, shrimp and pale fish gather in the warm water. None of them depend on sunlight. Their food chain starts with [bacteria](?bacteria) that turn chemicals from inside the Earth into food.

## A surprise in the dark

In 1977, a team of American geologists took the small submarine Alvin down about 2,500 m near the [Galápagos Islands](galapagos). They expected to find hot springs, because the sea floor there is slowly splitting apart over hot, [volcanic](volcano) rock. They did not expect animals.

Instead, their lights showed fields of clams and mussels, and clumps of huge white tubes with blood-red tips. The scientists had brought along equipment for studying rocks, not living things. They had to preserve some of their samples in vodka from the ship's supplies. Two years later, explorers found "black smokers" on the East Pacific Rise, chimneys belching water as black as ink and hot enough to melt lead.

## Food without sunlight

Almost every food chain on Earth starts with plants or algae capturing sunlight. At vents, it starts with bacteria that use hydrogen sulphide, the gas that smells like rotten eggs, as their energy source. This is called chemosynthesis, meaning "making with chemicals".

Giant tubeworms take this to the extreme. They have no mouth or stomach. Instead, a special organ inside each worm is packed with billions of bacteria. The worm's red plume soaks up chemicals and oxygen from the water, using a kind of [haemoglobin](?haemoglobin) like the one in your blood. The bacteria do the rest. Tubeworms can grow fast for animals in such a cold, dark world, and a colony can spring up on a new vent within a few years.

Vents do not last forever. A chimney may stop flowing after years or decades, and the animals around it die or move on. Their young drift through the dark water until, with luck, they settle on another vent, sometimes many kilometres away.

## Snails in iron armour

The vents of the Indian Ocean hold one of the oddest animals on Earth. The scaly-foot snail, found in 2001, builds its shell and the scales on its foot partly from iron sulphide, a metal mineral. It is one of the only animals known to use iron in its skeleton this way.

That metal-rich sea floor has also drawn interest from people. Several countries, including India, hold permits to explore parts of the Indian Ocean floor for minerals. In 2019, the scaly-foot snail became one of the first animals listed as endangered because of possible deep-sea mining. Scientists and governments are still debating how to protect vent life while studying these resources.

## Clues to life's beginnings

Vents give scientists a reason to look for life far from Earth. Jupiter's moon [Europa](europa) and Saturn's moon [Enceladus](space-weird-oceans-moons) seem to have salty oceans hidden under thick ice. If those oceans have hot vents too, they might offer the same kind of chemical energy.

Some researchers think life on Earth itself may have begun at deep-sea vents billions of years ago. Others favour shallow pools on land. Nobody knows yet, and the question of how the [first living things](first-life) appeared is still one of the biggest in science.
