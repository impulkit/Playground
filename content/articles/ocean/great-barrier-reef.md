## The short answer

The Great Barrier Reef is the largest coral reef system on Earth. It lies in the warm, shallow sea off the coast of Queensland, in north-eastern [Australia](australia). It is not one long wall but about 2,900 separate reefs and hundreds of islands, stretching about 2,300 km. That is roughly the distance from Delhi to Kanyakumari.

It was built by corals, tiny animals called [polyps](?polyp), each smaller than your fingernail. Each polyp builds a hard cup of limestone around its soft body. Over thousands of years, billions of these cups pile up into reefs. The reef is home to more than 1,500 kinds of fish, hundreds of kinds of coral, [sharks](sharks-myths), rays, dolphins, whales, [sea turtles](sea-turtles) and seabirds.

## How corals build

A coral polyp looks like a tiny tube with tentacles around its mouth. At night, many polyps stretch out their tentacles to catch drifting food. But most of their food comes from somewhere surprising: tiny algae living inside their bodies.

These algae use sunlight to make sugar, and they share it with the coral. In return, the coral gives them shelter. That is why reef-building corals need clear, sunny, shallow water. With this energy, the polyps slowly build their limestone skeletons. When polyps die, new ones grow on top. A large reef can be the work of thousands of years. Coral reefs in general are crowded with life, as you can read in [coral reefs](coral-reefs).

## A night of spawning

Once a year, many corals on the reef release eggs and sperm on the same night. It usually happens a few nights after a full moon in late spring, which in Australia means November or December. Scientists call this mass spawning. The water fills with tiny pink and white bundles rising like an upside-down snowstorm.

By spawning together, the corals give their eggs the best chance of meeting sperm from other colonies. The young corals drift for days before settling on rock to start new colonies. Divers come from around the world to see it.

## Coral bleaching

Corals are sensitive to heat. When the sea stays too warm for weeks, corals throw out their helpful algae. Without them, the coral turns white, which is called bleaching. Bleached coral is not dead yet, but if the heat goes on too long, it starves.

The Great Barrier Reef suffered mass bleaching in 1998 and 2002, and again in 2016, 2017, 2020, 2022, 2024 and 2025. Scientists link these events to warming seas caused by [climate change](climate-change). Some areas have recovered well between events, but the gaps between them are getting shorter. Outbreaks of the crown-of-thorns [starfish](starfish), which eats coral, add to the damage.

## People and the reef

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples have lived beside the reef and fished its waters for tens of thousands of years. Their stories and knowledge of the sea are much older than any map. In 1770, the British explorer James Cook's ship, the Endeavour, ran aground on the reef and had to be repaired on the shore.

The reef became a UNESCO [World Heritage](unesco-sites) Site in 1981, and most of it is protected as a [marine park](marine-parks-india). India has coral reefs too, though much smaller: in the Gulf of Mannar, the Gulf of Kutch, [Lakshadweep](lakshadweep) and around the [Andaman and Nicobar Islands](andaman). The USA has reefs off Florida and Hawaii. All of them face the same rising heat.
