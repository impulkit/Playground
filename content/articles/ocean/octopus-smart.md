## The short answer

An octopus's skin is packed with thousands of tiny, stretchy bags of colour, called chromatophores. Each one is ringed by little [muscles](muscles) wired straight to the brain. When the muscles pull, the bag stretches wide and its colour shows. When they relax, it shrinks to a dot. All of this can happen in less than a second.

Under the colour bags are layers of cells that reflect light, making shimmering blues and greens or a plain white. The skin can also raise bumps and spikes, so a smooth octopus can suddenly look like a lumpy rock or a clump of seaweed.

Octopuses use these tricks to hide, to hunt and to send signals. This kind of disguise is called [camouflage](camouflage).

## The colour-blind puzzle

Here is the strange part. Most octopuses seem to be colour-blind. Their eyes have only one kind of light-sensing cell, so they probably see the world in shades of one colour. Yet they match the colours around them very well.

Nobody knows exactly how. One clue is that octopus skin contains the same light-sensing proteins found in eyes. In a 2015 study, pieces of skin from a California octopus responded to light even when cut off from the brain. Another idea is that their oddly shaped pupils might help them pick out colours. Both ideas are still being tested.

## Brains in its arms

An octopus has about 500 million nerve cells, more than any other animal without a backbone. But about two-thirds of them are in its arms, not its head. Each arm can feel, grab and even taste on its own, because the suckers carry taste sensors. An arm can explore a crack in a rock and decide what to grab while the main brain thinks about something else.

Octopuses have [no bones](what-if-no-bones), only a hard beak like a parrot's. So an octopus can squeeze through any gap bigger than its beak, which helps explain its talent for escape.

## Escape artists and puzzle solvers

In 2016, an octopus called Inky slipped out of his tank at the National Aquarium of New Zealand. He crept across the floor, squeezed into a drainpipe and made it back to the sea. Staff found only a trail of wet marks.

Octopuses can unscrew jar lids to reach food inside. In Indonesia, veined octopuses have been filmed carrying coconut shell halves around and hiding inside them, a kind of tool use once thought rare outside mammals and birds, like the [clever crows](crow-smart). At an aquarium in [Seattle](seattle), giant Pacific octopuses learned to tell apart two keepers dressed in the same uniform. One fed them, and one gently poked them with a bristly stick. In 2010, an octopus named Paul in a German aquarium "picked" the winners of [World Cup](fifa-world-cup) football matches by choosing between two food boxes.

## A short, busy life

For all their cleverness, most octopuses live only a year or two. A mother lays her eggs in a den and guards them, cleaning them and blowing fresh water over them. She usually stops eating, and dies soon after they hatch.

One deep-sea octopus off California guarded her eggs for about four and a half years, the longest of any known animal. When danger comes before the end, octopuses have one more trick: a cloud of [ink](octopus-ink) to cover their escape. Their cousins the cuttlefish and squid can change colour too, and on a [coral reef](coral-reefs) all three put on light shows that scientists are still learning to read.

In 2022, the United Kingdom changed its animal welfare law to include octopuses, squid and cuttlefish, after a scientific review found strong evidence that they can feel [pain](pain). It was one of the first countries to do so.
