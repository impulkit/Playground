## The short answer

The deep ocean is explored by scientists, engineers and a few adventurers, using tough submersibles, remote-controlled [robots](robots) and instruments lowered from ships. It is one of the hardest places on Earth to reach. Below about 1,000 m, it is completely dark. The water is near freezing, and the pressure is enormous.

Only a few vehicles can carry people to the bottom of the deepest trenches. Most deep exploration is done by robots. Some are connected to a ship by a long cable and steered by pilots watching video screens. Others swim on their own, following a programmed route. Thousands of drifting floats also measure the temperature and saltiness of the sea and send their data by satellite. Much of the deep sea is still unexplored.

## To the bottom of the world

On 23 January 1960, Jacques Piccard of Switzerland and Don Walsh of the US Navy sank to the bottom of the Challenger Deep in the Pacific Ocean. Their craft, the Trieste, was a steel ball hanging under a huge float filled with petrol. The dive down took about five hours. At the bottom, nearly 11 km down, they stayed for about 20 minutes.

Nobody returned for 52 years. In 2012, the film-maker James Cameron dived there alone. Since 2019, more people have made the trip in newer submersibles.

## Alvin and the vents

The most famous research submersible is Alvin, run by the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution in the USA. It has carried scientists into the deep since 1964, and has been rebuilt many times.

In 1977, Alvin's scientists found [hot springs](geysers-hot-springs) on the sea floor near the [Galápagos Islands](galapagos). Around them lived giant red-tipped tubeworms, big clams and crabs, in total darkness. Their food chain starts with bacteria that use chemicals from the vents instead of sunlight. Some scientists think life on Earth may even have begun in places like this. It also made them wonder about [life on other worlds](alien-life), in oceans hidden under the ice of moons. In 1986, Alvin visited the wreck of the [Titanic](titanic).

## Mappers and divers

Some explorers never go down at all. In the 1950s, an American geologist named Marie Tharp used depth readings collected by ships to draw maps of the Atlantic sea floor. She found a long rift valley running down the middle of a huge underwater mountain range. Her maps helped convince scientists that the [continents](continents) move.

Others go down in person. In 1979, the American ocean scientist Sylvia Earle walked on the sea floor off Hawaii at about 380 m, wearing a heavy pressurised suit. She later led the US government's ocean agency as its chief scientist and has spent decades campaigning to protect the sea.

## India goes deep

India is building its own crewed submersible, called [Matsya 6000](ocean-exploration-india), as part of its Deep Ocean Mission. It is being developed at the National Institute of Ocean Technology in [Chennai](chennai). The round crew sphere is designed to carry three people to 6,000 m, deep enough to study the sea floor across most of the world's oceans.

Scientists hope to use it to study [deep-sea life](ocean-depths-life) and the minerals lying on the floor of the Indian Ocean. Like astronauts, its crew will travel in a small, sealed cabin, relying on machines for every breath. Exploring the deep and exploring space have a lot in common, as you can see in [life as an astronaut](astronaut-life).
