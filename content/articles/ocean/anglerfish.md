## The short answer

In the sea, sunlight fades fast. Below about 1,000 m there is none at all, as you can see in the [ocean's zones](ocean-zones). So many animals make their own light, a trick called [bioluminescence](bioluminescence). They mix a chemical called luciferin with oxygen and a helper molecule, and the reaction gives off a cold glow.

How common is it? In one study off California, scientists counted the animals filmed by robot [submarines](submarines). About three out of every four could make light. Down there, the animal without a light is the odd one out.

Deep-sea animals use their glow to hunt, to hide, to startle attackers and to find each other in the dark. Lanternfish, for example, carry rows of tiny lights in a pattern that differs from one kind to the next, which may help them spot their own species.

## A fishing rod with a lamp

The best-known glower is the deep-sea anglerfish. A female has a long spine on her head, like a fishing rod, with a glowing bulb at the tip. The light doesn't come from the fish itself. It comes from glowing bacteria living inside the bulb.

Small fish and shrimps swim towards the light, the way moths circle a street lamp. The anglerfish waits, then opens a mouth full of needle teeth and gulps.

In some kinds of anglerfish, the males are tiny. When one finds a female, he bites onto her and never lets go. Over time his body joins hers, and he lives on as a small bump on her side.

## Blue, the colour of the deep

Most deep-sea light is blue or blue-green. Blue travels farthest through seawater, while red fades out within a few metres. Because of this, most deep-sea eyes can only see blue.

One fish breaks the rule. The stoplight loosejaw, a kind of dragonfish, shines a red light from under its eye. Almost nothing else down there can see red, so it can light up its prey without being noticed.

Some animals glow to vanish. Hatchetfish have lights along their bellies that match the faint glow from above. Seen from below, their dark outline disappears. Scientists call this counter-illumination, a kind of [camouflage](camouflage).

## The squid that came to the light

Edith Widder, an American ocean scientist, has spent her life studying glowing animals. She noticed that one deep-sea jellyfish, called Atolla, flashes a spinning ring of blue light when it is attacked. Her idea was that the flashes work like a burglar alarm, calling bigger hunters to eat the attacker.

So she built a fake jellyfish out of blue lights, nicknamed the e-jelly, to copy that alarm. In 2012, on an expedition near Japan, the e-jelly helped draw in a [giant squid](giant-squid). Cameras filmed one alive in its deep-sea home for the first time.

## Glowing waves near the shore

You don't need a submarine to see bioluminescence. On some nights, waves on Indian beaches, from Juhu in [Mumbai](mumbai) to Chennai, glow electric blue. The light comes from a tiny drifting creature called sea sparkle, which flashes when the water is disturbed. In [Puerto Rico](puerto-rico), Mosquito Bay glows so brightly that a paddle leaves a trail of light.

On land, [fireflies](fireflies) use almost the same chemical trick. Scientists have even borrowed a glowing protein from a jellyfish to light up cells in the lab. The people who worked out how to use it shared the 2008 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
