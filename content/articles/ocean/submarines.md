## The short answer

A submarine dives and rises by changing how heavy it is for its size. Along its sides are big [ballast](?ballast) tanks. On the surface, the tanks are full of air, so the submarine floats like a ship. To dive, the crew opens valves and lets seawater flood into the tanks. Now the submarine is heavier than the water it pushes aside, and it sinks.

To come back up, the crew blows compressed air into the tanks. The air pushes the water out, and the submarine becomes light enough to rise. Small fins called hydroplanes, a bit like a plane's wings, tilt the submarine up or down as it moves forward. The same rule that keeps big steel ships afloat is at work here, as explained in [how ships float](floating-ships).

## Floating in the middle

The hardest trick is not sinking or rising but staying at one depth. A submarine wants to be exactly as heavy as the water it pushes aside, so it neither sinks nor floats up. This is called being neutrally buoyant.

To get it right, submarines have smaller trim tanks. The crew pumps water between them, or in and out, to balance the boat. They must keep adjusting as people walk around inside, as fuel gets used up and as the water changes. Cold or salty water is slightly heavier, so the submarine floats a little higher in it. Fish do something similar with a gas-filled bag inside them called a swim bladder.

## Squeezed by the sea

The deeper you go, the more water presses down from above. Every 10 m of depth adds about the same pressure again as the whole weight of the air above us. At 300 m, the water presses about 30 times harder than the air at the surface.

So submarines are built with a strong inner hull, usually round in cross-section, because a round shape spreads the squeeze evenly. Military submarines usually keep exact diving depths secret. Most travel a few hundred metres down. To reach the deepest parts of the sea, you need a much smaller, tougher vessel called a submersible, as you can read in [the deepest ocean](ocean-deepest).

## From wood to nuclear power

People dreamed of underwater boats for centuries. In the 1620s, a Dutch inventor named Cornelis Drebbel is said to have rowed a covered boat under the River Thames in London. In 1775, during the [American Revolution](american-revolution), an inventor named David Bushnell built the Turtle, a wooden, egg-shaped craft for one person, who turned propellers by hand. It tried to attach a bomb to a British warship but failed.

Modern submarines are far more powerful. Nuclear submarines can stay underwater for months, because their reactors need no air. They make fresh water from seawater, and split water to make oxygen for the crew. India's first home-built nuclear submarine, INS Arihant, joined the navy in 2016. Other Indian submarines are built at Mazagon Dock in [Mumbai](mumbai).

## Seeing with sound

Underwater, there are no windows to look out of and it is often dark. Submarines find their way using sonar, which works like the [echolocation](echolocation) of [bats](bats-fly) and dolphins. A sonar sends out a ping of sound and listens for echoes from the sea floor, ships or whales.

Most submarines also listen quietly, without pinging, because a ping could give away where they are. Their crews learn to recognise the sounds of different ships, and even of shrimps and whales. Inside, life is cramped. Crews may not see daylight for weeks, so the lights are dimmed at night to keep their [body clocks](sleep-why) steady.
