## The short answer

Male humpback whales sing, mostly during the winter breeding season, in warm waters where females give birth. The songs are long and complicated, made of moans, groans, squeals and whoops arranged in repeating patterns. A song can last 10 to 20 minutes, and a whale may sing it again and again for hours.

Because only males sing, and mostly when it is time to mate, scientists are sure the songs are linked to breeding. But they still argue about how. The song may attract females, a bit like a peacock's tail. It may tell other males to keep away, or help males size each other up. Some scientists suggest it could help whales locate each other in the vast ocean. The answer may be a mix of these.

## How a whale sings

Humpbacks don't sing the way we do. They have no vocal cords like ours, and they don't breathe out while [singing](singing). Instead, they push air back and forth inside their bodies, past a U-shaped fold in their voice box. The vibrations pass into the water through their throat and head.

Sound travels more than four times faster in water than in air, and low sounds can carry for many kilometres. Some whale sounds can probably be heard by other whales far away. To learn why water carries sound so well, read [how sound travels](sound-travel).

## Songs with themes

A humpback song has a structure, a bit like a [piece of music](music-notes). Short sounds form phrases. Repeated phrases form themes. Several themes, sung in a set order, make up the song. When one song ends, the whale usually starts again from the beginning.

All the males in a population sing roughly the same song. Over the season, they change it bit by bit, adding new phrases and dropping old ones, and they all change in step. Each year's song is a little different from the last. Humpbacks are among the few animals besides people known to keep changing a learned song like this.

## A hit song across the Pacific

In 2011, scientists published a study of humpback songs recorded across the South Pacific over more than ten years. New songs appeared near eastern Australia and then spread east, from one group of whales to the next. Within a few years, whales near French Polynesia were singing them too.

It works a bit like [a catchy tune](behind-theme-music) climbing the [music charts](music-charts). Males seem to prefer something new, and when they hear it from visitors, they copy it. Scientists call this cultural transmission, meaning knowledge passed on by learning, not by genes.

## The songs that changed minds

In the 1950s, a US Navy engineer named Frank Watlington recorded strange underwater sounds near Bermuda. Later, the American scientists Roger Payne and Scott McVay studied the recordings and showed they were songs. Their paper, published in 1971, amazed the public. An [album](record-music), Songs of the Humpback Whale, sold widely and helped grow the "Save the Whales" movement. In 1970, the American composer Alan Hovhaness wrote *And God Created Great Whales*, a piece for [orchestra](orchestra) and recorded whale song.

Whaling had hunted humpbacks close to extinction. In 1986, a worldwide pause on commercial whaling began, and many humpback populations have grown since. Whale song was also placed on the [golden records](space-golden-record) carried by the [Voyager](voyager) spacecraft in 1977. In the Arabian Sea, off Oman and western India, lives a small group of humpbacks that never migrates, thought to be the most isolated humpback population in the world. Their songs differ from those of humpbacks elsewhere.
