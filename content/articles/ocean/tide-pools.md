## The short answer

A tide pool is a pocket of seawater left behind among the rocks when the [tide](tides) goes out. Rocky shores are full of them, from tiny cracks to pools big enough to wade into. Each one becomes a small world for a few hours, cut off from the open sea.

Many animals live there. [Sea anemones](clownfish) open like flowers to catch food. Barnacles, mussels and limpets cling to the rocks. Snails graze on slimy films of algae. [Crabs](crabs) hide under stones, and small fish dart into cracks. You may find starfish, sea urchins, sea slugs and sometimes a small octopus. Seaweeds give shade and shelter. All of them must cope with a home that changes completely twice a day.

## A hard place to live

The shore between high and low tide is called the [intertidal](?intertidal) zone. It is one of the toughest places on Earth to live. When the tide is in, animals are pounded by [waves](waves-how). When it goes out, they may bake in the sun or freeze on a cold night.

A tide pool can change fast. On a hot day, the water warms up, loses oxygen and gets saltier as it evaporates. When it rains, the pool gets less salty. Animals that live there must survive all of this until the sea comes back and refreshes the water.

## Living in layers

Rocky shores often have bands of life, like stripes on the rock. High up, where the sea only splashes, live animals that can survive long hours in the air, such as small periwinkle snails and some barnacles. Lower down, mussels crowd together. The lowest band, uncovered only at the lowest tides, has the most delicate animals, like soft sea anemones, starfish and sea urchins.

Some animals have clever tricks. Limpets clamp down so tightly that it is very hard to pull them off. Many limpets also return to the same spot on the rock after feeding, where their shell has worn a perfect fit. Barnacles start life as tiny swimming larvae, then [glue](glue-sticky) their heads to a rock and build a shell around themselves. They spend the rest of their lives kicking food into their mouths with their legs.

## Hunters in the pools

Tide pools can be fierce places. Sea anemones are animals, cousins of [jellyfish](jellyfish-old), and their soft tentacles carry tiny stinging cells that catch passing shrimps and fish. Starfish crawl slowly over mussel beds and open them, as you can read in [how a starfish eats](starfish).

Some crabs and small fish change colour to match the rocks. Octopuses sometimes hunt in larger pools and can squeeze through any gap bigger than their beak. Shore birds, such as herons and oystercatchers, visit at low tide to pick off anything they can find.

## Exploring safely

The best time to explore is at low tide, especially during spring tides, when the water goes out farthest. These happen around the new and full moon, as you can read in [Moon phases](moon-phases). Always go with an adult, check the tide times, and keep an eye on the sea, because waves and the returning tide can come in fast.

On the Pacific coast of the USA, places like Monterey in California are famous for their pools. In 1939, the marine biologist Ed Ricketts wrote a guidebook to them with Jack Calvin, called Between Pacific Tides, still read today. In India, groups in [Mumbai](mumbai) lead walks along the city's rocky shores at low tide, finding sea anemones, octopuses and [corals](coral-reefs) right beside the busy city. Look closely, touch gently, and put back any stone you turn over.
