## The short answer

Yes. The oldest shark-like scales ever found are about 450 million years old. The first trees appeared around 385 million years ago. So sharks were cruising the seas for tens of millions of years before any forest grew on land.

Sharks are also much older than dinosaurs, which appeared about 230 million years ago. Since then, sharks have lived through [several mass extinctions](dino-extinction-theories), including the one that [wiped out the dinosaurs](what-if-dinos-lived), as you can read in [who survived](who-survived).

The sharks of long ago didn't look exactly like today's. But the basic plan, a fast, streamlined hunter with a skeleton of rubbery cartilage, has lasted an astonishingly long time.

## Clues in teeth and scales

Shark skeletons are made of cartilage, the same bendy stuff as the tip of your nose. Cartilage rots away quickly, so whole fossil sharks are rare. Instead, scientists mostly find teeth and tiny scales, which are hard like bone.

Luckily, sharks shed teeth all their lives. A shark has rows of spare teeth lined up behind the front ones, moving forward like a conveyor belt. One shark can lose thousands in its lifetime. Compare that with your own [teeth](teeth): just two sets. Beaches near Venice, Florida, are so full of fossil shark teeth that people sift the sand for them.

## The giant that disappeared

The biggest shark ever was [Megalodon](megalodon). From its teeth, some as long as your hand, scientists estimate it grew to around 15 to 18 m, longer than a school bus. It probably hunted whales.

Megalodon died out about 3.6 million years ago. The seas were cooling, and its prey was changing. Some scientists think the great white shark, a smaller and nimbler rival, may have added to its troubles.

## Senses from another world

Sharks can [smell](nose-smell) blood in the water from a long way off. A line of sensors along each side of the body feels the tiniest ripples. And tiny jelly-filled pores on a shark's snout can pick up the faint electric signals made by the muscles of a hiding fish.

Some sharks live astonishingly long. In 2016, scientists studied [Greenland sharks](extreme-longest-living) and estimated that one was nearly 400 years old, give or take more than a century. Even the low end of that guess makes it the longest-living animal with a backbone known.

## Gujarat's gentle giant

Today's biggest fish is the whale shark, which can grow longer than a city bus. Despite its size, it eats only tiny plankton and small fish. Every year, whale sharks visit the coast of Gujarat.

Fishermen there once hunted them. In 2001, India made the whale shark the first fish protected under its wildlife law. Campaigners worked with local leaders, including the spiritual teacher Morari Bapu, who compared the whale shark to a daughter coming home to her parents. Now many fishermen cut their own nets to free a trapped whale shark.

[Sharks bite fewer than a hundred people](sharks-myths) worldwide in a typical year. The 1975 [Hollywood](hollywood) film *Jaws* made audiences around the world afraid of them anyway, and the novel's author, Peter Benchley, later campaigned to protect sharks. People, meanwhile, catch many millions of sharks every year. Many are caught by accident in nets meant for other fish, and some are killed only for their fins. Today about a third of all kinds of sharks and rays are threatened.

That is a strange twist. Sharks were already ancient when the first trees took root, long before giants like the [redwoods](redwoods) appeared. After 450 million years, the biggest danger they have ever faced may be us.
