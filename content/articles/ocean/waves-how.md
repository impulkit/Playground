## The short answer

Most ocean waves are made by wind. When wind blows across calm water, it rubs the surface and makes small ripples. The ripples give the wind something to push against, so they grow into bigger and bigger waves. The [wind](wind-blows) passes its energy to the water.

Three things decide how big waves get: how strong the wind is, how long it blows, and how much open water it blows across. Sailors call that stretch of open water the fetch. A light breeze over a pond makes ripples. A powerful storm blowing for days over thousands of kilometres of open ocean can raise waves taller than a house. Once made, waves can travel huge distances, long after the storm that made them has died away.

## Energy on the move

A wave is a moving bump of energy, not a moving river of water. As a wave passes, each bit of water near the surface moves in a circle: up, forward, down and back. Then it is roughly where it began. That is why [a floating bottle](ocean-currents-travel) out at sea mostly bobs in place.

Waves on a skipping rope work the same way. If you shake one end, a wave travels to the other end, but the rope itself doesn't go anywhere. Sound is a wave too, travelling through air instead of water, as you can read in [how sound travels](sound-travel).

The circles get smaller with depth. A few tens of metres down, even a large storm wave is hardly felt. [Submarines](submarines) dive below rough weather for this reason.

## Why waves break

Near the shore, things change. As a wave moves into shallow water, its bottom starts to drag on the sea floor and slows down. The top keeps going faster. The wave grows taller and steeper, until the top tips over and crashes forward. That is a breaking wave.

The shape of the beach decides how waves break. On a gently sloping beach, waves spill over slowly in a line of white foam. On a steep beach or a sudden reef, they curl over into a hollow tube before they crash, the kind surfers love. [Surfing](sport-surfing) itself grew up in Hawaii, where people rode waves on wooden boards long before European sailors arrived, as you can read in [Hawaiian culture](hawaii-culture).

## Monster waves

Most waves are a few metres high, but some are giants. On 1 January 1995, instruments on the Draupner oil platform in the North Sea measured a single wave about 25.6 m tall, far bigger than the waves around it. Sailors had long told stories of such rogue waves. Many scientists doubted them, until this measurement proved they were real. They can form when several waves pile on top of each other at the same moment.

At Nazaré in Portugal, an underwater canyon focuses the waves of winter storms. Surfers there have ridden waves taller than an eight-storey building.

## Not all waves come from wind

A [tsunami](tsunami) is a different kind of wave. Its name is Japanese for "harbour wave", one of many [words English has borrowed](words-borrowed). It is usually caused by an [earthquake](earthquakes) under the sea, which suddenly lifts or drops a huge amount of water. Out in the open ocean, [a tsunami](tsunami-warning) may be less than a metre high and pass under ships unnoticed, but it moves as fast as a jet plane. When it reaches shallow water, it rears up into a wall of water. The 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami struck the coasts of India, Sri Lanka, [Thailand](thailand) and Indonesia.

Tides are waves too, of a sort: the longest waves on Earth, pulled along by the Moon and Sun, as explained in [tides](tides). On India's west coast, the monsoon brings rough seas from June, and fishing boats stay home for safety. The people who fish there learn to read waves from childhood, as you can see in [how India's fishing communities read the sea](fishing-boats).
