## The short answer

A lighthouse is a tower with a powerful light at the top that warns ships of danger and guides them safely to harbour. Lighthouses stand on rocky headlands, at the mouths of rivers and ports, and on lonely reefs far out at sea. At night, sailors look for their light. By day, they recognise each tower by its shape and painted stripes.

The light does not just shine. It flashes in a pattern called the lighthouse's character. One might flash white twice every ten seconds, another might show a long red flash. Sailors compare the pattern with their charts to know exactly which lighthouse they are seeing, and so where they are. The beam can often be seen 30 km or more away.

## Bending light into a beam

A bare lamp spreads light in every direction, and most of it is wasted. Early lighthouses burned wood, coal or oil, and much of the glow disappeared into the sky. In 1822, a French scientist named Augustin-Jean Fresnel invented a new kind of lens. It was made of rings of [glass](glass-made) prisms arranged around the lamp like a beehive.

Each ring bends the light so that it all heads out in the same direction, in a strong, flat beam. A Fresnel lens is much thinner and lighter than an ordinary lens of the same power. Many lenses turned slowly on a bed of liquid mercury, so the beam swept round like a clock hand, giving a flash each time it passed. You can learn how lenses bend light in [lenses and glasses](lenses-glasses).

## Why height matters

Earth is curved, so a light at sea level disappears below the horizon quite quickly. The higher the light, the farther it can be seen. A lamp 50 m above the sea can be seen from a ship's deck roughly 30 km away, well before the ship reaches danger.

That is why lighthouses are tall, or are built on top of cliffs. The famous Pharos of Alexandria in Egypt, built more than 2,000 years ago, may have been around 100 m tall. It was one of the [Seven Wonders](seven-wonders) of the ancient world and stood for many centuries before earthquakes brought it down.

## Lighthouses in India

India has nearly 200 lighthouses along its coasts and islands, looked after by the Directorate General of Lighthouses and Lightships. On the Marina in [Chennai](chennai), a modern lighthouse lets visitors look out over one of the longest city beaches in the world. At [Mahabalipuram](pallavas), an ancient rock temple on a hill is said to have been used as a beacon long ago, and a British lighthouse from the 1880s stands next to it.

Some lighthouses guard very remote places, such as tiny islands in the Andaman and Nicobar Islands and [Lakshadweep](lakshadweep). In recent years, many Indian lighthouses have opened to visitors, with museums and viewing galleries.

## Keepers and machines

For centuries, lighthouse keepers lived in or beside the towers. They trimmed wicks, polished lenses, wound up the clockwork that turned the light and sounded foghorns in bad weather. It was lonely work, especially on rocks far out at sea.

[Electric lamps](electricity-history) replaced flames, and then the lights were automated. Today most lighthouses run on their own, often powered by [solar panels](solar-panels), and are checked by visiting technicians. Ships now rely mainly on [GPS](gps-how) and electronic charts. But a satellite signal can fail, and a light on a dark coast still means the same thing it did in ancient Alexandria: danger here, steer clear.
