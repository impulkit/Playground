## The short answer

When you flush, [water from the tank](how-water-tank-overflow) rushes into the bowl. It fills the curved pipe below so fast that it starts a siphon, a strong pull that sucks the water and waste down and away. Then the bowl refills, leaving a little water at the bottom to block smells.

From there, the waste water travels through pipes under your home into bigger pipes under the street, called sewers. They slope gently downhill, so [gravity](gravity-fall) does most of the work, with pumps helping where the ground is flat. The sewers lead to a treatment plant, where the water is cleaned and returned to a river or the sea. In many Indian towns and villages, homes use septic tanks instead, which hold and break down the waste underground.

## The bend that saves your nose

The pipe below every toilet, sink and shower has a bend in it, shaped like a U or an S. Water always settles in the low part and stays there. This small pool seals the pipe, stopping foul-smelling gases in the sewer from drifting back up into your bathroom.

The Scottish watchmaker Alexander Cumming patented this S-shaped bend in 1775. It was a simple idea, but it made indoor toilets much more pleasant. Before it, many homes in Europe used chamber pots, and many Indian homes had toilets outside, away from the living rooms. A bend full of water let the toilet move indoors.

## Drains of the Indus cities

Some of the world's earliest drainage systems were built in the [Indus Valley](indus-cities), in what is now Pakistan and northwest India. In cities like Mohenjo-daro, Harappa and Dholavira, about 4,500 years ago, many houses had bathing areas connected to brick drains. These drains ran along the streets and were covered with bricks or stone slabs, with openings so workers could clean them.

Few ancient cities had anything so organised. [Rome](rome-city) later built a great sewer called the Cloaca Maxima, but many cities around the world simply tipped waste into streets and rivers for thousands of years.

## London's Great Stink

By the 1850s, [London](london) had more than two million people, and much of their waste flowed straight into the River Thames. Cholera outbreaks killed thousands. In the hot summer of 1858, the smell from the river was so bad that it became known as the Great Stink. Members of Parliament, sitting right beside the Thames, hung curtains soaked in chemicals over the windows.

That finally pushed the government to act. The engineer Joseph Bazalgette designed a network of huge brick sewers that carried waste east, away from the city centre. Much of it, built from 1859 into the 1870s, is still in use today.

## Bacteria do the cleaning

At a modern treatment plant, the first step is to screen out rags, plastic and grit. Then the waste water rests in big tanks so heavier material sinks. Next comes the clever part. Air is bubbled through the water, and huge numbers of helpful [microbes](microbes-friendly) feed on the waste, breaking it down. The cleaned water settles once more and may be disinfected before it flows into a river.

The leftover sludge can even be turned into fertiliser or into [biogas](biogas), a fuel. Since 2014, India's Swachh Bharat Mission has built many millions of toilets. The UN marks World Toilet Day every 19 November, because billions of people still lack safe sanitation.
