## The short answer

When glue is runny, it can flow into every tiny bump and crack on a surface. Even [paper](paper-made) or wood that feels smooth is full of microscopic hills and valleys. The glue seeps into them and gets very close to the surface itself. Up close, its [molecules](?molecule) are attracted to the molecules of the surface, a pull called adhesion.

Then the glue changes from liquid to solid. Some glues dry as water or another liquid escapes. Others harden through a chemical reaction. As they set, the glue's own molecules grip each other tightly, a pull called cohesion. Now you have a solid bridge, gripping one surface on one side and the other surface on the other.

A good glue needs both kinds of grip. If either one fails, the join comes apart.

## The first glue makers

People have been making glue since long before history was written. In Italy, archaeologists have found [stone tools](stone-tools) with traces of birch tar on them, left by [Neanderthals](neanderthals) more than 100,000 years ago. Birch tar is made by heating birch bark without much air, until a sticky black goo oozes out. It was used to fix stone blades onto wooden handles.

Later peoples boiled animal skins and bones to make glue, and the ancient Egyptians used it on furniture. In India, craftspeople have long used natural resins from trees, and in many homes a paste of cooked rice or flour still sticks paper together. [Stone Age](stone-age) toolmakers were, in a sense, the first chemists.

## How modern glues set

White school glue is sold as PVA, and under brand names like Fevicol in India and Elmer's in the USA. It is tiny bits of sticky plastic floating in water. As the water soaks away and evaporates, the plastic bits crowd together and fuse into a clear, bendy film.

[Superglue](accident-superglue) works differently. It reacts with the tiny amount of water that is almost always present in the air and on surfaces. That reaction links its molecules into long chains within seconds. Epoxy glue comes in two tubes. Mixed together, the two liquids react and harden into a tough [plastic](plastic-made).

## Happy accidents

In 1942, the American chemist Harry Coover was trying to make clear plastic for gun sights. One of his chemicals stuck to everything and was set aside as useless. Years later, in 1951, Coover and a colleague came across it again and saw what it could be. It went on sale in 1958 as superglue.

[Sticky notes](accident-post-it) started with a failure too. In 1968, Spencer Silver at the American company 3M made a glue that was too weak. It stuck, but peeled off easily. Years later, his colleague Art Fry used it on bookmarks for his church songbook, so they wouldn't fall out. The little yellow notes went on sale in 1980.

## Nature's glues

Living things make glue too. Mussels in [tide pools](tide-pools) spin threads tipped with a protein glue that holds firm underwater, something most human glues can't do. Scientists are copying it to make glues for surgery.

Spiders coat parts of their webs with sticky droplets to trap insects. Some caddisfly larvae glue tiny stones into tube-shaped homes. And [geckos](power-gecko-feet) climb walls without any glue at all, using millions of tiny hairs on their toes that are attracted to the surface, the same pull that helps glue hold on.
