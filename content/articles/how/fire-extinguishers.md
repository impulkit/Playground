## The short answer

A fire is a fast chemical reaction called [combustion](?combustion). It needs heat, fuel and oxygen, a trio firefighters call the fire triangle. Take away any one side and the flames die.

A fire extinguisher is a metal can full of something that attacks one side of that triangle. Water cools the fuel below the temperature where it can burn. Carbon dioxide and foam smother the flames so oxygen cannot reach them. Dry powder gets in the way of the chemical reaction itself. Inside the can, gas under high [pressure](?pressure) waits to blast the contents out the moment you squeeze the handle.

## What is inside the red can

Most portable extinguishers have the same basic parts: a strong steel or aluminium cylinder, a valve with a handle and a hose or nozzle. A safety pin stops the handle being squeezed by accident.

In a stored-pressure extinguisher, the powder or water shares the can with squashed nitrogen or air. Pull the pin, squeeze the lever, and the valve opens. The gas pushes everything out through the nozzle in a jet. A carbon dioxide extinguisher is different. It holds CO₂ squeezed so hard that it turns liquid, and it comes out as a freezing white cloud. That is why it has a wide horn and why you should never touch the horn while using it.

## Different fires, different weapons

Firefighters sort fires into classes. Burning wood, paper and cloth can be cooled with water. Burning petrol, oil or paint needs foam or powder, because water just floats the burning liquid around. Fires in electrical equipment need something that does not carry electricity, like CO₂ or powder.

Kitchen fires get their own class. A pan of oil can reach over 300°C. A splash of water sinks, boils instantly and throws burning oil into the air. Wet chemical extinguishers instead spray a liquid that reacts with the hot fat to form a soapy crust on top, sealing off the air. The lid that cooks are taught to slide over a small pan fire, one of the first rules of [kitchen safety](kitchen-safety), works on the same smothering idea.

## Pull, aim, squeeze, sweep

Small extinguishers empty fast, often in under 20 seconds. So people are trained to use them with a simple method, remembered as PASS. Pull the pin. Aim at the base of the fire, where the fuel is, not at the dancing flames on top. Squeeze the handle. Sweep from side to side.

Extinguishers are for small fires that have just started. If a fire is spreading, the right move is to get out and call the fire service: 101 or 112 in India, 911 in the USA. Using one is a job for adults. Children should leave, raise the alarm and, if anyone is burned, let a grown-up start [first aid](first-aid) with cool running water.

## A short history of putting fires out

For most of history, fires were fought with buckets passed hand to hand. In 1723, a chemist in London named Ambrose Godfrey patented a device that used a small explosion to scatter fire-fighting liquid. It was clever but dangerous.

The ancestor of today's extinguisher came in 1818. George Manby, an English captain, had watched firefighters struggle to reach the upper floors of a burning building in Edinburgh. He designed a copper can holding about 13 litres of water with compressed air above it. Ordinary people could carry it to a fire. Since then the chemicals have changed, but the idea of squashed gas pushing out something that breaks the [fire triangle](fire-burns) has stayed the same. Some homes now also keep a fire blanket, which is just that idea folded up in a pouch.
