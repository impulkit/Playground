## The short answer

Pure water freezes at 0 °C. Salty water has to get colder than that before it freezes. Scientists call this freezing point depression.

On a winter road, the top of the ice is always coated with a thin film of liquid water. When salt dissolves into that film, it makes salty water, which can stay liquid below 0 °C. The salty water eats into the ice around it, which makes more salty water, and the ice slowly turns to slush. Snowploughs can then push the slush away, and [car tyres](tyres) can grip the road again.

Salt works best when it's not too cold. Below about minus 10 °C, ordinary rock salt struggles, and road crews switch to other chemicals or to sand and grit.

## Why salt stops freezing

When water freezes, its [molecules](?molecule) lock together into a neat, six-sided pattern of ice crystals. That's also why [ice floats](why-ice-floats): the pattern takes up more space than liquid water.

Dissolved salt breaks into tiny charged particles that get in the way. They make it harder for water molecules to line up and join the crystal. So the water has to be colder before it can freeze. The more salt, the colder it has to get, down to about minus 21 °C for the saltiest possible mixture.

Sea water, which is salty, freezes at about minus 2 °C. That's why the ocean around Antarctica can stay liquid when the air is far below freezing.

## A mountain of salt

Cold countries use a lot of road salt. In some winters the USA spreads more than 20 million tonnes, according to the US Geological Survey. Cities around the [Great Lakes](great-lakes), like [Chicago](chicago), store it in giant domes and send out fleets of trucks when snow is forecast.

Crews often spray salty water called brine onto roads before a storm arrives. The brine stops ice from bonding firmly to the road, so the snow is easier to clear. Some places have tried unusual extras. Towns in Wisconsin have used salty leftover brine from cheese-making, and others mix in sugar beet juice, which helps salt stick and work at colder temperatures.

## Kulfi and the ice-salt trick

The same science that clears roads can [freeze a dessert](ice-cream-freezing). When salt is mixed with crushed ice, the ice starts melting. Melting needs heat, which it takes from its surroundings, so the mixture gets much colder than plain ice, down to around minus 20 °C.

For centuries, cooks in India set kulfi in cone-shaped metal moulds packed into earthen pots of ice and salt. The milk mixture froze solid in the extra-cold slush. Before home freezers, American families made [ice cream](ice-cream) in a hand-cranked churn the same way, with a bucket of ice and rock salt around a metal can.

## The trouble with salt

Salt is cheap and works, but it causes problems. Salty water speeds up [rust](rust) on cars, bridges and the steel inside concrete. As snow melts, the salt washes into streams, lakes and groundwater. The US Geological Survey has found rising salt levels in many rivers in the northern USA, which can harm fish, frogs and plants.

Cities are trying to use less, by measuring roads' temperature, spreading only what's needed and using more brine. In the high Himalayas of India, where passes freeze for months, road crews depend more on snow-clearing machines and closures than on salt. Every icy road is a balance between safety today and cleaner water tomorrow.
