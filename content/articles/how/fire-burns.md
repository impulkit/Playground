## The short answer

Fire is what we see when something burns. Scientists call it [combustion](?combustion). It's a chemical reaction in which a fuel, such as wood, gas, wax or paper, joins with oxygen from the air. The reaction releases a lot of energy as heat and light, very quickly.

For a fire to start and keep going, it needs three things: fuel to burn, oxygen to join with it, and enough heat to get the reaction going. [Firefighters](jobs-firefighter) call this the fire triangle. Take away any one side, and the fire goes out.

Once a fire starts, it makes its own heat. That heat warms nearby fuel, which starts to burn too. That's why a small flame can grow so quickly, and why fires need to be treated with great care.

## What's in a flame

When wood or wax gets hot, it doesn't burn as a solid. It first breaks down and gives off gases. Those gases mix with oxygen and burn, and that glowing region is the flame.

Most [candle](candles) and wood flames are yellow-orange because they contain tiny specks of soot, bits of unburned carbon. The specks get so hot that they glow, like the filament in an old [light bulb](light-bulb). A [gas stove](cooking-gas) flame is usually blue, because the gas is mixed well with air before it burns. It burns more completely, with very little soot, and the blue comes from the reacting gas itself.

Flames point upwards because the hot gases are lighter than the cooler air around them. They rise, pulling fresh air in from below.

## A flame in space

On the International [Space Station](space-station), there's no "up" for hot gases to rise to. NASA has studied this with careful experiments. Without rising air, a candle flame forms a small, dim, round ball, often blue. It burns slowly, because fresh oxygen only drifts in gradually.

These experiments help scientists understand burning better, and they help keep spacecraft safe. A fire in a sealed cabin is one of the most dangerous things that can happen in space. Astronauts can't step outside or open a window, so the station carries special extinguishers and smoke detectors in every module.

## Our oldest tool

Fire changed human history. It gave people warmth, light, protection from animals and a way to cook food. Cooking makes many foods softer and safer to eat and gives the body more energy from them.

When people first learned to control fire is hard to pin down. In Wonderwerk Cave in South Africa, scientists found burnt bones and plant ash about a million years old, which may be some of the earliest evidence. Other researchers think regular, everyday use of fire came much later, perhaps a few hundred thousand years ago.

## Sunlight stored in wood

The energy in a burning log came from the Sun. While the tree was growing, its leaves used sunlight to build sugars from water and carbon dioxide, a process called [photosynthesis](photosynthesis). Burning releases that stored energy again, and gives the carbon dioxide back to the air.

Burning and [rusting](rust) are close relatives. Both happen when a substance joins with oxygen. Rusting is just very slow, and you can't feel the warmth it gives off. Fire is the same kind of reaction, sped up so much that it glows. [Car engines](cars-engine), gas stoves and power stations all use burning, carefully controlled, to make heat and movement.
