## The short answer

A candle is a small fuel factory. The heat of the flame melts a pool of wax at the top of the candle. The wick soaks up that liquid wax and carries it upwards, the way a strip of cloth soaks up spilt water.

Near the flame, the liquid wax gets so hot that it turns into a gas. That gas mixes with oxygen from the air and burns. The burning makes more heat, which melts more wax, and the cycle keeps going until the wax runs out.

So the solid candle you hold is a store of fuel. Only the thin, invisible layer of wax gas around the wick is ever on fire.

## A pump made of cotton

A wick is a braid of cotton threads with tiny gaps between them. Liquids creep up narrow gaps like these on their own. Scientists call it [capillary](?capillary) action, and it is the same effect that pulls water up the fine tubes in a plant.

The wick has to be the right size. Too thin, and it can't lift enough wax, so the flame shrinks and dies. Too thick, and it lifts more than the flame can burn, which makes a smoky, flickering candle. Many modern wicks are braided so they curl over as they burn. The tip pokes into the hottest part of the flame and burns away, so nobody has to trim it.

## Reading the colours of a flame

Look closely at a calm candle flame and you can see layers. Right at the bottom, near the wick, there is a small blue zone. Here the wax gas meets plenty of oxygen and burns cleanly.

Higher up, the flame turns bright yellow. Inside it, there isn't quite enough oxygen to burn everything at once. Tiny specks of carbon, called soot, form and get so hot that they glow, like the coil in an old [light bulb](light-bulb). That glow is most of a candle's light. The specks burn up before they escape, unless something disturbs the flame.

When the burning is complete, the [combustion](?combustion) leaves behind mostly carbon dioxide and water vapour. Hold a cold steel spoon well above a flame, and an adult can show you the mist of water that forms on it.

## A flame in space

On Earth, hot gas from a flame is lighter than the cooler air around it, so it rises. Fresh air rushes in at the bottom. That flow stretches the flame into its teardrop shape.

On the [International Space Station](space-station) there is no "up" for hot gas to float towards. NASA experiments have shown that flames there become round, small and blue. Oxygen can only drift in slowly, so they burn gently and are easy to put out.

## From animal fat to paraffin

For centuries, most people made candles from tallow, which is animal fat. Tallow candles were cheap, but they smoked and smelled of cooking. Beeswax candles burned cleaner and smelled sweet, so churches and rich families used them. Bees make that wax in their hives, the same hives they guide each other to with a [waggle dance](bees-dance).

In the 1850s, chemists learned to make paraffin wax from petroleum, the same [oil](oil-coal) that becomes petrol. Paraffin was cheap and clean, and most candles today are still made from it. In India, many families light oil lamps called diyas for [Diwali](diwali-lights), which work on the same idea: a cotton wick lifting oil to a flame.

In 1848, the scientist Michael Faraday gave a series of Christmas lectures for children in London about a single candle. They became a book that is still in print, and he explained almost everything above with nothing more than a flame and a few jars.
