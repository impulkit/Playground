## The short answer

A whistle makes sound by making air wobble very fast. When you blow into a whistle, a narrow jet of air shoots across a hole and hits a sharp edge. The jet can't decide which side of the edge to go, so it flips back and forth, hundreds or thousands of times a second.

Behind the edge is a hollow chamber. The air inside it starts vibrating in time with the flipping jet, making the vibration much louder and steadier. That vibration spreads out through the air as [sound](sound-travel).

The size of the chamber sets the note. A small chamber vibrates fast and makes a high, shrill note. A big one vibrates slowly and makes a low, hooting sound.

## Edges and echoes inside

The same idea works in many instruments. A recorder, a flute and a bansuri are all, at heart, whistles. Air is blown against a sharp edge, and a column of air inside the tube vibrates. When a player covers or uncovers holes, they change the length of that air column, and so the note.

Blowing across the top of a bottle works the same way. Your breath flips across the bottle's rim, and the air inside vibrates. Pour in some water, the air space shrinks, and the note rises. Instruments in the same [family](music-instruments-families) as the flute are sometimes called edge-blown instruments for this reason.

## The pea in the whistle

A police or referee's whistle has a small ball inside, often made of cork or plastic, called a pea. As air swirls round the chamber, it bounces the pea around, which keeps interrupting the airflow. That makes the note warble, a trilling sound that's easy to hear over shouting crowds and traffic.

One of the best-known makers was Joseph Hudson, a toolmaker in Birmingham, England. His company, Acme, began making whistles in the 1870s. In the 1880s, London's Metropolitan Police chose his loud whistle to replace the wooden rattles constables had used to call for help. Acme still makes whistles used in football matches around the world.

## A village of whistled names

In Kongthong, a Khasi village in the hills of [Meghalaya](meghalaya), mothers make up a short tune for each new baby. The tune becomes that person's name, used alongside their spoken name. Villagers call to each other across the forest using these melodies, which carry further than shouted words.

Elsewhere, people whistle whole sentences. On La Gomera, one of Spain's Canary Islands, shepherds developed Silbo Gomero, a [whistled form of Spanish](whistled-languages), to talk across deep valleys. UNESCO added it to its list of the world's living cultural heritage in 2009, and children on the island learn it at school.

## Whistles you can't hear

Human [ears](ears-hear) hear notes up to about 20,000 vibrations a second, and fewer as we get older. Dogs can hear notes far higher than that. In 1876, the English scientist Francis Galton made a tiny adjustable whistle to test how high different animals could hear. Dog trainers still use such "silent" whistles, which sound loud to a dog and faint or silent to people.

At home, the whistle you know best may be the one on a pressure cooker. That isn't quite the same thing. Steam pressure lifts a heavy weight on the lid, and the steam rushes out with a shriek. A [pressure cooker](pressure-cooker) counting its whistles is a sound of dinner time in millions of Indian kitchens.
