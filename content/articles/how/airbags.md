## The short answer

An airbag is a folded nylon bag hidden in the steering wheel, the dashboard, the seats or the side of the roof. It inflates only in a crash.

Sensors in the car feel for a very sudden slowdown, much sharper than any normal braking. When that happens, a small computer sends an electric signal to a device called an inflator. The signal sets off a quick chemical reaction that produces a large puff of nitrogen gas. The gas rushes into the bag and fills it in about 30 thousandths of a second, faster than a blink.

The bag then starts to empty at once through small holes, so it cushions you instead of bouncing you back.

## Sensing a crash

In a crash, the car stops almost instantly, but the people inside keep moving forward. The airbag has to be in place before they reach the steering wheel or dashboard. Human [reflexes](reflexes) are far too slow to help: by the time your brain notices a crash, it is already over. The car has to think for you.

Modern cars use electronic [sensors](?sensor) called accelerometers, small chips that measure how fast speed is changing. Many smartphones contain the same kind of chip to know which way up they are. The car's computer compares the readings with patterns of real crashes. A bump in the road or a hard stop shouldn't trigger the bag. A crash into a wall at speed should.

## Gas from a pellet

Blowing up a big bag with a pump would take far too long. Instead, the inflator contains a small amount of solid chemical. When the electric signal reaches it, the chemical reacts in a flash, producing a lot of hot gas, mostly nitrogen, the same gas that makes up most of the air you breathe.

That gas pours through a filter into the bag. Within a fraction of a second, a bag the size of a beach ball is ready. You might see a puff of powder afterwards. That's usually just cornflour or talc used to stop the folded bag sticking to itself.

## Catching you gently

Stopping suddenly is what hurts in a crash. An airbag spreads that stop over a slightly longer time and a larger area of your body. Instead of your head hitting a hard wheel, it sinks into a soft, emptying cushion.

But an airbag opens with great force. It is designed for an adult wearing a [seatbelt](seatbelts), which holds the body in the right place. That's why safety agencies in the USA and elsewhere say children under 13 should ride in the back seat. A baby's rear-facing car seat must never go in front of an active airbag.

## How airbags spread

Inventors in the USA and Germany patented early airbag ideas in the early 1950s, but the technology for fast sensors and safe inflators took decades. Some luxury [cars](cars-history) offered airbags in the 1970s and 1980s.

In the USA, front airbags became compulsory for both front seats in all new cars from 1998. In India, the government made front airbags compulsory in new cars in the early 2020s. Many cars now have six or more airbags, including curtains that drop down beside the windows. Safety rules took years of argument, testing and crash data, and [road safety](road-safety) experts still say the cheapest protection of all is the belt.
