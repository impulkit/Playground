## The short answer

Every camera does the same basic job. Light bounces off the thing you are photographing and enters the camera through a small opening. A lens bends that light so it lands in a sharp, upside-down picture on a surface at the back.

In old cameras that surface was film, coated with chemicals that darkened where light hit them. In a [phone](smartphones) or digital camera it is a [sensor](?sensor), a chip covered with millions of tiny light-catchers. Each one measures how much light falls on it and turns that into a number. A computer inside the camera puts all those numbers together into the photo you see.

## A dark room with a hole

Long before film, people noticed something odd about dark rooms. Light coming through a small hole paints an upside-down picture of the outside world on the far wall. This is called a *camera obscura*, Latin for "dark room". Around the year 1000, the scientist Ibn al-Haytham, working in Cairo, described how it worked in his *Book of Optics*.

Your own eye works the same way. The pupil is the hole, the lens focuses the light, and the retina at the back catches the picture. Your brain quietly turns it the right way up.

## Catching the picture for good

Seeing the picture was easy. Keeping it was the hard part. Around 1826–27, the French inventor Nicéphore Niépce pointed a camera out of an upstairs window and coated a metal plate with a substance that hardened in light. The exposure took many hours. The blurry rooftops it shows are the oldest photograph that still survives.

Inventors soon found faster chemicals. By 1888, the American company Kodak was selling a simple box camera loaded with film. You took your pictures, posted the whole camera back, and they returned your prints. The story of those early years is in [the history of photography](photography-history).

## From film to numbers

In 1975, a young Kodak engineer named Steven Sasson built the first digital camera. It was about the size of a toaster. Its pictures were 100 dots wide and 100 dots tall, and saving each one to tape took about 23 seconds.

Today's sensors hold millions of light-catchers, each one making one [pixel](pixels) of the final image. On their own, they can only measure brightness, not colour. So each one sits under a tiny red, green or blue filter, laid out like a chessboard. The camera then works out the true colour of every dot by comparing it with its neighbours.

## Three controls that make a photo

Photographers juggle three settings. The **aperture** is the size of the opening, like your pupil getting wider in the dark. The **shutter** decides how long light is let in: a thousandth of a second [freezes a cricket ball in the air](how-cameras-speed), while a few seconds turns car headlights into streaks. The third is how sensitive the sensor is set to be.

A phone makes all three choices for you in a blink. It often takes several shots at once and blends them, which is why phone photos can look bright even in a dim room. The [lens](lenses-glasses) is still doing the oldest job of all: bending light to a point.
