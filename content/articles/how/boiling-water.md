## The short answer

Water is made of tiny [molecules](?molecule) that are always moving and bumping into each other. Heating water makes them move faster. At the bottom of a pan, the water closest to the flame gets hottest. When it reaches its boiling point, some molecules move so fast that they break free of their neighbours and become a gas, called water vapour or steam.

That gas forms [bubbles](bubbles-round) at the bottom of the pan. The bubbles are lighter than the water around them, so they rise, burst at the surface and release the steam into the air. At sea level this happens at 100°C. As long as the water keeps boiling, it stays at that temperature, even if you turn the flame up.

## The bubbles come in stages

If you watch a pan heating up, you'll see things happen in order. First come tiny bubbles that cling to the sides. These are air, which was dissolved in the water and comes out as the water warms.

Next comes a hissing or singing sound. Steam bubbles are forming on the hot bottom, then collapsing as they rise into cooler water above. Each collapse makes a tiny crackle. Once all the water is hot enough, the steam bubbles survive the trip to the top and the water rolls and churns. That is a full boil.

## Steam you can't see

Real steam is invisible. Look closely at the spout of a boiling kettle and you'll see a small clear gap just above it. The white cloud starts a little further away. That cloud is made of tiny droplets of liquid water, formed when the hot vapour hits cooler air and turns back into liquid.

Clouds in the sky form the same way, when water vapour cools high above the ground. The [water cycle](water-cycle) is really one enormous, slow version of a kettle: the Sun heats the sea, vapour rises, and it cools into clouds and rain.

## Why mountains change the rules

Water boils when its molecules can push back against the air pressing down on the surface. At sea level, the weight of all the air above is fairly heavy. High up a mountain there is less air on top, so the water molecules escape more easily and the water boils at a lower temperature.

In Leh, in [Ladakh](ladakh), about 3,500 metres up, water boils at around 88°C. On top of [Everest](mountaineering) it boils at about 70°C. Water that boils at a lower temperature cooks food more slowly, so dal and rice take much longer. That's why people in high places rely on the [pressure cooker](pressure-cooker), which raises the pressure and lets water get hotter before it boils.

## Small bubbles, big power

When water turns to steam, it spreads out to take up about 1,600 times more room. Trap that steam in a closed space and it pushes very hard. This push drove the [steam engines](steam-engine) that powered factories, ships and the first railways in the 1800s, including India's first passenger train in 1853.

Boiling also makes water safer to drink. Keeping water at a rolling boil for a minute kills most of the germs that cause illness. Steam is hotter than it looks, though, and a jet of it can scald skin badly, so kettles and cookers are best left to adults.
