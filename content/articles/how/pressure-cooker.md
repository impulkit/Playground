## The short answer

In an open pot, water can't get hotter than its [boiling](boiling-water) point, which is about 100°C at sea level. Any extra heat just turns more water into steam, and the steam drifts away.

A pressure cooker has a lid that locks on and a rubber ring, called a gasket, that seals the gap. When the water boils, the steam can't escape. It builds up and squeezes everything inside, raising the pressure. Under higher pressure, water must get hotter before it boils, about 120°C in a typical cooker. Food cooked at 120°C instead of 100°C softens much faster. Rajma that might take more than an hour in an open pot can be ready in well under half that time.

## The whistle and the weight

On top of the lid sits a small metal weight over a narrow pipe. While the pressure is low, the weight sits snugly and blocks the pipe. As steam builds up, it eventually pushes hard enough to lift the weight. A jet of steam rushes out with a [loud whistle](whistles), the pressure drops a little, and the weight drops back down.

So the whistle is really a pressure limiter. It keeps the inside of the cooker at roughly the same high pressure the whole time. In millions of Indian kitchens, cooks count whistles as a kind of timer: two whistles for rice, perhaps five for chana. They aren't measuring pressure, just how long the food has been cooking hot.

## Papin's bone digester

In 1679, the French scientist Denis Papin, working in London, showed members of the Royal Society a heavy iron pot with a tightly clamped lid. He called it a "steam digester". It could soften bones until they crumbled, and he later cooked a supper in it for the society's members.

Papin also added a safety valve, a weighted [lever](lever-lift) that let steam escape before the pot burst. Many inventors after him experimented with the push of steam, and similar ideas later helped shape the first [steam engines](steam-engine).

## Why India loves the cooker

Indian cooking relies heavily on [dal](pulses-dal), beans, chickpeas and rice, all of which need a long time in hot water. A pressure cooker saves time and fuel, which matters when [gas cylinders](cooking-gas) cost money. Companies such as Hawkins and Prestige have sold cookers in India for decades, and in many families a cooker is one of the first things bought for a new home.

Cookers are popular in the USA too, often as electric "multi-cookers" with buttons instead of whistles. These use the same idea, just with a sensor and a chip deciding when the pressure is right. When cooking is done, the pressure has to come down before the lid can open. Many cooks simply wait for the cooker to cool, so the steam slowly turns back into water and the pressure falls on its own.

## High in the mountains

Cookers matter most where the air is thin. In Leh, in [Ladakh](ladakh), water in an open pot boils at about 88°C. Potatoes and rice stay hard for a long time, and some foods never cook properly. A pressure cooker raises the pressure back up, so the water can get hot enough again.

Mountaineers on Himalayan expeditions carry small cookers for the same reason. [Soldiers posted](people-army-life) at [high altitude](altitude) along India's northern borders use them every day. Without the trapped steam, a simple bowl of hot dal would be very hard to make.
