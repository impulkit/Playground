## The short answer

A pulley is a [wheel](wheel-invented) with a groove around its edge for a rope. By itself, a single pulley fixed to a beam doesn't make a load lighter. What it does is let you pull *down* to lift something *up*. That's easier, because you can use your own body weight to help. This is how a flag goes up a flagpole and how a bucket comes up a village well.

The real magic happens when a pulley moves with the load. Now the load hangs from two lengths of rope instead of one, and each holds half the weight. Add more pulleys and more rope, and the load is shared between even more strands. With four strands, you pull with only a quarter of the force. The catch is that you must pull four times as much rope.

## Trading force for distance

Scientists call the amount of help a machine gives its *mechanical advantage*. A [lever](lever-lift) gets it by having a long arm and a short arm. A set of pulleys gets it by sharing the weight between many strands of rope.

Here's the trade in numbers. Say you want to lift a 100 kg crate onto a truck, 1 metre up. With a single fixed pulley you pull 1 metre of rope with the force of 100 kg, more than most adults can manage. With a set of pulleys that gives four strands, you pull with the force of about 25 kg, but you pull 4 metres of rope. The same work gets done, just spread over a longer distance. A little extra is always lost to [friction](friction) in the wheels.

## Archimedes and the ship

The Greek thinker Archimedes, who lived in Syracuse in Sicily over 2,200 years ago, studied levers and pulleys. The writer Plutarch tells a story about him written down about three centuries later. King Hiero challenged Archimedes to show what his machines could do. Archimedes rigged up a system of pulleys and, sitting calmly, drew a fully loaded ship onto the shore with one hand.

We can't be sure the story happened exactly that way. But the physics is sound. With enough pulleys, one person really can move something far heavier than themselves. It just takes a lot of rope and patience.

## Pulleys all around you

Look up at a building site and you will see pulleys on top of tall cranes. Steel cables run over them, so the crane's motor can lift tonnes of concrete. [Lifts](elevators) in tall buildings hang from steel ropes running over a big pulley wheel, with a heavy counterweight on the other side to balance the car.

Sailing ships once carried hundreds of pulleys, called blocks, so sailors could raise heavy sails by hand. Gyms use pulleys in weight machines. Window blinds and old-style curtain cords use them too.

## A pulley in every village

Many [Indian villages](villages-india) drew water from deep open wells. A rope ran over a simple pulley, often called a *ghirni* in Hindi, fixed to a frame above the well. The pulley let people pull downward with their body weight instead of hauling each pot straight up, hand over hand.

The builders of India's [great stone temples](architecture-temples) and Egypt's [pyramids](pyramids) probably used ramps, levers and simple lifting frames to raise heavy blocks. Exactly how they did it is not always recorded. Whatever the method, they were using the same rule a pulley follows: a smaller push, spread over a longer distance, can move almost anything.
