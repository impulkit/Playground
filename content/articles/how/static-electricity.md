## The short answer

Everything is made of tiny [atoms](?atom). Each atom has even tinier particles called [electrons](?electron), which carry a negative electric charge. Usually, things have a balanced amount of charge, so nothing happens.

When you rub [a balloon](made-balloons) on your [hair](hair-grow), some electrons get scraped off your hair and stick to the balloon. Now the balloon has extra electrons and a negative charge. Your hair has lost some and has a positive charge. Opposite charges attract, so the balloon and your hair pull towards each other and the balloon can cling.

This kind of electricity is called static, meaning it stays put instead of flowing through a wire like the [electricity](electricity-made) in your home. It sits on the surface until it finds a way to escape, sometimes with a little spark.

## Hair that stands on end

Each strand of hair has lost electrons, so each one now carries a positive charge. Same charges push each other away. So your hairs try to get as far from one another as they can, and they spread out and stand up.

The same thing happens when you pull off a woollen sweater on a winter morning and hear tiny crackles. Those crackles are little sparks as charges jump between the sweater and your clothes or hair. In a dark room, you can sometimes see them flash.

## Amber and the ancient Greeks

About 2,600 years ago, the Greek thinker Thales of Miletus is said to have noticed something odd. He saw that amber rubbed with fur could pick up feathers and bits of straw. The Greek word for amber was *elektron*, and that is where our words "electron" and "electricity" come from.

[Amber](amber-fossils) is fossil tree resin, and some pieces have insects trapped inside from millions of years ago. For centuries, rubbed amber was a curiosity. Only in the 1600s and 1700s did European scientists start building machines that made static on purpose, rubbing spinning glass globes and collecting the charge in jars.

## The biggest spark of all

Inside a thundercloud, ice crystals and soft hail bump into each other as air currents toss them up and down. Those collisions move charge around, much like rubbing a balloon. The top of the cloud becomes positive and the bottom negative. When the difference grows too big, the charge escapes as a gigantic spark: [lightning](lightning).

In 1752, the American inventor [Benjamin Franklin](benjamin-franklin) described flying a kite in a storm to show that [lightning is electrical](lightning-rods). It was a very dangerous experiment. In 1753, a scientist in Russia named Georg Richmann was killed by lightning while trying something similar. Franklin went on to invent the lightning rod, a metal rod that carries a strike safely into the ground.

## Static at work

Static isn't only a nuisance. [Photocopiers](photocopiers) and [laser printers](printers) use it to make powdered ink cling to exactly the right parts of the paper. Factory chimneys can use charged plates to pull smoke particles out of the air before they escape. Some car paint sprayers charge the paint so it wraps evenly around the metal.

Static can also be a danger. At petrol pumps, a spark near fuel vapour could start a fire, which is why pumps are carefully earthed and phone use is often discouraged. Workers who build [computer chips](chips-silicon) wear special straps to stop static from frying the tiny circuits.
