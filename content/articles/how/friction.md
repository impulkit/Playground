## The short answer

Things slow down because of friction. When one surface slides or rolls over another, the two rub and grip. Friction is the force that resists that movement, and it always pushes against the direction something is moving.

Even surfaces that feel smooth are rough when seen very close up. Under a microscope, polished steel looks like a range of tiny hills and valleys. When two surfaces touch, those bumps catch on each other, and at the points where they press hard together, atoms on each side stick a little. Breaking those contacts takes effort. That effort becomes heat, which is why your hands warm up when you rub them together.

Air causes friction too. It's called air resistance, or drag, and it's why a dropped feather drifts while a stone plunges.

## Why motion doesn't "run out"

For about two thousand years, many thinkers followed the Greek philosopher Aristotle, who taught that moving things naturally come to rest. In the early 1600s, [Galileo](galileo) tested this by rolling balls down slopes and up again. He noticed that the smoother he made the slopes, the further the balls rolled. He suggested that, with no friction at all, a ball on a flat surface would roll for ever.

[Isaac Newton](newton-life) later turned this into his first law of motion: an object keeps moving at the same speed in a straight line unless a force acts on it. On Earth, friction is that force nearly every time. Far out in space there is almost nothing to rub against, so [Voyager 1](voyager), launched in 1977, is still coasting away from the Sun at about 17 km every second.

## Leonardo's sketches

Leonardo da Vinci, the painter of the *Mona Lisa*, filled notebooks with drawings of blocks being dragged across tables. Around 1500, he worked out that the friction on a block depends on how hard it presses down, not on how big its bottom surface is. His notes stayed unpublished for centuries. The French scientist Guillaume Amontons found the same rules again in 1699, and today they are often named after him.

## Friction is your friend

It's easy to think of friction as the enemy. Without it, though, you couldn't walk. Your shoes would skid on the floor as if it were oiled ice. Knots would slip undone, nails would slide out of wood, and [a pencil](pencils) would leave no mark on paper.

[Car tyres](tyres) and [running shoes](shoes-history) have patterned treads to grip the road. Brakes work by pressing pads against a spinning disc or wheel, turning the car's motion into heat. Cricket fast bowlers shine one side of the ball on their trousers. A smooth side and a rough side drag the air differently, which helps the ball [swing](cricket-swing).

## Fighting friction

When we want things to move freely, we fight friction. Oil and grease fill in the tiny bumps so surfaces slide past each other. Ball bearings, small steel balls inside a wheel's hub, let parts roll instead of scrape. The [wheel](wheel-invented) itself is the oldest friction-beater of all: rolling a load is far easier than dragging it.

Ice skaters glide because ice has a very thin, slippery layer on top. Scientists are still arguing about exactly what makes that layer, but it lets a skater cross the rink with a single push. A bobsled run goes further, smoothing and icing the whole track so sleds can reach well over 100 km/h.
