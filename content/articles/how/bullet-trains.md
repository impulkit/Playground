## The short answer

A bullet train goes fast because almost everything about it is designed for speed. It runs on its own special tracks, with gentle curves, no level crossings and long, smooth welded rails. It is powered by electricity from overhead wires, with motors spread along many carriages rather than one big engine at the front. And its long, sleek nose slices through the air.

At high speed, air resistance is the main thing a train has to fight. Double the speed, and the air pushes back about four times as hard. So engineers shape the nose, smooth the sides and hide gaps between carriages. Japan's fastest Shinkansen trains now run at about 320 km/h, covering a [distance](speed-distance) the length of a cricket pitch in about a quarter of a second.

## Japan's 1964 dream

In 1964, [Tokyo](tokyo) was getting ready to host the [Olympic Games](olympics). Just nine days before they began, on 1 October, Japan opened the Tokaido Shinkansen, a new railway linking Tokyo and Osaka. Its trains reached 210 km/h, faster than any passenger train in regular service had gone before. People called them "bullet trains" because of their rounded noses and speed.

The idea spread. France opened its TGV in 1981. China began building high-speed lines in the 2000s, and it now has the largest network in the world, more than 40,000 kilometres.

## The kingfisher's beak

In the early 1990s, Japanese trains had a noisy problem. When a train rushed into a tunnel, it squashed the air ahead, which burst out of the far end with a boom that could be heard hundreds of metres away. People living near the tunnels complained.

An engineer named Eiji Nakatsu was also a keen birdwatcher. He knew that [kingfishers](kingfishers) dive from air into water, which is much thicker, with hardly a splash. Their long, sharp beaks part the water smoothly. His team tested nose shapes and found that one like a kingfisher's beak cut the boom and saved energy. The 500 Series train, with its long pointed nose, entered service in 1997.

## Floating on magnets

A [maglev train](maglev) doesn't touch the track at all. Powerful [magnets](magnets-how) lift it a few centimetres into the air and push it forward, so there are no wheels to rub. In 2015, Japan's test maglev reached 603 km/h. Shanghai has run a maglev line to its airport since 2004.

Maglevs are very expensive to build, though, because they need completely new tracks. So most of the world's high-speed trains still use steel wheels on rails, just better rails, better wheels and better shapes than ordinary trains.

## Coming to India

India is building its first high-speed line, between Mumbai and Ahmedabad. It will be about 500 kilometres long and is being built with Japanese help, using Shinkansen technology. Part of it will run through a tunnel under the sea near Thane Creek. When finished, trains are expected to cover the distance in around two to three hours, compared with about five to seven hours on regular trains now.

Bullet trains also have to be extremely safe. In Japan, [earthquake](earthquakes) sensors along the line can cut the power and brake trains within seconds of the first tremor. That's part of why, in more than 60 years, no passenger has died in a Shinkansen crash or derailment.
