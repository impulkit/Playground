## The short answer

A fan makes you feel cooler by moving air across your skin. It doesn't make the air colder. It helps your body lose heat in two ways.

First, your body is usually warmer than the air around it, so it warms a thin layer of air right next to your skin. On a still day that warm layer just sits there, like an invisible blanket. A fan blows it away and replaces it with cooler air.

Second, your skin is often a little damp with [sweat](sweat). When sweat evaporates, turning from liquid into gas, it carries heat away from your body. Moving air speeds up evaporation, so you cool down faster. A breeze of just a few kilometres an hour can make a warm room feel two or three degrees cooler to your body, even though the thermometer doesn't move.

## A machine that cools people, not rooms

Because a fan works on skin, it can't cool an empty room. A thermometer in front of a fan shows no drop at all, since thermometers don't sweat. The fan's electric motor even gives off a small amount of heat as it runs.

So switching off the fan when you leave a room saves electricity and costs you nothing. An [air conditioner](air-conditioning) is different: it really does pull heat out of a room and push it outdoors.

## The punkah-wallahs

Long before electricity, people in hot countries used hand fans made of palm leaves, feathers, paper or silk. In India, big houses, offices and courtrooms had a punkah, a wide cloth or wooden frame hanging from the ceiling. A worker called a punkah-wallah pulled a rope to swing it back and forth, sometimes for hours, often sitting outside the room.

Punkahs were common in the time of the [East India Company](east-india-company) and British rule, especially in the hot plains. It was tiring, poorly paid work. Electric fans slowly replaced it from the early 1900s.

## The fan from America

In 1882, Philip Diehl, an engineer in the USA, took a small electric motor he had designed for [sewing machines](sewing-machines) and mounted it on the ceiling with blades. It is usually counted as the first electric ceiling fan. Others soon added lights and better blades. Table fans and pedestal fans followed, and in India the ceiling fan became so common that many homes have one in every room.

Inside every electric fan, a motor uses [magnets](magnets-how) and coils of wire to turn electricity into spinning. Modern fans called BLDC fans use cleverer motors and electronics, and use roughly half the electricity of older ones. Since 2023, every ceiling fan sold in India has needed an energy star label.

## When a fan stops helping

Fans work best when the air is dry, because sweat evaporates easily. In the dry heat of a Delhi May afternoon, a fan can make a real difference. In very humid weather, like Mumbai in the monsoon, sweat just drips off instead of evaporating, and the fan feels less helpful.

In extreme heat, fans have another limit. If the air is hotter than your skin, about 35 °C, a fan blows hot air onto you and can add heat. In severe [heatwaves](heatwaves), health bodies such as the World Health Organization advise drinking water and staying in the shade. Cool, wet cloths and a cooler place help too. A fan helps most when it's paired with sense.
