## The short answer

A nuclear power plant boils water, just like a coal power plant. The difference is how it makes the heat. Instead of burning fuel, it splits [atoms](?atom) of uranium.

The centre of a uranium-235 atom, its nucleus, is big and a little unstable. When a tiny particle called a neutron hits it, the nucleus can split into two smaller pieces. This is called fission. It releases a burst of heat and two or three more neutrons. Those neutrons can split more uranium atoms, which release more neutrons, and so on. That's a chain reaction.

In a reactor, the heat from this chain reaction warms water to make steam. The steam spins a turbine, and the turbine spins a generator that makes electricity.

## Under the stadium

The first nuclear reactor was built at the University of [Chicago](chicago), in a squash court beneath the stands of a sports field. A team led by the Italian-born physicist Enrico Fermi stacked tens of thousands of graphite blocks into a rough pile, with lumps of uranium tucked inside.

On 2 December 1942, they slowly pulled out cadmium rods that soaked up neutrons. The chain reaction started and kept itself going for about four minutes before they shut it down. This happened during the [Second World War](world-war-two), and the same science was soon used to build atomic bombs. After the war, many countries turned to using it for electricity.

## Keeping it under control

A power reactor must never let its chain reaction run away. Control rods, made of materials that soak up neutrons, slide in and out of the fuel. Push them in and the reaction slows. Pull them out and it speeds up. Huge flows of water carry heat away and keep the core cool.

The fuel itself comes as small ceramic pellets, each about the size of a fingertip, packed into long metal tubes. A single pellet can release roughly as much energy as a tonne of coal. Thick steel and concrete walls surround the reactor to hold in radiation.

## Nuclear power in India and the USA

[Homi Bhabha](homi-bhabha) founded India's atomic energy programme in the 1940s and 1950s. India's first nuclear power station opened at Tarapur, on the coast north of Mumbai, in 1969. Today India runs over 20 reactors, and plans more, but they make only about 3% of its electricity.

The USA has more nuclear reactors than any other country, over 90 of them. Together they make close to a fifth of American electricity.

## Benefits and worries

Nuclear plants run day and night in any weather and give off almost no carbon dioxide, the main gas warming our planet. That's why some people see them as part of the answer to [climate change](climate-change).

They also have serious risks. Accidents at Chernobyl in the Soviet Union in 1986 and at Fukushima in Japan in 2011 forced many thousands of people to leave their homes. Used fuel stays dangerously radioactive for thousands of years, and countries are still working out where to store it safely. Its slow fading is described by its [half-life](half-life). New plants are also very expensive and slow to build.

Scientists are also trying to master [fusion](future-energy), joining small atoms together the way the [Sun](sun-what) does. If it works, it could one day give power with far less long-lived waste.
