## The short answer

Plastics are made of [polymers](?polymer). A polymer is a giant molecule made of small units, called monomers, joined end to end in a long chain, like beads on a necklace. A single chain can contain thousands of units.

Most plastics start with oil or natural gas. At chemical plants, these are broken into small molecules. One of the most common is ethylene, a gas. Under the right heat, pressure and help from other chemicals, ethylene molecules link up into long chains called polyethylene, or polythene. That's the plastic in carry bags, milk pouches and many bottles.

By choosing different starting molecules and adding colours or softeners, chemists can make plastics that are clear or coloured, soft or hard, stretchy or stiff. That flexibility is why plastic is used for almost everything.

## Chains of molecules

Picture a bowl of cooked noodles. The noodles slide past each other, but they also tangle. Polymer chains behave a bit like that, which is why many plastics can bend and stretch without breaking.

In some plastics, the chains are only tangled, not tied together. Heat them and they soften and can be moulded again. These are called thermoplastics, and they include the plastics in most bottles and boxes. In others, the chains are linked to each other by strong cross-bonds. Once set, they won't melt. These are used for things like [electrical switches](fuses-switches) and pan handles.

## Nature makes polymers too

Polymers aren't only made in factories. [Silk](silk) is a polymer spun by silkworms. [Cotton](clothes-cotton) and wood are made of cellulose, a polymer of sugar. Natural rubber comes from the sap of rubber trees, which grow in Kerala. Even [DNA](dna), the instructions in your cells, is a polymer.

The first plastics borrowed from nature. In 1862, the British inventor Alexander Parkes showed Parkesine, made from plant cellulose. In 1907, the Belgian-American chemist Leo Baekeland made Bakelite from chemicals alone. It was hard, heat-resistant and didn't conduct electricity. It replaced shellac, a resin produced by lac insects that was harvested in India and used for electrical insulation and gramophone records.

## A plastic world

Plastic production took off after [World War Two](world-war-two). Nylon stockings, plastic toys, polythene bags and soft drink bottles became normal within a few decades. Today the world makes more than 400 million tonnes of plastic a year, close to the combined weight of every person on Earth.

Plastic is light, cheap, [waterproof](raincoats-waterproof) and strong. It keeps medicines clean, food fresh and electrical wires safe. Car makers use it to make vehicles lighter, so they use less fuel. Hospitals rely on it for syringes, drips and gloves that are used once and thrown away, which helps stop germs spreading from one patient to another.

## The trouble with plastic

The same toughness causes problems. Most plastics don't rot. Instead, sunlight and waves break them into smaller and smaller pieces called microplastics, which have been found in the deepest oceans, on mountain tops and in the bodies of animals. Much of the plastic that escapes into rivers ends up as [ocean plastic](ocean-plastic).

Only a small share of plastic has ever been [recycled](recycling). Scientists are working on plastics made from plants, ones that break down more easily, and better ways to recycle them. In 2022, India banned a list of single-use plastic items, including plastic straws, plastic cutlery and thin carry bags.
