## The short answer

Most electricity is made by spinning a magnet inside coils of wire, in a machine called a generator. As the magnet turns, it pushes [electrons](?electron) along the wire, and that flow is an electric current.

Something has to do the spinning. In most power stations, it's steam. Burning coal or gas, or splitting atoms in a nuclear plant, boils water into high-pressure steam, which blows through a turbine, a giant fan on a shaft. In [dams](dams-power), falling water turns the turbine. In [wind farms](wind-turbines), the wind does.

India makes electricity from coal, water, wind, the Sun and atoms. Tamil Nadu and Gujarat have huge wind farms.

Electricity is moving electrons, tiny particles inside [atoms](?atom).

## Faraday's discovery

In 1831, the English scientist Michael Faraday moved a [magnet](magnets-how) in and out of a coil of wire and saw a needle twitch. Electricity was flowing, but only while the magnet moved. When it stopped, the current stopped.

That simple discovery runs almost every power station on Earth. A generator is just Faraday's experiment made enormous, with tonnes of magnets and wire spinning many times a second.

## Lighting up cities

In 1882, Thomas Edison opened the Pearl Street Station in New York, one of the world's first public power stations. At first it supplied a few dozen customers in lower Manhattan, lighting a few hundred lamps. Soon there was a fierce argument over the best kind of current, which you can read about in [Tesla and Edison](tesla-edison).

India wasn't far behind. In 1897, a small hydroelectric station at Sidrapong, near Darjeeling, began making power from mountain streams. It was one of the first in Asia.

## Solar is different

[Solar panels](solar-panels) don't spin anything. They're made of thin layers of silicon. When sunlight hits them, it knocks electrons loose, and wires collect them as current. The energy really comes from the [Sun](sun-what).

India has built some of [the world's largest solar parks](solar-india), including the Bhadla Solar Park in the Rajasthan desert. Coal still makes the biggest share of India's electricity, but solar and wind are growing fast.

## The journey to your switch

Power stations are often far from cities. To send electricity a long way without wasting too much, machines called transformers raise it to very high voltages, hundreds of thousands of volts. Tall steel pylons carry it across the country. Near your home, more transformers lower it again.

Indian homes get 230 volts, and American homes get 120 volts. That's why some gadgets bought in one country need an adapter in the other. The plugs are shaped differently too, so [a laptop charger](chargers) from New York won't fit a socket in Pune without a converter.

## Nature's spark

[Lightning](lightning) is electricity too. During a storm, bits of ice and water in clouds bump together and build up huge electric charges. One bolt carries enough energy to run a home for days, but it's gone in a fraction of a second, far too fast and wild to catch and store.

## Saving it for later

[The grid](what-if-no-electricity) has a problem: electricity has to be used the moment it's made. Solar panels make nothing at night, and wind can drop. So engineers are building giant [batteries](batteries), and some places pump water uphill when power is cheap and let it flow down through turbines when it's needed. Keeping the lights on means matching supply and demand every second of the day.
