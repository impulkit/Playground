## The short answer

A loudspeaker turns an electrical signal back into sound. Inside is a cone, often made of paper or plastic, with a coil of wire glued to its back. The coil sits in the gap of a strong magnet.

When the music signal flows through the coil, the coil becomes a magnet too, and it is pushed and pulled by the fixed magnet. The coil jerks back and forth, and the cone moves with it, pushing on the air in front. Those pushes travel out as [sound waves](sound-travel) and reach your ears. A low note makes the cone move slowly. A high note makes it shiver very fast.

## Electricity becomes movement

Wire carrying electricity acts like a weak magnet. Wind the wire into a coil and the effect gets stronger. Put that coil near a permanent [magnet](magnets-how), and the two push or pull each other depending on which way the current flows.

A music signal is a current that keeps changing direction, many times every second, in exactly the pattern of the original sound. So the coil keeps being shoved forward and dragged back in the same pattern. A soft rubbery ring around the cone, called the surround, lets it move freely and pulls it back to the middle.

## Woofers and tweeters

People can hear sounds from about 20 [vibrations](?frequency) a second, a deep rumble, up to about 20,000, a thin whistle. One cone struggles to do all of that well. Low notes need a big cone that moves a lot of air. High notes need something tiny and light that can flick back and forth very quickly.

So many speaker boxes contain two or three drivers. A big "woofer" handles bass, and a small "tweeter" handles high notes, with an electronic filter sending each one the right part of the music. Home theatre systems often add a separate "subwoofer" for the deepest booms you feel in your chest.

## How it began

Early gramophones made sound without electricity. A needle shook a small diaphragm, and a big flared horn helped the sound spread. In 1925, two engineers at the General Electric company in the USA, Chester Rice and Edward Kellogg, developed the moving-coil loudspeaker. Their basic design, a coil, a magnet and a cone, is still used in most speakers a century later.

Speakers soon filled radios, cinemas and stadiums. Public address systems let one voice reach a whole crowd, whether at a political rally, a cricket ground or a railway platform announcing your train.

## Tiny speakers, clever tricks

Phones and earbuds have speakers smaller than a fingernail. To sound good, they rely on tricks. Earbuds sit right next to your eardrum, so a tiny movement is enough. Phone makers use software to boost certain notes and fool your brain into hearing deeper bass than the speaker really makes.

[Noise-cancelling](noise-cancelling) headphones go further. A microphone listens to the hum around you, and the speaker plays the exact opposite wave. When the two meet, they cancel each other out, and the noise seems to fade.

## The microphone's twin

A [microphone](microphones) and a speaker are mirror images. In a speaker, current moves the coil and the coil moves air. In a dynamic microphone, air moves the coil and the coil makes current. Some intercoms and walkie-talkies use the same part for both jobs.

## Too loud

Speakers can be very loud, and very loud sound can damage your hearing over time. Loudness is measured in [decibels](?decibel). In India, the Noise Pollution Rules of 2000 limit loudspeakers in public places at night, between 10 pm and 6 am, with a few exceptions. A good rule for earbuds is that if someone next to you can hear your music, it is probably too loud for your [ears](ears-hear).
