## The short answer

Your body is made of about 30 trillion [cells](?cell), and lives with even more tiny bacteria, mostly in your gut. A 2016 estimate put the bacteria at about 38 trillion, a little more than your own cells.

Different cells do different jobs: nerve cells carry messages, red blood cells carry oxygen. [Muscle](muscles) cells pull, skin cells protect, and fat cells store energy. There are a few hundred different kinds.

Cells are built from [atoms](?atom), many of which were made in stars long ago.

## A tiny living factory

Each cell is a little bag of watery jelly wrapped in a thin skin called a membrane. Inside, thousands of tiny machines are at work. Some turn food into energy. Some build proteins, the materials your body is made from. Others carry things around or break down waste.

In most cells, a central control room called the nucleus holds your [DNA](dna). That's the instruction book that tells the cell what to build and when. Every one of your cells started from the same instructions, copied from one single cell: the fertilised egg you grew from.

So why is a skin cell different from a brain cell if they carry the same book? Each kind of cell reads only some pages and keeps the rest closed. A muscle cell reads the pages for pulling, and a young red blood cell reads the pages for making the protein that carries oxygen.

## Most of you is red

By count, the most common cells in your body are [red blood cells](blood). A 2016 study by scientists in Israel worked out that they make up about 84% of all your cells. They're so small that a single drop of blood holds millions of them.

Other cells are far bigger. A human egg cell is just large enough to see without a microscope. Some nerve cells are the longest, stretching from the bottom of your spine to your toes, about a metre in a tall adult.

## The first look

Nobody knew cells existed until there were [microscopes](microscopes). In 1665, the English scientist Robert Hooke looked at a thin slice of cork and saw rows of tiny boxes. They reminded him of the small rooms monks lived in, called cells, so that's what he named them.

A few years later, the Dutch shopkeeper Antonie van Leeuwenhoek built tiny but powerful lenses of his own. He was among the first to see living bacteria and blood cells, which he described as "little animals" in his letters.

## Always being replaced

Your body replaces huge numbers of cells every day. Cells lining your gut last only a few days before they're worn out. Skin cells flake away and are replaced from below. Red blood cells last about four months.

Some cells hardly change at all. Many nerve cells in your brain and the cells in the lenses of your eyes stay with you for life. So the idea that you become a completely new person every seven years isn't true.

## From cells to you

Cells team up. A group of similar cells forms a tissue, such as muscle. Tissues join to make organs, like your [heart](heart-beat). Organs work together in systems, like the heart and blood vessels moving blood around.

All of it is built from the same few kinds of atoms: mostly oxygen, carbon, hydrogen and nitrogen. Apart from hydrogen, nearly all of those atoms were made inside ancient [stars](stars-born), which scattered them into space when they died, long before the Sun was born. Every time you breathe, you're using star leftovers.
