## The short answer

Sunlight looks white, but it is a mix of all the colours of the [rainbow](rainbow-how). As it passes through the air, the tiny gas molecules bump blue light off in every direction much more than red light. This is called *scattering*.

At noon, the Sun is high and its light takes a short path through the air. Plenty of blue gets scattered across the sky, which is why the [daytime sky is blue](sky-blue). At sunset, the Sun is low and its light has to travel sideways through dozens of times more air. By the time it reaches you, most of the blue and green have been scattered away. What's left is orange and red.

## A long road through the air

The layer of air around Earth is thin, a bit like the skin on an apple. Straight up, sunlight crosses it quickly. Near the horizon, the light skims through the layer at a low angle, the way a cricket ball skids a long way along the ground after a low throw.

Along that long road, blue light is scattered out of the beam again and again. Red light, with its longer waves, mostly keeps going. The Sun itself turns orange, and any clouds overhead are lit from below in pink, gold and red. Lord Rayleigh, a British scientist, worked out the maths of this scattering in 1871.

## Volcanoes paint the sky

Tiny particles in the air can make sunsets even stronger. In 1883, the [volcano](volcano) Krakatoa in Indonesia exploded. It threw fine dust and gas so high that the winds spread it around the planet. For months, people as far away as Europe and the United States saw blazing red skies. In some places fire brigades rushed out, thinking a distant town was burning.

Some art historians think the fiery sky in Edvard Munch's painting *The Scream* was inspired by those sunsets. Others think it simply shows the painter's feelings. Nobody can be sure.

## Blue sunsets on Mars

On [Mars](mars-sky), everything is flipped. In 2015, NASA's [Curiosity rover](tech-robots-space) photographed the Sun setting over a crater. The sky near the Sun glowed a cool blue. Mars has very thin air but lots of fine dust, and that dust happens to scatter blue light forwards, in the direction the sunlight is already moving.

## Haze, smoke and sea air

Dust and smoke near the ground often make sunsets look dull and muddy rather than bright. In winter, Delhi's hazy evenings often show a pale orange Sun without much colour around it. Clearer air with some high clouds tends to give the best displays, like the famous evenings over the sea at [Kanyakumari](kanyakumari) or the red cliffs of the [Grand Canyon](grand-canyon) in [Arizona](grand-canyon-state).

An old English rhyme says "Red sky at night, shepherd's delight". Over much of Europe and the USA, weather usually travels from west to east. There, a glowing sunset can mean clear, dry air lies to the west and is heading your way. It's a rule of thumb, though, not a forecast.

## Squashed and late

Near the horizon, the air bends sunlight a little, the same way water bends a straw in a glass. This bending lifts the image of the [Sun](sun-what) slightly, so you can still see it for a couple of minutes after it has dipped below the horizon. It also squashes the Sun into a flattened oval. The Sun you watch setting is, for a few moments, already gone.
