## The short answer

A stepwell is a deep well with flights of steps leading down to the water. In the dry parts of western India, the water underground rises after the [monsoon](monsoon) and sinks during the long, hot months that follow. A normal well with a rope and bucket works only if the rope is long enough. With steps, people could simply walk down to wherever the water was.

Stepwells were dug into the ground until they reached [groundwater](groundwater), the water stored in sand and rock below. Water seeped in from the sides and collected in a pool at the bottom. Many stepwells also caught rain flowing off the land around them. Over time, builders turned them into works of art, with carved pillars, galleries and statues of gods.

## Digging down to the water

Builders began by digging a long trench. At one end, a round well shaft went straight down to the water. Along the trench, they built stone steps, landings and pillared floors, one below another, like an upside-down building.

The deepest stepwells go down the height of a ten-storey building. Thick stone walls held back the soil. Pillars and beams kept the walls from caving in. At the bottom, the air was shady and noticeably cooler than at the surface, so people rested there on summer afternoons.

## More than a water tap

Stepwells were meeting places. Women came to fill pots and to talk. Travellers on trade routes stopped to drink and rest. Many stepwells had small shrines, and people prayed there, because water was sacred as well as precious.

That is why so many are beautifully carved. Builders used the same skills they used in [temple architecture](architecture-temples): elephants, dancers, flowers and gods were cut into the stone walls. Going down the steps could feel like walking into a hidden temple.

## Three famous stepwells

Chand Baori, in the village of Abhaneri in [Rajasthan](rajasthan-forts), is about 1,200 years old. Its roughly 3,500 steps form a dizzying pattern of zigzags on three sides, dropping about 13 levels to the water.

Rani ki Vav, at Patan in Gujarat, was built in the 11th century in memory of King Bhima I, and is linked to his queen, Udayamati. It is about 64 metres long and lined with hundreds of carved sculptures. Floods from a nearby river later filled it with silt, which protected the carvings. After archaeologists cleared it, it became a [UNESCO World Heritage Site](unesco-sites) in 2014.

The Adalaj stepwell near Ahmedabad, finished in 1499, has five storeys of carved balconies. Local stories say it was completed by Queen Rudabai. Its carvings mix Hindu and Jain designs with Islamic patterns, a reminder that it was begun under one ruler and finished under another.

Delhi has stepwells too. Agrasen ki Baoli, a tall, narrow stepwell with about 100 steps, stands among office towers near Connaught Place. Its exact age is unknown. On a hot afternoon, its lower steps are still a few degrees cooler than the street outside.

## Forgotten, then found again

During British rule, officials worried that stepwells spread disease, because people bathed and drew drinking water from the same pool. Some were closed or filled in. Later, piped water, hand pumps and [borewells](wells-borewells) meant few people needed the steps any more. Many stepwells filled with rubbish or were left to crumble.

In recent years, people have started to look at them again. Some cities have cleaned out old stepwells so they can catch rain and recharge the ground. Visitors now travel to see them, and photographers love their patterns of light and shadow. The lesson they teach is the same one behind modern rainwater harvesting: hold on to the monsoon for as long as you can.
