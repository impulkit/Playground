## The short answer

A wind turbine is a windmill that makes electricity. Wind pushes on its long blades and makes them spin. The blades turn a shaft inside the box at the top, called the nacelle. Usually a gearbox speeds up the slow turning of the blades, which might spin only 10 to 20 times a minute, into the fast spin a generator needs.

The [generator](?generator) is where movement becomes electricity. Inside it, [magnets](magnets-how) spin past coils of copper wire, which makes electrons flow in the wire. Thick cables carry the electricity down the tower and off to homes, schools and factories. A large modern turbine can make enough electricity for a few thousand Indian homes.

## Blades shaped like wings

Turbine blades aren't flat paddles. Each one is curved like the wing of an [aeroplane](airplanes-fly). As wind flows over it, the curved shape creates a force called lift, which pulls the blade sideways and keeps the rotor turning. The blades are twisted along their length, because the tip moves much faster than the part near the hub.

Big land turbines often have blades 50 to 80 metres long. The tip of such a blade can race through the air at more than 250 km/h, faster than most express trains, even though the rotor seems to turn lazily. A small motor swivels the whole top so the blades always face into the wind.

## From grinding grain to making power

People have used wind for thousands of years, to fill sails and to turn millstones that ground grain. Windmills with sails turning around an upright post were built in Persia, in present-day Iran, more than a thousand years ago, and some old ones still stand near the town of Nashtifan.

The first windmills to make electricity came in the 1880s. In the winter of 1887–88, an inventor named Charles Brush built a giant windmill in his back garden in Cleveland, Ohio. Its wooden rotor was 17 metres across, and it charged batteries in his basement that powered his house lights for about 20 years.

## Where the wind blows

Turbines need steady, strong wind, so they are built on open plains, hilltops, coasts and out at sea. In India, the [windiest](extreme-windiest) areas include the gaps in the Western Ghats in Tamil Nadu, and the coasts of Gujarat. Near Muppandal in Tamil Nadu, hundreds of turbines line the fields, catching wind that funnels through a pass in the hills. India is now among the top five countries in the world for wind power.

In the USA, [Texas](texas) makes more electricity from wind than any other state. Offshore turbines, standing in the sea, can be even bigger, because ships can carry huge parts that would never fit on a road. Winds at sea are also steadier, with no hills or buildings to slow them down.

## Not perfect

Wind turbines make no smoke or [greenhouse gases](climate-change) while running, but they have downsides. The wind doesn't always blow, so turbines must work alongside other power sources and storage. Some people dislike the look and the swishing noise. Turbines can also kill birds and [bats](bats-fly) that fly into the blades. Builders try to keep them away from [migration](migration) routes, and some wind farms pause their turbines when large flocks pass. In Rajasthan, wind farms and their power lines are part of the danger facing the rare great Indian bustard.
