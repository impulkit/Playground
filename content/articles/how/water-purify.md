## The short answer

Most cities take water from rivers, lakes or underground. [Before it reaches your tap](tap-water-pressure), it passes through a treatment plant in several steps.

First, screens catch big things like sticks, leaves and plastic. Next, a chemical such as alum is stirred in. It makes tiny bits of mud stick together into larger clumps, which sink to the bottom of big settling tanks. The water then trickles through deep beds of sand and gravel, which trap smaller particles. Finally, it is disinfected, usually with chlorine, to kill any germs that remain. A little chlorine stays in the water to keep it safe all the way through the pipes to your home.

Each step removes something the last one missed. Along the way, workers test samples many times a day, checking for germs, cloudiness and the right amount of chlorine. If something looks wrong, they can stop the water before it leaves the plant.

## Germs you can't see

Dirty water can carry tiny living things, such as bacteria, viruses and parasites. Some cause diseases like cholera, typhoid and diarrhoea, which can be very dangerous, especially for young children. The World Health Organization estimates that about 2 billion people around the world still lack safely managed [drinking water](water-drink) at home.

Not all [microbes](microbes-friendly) are harmful. Your gut is full of helpful ones. But the ones in sewage-polluted water can make people seriously ill. That's why water treatment has saved more lives than almost any other public health measure in history.

## The doctor and the pump

In 1854, cholera swept through Soho, a crowded part of [London](london). Many people believed diseases spread through bad-smelling air. A doctor named John Snow suspected the water. He marked every death on a map and found that most were clustered around one public water pump on Broad Street.

Snow convinced local officials to remove the pump's handle so nobody could use it. New cases fell away, though the outbreak was already slowing. Later it was found that a leaking cesspit sat right next to the well. Snow's map became one of the most famous in science, and it helped show that dirty water spreads disease.

## Chlorine and other cleaners

In 1908, Jersey City in New Jersey became the first US city to add chlorine to its drinking water continuously. Deaths from typhoid in American cities fell sharply over the following decades as others did the same. Chlorine is still the most common disinfectant in the world.

Some plants use ozone gas or strong ultraviolet light instead, which kill germs without leaving a taste. Ultraviolet light from the Sun works too. In some villages, people leave clear plastic bottles of water in strong sunlight for several hours to kill germs, a method recommended by health groups.

## At home in India

Many Indian homes treat their water again, even after it arrives from the city supply. [Water purifiers](water-tech) with filters, ultraviolet lamps or reverse osmosis are common. Reverse osmosis pushes water through a very fine sheet that lets water through but stops most dissolved salts. It also wastes a lot of water, so it is best used where water really is salty or hard.

[Boiling](boiling-water) is the oldest method of all. A minute at a rolling boil kills most germs. It doesn't remove chemicals or mud, but in an emergency it is one of the simplest ways to make water safer.
