## The short answer

An Indian fighting kite, or *patang*, is made from a square of very thin paper stretched over two strips of split bamboo. One strip, the *dhaddha*, runs straight down the middle. The other, the *kamaan*, is bent into a bow across the top. Paper is glued around them, and a small triangle of paper is often added at the bottom.

What makes it a fighting kite is the string. Flyers use *manja*, cotton thread coated with a paste of glue and powdered glass. When two kites cross in the sky, their strings rub, and the sharper or more cleverly handled one cuts the other free. The cut kite drifts away, and children on rooftops and in lanes run to catch it.

## A kite with no tail

Most children's kites have tails to keep them steady. A patang doesn't. That makes it unstable: it twists and dips with every gust. A beginner finds it frustrating. An expert loves it.

By tugging and loosening the string, a skilled flyer can make the patang turn, dive and climb in a second. That control is what wins a fight. The light bamboo frame flexes just enough, and the [bamboo](bamboo) itself is strong for its weight. Bamboo is a grass, and some kinds can grow nearly a metre in a single day.

## Making manja

Traditional manja is made by hand. Cotton thread is stretched between poles, sometimes hundreds of metres long, and coated with a paste. Recipes vary, and families guard their secrets. The paste can include rice or flour glue, colours and finely crushed glass. When it dries, the thread is rough like sandpaper.

Cities such as Bareilly in Uttar Pradesh are famous for manja. When two strings cross, the cut comes from rubbing, a kind of [friction](friction). Some flyers pull in fast. Others let out lots of string. Each style has its fans.

## Kai po che

The biggest kite day in India is Makar Sankranti, called Uttarayan in Gujarat, around 14 January. In [Ahmedabad](ahmedabad), families spend the day on their terraces with food, music and stacks of patangs. When someone cuts a rival's kite, they shout "Kai po che!", meaning roughly "I've cut it!" Gujarat has hosted an International Kite Festival since 1989, with flyers from dozens of countries.

Kites also fill skies on Independence Day in Delhi, on Basant Panchami in Punjab and during Sankranti in Hyderabad and Jaipur. Each region has its own words and its own favourite kite shapes.

## The dark side of the string

Manja is sharp enough to cut skin. Every kite season, hospitals treat people with cuts, and there have been serious accidents when loose strings stretched across roads where people rode two-wheelers. Thousands of birds, including pigeons, kites and even [vultures](vultures-india), get tangled every year.

In 2017, India's National Green Tribunal banned nylon and other synthetic manja, often called Chinese manja, because it doesn't break and can't rot. Bird hospitals in Ahmedabad run special rescue camps every Uttarayan. Many families now fly with plain cotton thread instead.

## A very old toy

Kites probably began in China more than 2,000 years ago, and travelled along trade routes to India. Mughal paintings show people flying kites, and kite fighting was a favourite sport in the courts of Lucknow. Today, plastic kites are cheaper, but paper and bamboo patangs are still made by hand in small workshops. Each one is light enough to rest on a fingertip, and each one, given a good breeze, can climb hundreds of metres. How it stays up there at all is the story of [lift and air pressure](kite-flying).
