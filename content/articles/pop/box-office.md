## The short answer

The box office is all the money people pay for cinema tickets. When news says a film "made 1,000 crore", it usually means tickets worth that much were sold.

But that money is shared. The government takes tax. The cinema keeps a big slice. What is left goes to the distributor and the producer, who must first earn back what the film cost before they make any profit.

## How it works

Think of a mango seller at a village mela. The seller sells a mango for 100 rupees. Part goes to the panchayat as a fee. Part goes to the owner of the ground who rents out the stall. Only the rest stays with the seller and the farmer who grew the mango. A film ticket works in the same way.

Here is what happens to a 300 rupee ticket:

1. **Tax comes out first.** Cinema tickets carry [GST](gst). The government sets the [percentage](?percentage) and can change it. For example, under the rates set in 2025, tickets above 100 rupees carried 18% and cheaper tickets 5%. At 18%, about 46 rupees of the 300 goes to the government.
2. **Gross and net.** The full 300 rupees is called the gross. The 254 rupees left after tax is called the net. News reports use both, so always check which one you are reading.
3. **The cinema takes its share.** The cinema, called the exhibitor, keeps a share of the net, often around half. The film's share is usually biggest in its first week and smaller in later weeks.
4. **The film's side.** The rest, about 125 rupees, goes to the distributor, the company that delivers the film to cinemas, and then to the producer, who paid for the film.

Box office is only one way films earn. Makers also sell the rights to show the film on streaming apps and TV channels, sell the songs, and sell dubbed versions. Cinemas earn extra from [popcorn](popcorn) and drinks, which they keep for themselves.

## Where it came from

The phrase "box office" comes from theatres. A small booth, or office, sold tickets for the boxes, the private seats, and later for every seat. English speakers were using the phrase by the late 1700s.

Films came later. In 1895, the Lumière brothers showed the [first paid film show](hist-first-film) in Paris. In 1913, [Dadasaheb Phalke](dadasaheb-phalke) released Raja Harishchandra, India's first feature film.

For much of the 20th century, success was counted in weeks. A film that ran 25 weeks in a cinema celebrated a silver jubilee, and 50 weeks a golden jubilee. Sholay, released in 1975, ran for five years at Mumbai's Minerva cinema, one of the [classic films](bollywood-classics) everyone still quotes.

In 1997, India's first multiplex, PVR Anupam, opened in Saket, Delhi, with several screens under one roof. Big films could now open on thousands of screens at once. That changed everything: a film's money now arrives in a rush in its first few days, so opening weekend numbers became the new way to keep score.

## India's story

India makes more than 1,000 films a year in many languages, more than almost any country. [Bollywood](bollywood) in Mumbai is the best known, but Telugu, Tamil, Kannada and Malayalam films now earn huge sums too. That is why [South Indian films have grown so big](cinema-south). Many films are now [dubbed into other languages](dubbing-subtitles) so one film can sell tickets across India and abroad. Tickets sold abroad are paid in other [currencies](?currency), such as dollars, and counted in rupees for the news.

India's biggest hits can earn more than 1,000 crore rupees around the world. A milestone came with Dangal, released in 2016. By trade estimates it crossed about 2,000 crore rupees, much of it from China, where it became a surprise hit in 2017. It showed that an Indian film could earn hugely in countries far from home.

Big films cost big money. Making one means paying actors, building sets, adding [special effects](special-effects) and advertising across the country. So a film can sell a mountain of tickets and still lose money, if it cost even more to make. Trade watchers usually call a film a hit only when the makers' share, not the gross, is bigger than what the film cost. That is why a "500 crore" film can be a flop and a "50 crore" film a hit.

## The numbers that matter

- **About 15 rupees in every 100**: roughly how much of a ticket's price is tax when the GST rate is 18%. The tax is hidden inside the price, so the gap between gross and net is mostly GST.
- **About half**: the share of the net that a cinema usually keeps in a film's first week. In later weeks the cinema's share usually grows, often to 60% or more. So most of the makers' money arrives in the first few days.
- **Fewer than 10 screens for every 10 lakh people**: about how many India has. The USA has more than 100 for the same number of people. That is why many Indian towns have no cinema at all, and why big films are often sold out on opening weekend.
- **About a third**: how much of a big multiplex chain's income usually comes from food and drinks. The cinema keeps all of the popcorn money but only part of the ticket money, so snacks matter a lot to it.
- **More than 1,000 films a year**: about how many India makes, in many languages. Trade watchers say only a small share of them earn back what they cost, a busy but risky corner of India's [economy](?economy).

## What people argue about

**Are the numbers real?** India has no official body that counts every ticket. Figures come from trade trackers and from the films' own makers, and they often differ. Some people say makers boost their numbers to look successful. Others say the trackers are careful and that the big totals are broadly right.

**Are tickets too expensive?** Many families say a cinema trip, with snacks, costs too much. Cinema owners reply that rent, electricity and staff are costly, and that cheap seats are still sold in single-screen halls. Governments have sometimes lowered the tax on cheap tickets, hoping to help smaller cinemas and the families who use them.

**Cinema or phone?** Films now reach streaming apps a few weeks after release. Some say this keeps people at home. Others say a big film on a big screen is still an event no phone can replace.

## Myths and mix-ups

**"Gross and net are the same thing."** No. Gross includes tax. Net is after GST. A headline can make a film look bigger just by quoting the gross. Learn to spot the difference using [percentages](percentages).

**"Box office is the film's profit."** It is not even the film's income. After tax and the cinema's share, the makers get well under half, and then they must pay back the cost.

**"A hit in India is a hit everywhere."** Each place counts separately. Some Indian films earn more abroad than at home, and some flop overseas while doing well in India.

## What it means for you

Every time your family buys a cinema ticket, your money is split many ways: the government, the cinema, the distributor and the people who made the film. The popcorn you buy helps keep the cinema running.

Whenever you see a giant number in a headline, ask three questions. Is it gross or net? Is it India only, or worldwide? Who counted it? Big numbers in [lakh and crore](lakh-crore) are easy to quote and hard to check. Box office also follows [supply and demand](supply-demand): when everyone wants to see the same film on the same weekend, cinemas add shows and prices rise. And like a [company's market value](market-value), a film's box office tells you what people were willing to pay, not how much money anyone actually kept. To see what all that money pays for, read [how a film is made](behind-film-making).
