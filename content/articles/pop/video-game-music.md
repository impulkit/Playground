## The short answer

Video game music is written by composers, much like film music. The big difference is that a game doesn't know in advance what the player will do. You might stand still for ten minutes or race through a level in thirty seconds. So game music has to loop smoothly, change with the action and never become annoying, even after hours of play.

Early game composers worked with tiny computer chips that could make only a few beeps at once. Today they can use full orchestras, [choirs](choirs) and any instrument they like. The challenge is still the same: music that makes the game feel more exciting, scary or beautiful while you play.

## Beeps and bleeps

The first arcade games of the 1970s made little more than blips. *Pong* in 1972 had a simple beep when the ball hit a paddle. In 1978, *Space Invaders* added something new: a thumping loop of four low notes that speeds up as the aliens march closer. Players' hearts sped up with it.

[Home consoles](tech-game-consoles) in the 1980s had special sound chips. The Nintendo Entertainment System had just five channels. Two made sharp, buzzy tones, one made a softer tone often used for bass, one made hissing noise for drums, and one could play very short recorded sounds. Composers had to squeeze melody, harmony and rhythm into those five voices. This style is now called chiptune, and some musicians still make it on purpose.

## The tune everyone knows

Koji Kondo, a young composer at Nintendo, wrote the music for *Super Mario Bros.* in 1985. He has explained that he shaped the main theme around the feel of Mario running and jumping, so that the rhythm matched the action. The bouncy tune became one of the most recognised pieces of music in the world.

Kondo also had to think about repetition. A player might hear the same loop dozens of times. The music had to be catchy without being irritating, a balance game composers still worry about.

## Music that listens to you

Modern games use what is called adaptive music. Composers write pieces in layers: perhaps strings, then percussion, then brass. When you explore quietly, only the strings play. When an enemy appears, drums fade in. When the fight gets fierce, the brass joins.

Another method switches between sections that are built to join neatly together. Even in 1990, *Super Mario World* added a layer of bongo drums whenever Mario hopped onto his dinosaur friend Yoshi. Players might not notice these tricks, but they feel them. That is how music works on the [brain](music-brain): it can change your mood before you realise why.

## From chips to orchestras

As computers grew more powerful, games could play recorded sound instead of beeps. Composers began writing for real instruments. Many now record with a full [orchestra](orchestra), the same way film scores are made. Others build soundscapes with [electronic music](electronic-music) tools, or mix folk instruments from the world the game is set in.

Game music has also left the screen. Orchestras around the world play concerts of music from series like *Final Fantasy* and *The Legend of Zelda*. In 2011, "Baba Yetu", a song in Swahili written by Christopher Tin for *Civilization IV*, won a Grammy. Since 2023, [the Grammys](music-awards) have had a separate award for the best video game soundtrack.

## Sound effects matter too

Music is only half the story. Sound designers create every footstep, sword clang and coin jingle. Some record real objects: crunching celery for breaking bones, or a stretched metal spring for a laser. Others build sounds from scratch on computers.

The best sounds tell players what's going on without a word. A rising chime says "you did something good". A low thud says "watch out". In the history of [video games](video-game-history), a few tiny sounds, like Mario's coin, have become as famous as any song.
