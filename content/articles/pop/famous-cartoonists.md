## The short answer

R.K. Laxman was one of India's best-loved cartoonists. Every day for more than half a century, he drew a small cartoon called *You Said It* on the front page of *The Times of India*. In a few lines of ink and one sentence of text, he made fun of politicians, potholes, power cuts and endless promises.

His most famous character was the Common Man: a short, balding man with glasses, a checked coat and a dhoti. He stood quietly at the side of the scene while important people talked. He stood for ordinary Indians who put up with whatever their leaders did next.

## A boy who drew on the walls

Laxman grew up in Mysore, the youngest child in a large family that loved books. As a small boy he drew on walls, doors and floors: crows on a tree, his teachers, the neighbours. He later said that a British cartoonist in an old magazine, David Low, made him want to draw cartoons for a living.

When he applied to the J.J. School of Art in Bombay, he was rejected. He kept drawing anyway. He sent cartoons to newspapers, and drew pictures for the stories of his older brother, [R.K. Narayan](rk-narayan), including the tales of the town of Malgudi.

## The Common Man

Laxman joined *The Times of India* in Bombay in 1947. In 1951 he began *You Said It*. According to Laxman, the Common Man arrived slowly. At first he drew crowds, then fewer people, until one figure was left watching.

He never gave the Common Man a name, a job or a home. That was the point. Anyone could see themselves in him: the clerk waiting for a bus, the shopkeeper hit by a new tax, the family whose street had not been repaired in years. Because the cartoon was small and sat on the front page, it was often the first thing a reader saw each morning. In 1988, India Post put him on [a stamp](hist-first-stamp-india), and a bronze statue of him stands in Pune.

## Laughing at the powerful

A political cartoon works like a joke with a sting. It shows what a leader said next to what actually happened, and lets the reader notice the gap. In a [democracy](democracy-what), that kind of teasing helps people question those in charge.

Laxman's cartoons were sharp but rarely cruel. He drew prime ministers for decades, starting with Jawaharlal [Nehru](nehru). During [the Emergency](emergency-1975) of 1975 to 1977, when newspapers were censored, cartoonists had to be careful about what they printed.

He also created a cheerful mascot. In 1954 he drew Gattu, the paintbrush-holding boy used for years in advertisements for Asian Paints.

## Cartoonists around the world

India has other famous cartoonists. K. Shankar Pillai, known as Shankar, started *Shankar's Weekly* in 1948 and later founded a [dolls museum](pop-barbie-dolls) in Delhi. Mario Miranda filled his drawings of Goa with crowds of tiny, funny people. Pran created Chacha Chaudhary, whose brain is said to work faster than a computer, a favourite of [Indian comics](comics-india) readers.

In the USA, Charles Schulz drew the *Peanuts* comic strip, with Charlie Brown and his dog Snoopy, for about 50 years, from 1950 to 2000. Like Laxman, he drew nearly every strip himself. Both men showed that a few lines on paper, repeated every day, can become part of a country's daily life.
