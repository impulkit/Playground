## The short answer

The oldest board games we know of are about 4,500 to 5,000 years old. Senet, from ancient Egypt, and the Royal Game of Ur, from [Mesopotamia](mesopotamia), in present-day Iraq, are among the oldest whose boards survive. Both are race games, where players roll dice-like pieces and move counters along a track.

Other ancient games include Go, from China, and mancala games, where seeds or stones are moved between pits. India gave the world chaturanga, [the ancestor of chess](pop-chess-pieces), and pachisi, the ancestor of [Ludo](ludo).

## Senet, the game of passing

Senet boards have 30 squares in three rows. Players threw casting sticks, like flat dice, and moved pieces along the board. Pictures in tombs show Egyptians playing it more than 4,500 years ago.

Over time Senet took on religious meaning, as a symbol of the soul's journey after death. Several Senet boards were buried with Tutankhamun so he could play in the afterlife. We still do not know the exact rules of [ancient Egypt's](ancient-egypt) favourite game. Modern versions are educated guesses.

## The Royal Game of Ur

In the 1920s, the archaeologist Leonard Woolley dug up the royal tombs at Ur. He found beautiful game boards decorated with shell and red limestone, now in the British Museum. Each board had 20 squares, and players used pyramid-shaped dice.

For thousands of years nobody knew how to play. Then Irving Finkel, a curator at the British Museum, translated a clay tablet written by a Babylonian scholar in 177 BCE that explained the rules. The game had been played across the Middle East for about 3,000 years. Finkel later learned that Jewish families in [Kochi](kochi), in [Kerala](kerala), were still playing a version of it in the 20th century.

## Games of India

Archaeologists have found dice and what may be gaming pieces at [Indus Valley](indus-cities) sites such as Mohenjo-daro, about 4,500 years old. Dice games appear in the [Mahabharata](mahabharata), where Yudhishthira loses his kingdom in a game of dice.

Pachisi, played on a cross-shaped board, was a royal favourite. At Fatehpur Sikri, there is a large board marked out in the paving of a courtyard. Stories say [Akbar](akbar) played there with people as pieces. Chaturanga, a war game with [elephants](oddity-elephants-war), horses, [chariots](wheel-to-chariot) and foot soldiers, grew into [chess](chess-india). Gyan chaupar became [Snakes and Ladders](snakes-ladders).

## Go and mancala

Go, called weiqi in China and baduk in Korea, is played with black and white stones on a grid of 19 by 19 lines. It is at least 2,500 years old and its rules are simple, yet it is one of the hardest games in the world to master. In 2016 a computer program, AlphaGo, beat one of the world's best players.

Mancala games are played across Africa and Asia. In south India, a version called pallanguzhi uses a wooden board with 14 pits and cowrie shells or seeds.

## Why games last

Board games survive because they need little more than a board and some pieces, yet each game is different. Many mix luck and skill, so a beginner can win sometimes. Children and grandparents can play together.

Modern hits like Monopoly, first sold in 1935, are based on earlier games too. Monopoly grew from The Landlord's Game, patented in 1904 by Lizzie Magie. When you play Ludo on a phone today, you are playing a game that Indian royalty knew centuries ago.
