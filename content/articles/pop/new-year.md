## The short answer

Different places celebrate New Year on different days because they use different calendars. Most of the world uses the Gregorian calendar for everyday dates, so 1 January is a holiday almost everywhere. But many cultures also keep older calendars based on the Sun, the Moon or both, and each has its own New Year.

In India alone, people celebrate Ugadi, Gudi Padwa, Vaisakhi, Vishu, Puthandu, Pohela Boishakh, Navroz and more, at different times of year. Chinese communities celebrate Lunar New Year in January or February. Even 1 January itself arrives at different moments around the world, because of [time zones](time-zones).

## Why 1 January?

The Romans first named 1 January as the start of their year. In 46 BCE, Julius Caesar [reformed the calendar](calendar-maths), and from 45 BCE the year began on 1 January. The month was named after Janus, a Roman god with two faces, one looking back and one looking forward.

Later, much of Christian Europe moved the start of the year to other days, such as 25 March. England kept 25 March until 1752, when it [switched to the Gregorian calendar](oddity-calendar-missing-days) and to 1 January.

## New Years of the Moon

Some calendars follow the [Moon](moon-phases). A lunar month, from one new moon to the next, is about 29.5 days. Twelve of them make about 354 days, shorter than a solar year.

The Islamic year is purely lunar, so its New Year, like [Eid](eid), moves about 11 days earlier each year. The Chinese calendar is lunisolar: it follows the Moon but adds an extra month every few years to keep pace with the [seasons](seasons-why). That's why Lunar New Year always lands between late January and mid-February.

## India's many New Years

India has used many calendars, as [India's calendars](indian-calendars) explains. Some follow the Moon and the Sun together, and New Year falls on the new moon of Chaitra, in March or April. That is Ugadi in Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Karnataka, and Gudi Padwa in Maharashtra, where families hang a decorated pole with a silk cloth and an upturned pot outside their homes.

Other regions use a solar calendar, and New Year falls around 14 April, when the Sun enters the sign of Aries. That is Vaisakhi in Punjab, Vishu in Kerala, Puthandu in Tamil Nadu, [Bohag Bihu](art-bihu) in Assam and Pohela Boishakh in Bengal. Many Gujarati families celebrate New Year the day after [Diwali](diwali-lights).

## Navroz and spring

Nowruz, meaning "new day", is the Persian New Year, held at the spring equinox around 20 or 21 March. It is celebrated in Iran, Central Asia and by many communities elsewhere. In India, Parsis celebrate Jamshedi Navroz in March.

Many Parsis also celebrate another New Year in August. Their traditional calendar never added leap days, so over the centuries it drifted away from the seasons. A missing quarter-day each year, as [leap years](leap-years) explains, adds up to months over a thousand years.

## The first and last to celebrate

The world is divided into time zones, so midnight on 31 December travels around the globe. The Line Islands of Kiribati, in the Pacific, are the first inhabited place to reach 1 January. Samoa and [New Zealand](new-zealand) follow soon after, then Australia, Asia, Europe and the Americas.

India, at five and a half hours ahead of London, celebrates midway through the wave. In New York, a lit ball has dropped from a pole in [Times Square](times-square) at midnight since 1907. The last inhabited places to enter the new year are American Samoa and nearby islands, about 25 hours after Kiribati.
