## The short answer

A movie shows still pictures, called frames, one after another, usually [24 every second](how-cameras-speed).

Shown fast enough, your brain fills in the gaps and sees smooth movement. Each frame is a tiny bit different from the one before: an arm a little higher, a car a little further along. Your brain joins the changes into motion, the same way it tracks a cricket ball or a moving bus.

India's first full-length film, Raja Harishchandra (1913), was made by [Dadasaheb Phalke](dadasaheb-phalke). India's top film award is named after him.

## The galloping horse

In 1878, Eadweard Muybridge set up 12 cameras in a row to photograph a galloping horse. His pictures proved that, for a moment, all four hooves leave the ground.

The horse ran past the cameras, tripping threads that fired each shutter in turn. Later, Muybridge put the pictures on a spinning glass disc and projected them, so audiences saw the horse gallop. It was one of the first steps towards cinema. Modern [cameras](cameras-how) do the same job far faster.

## Your brain at work

Older books said that movies work because each picture "persists" on the eye for a moment. Scientists now think that is not the real reason. Your [eyes](eyes-see) send signals to your brain, and special parts of the brain detect motion by comparing what changes from moment to moment. Show the brain pictures that change smoothly, and it reports movement.

There is a second trick. At 24 frames a second, the screen would flicker. So [projectors](projectors) flash each frame two or three times before moving on. Your eyes see 48 or 72 flashes a second, too fast to notice the flicker.

## Films come to India

The Lumière brothers of France held one of the [first paid film shows](hist-first-film) in [Paris](paris) in 1895. Just months later, in July 1896, their films were shown at Watson's Hotel in Bombay. Indians soon started making short films of their own.

Dadasaheb Phalke made Raja Harishchandra after watching a film about the life of Jesus. With no actresses willing to act, a man played the queen. The film was shown in Bombay in 1913 and was a hit. Phalke went on to make many more films about gods and heroes, using clever camera tricks.

## Why 24?

Silent films had no fixed speed, and cameras were often cranked by hand at about 16 to 18 frames a second. When sound arrived in the late 1920s, the sound was printed on the film itself, so the speed had to be steady. Studios settled on 24 frames a second, and cinema has mostly kept it ever since.

TV is a little different. In India, TV pictures traditionally ran at 25 frames a second, while in the USA they ran at about 30, because of the different [electricity](electricity-made) supply in each country. A few films, such as *The Hobbit* in 2012, have tried 48 frames a second for extra smoothness, but many viewers said it looked too real, like a TV show.

## From film to files

For about a century, movies were shot on long strips of film. A two-hour movie used kilometres of it. Today, most films are shot and shown digitally, as files of numbers on hard drives. But the idea is the same as Muybridge's horse: still pictures, shown fast, and a brain that turns them into a story. From [Satyajit Ray](satyajit-ray) to the latest cartoon, every film depends on it.
