## The short answer

Sports science uses biology, physics, chemistry and psychology to help athletes train better, perform better and stay healthy. Scientists measure how hearts, [lungs](lungs-breathe) and [muscles](muscles) respond to training. They film movements with fast cameras, design better equipment and study [food](sports-nutrition), sleep and the mind.

Small gains add up. In top sport, the gap between first and fourth can be one hundredth of a second, so anything that helps a little can matter a lot.

## Measuring the body

Sports scientists track how hard an athlete is working. Heart-rate monitors show how fast the [heart](heartbeat-exercise) beats during training. Lab tests measure VO2 max, the most oxygen the body can use in a minute, which shows how fit a runner or cyclist is.

Coaches use these numbers to plan training so athletes push hard on some days and recover on others. Too little rest leads to injury and tiredness. That is why many teams now treat [sleep](sleep-why) as part of training.

Nutritionists plan meals so athletes get enough energy before hard sessions and enough protein to repair muscles afterwards. Sports psychologists help athletes handle nerves. One common method is visualisation, rehearsing a race or a penalty kick in the mind before doing it for real.

## Movement on camera

High-speed [cameras](cameras-how) can film thousands of frames a second. Slowed down, they show exactly how a bowler's wrist moves or a sprinter's foot strikes the ground. This study of movement is called biomechanics.

Cricket uses ball-tracking systems such as Hawk-Eye, which use several cameras to work out where a ball went and where it would have gone. Since 2008 these systems have helped umpires make decisions. Coaches also use them to study how bowlers make the ball [swing](cricket-swing) or spin.

## Thinking differently

Sometimes science changes a whole technique. In the 1960s, [Dick Fosbury](sport-science-high-jump) of the USA started high-jumping backwards, head first, arching his back over the bar. Soft foam landing mats made this safe. He won gold at the 1968 Olympics in [Mexico](mexico) City.

The Fosbury Flop works partly because the athlete's centre of mass, the balance point of the body, can pass just under the bar while the arched body goes over it. Almost every top high-jumper now uses it.

## The four-minute mile

For years, people wondered whether anyone could run a mile, about 1.6 km, in under four minutes. Roger Bannister, a British medical student, studied his own breathing and training in the lab.

On 6 May 1954, on a track in Oxford, he ran 3 minutes 59.4 seconds, helped by friends who took turns setting the pace. Within weeks someone else had run faster. Today pacemakers are used in races, including record attempts in the [marathon](running-marathon).

## Shoes, suits and fair play

Since about 2017, [running shoes](sport-science-shoes) with thick foam and a carbon plate have helped runners save energy. In early lab tests, runners used about 4% less energy in them. Records tumbled, and World Athletics introduced limits on how thick soles can be.

Not all help is allowed. Some athletes have cheated by taking banned drugs that build muscle or add red blood cells. The World Anti-Doping Agency, set up in 1999, tests athletes and keeps a list of banned substances.

In India, the Sports Authority of India runs centres where scientists work with athletes. The best advice from all this research is simple enough for anyone: train regularly, eat well, drink water and sleep enough.
