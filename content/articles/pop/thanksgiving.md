## The short answer

Thanksgiving is a national holiday in the United States, held on the fourth Thursday of November. Families gather for a big meal, usually with roast turkey, stuffing, cranberry sauce, mashed [potatoes](potatoes) and pumpkin pie. It is a day for giving thanks, especially for the harvest and for family.

The holiday is often traced to a harvest feast in 1621 at Plymouth, in what is now Massachusetts. English settlers called the Pilgrims shared it with Wampanoag people, who had lived in the area for thousands of years. [Canada](canada) has its own Thanksgiving, on the second Monday of October.

## The 1621 harvest

In 1620, a ship called the *[Mayflower](pilgrims-mayflower)* carried about a hundred English settlers across the Atlantic. Many were seeking freedom to worship in their own way. They arrived as winter began, and about half died in the first months.

The Wampanoag, led by Massasoit, had their own reasons to make an alliance. A Patuxet man named Tisquantum, or Squanto, who spoke English, helped the settlers plant corn. In autumn 1621, after their first harvest, the settlers held a celebration. About 90 Wampanoag men joined them, and the feast lasted three days.

## What they really ate

Only two short written accounts describe the event. One mentions birds, which might have included wild turkey or ducks, and five deer brought by the Wampanoag. There was probably corn, and perhaps fish and shellfish from the coast.

There was almost certainly no pumpkin pie, because the settlers had no ovens or flour for pastry. Potatoes were not yet common in New England. Much of the menu Americans eat today came later, during the 1800s. Turkey became the centre of the meal partly because it was large enough to feed a whole family and was raised on many American farms. Cranberries, which grow in bogs in New England, became the standard sauce.

## Becoming a national holiday

For two centuries, different colonies and states held days of thanksgiving at different times. A magazine editor named Sarah Josepha Hale wrote letters for about 17 years asking for one national day.

In 1863, during the [Civil War](us-civil-war), President [Abraham Lincoln](abraham-lincoln) declared a national day of thanksgiving on the last Thursday of November. In 1941, Congress fixed it as the fourth Thursday. That's why it moves between 22 and 28 November, as [calendars](calendars) can explain.

## Parades, football and turkeys

Since 1924, Macy's department store has held a Thanksgiving Day parade in New York City, with [giant balloons](made-balloons) floating between [skyscrapers](skyscrapers). Professional [American football](american-football) games on Thanksgiving have been a tradition since the 1920s, and millions watch on TV after lunch.

Every year, the President "pardons" a turkey at the [White House](white-house), so it is not eaten. The day after Thanksgiving, called Black Friday, is one of the busiest shopping days of the year. For many families, though, the best part is simpler: relatives who live far apart travel home, and airports and highways are packed for days before the holiday.

## A day of mourning too

For many Native Americans, Thanksgiving recalls the start of great losses. Disease, war and the taking of land devastated [Native nations](native-nations) after the settlers arrived. Since 1970, some Native Americans have gathered at Plymouth on Thanksgiving for a National Day of Mourning.

Many schools now teach both sides of the story. The idea of a harvest festival of thanks is shared by cultures around the world. India has [Pongal and Onam](onam-pongal), Lohri and [Bihu](art-bihu), each celebrating a harvest with its own [special food](festival-foods).
