## The short answer

Makar Sankranti marks the moment the Sun appears to move into the sign of Makara, or Capricorn, in the Indian [zodiac](?zodiac). From now on, the days in India grow longer and the cold begins to ease. Farmers bring in their winter crops, and families celebrate with sesame sweets, bonfires and, in many places, kites.

Nobody can say for certain why kites became part of the day. Some say it gets people outdoors into the healthy winter sun. Others link it to the Sun's "climb" in the sky. Mostly, it is a tradition that grew because it is joyful, and once a whole city does it together, it sticks.

## A festival with many names

The same day is celebrated across India under different names. In Gujarat it is Uttarayan, from the Sanskrit for the Sun's northward journey. Tamil Nadu celebrates [Pongal](onam-pongal) by boiling new rice until it overflows. Punjab lights Lohri bonfires the night before, and Assam feasts during [Magh Bihu](art-bihu).

Because Sankranti follows the Sun, it lands on 14 or 15 January almost every year. Festivals that follow the Moon, such as Holi or Diwali, wander around by several weeks.

## Rooftops at war

In [Ahmedabad](ahmedabad), Uttarayan is a two-day battle. Families climb onto their roofs at dawn with stacks of kites and spools of string. The aim is to cut a rival's string with your own. When a kite falls, the winners shout "Kai po che!", [Gujarati](marathi-gujarati) for "it's cut!". Children then race through the lanes to catch the loose kite drifting down.

Since 1989, Gujarat has also held an International Kite Festival. Flyers arrive from dozens of countries with giant dragon kites, kites shaped like fish and long trains of kites on a single line. [Jaipur](jaipur), Hyderabad and many towns in Rajasthan and Telangana have their own kite days too, and in Delhi people fly kites on Independence Day instead.

## How a fighter kite works

An [Indian fighter kite](pop-kites-patang), or *patang*, is a diamond of thin paper on two [bamboo](bamboo) sticks, with no tail. That makes it unstable, which sounds bad but is the point. A skilled flyer can make it dart and dive with tiny tugs of the string. Letting the string out quickly makes the kite swoop; pulling it in makes it climb. In a fight, the trick is to slide your string across your rival's at speed, so it saws through.

The string near the kite, called manja, was traditionally coated with rice paste and powdered glass to make it sharp. To learn how any kite stays up at all, see [how kites fly](kite-flying).

## The problem with sharp string

Sharp manja cuts more than other strings. Every January, animal hospitals in Gujarat and Rajasthan treat thousands of injured pigeons, kites (the birds) and even [vultures](vultures-india). Riders on two-wheelers have also been badly hurt by strings stretched across roads.

Cheap nylon string, often called "Chinese manja", is worse because it barely breaks. In 2017, the National Green Tribunal banned nylon and synthetic manja across India. Many cities now run bird rescue camps during the festival, and plain cotton string is making a comeback.

## Why the date has drifted

About 1,700 years ago, Makar Sankranti fell close to the winter [solstice](?solstice), the shortest day of the year. The Earth's spin axis wobbles very slowly, like a spinning top, and this has shifted the stars against the seasons. Today the solstice comes around 21 December, while Sankranti stays in mid-January. The longer days that kite flyers celebrate had actually started three weeks earlier.
