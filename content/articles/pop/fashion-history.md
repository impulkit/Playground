## The short answer

Fashion keeps changing because clothes say something about the people who wear them. When a style becomes common, people who want to stand out move on to something new. Others then copy that, and the cycle starts again.

New inventions, trade and events also push fashion along. A new dye, fabric or machine can make a style cheap enough for everyone. Wars, music, films and now [social media](social-media) can make a look popular almost overnight. Some sociologists think fashion moves in cycles, with old styles returning every few decades, though that is hard to prove.

## Standing out, then fitting in

In 1904, the German thinker Georg Simmel suggested that fashion "trickles down". Rich people wear something new to show their status. Others copy it, and the rich move on. In the past, this was easy to see, because fine cloth and dyes were costly.

Today the flow often goes the other way. Styles from skateboarders, musicians and teenagers, such as [sneakers](sneakers) and hoodies, are picked up by designers and sold in expensive shops. Either way, fashion is a balance between fitting in with a group and standing out from it.

## Colour that cost a fortune

For most of history, bright colours were hard to make. Red came from insects or roots, and blue came from indigo plants, much of it grown in India. Purple was the rarest of all. In the ancient Mediterranean it came from sea snails, and thousands were needed to dye one robe.

Then, in 1856, [William Perkin](accident-mauve), an 18-year-old student in London, was trying to make quinine, a malaria medicine, from coal tar. Instead, he got a purple goo. He realised it could dye silk, and named it mauve. Within a few years, mauve dresses were everywhere, and chemists rushed to invent new colours.

## Machines and factories

During the [Industrial Revolution](industrial-revolution), machines began spinning and weaving cloth far faster than people could by hand. Cotton became cheap. In the 1850s, the [sewing machine](sewing-machines) spread, and factories could make ready-made clothes in standard sizes.

Before this, most people owned only a few outfits, often mended for years. Now, clothes could change with the seasons. Shops published catalogues, and magazines told readers what was "in". Later, cheap synthetic fibres such as nylon and polyester arrived in the 1900s.

## Clothes as a message

Sometimes clothes carry a political message. During India's [freedom struggle](freedom-struggle), Mahatma Gandhi urged Indians to spin and wear [khadi](khadi), handwoven cloth, instead of factory cloth from Britain. Burning foreign cloth and wearing khadi became a way of saying "we want to rule ourselves".

Other events changed fashion too. During the [Second World War](world-war-two), cloth was rationed, so skirts got shorter and suits simpler. In the 1960s, young people in India and the USA wore bell-bottom trousers, bright prints and long hair, partly to show they were different from their parents.

## Fast fashion and slow fashion

Today, some companies can design a style, make it and put it in shops within a few weeks. This is called fast fashion. It makes clothes cheap, but it also uses huge amounts of water and energy, and many clothes are thrown away after being worn only a few times.

Some people now choose "slow fashion": buying fewer, better clothes, mending them and swapping or buying second-hand. A handloom saree passed from grandmother to granddaughter is slow fashion that has been around for centuries, as [saree weaving](weaving-saree) shows.
