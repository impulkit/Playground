## The short answer

India has loved comics for decades. In the 1960s and 1970s, children read American heroes such as the Phantom and Mandrake in Indrajal Comics, printed by the Times of India group from 1964. Then came Indian creations: *Amar Chitra Katha* retold myths and history, *Chacha Chaudhary* solved problems with his quick brain, and *[Tinkle](tinkle)* [filled magazines](childrens-magazines) with Suppandi and Shikari Shambu.

In the 1980s and 1990s, Raj Comics brought Indian superheroes such as Nagraj and Super Commando Dhruva. Many of these characters are still in print, now also in apps, cartoons and films. To learn how comics are drawn, see [the art of comics](comics-art).

## A quiz that started a company

In 1967, Anant Pai, an editor in Bombay, watched [a TV quiz](tv-quiz-shows) from Delhi. The students knew the answers about Greek myths but could not say who Rama's mother was. Pai decided Indian children needed their own stories in a form they enjoyed.

He started *Amar Chitra Katha*, which means "immortal picture stories". At first the series printed Western [fairy tales](fairy-tales), but from 1969 it focused on Indian epics, gods, saints, kings and freedom fighters. Children called Pai "Uncle Pai".

## Chacha Chaudhary and Sabu

In 1971, the cartoonist Pran Kumar Sharma, known as Pran, created Chacha Chaudhary. He is a small old man with a white moustache, a red turban and a walking stick. He outwits robbers and fixes problems with common sense.

His friend Sabu is a giant from the planet [Jupiter](jupiter-giant). When Sabu gets angry, a volcano erupts somewhere on Jupiter, or so the comics say. Chacha Chaudhary appeared in Hindi and many other languages, and later became a TV series.

## Tinkle and its readers

Anant Pai also founded *Tinkle* magazine in 1980. It mixed comics with puzzles, science facts and stories. Its best-known character, Suppandi, is a servant who takes every instruction literally. Shikari Shambu is a hunter who is terrified of animals but somehow always ends up a hero.

Tinkle often printed ideas sent in by readers, so a child in Pune or Patna might see their own joke turned into a comic. Stories from the [Panchatantra](panchatantra-tales) and tales of [Akbar and Birbal](akbar-birbal) also appeared often in its pages.

## Raj Comics and Indian superheroes

In the 1980s, Raj Comics, based in Delhi, created a wave of Indian superheroes. [Nagraj](myths-indian-creatures), the "snake king", first appeared in 1986 and can release tiny snakes from his body. Super Commando Dhruva, who began in 1987, has no superpowers at all. He relies on training, science and his ability to talk to animals.

These comics were sold at railway station bookstalls and lent out by small "comic libraries" for a few rupees a day. Children swapped them, collected them and argued over who was strongest. They borrowed ideas from American [superheroes](superheroes-history) but gave them Indian cities and Indian myths.

## Comics today

Fewer comics are sold on paper than in the 1980s, but many have moved online. New Indian [graphic novels](graphic-novels) for older readers tell stories about history, cities and everyday life. Some cartoonists post comics on [social media](social-media) and gather huge followings.

Old favourites still sell too. Parents who grew up with *Amar Chitra Katha* and *Tinkle* now buy them for their children, and the jokes about Suppandi still land.
