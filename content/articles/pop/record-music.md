## The short answer

[Sound](?sound) is a wobble in the air. The first recorders turned that wobble into a wiggly groove.

Play the groove back, and a needle wobbles the same way, rebuilding the sound.

Today, music is stored as numbers ([binary](zero-to-computers)) and sent over [the internet](internet-how). In between came wax cylinders, shellac discs, vinyl records, magnetic tape and CDs, each one making music cheaper, longer and easier to carry.

## The voice on paper

In the 1850s, a French printer named Édouard-Léon Scott de Martinville built a machine he called the phonautograph. Sound made a thin sheet vibrate, and a bristle attached to it traced wavy lines onto paper blackened with soot. He wanted to study the shapes of sounds, not hear them again.

In 2008, scientists in the USA scanned one of his papers from 1860 and turned the lines back into sound using a computer. Out came a faint voice singing a French folk song, "Au clair de la lune". It is the oldest known recording of a human voice.

## Edison's tinfoil

In 1877, Thomas Edison in New Jersey built the phonograph. He shouted into a mouthpiece, and a needle pressed a groove into tinfoil wrapped around a turning cylinder. When he ran the needle back along the groove, the machine spoke back: "Mary had a little lamb."

Ten years later, Emile Berliner invented the gramophone, which used flat discs instead of cylinders. Discs could be stamped out by the thousand from one master copy, which made records cheap enough for ordinary families.

## Gauhar Jaan in Calcutta

In 1902, the Gramophone Company sent [recording engineers](behind-music-recording) to [Calcutta](kolkata). There they recorded Gauhar Jaan, a famous singer and dancer. She was one of the first Indian singers recorded on a gramophone disc, and became India's first recording star. She went on to record hundreds of songs in more than ten languages.

Each side of a disc held only about three minutes, so singers squeezed long ragas into short versions. At the end of each recording, Gauhar Jaan announced, "My name is Gauhar Jaan", so the engineers could label the discs.

## Tape, vinyl and cassettes

Early recordings were made without [electricity](electricity-made): performers crowded around a big horn. From the 1920s, [microphones](microphones) turned sound into electrical signals, giving much clearer recordings. During the 1930s and 1940s, magnetic tape arrived, letting engineers cut and join recordings.

In 1948, long-playing vinyl records held over 20 minutes a side. Bands like the [Beatles](beatles-india) sold millions. In 1963, Philips introduced the compact cassette. Cheap cassette players and recorders arrived in India in the 1980s, and companies such as T-Series sold [film songs](film-songs) and devotional music at low prices. Music shops opened in towns that had never had a record shop.

## Music as numbers

In 1982, compact discs went on sale. Inside a CD, sound is measured 44,100 times every second, and each measurement is stored as a number in [binary](binary-code). A laser reads the numbers, and a chip turns them back into sound.

Then came MP3 files, which shrank music by throwing away sounds most ears do not notice. Today, [streaming](streaming) services send those numbers to your phone over the internet in a moment. A song that once needed a needle, a horn and a turning disc now fits in your pocket, along with millions of others.
