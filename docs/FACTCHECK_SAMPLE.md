## Articles with problems

### sky/airplanes-fly.md

1. **WRONG.** Fact box: "India's first airmail flight: 1932, flown by J.R.D. Tata".
   What's true: India's first official airmail flight was on 18 February 1911. The French pilot Henri Pequet flew about 6,500 letters from Allahabad to Naini. Tata's 15 October 1932 Karachi–Bombay flight was the first flight of Tata Air Services, India's first Indian-owned commercial airline. It was not the first airmail.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Pequet ; https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/106718-first-airmail-flight
2. **DOUBTFUL.** Body: "It was India's first scheduled airmail flight."
   What's true: this can't safely be called "first". Imperial Airways was already flying scheduled airmail to Karachi, then part of British India, from 1929. A safer wording: "the first flight of India's first Indian-owned airline, carrying mail".
   Source: general aviation history. The 1929 Imperial Airways service is from my background knowledge and should be confirmed before editing. The overview at https://kbhargava.com/flyingtales/aviation-in-india-a-peep-into-its-early-history.html came up in search but I did not read it in full.

### places/antarctica.md

1. **WRONG.** "In 1959, twelve countries signed the Antarctic Treaty. … Military bases, weapons tests and mining are banned."
   What's true: the 1959 Treaty bans military activity and weapons tests. The mining ban came 32 years later, in the 1991 Madrid Protocol (Protocol on Environmental Protection), Article 7.
   Source: https://www.antarctica.gov.au/about-antarctica/law-and-treaty/the-madrid-protocol/
   - Minor note, not counted: the hook "Nobody is born and raised in Antarctica" is accurate as worded. But about 11 babies have been born there, starting with Emilio Palma in 1978, so a child repeating it as "nobody is born in Antarctica" would be wrong.

### wild/bees-dance.md

1. **WRONG.** "India is home to three wild honeybee species." The fact box also lists only the giant rock bee, the Asian hive bee and the dwarf honeybee.
   What's true: India has at least four native *Apis* species. The fourth is the Himalayan giant honeybee, *Apis laboriosa*. A fifth, the black dwarf honeybee *A. andreniformis*, is recorded in Northeast India. The introduced Western honeybee is also widely kept. Correct wording would be "India has several native honeybee species, including…".
   Source: https://wgbis.ces.iisc.ac.in/biodiversity/sahyadri_enews/newsletter/issue46/bibliography/47_Distributions%20of%20recently%20recognized%20species%20of%20honey%20bees.pdf ; https://thewildcone.com/meet-indias-honey-bees-from-little-florea-to-giant-dorsata/beekeeping/

### lost/trex-compare.md

1. **DOUBTFUL.** "Rajasaurus length: about 8–9 m", "probably a few tonnes at most". The article is also marked `certainty: sure`.
   What's true: published estimates range widely. Grillo & Delcourt (2016) give about 6.6 m. Gregory S. Paul (2010) gives about 11 m and about 4 tonnes. The 8–9 m middle value is reasonable, but it should be shown as an estimate with a range. `certainty: sure` contradicts the article's own certainty note.
   Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rajasaurus

### food/tomatoes.md

1. **WRONG.** "In 1544, an Italian writer named Pietro Andrea Mattioli described a yellow kind and called it *pomi d'oro*."
   What's true: Mattioli first described the tomato in 1544 without naming it. He first called it *pomi d'oro* in a later edition of his herbal, about ten years later (dated 1554 or 1555 depending on the source).
   Source: https://tenochtitlan.omeka.net/exhibits/show/gazpacho/tomato--pomodoro--pomi-d-oro
2. **DOUBTFUL.** Fact box: "First farmed: Mexico".
   What's true: this is debated. A 2020 genomic study (Razifard et al.) suggests people first cultivated a semi-domesticated tomato in Ecuador and Peru. Weedy forms then spread north and were domesticated again in Mexico. Something like "Fully domesticated: Mexico (early steps probably in Peru and Ecuador)" would be accurate.
   Source: https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/01/200107170110.htm

### space/space-junk.md

1. **OUTDATED.** Fact box: "Tracked pieces bigger than 10 cm: more than 35,000". Body: "more than 35,000 pieces larger than 10 cm … big enough to track".
   What's true: ESA's Space Environment Report 2025 says about 40,000 objects are tracked, and it estimates more than 50,000 objects larger than 10 cm. The 35,000 figure is from older reports.
   Source: https://www.esa.int/Space_Safety/Space_Debris/ESA_Space_Environment_Report_2025

### places/tokyo.md

1. **SOURCE MISATTRIBUTED.** "Tokyo Metropolitan Government, disaster prevention and the Metropolitan Area Outer Underground Discharge Channel."
   What's true: the Discharge Channel is in Kasukabe, Saitama Prefecture, and is run by the national Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (Kanto Regional Development Bureau, Edogawa River Office). The Tokyo Metropolitan Government doesn't run it, so this source as written probably doesn't exist.
   Source: https://www.ktr.mlit.go.jp/ktr_content/content/000812778.pdf ; https://www.japan.travel/en/japans-local-treasures/underground-wonder-protecting-tokyo/
2. **DOUBTFUL (minor).** Tank "177 m long and about 25 m high".
   What's true: the length (177 m) and the 59 pillars are right. For the height, sources give either 18 m or about 25 m, so "about 25 m" isn't safe as a single figure.
   Source: https://www.japan.travel/en/japans-local-treasures/underground-wonder-protecting-tokyo/

### sky/monarch-migration.md

1. **WRONG.** surprising_fact: "The butterflies that fly back north are the great-great-grandchildren of the ones that left."
   What's true: the butterflies that leave Mexico in spring and fly north are the same individuals that flew south (the long-lived "super generation"). The article's own body says so: "In spring, the same butterflies fly north to Texas". It's the butterflies that fly south the *next* autumn that are the great-great-grandchildren. The fact contradicts the body.
   Source: the article's own body text (above). Also Reppert & de Roode (2018), "Demystifying monarch butterfly migration", *Current Biology* 28, which the article itself cites. The contradiction can be seen inside the article, so I fetched no web page for this one.
2. **OUTDATED.** "In 2020 the volunteers found fewer than 2,000 … The next year they counted about 2.5 lakh. Scientists are still working out … whether the recovery will last."
   What's true: the recovery did not last. The western count fell to 9,119 in the 2024 season, down 96% from 233,394 in 2023 and the second-lowest on record. The 2025 season count (released January 2026) stayed near that historic low.
   Source: https://globalnews.ca/news/11001369/monarch-butterfly-count-drop-low-record/amp ; https://xerces.org/press/western-monarch-numbers-remain-at-historic-low
   - Note, not counted: "In 2024, the US government proposed protecting the monarch as a threatened species" is still true. Readers may assume it was finalised, though. The December 2025 deadline was missed, and a September 2026 consent decree gives the US Fish and Wildlife Service until 6 June 2030 to decide. Source: https://www.dtnpf.com/agriculture/web/ag/news/article/2026/10/23/monarch-listing-decision-delayed

### lost/stegosaurus.md

1. **SOURCE LOOKS INVENTED.** "Maidment S.C.R. et al. (2015) Body mass and posture of Stegosaurus. PLoS ONE 10."
   What's true: no paper with this title exists. It looks like two real 2015 papers merged into one. The first is Maidment, Brassey & Barrett (2015), "The postcranial skeleton of an exceptionally complete individual of the plated dinosaur *Stegosaurus stenops*…", *PLoS ONE* 10(10): e0138352. The second is Brassey, Maidment & Barrett (2015), "Body mass estimates of an exceptionally complete *Stegosaurus*…", *Biology Letters* 11: 20140984.
   Source: https://research.brighton.ac.uk/en/publications/the-postcranial-skeleton-of-an-exceptionally-complete-individual-/
   - The article's facts all checked out: 1877 naming by Marsh, the 1982 *Far Side* "thagomizer", Sophie at the NHM, the Allosaurus vertebra wound, and the Portugal finds.

### numbers/fibonacci-nature.md

1. **DOUBTFUL.** "Similar ideas link these numbers to the golden ratio, and to the notes of a sitar." The interactive also says Pingala "found this pattern", but the body says Pingala's work only "hints at this".
   What's true: there is no established link between Fibonacci numbers and sitar notes. The sentence reads like a cross-link was forced into the text. On Pingala, the body is correct: his *Chandaḥśāstra* hints at the pattern, and Virahanka is the first to state it clearly. The interactive frame overstates this.
   Source: Singh P. (1985), "The so-called Fibonacci numbers in ancient and medieval India", *Historia Mathematica* 12. This is the article's own cited source, and it covers Pingala and Virahanka with no mention of the sitar.

## Suitability and tone

**Suitability for 8–13 year olds.** Nothing is unsuitable. Three articles handle sensitive topics carefully and at the right level: *puberty-basics* (periods, body changes, a nudge to talk to a trusted adult), *jazz* (slavery and segregation) and *silk* (silkworm pupae are killed, with ahimsa silk mentioned). The try_this activities that need sharp tools or heat say to involve an adult.

**Signs of machine-written text.** The prose is mostly clean, plain and readable. A few patterns do read as generated rather than written by a person:
- **Forced cross-links** that bolt an unrelated topic onto a paragraph. Examples: the *fibonacci* sitar sentence; *trex-compare* moving from dinosaurs to lions and tigers in Gir; *bees-dance* adding "Some plants, like figs, rely on other insects instead"; and *cricket-swing* ending "Like the spinners…".
- **Link-dump endings.** The last paragraph of *panchayat* packs in municipalities, states and village life, ending "To see the wider picture, read how…". There are also tacked-on "Read more in [x]" / "Read how … in [x]" sentences (*indus-cities*, *jazz*, *beethoven*, *clouds-rain*).
- **Template sameness.** Every article follows the same structure: "The short answer", a vivid hook, a "{{story}}" mid-break, and a neat summarising last line ("frozen in stone", "a brain that turns them into a story"). *clouds-rain* opens with one-sentence paragraphs that look like a list turned into prose.
- **Internal inconsistencies between front matter and body** (monarch generations, Pingala, Rajasaurus `certainty: sure`). These suggest the parts were written separately and never read side by side.

## Overall assessment

Kyun's accuracy is good: across about 410 checked claims, 30 of the 40 sampled articles had no problems at all, and only 5 claims were clearly wrong, about one per eight articles. Every quiz answer was correct. Dates, measurements and records were overwhelmingly right, including many India-specific details that are easy to get wrong. The errors that do occur fall into four patterns:
1. **"First" or "only" claims stated too strongly or tied to the wrong event:** India's first airmail, the 1959 Treaty mining ban, three honeybee species, the date of *pomi d'oro*.
2. **Front-matter "fact" lines that drift from or contradict the body:** monarch generations, Pingala, Rajasaurus certainty. The short fact lines are the most likely to be repeated, so they need the closest checking.
3. **Changing numbers that have gone stale:** space-debris counts and western monarch numbers.
4. **Contested estimates presented as settled fact:** Rajasaurus size, where tomatoes were first farmed.

Two of the roughly 100 citations look invented or misattributed: the garbled Stegosaurus PLoS ONE title, and the Tokyo Metropolitan Government credited for a national-ministry facility. A quick check of every source list for exact titles is worthwhile.
